Part X — Stalinism and the Second World War
60

Victory, Reconstruction, and Late Stalinism, 1943–1953

A History of Russia: From the Eurasian World to the Russian Federation

Chapter Opening

Victory in 1945 ended the war against Nazi Germany, but it did not end the war's consequences. It left the Soviet Union simultaneously triumphant, devastated, and newly powerful — and it left Soviet citizens to live with all three conditions at once.

In the last hours of 8 May 1945 by Central European time, in a former military engineering school in the Berlin suburb of Karlshorst, representatives of the German High Command signed an act of unconditional surrender before Soviet, American, British, and French officers. Marshal Georgy Zhukov presided for the Soviet side; Field Marshal Wilhelm Keitel signed for Germany. Because Moscow time was two hours ahead of Berlin, the ceremony ended after midnight by Soviet reckoning, and the Soviet government therefore announced and has ever since commemorated the victory on 9 May, whereas most of Western Europe marks 8 May. The discrepancy is a small example of a large problem that this chapter addresses: the same war was experienced, dated, named, and remembered differently by different societies and states.

That same war had also been fought within the Soviet Union's own population in ways that the Karlshorst ceremony did not record. Fifteen months earlier, in February 1944, tens of thousands of Chechen and Ingush families in the North Caucasus had been loaded onto freight trains by NKVD troops and carried to Kazakhstan and Kyrgyzstan on the accusation, made against entire peoples, of collective treason. Many of the men of those peoples were at that moment serving in, or had served in, the Red Army. This chapter therefore studies victory and coercion together. The Red Army that destroyed the Wehrmacht's eastern armies was also an instrument of a state that deported nationalities, screened its own returning prisoners, and extended its political control over territories it had liberated from German occupation.

The chapter begins in the first weeks of 1943, with the destruction of the German Sixth Army at Stalingrad, where Chapter 59 ended, and it concludes with Stalin's death on 5 March 1953, where Chapter 61 begins. Its central question is how the Soviet Union achieved military victory in 1943–1945, transformed that victory into international power, and then confronted the contradictions of reconstruction and renewed Stalinist control between 1945 and 1953. The chapter is organized in six divisions: the strategic turning point of 1943; liberation and the campaigns of 1944; the defeat of Germany and the end of the war; the human cost of victory and the new international order; reconstruction and postwar society; and the political, ideological, and security campaigns of late Stalinism.

The people who experienced these years were extraordinarily varied. They include Red Army soldiers drawn from every Soviet nationality, partisans in Belarus and Ukraine, Jewish survivors of occupation, Poles in Warsaw and Lviv, Germans fleeing the Red Army, Baltic peasants resisting renewed Sovietization, deported Karachai, Kalmyks, Chechens, Ingush, Balkars, and Crimean Tatars, women who rebuilt cities while mourning husbands and brothers, collective farmers in drought-stricken Moldova and Ukraine, writers and composers condemned by party resolutions, Jewish intellectuals arrested as "cosmopolitans," and the leaders of a party-state that prepared for the Cold War. The Soviet Union was not Russia; its victory was won, and its costs were borne, by many peoples, and the chapter distinguishes among them wherever the evidence permits.

The evidence is uneven. Wartime Soviet communiqués, military memoirs (notably those of Zhukov and other commanders, many written under censorship), and Stalin-era histories were shaped by political demands. Since 1991, archival openings in Moscow, Kyiv, Minsk, Vilnius, Riga, and Tallinn have made available operational records, NKVD reports, Politburo decisions, and correspondence that allow historians to test older narratives, although many files, especially in security-service archives, remain restricted. Casualty statistics are contested, and the categories in which they are compiled differ. The chapter therefore tries to distinguish documented events from estimates and from later memory, in keeping with the method developed throughout this textbook.

Finally, the chapter connects the Soviet experience to larger international developments: the Grand Alliance with Britain and the United States, the political reconstruction of Eastern and Central Europe, the Pacific war, the origins of the Cold War, and the formation of a bipolar international system. It prepares for Chapter 61, which treats the succession struggle, the Secret Speech, and de-Stalinization, and for Chapter 62, which examines Khrushchev-era Cold War confrontation. Students should resist reading these years as a mere prelude to what followed. Contemporaries did not know that Stalin would die in March 1953; they lived in a society that had won a great war, lost a vast portion of its people, and was ruled by a state that retained enormous capacity for both construction and coercion.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why the Red Army's operational effectiveness increased between Stalingrad (1943) and Bagration (1944), considering planning, logistics, Allied assistance, and German strategic errors.
  2. Trace the principal campaigns from Stalingrad to Berlin and to Manchuria, locating them geographically and chronologically.
  3. Distinguish liberation from Nazi occupation from the establishment of subsequent Soviet political control in the Baltic region, western Ukraine, Poland, and Southeastern Europe.
  4. Analyze the 1943–1944 deportations of Karachai, Kalmyks, Chechens, Ingush, Balkars, Crimean Tatars, and others as collective punishment, using documentary evidence.
  5. Evaluate the evidence and methodological problems behind estimates of Soviet human and material losses.
  6. Assess how Yalta, Potsdam, and Soviet occupation policies contributed to the political transformation of Eastern Europe, while recognizing differences among individual countries.
  7. Explain the transition from the wartime alliance to the early Cold War without treating Soviet or Western actions as the sole cause.
  8. Compare industrial reconstruction with household living standards, agricultural conditions, and the famine of 1946–1947.
  9. Interpret the postwar ideological campaigns in literature, music, science, and the humanities as instruments of late Stalinist governance.
  10. Distinguish documented facts from disputed hypotheses concerning the anti-Jewish campaigns, the Doctors' Plot, and Stalin's final years.
  11. Evaluate competing interpretations of late Stalinism and the legacy of Stalin's rule.
  12. Connect the chapter's developments to Chapter 61 and to later questions of Soviet nationality, memory, and international power.

Historical Context

Chapters 55 through 58 traced the construction of the Stalinist system in the 1930s: forced industrialization, collectivization, the famines of 1932–1933, the Great Terror, the Gulag, socialist realism, and the shifting nationality policy that culminated in deportations of "diaspora nationalities" before the war. Chapter 59 then followed the Soviet Union from the Molotov-Ribbentrop Pact and the annexations of 1939–1941 through Operation Barbarossa, the German occupation, the sieges and mass violence, the Battle of Moscow, and the extraordinary mobilization of the economy and population. By November 1942 the Red Army had survived two summers of catastrophic defeats and had prepared a counteroffensive at Stalingrad on the Volga. The state that entered 1943 had been transformed by war: industries had been evacuated to the Urals, western Siberia, and Central Asia; the party had made concessions to Russian national symbolism and to the Orthodox Church; the Comintern was dissolved in May 1943; and commanders had acquired greater professional autonomy than they had possessed before 1941.

The war's scale on the Eastern Front shaped the entire period. Most of the German army's fighting strength was committed against the Soviet Union until the last months of the war, and the great majority of German military deaths occurred there. The Soviet war effort also depended on a combination of domestic and external resources. The evacuated heavy industry produced tanks, aircraft, and artillery in large volume, while Allied deliveries under American Lend-Lease, especially of trucks, locomotives and rails, aviation fuel, food, and industrial materials, supplemented Soviet production and improved mobility from 1943 onward. Historians differ on how much weight to assign Allied aid relative to Soviet production, but there is broad agreement that it mattered most in transport, specialized materials, and food supply rather than in the first-line weapons that defined the battles of 1942–1943.

The territories that the Red Army reconquered had themselves been transformed. Parts of the western Soviet Union had been annexed in 1939–1940 (eastern Poland, the Baltic states, Bessarabia and northern Bukovina), had endured a Soviet occupation, and had then lived under German or allied Romanian administration, in which mass killing of Jews, repression of civilians, forced labor deportations to the Reich, and brutal anti-partisan warfare were routine. Local populations had responded in many ways: resistance, survival, accommodation, collaboration, and, in some regions, the formation of nationalist armed movements that opposed both Germany and the Soviet Union. When Soviet power returned it was often welcomed as the end of German occupation, but it also revived the issues of 1939–1941 that populations of the borderlands had not forgotten.

Students should also understand the terminology. The Second World War, as the global conflict, is conventionally dated 1939–1945, with its Asian and Pacific theaters ending formally on 2 September 1945. The Great Patriotic War (Velikaya Otechestvennaya voina) is the Soviet and Russian name for the war against Germany and its allies from 22 June 1941 to 9 May 1945. The name evokes the Patriotic War of 1812 (see Chapter 36) and was adopted in 1941 in the wartime appeal to national defense. It excludes the Soviet-German partnership of 1939–1941 and the Soviet war against Japan, a periodization that this chapter will keep in view when it discusses both memory and history.

Division I — The Strategic Turning Point, 1943

Section 60.1

Stalingrad: The Destruction of the Sixth Army

Chapter 59 left the German Sixth Army and elements of the Fourth Panzer Army encircled in the ruins of Stalingrad after Operation Uranus, launched on 19 November 1942, closed the ring around them on 23 November. Hitler, relying on Hermann Göring's assurance that the Luftwaffe could supply the pocket by air, forbade a breakout. The airlift never approached the roughly several hundred tons a day the garrison required, and rations, fuel, and ammunition steadily declined. Manstein's relief attempt, Operation Winter Storm, began on 12 December and came within roughly fifty kilometers of the pocket before it was halted by Soviet reserves and by the simultaneous Soviet Operation Little Saturn, which struck the Italian Eighth Army on the middle Don and threatened to cut off the relief force and the entire German southern wing. By Christmas the Sixth Army's last chance of rescue had passed.

Inside the pocket, hundreds of thousands of men, including Romanian and Croatian units and several thousand Soviet auxiliaries (Hiwis) serving the Germans, endured freezing temperatures, hunger, typhus, and bombardment. On 10 January 1943 the Soviet Don Front under Konstantin Rokossovsky began Operation Ring (Koltso), preceded by an ultimatum that the German command rejected. Soviet forces compressed the pocket in three weeks of attritional fighting; the airfields were overrun and the last wounded could no longer be evacuated. On 30 January Hitler promoted Friedrich Paulus to field marshal, an implicit invitation to die rather than surrender. Paulus surrendered in the southern pocket on 31 January, and the northern pocket under General Karl Strecker capitulated on 2 February 1943.

Roughly 90,000 Axis prisoners were taken at the end, a figure commonly given at about 91,000; many were already weakened by disease and starvation, and only a small fraction, often estimated at about 5,000, ever returned from Soviet captivity. The surrounding battle, which had begun in the summer of 1942, cost the Red Army very heavy casualties, and the city lay almost entirely in ruins; civilians who remained had endured bombing and street fighting throughout. The military consequences were significant: the Germans lost a field army, the southern flank collapsed temporarily, and Axis allies were badly damaged. The psychological and political consequences were equally important. The Soviet press celebrated the victory; Stalin was named Marshal of the Soviet Union in March 1943; and German propaganda began to speak of "total war." Historians remain careful, however, not to treat Stalingrad as the single decisive event of the war; it is better understood as the most dramatic moment in a series of Soviet victories, and the German army remained a formidable opponent for two more years.

Section 60.2

The Winter Campaigns of 1943 and the Struggle for the Strategic Initiative

Encouraged by the Stalingrad victory and by the collapse of the Axis position on the Don, the Stavka launched a series of ambitious offensives in January–February 1943 to exploit German disarray across the southern front. Soviet forces retook Voronezh, Kursk (8 February), Belgorod, and, on 16 February, Kharkiv. In the Caucasus, German forces withdrew from the Kuban and the oil regions they had never captured in full. The Soviet general staff, however, overestimated German weakness and underestimated the speed with which Manstein could concentrate his remaining armor. Soviet armies, advancing rapidly after weeks of fighting, were overstretched, short of fuel and ammunition, and operated on lengthening lines of supply across thawing ground.

Manstein's counteroffensive, launched in mid-February 1943 with fresh panzer divisions including the SS Panzer Corps, struck the exposed Soviet armies in the Donbas and around Kharkiv. German forces retook Kharkiv on 14–15 March and Belgorod soon afterward, and the front stabilized with the Soviet-held salient around Kursk bulging into German lines. The setback demonstrated that the Red Army could win defensive battles and encircle isolated forces but did not yet consistently conduct successful deep offensives against a prepared and skilled opponent. It also showed that German operational skill, especially in mobile counterattack, remained dangerous, and that Soviet planning had not yet integrated logistics and reserves as effectively as it would in 1944.

The spring thaw (rasputitsa) halted large operations. In March 1943 the Germans evacuated the Rzhev salient in front of Moscow (Operation Buffalo), shortening their front, and both sides rebuilt. For the Soviet side, the interval involved the rapid replacement of equipment, the expansion of tank armies, and organizational changes such as increased use of combined arms, reserves held at the Stavka's disposal, and greater artillery concentration. For the Germans, the interval led to the planning that produced Operation Citadel. The struggle for the strategic initiative in spring 1943, in other words, was not simply a prelude to Kursk; it taught both sides lessons that influenced the decisions made there.

Section 60.3

The Battle of Kursk, July–August 1943

By the spring of 1943 the Soviet-held salient near Kursk, roughly two hundred kilometers wide, offered the German command an apparent opportunity: pincer attacks from north and south could cut off large Soviet forces and perhaps restore the initiative lost at Stalingrad. The plan, Operation Citadel (Zitadelle), was postponed several times while Hitler awaited new Panther and Tiger tanks and self-propelled guns. The delay gave the Soviet side time. Soviet intelligence, including reports from agents in Switzerland and Britain and the evidence of aerial reconnaissance, indicated the likely timing and axes of attack. In April the Stavka and its senior commanders, most prominently Zhukov and Vasilevsky, debated whether to strike first or to absorb the German attack in deep defense and then counterattack. Stalin, initially inclined toward an early offensive, accepted the defensive option.

The result was an extraordinarily dense defensive system, with several defensive belts reaching depths on the order of a hundred and fifty kilometers or more, minefields, anti-tank strongpoints, and the mobilization of hundreds of thousands of civilians, many of them women, to dig trenches and fortifications. Estimates of the forces involved vary by source and by what is counted; Soviet forces committed to the wider operation, including reserves, substantially outnumbered the German forces, and Soviet superiority in artillery and in prepared positions was large. Students should treat precise strength and loss figures cautiously, since sources differ in the categories counted (the immediate salient or the broader operational area, the defensive phase alone or the subsequent counteroffensives).

The German offensive began on 5 July 1943. In the northern sector, General Walter Model's Ninth Army made limited progress against heavily fortified positions under Rokossovsky's Central Front; in the south, Hermann Hoth's Fourth Panzer Army and Army Detachment Kempf penetrated deeper against Nikolai Vatutin's Voronezh Front. The fighting near Prokhorovka on 12 July involved the Soviet Fifth Guards Tank Army, which counterattacked German armor in a large engagement that Soviet and Russian accounts long presented as the largest tank battle in history. More recent scholarship, drawing on operational records from both sides, has revised that image: the engagement was a major, costly, and confused series of clashes, in which the Soviet side suffered very heavy tank losses, but it was not a single decisive clash and did not by itself determine the battle's outcome. By 13 July Hitler had halted Citadel, partly because of the Allied landing in Sicily on 10 July and the Soviet counteroffensive against the Orel salient launched on 12 July.

The Soviet counteroffensives, Operation Kutuzov at Orel and Operation Rumyantsev against Belgorod and Kharkiv, brought the recapture of Orel and Belgorod on 5 August (celebrated in Moscow with the first artillery salute of the war) and of Kharkiv on 23 August 1943. The significance of Kursk lies less in its immediate territorial results than in what followed. After Citadel the German army on the Eastern Front never again mounted a strategic offensive of comparable scale, and the Red Army conducted a nearly continuous series of offensives from July 1943. Soviet casualties at Kursk and in the counteroffensives were heavy, probably heavier than German losses in absolute terms, a fact that illustrates the costs at which the Red Army's operational improvement was achieved.

Historical Vocabulary
Strategic Initiative and Operational Depth

Definition: The strategic initiative is the ability of one side in a war to choose where and when major offensive operations occur, forcing the opponent to respond. Operational depth refers to the extent of the area, behind the enemy's immediate front line, in which a military operation is planned to produce effects, using successive echelons of forces, mobile formations, artillery, and air power.

Historical context: Soviet military theorists in the 1930s had developed the concept of "deep operations," which the purges of 1937–1938 and the failures of 1941 disrupted. During 1942–1944 the Red Army rebuilt its practice, forming tank and mechanized armies, concentrating artillery, improving staff work, and relying on logistics supplied partly by Allied trucks.

Why it matters: The concepts help students avoid the oversimplified claim that the Red Army won "because of numbers." Numbers mattered, but so did the ability to plan, supply, and conduct operations over great distances.

Section 60.4

The Soviet Advance to the Dnieper, 1943

After Orel, Belgorod, and Kharkiv, Soviet forces advanced on a broad front from the Smolensk region in the north to the Black Sea coast in the south. Smolensk was retaken on 25 September 1943, Poltava on 23 September, and the industrial city of Zaporizhzhia in mid-October. The Germans, ordered to hold the so-called "Eastern Wall" along the Dnieper, destroyed bridges, crops, and settlements in a scorched-earth retreat and deported or forcibly evacuated parts of the population. The Soviet advance outran its supply and the Germans' prepared line proved less formidable than its name implied, since few fortifications had actually been built.

The Dnieper, one of the great rivers examined in Chapter 2, was nonetheless a serious obstacle, over a kilometer wide in places, with high western banks. Soviet units began crossing in late September with improvised rafts, fishing boats, and whatever floated, often without waiting for bridging equipment, to establish bridgeheads before the Germans could organize a defense. The Bukrin bridgehead south of Kyiv proved too constricted for a breakout, and the Stavka shifted the main effort secretly north to the Lyutezh bridgehead. The assault from Lyutezh liberated Kyiv on 6 November 1943, timed for the anniversary of the October Revolution. In the process the Red Army suffered large casualties among the units that crossed first, and the title of Hero of the Soviet Union was awarded to many hundreds of soldiers for the crossings.

For the inhabitants of Ukraine, the recapture of Kyiv ended two years of occupation. The city had been the site of the Babyn Yar massacres of September 1941, in which tens of thousands of Jews were shot, and the Soviet Extraordinary State Commission began documenting German crimes as territories were retaken. The Dnieper campaign also illustrates growing Soviet operational competence: successive offensives were coordinated across fronts, deception was used to shift forces unseen, and tank armies were committed after infantry breakthroughs. Yet it also showed the human cost of an approach that sometimes valued speed and anniversary dates over casualty reduction.

Section 60.5

The Soviet Union and the Grand Alliance in 1943

The alliance among the Soviet Union, Britain, and the United States was a coalition of states with different interests, brought together by the common enemy and held together by mutual need. Soviet leaders had pressed for a "second front" in western Europe since 1941. The Western Allies, after landings in North Africa in November 1942, in Sicily in July 1943, and in Italy in September 1943, argued that Mediterranean operations, strategic bombing, and Atlantic convoys were already engaging German resources. Stalin regarded these as insufficient; the western Allies' postponement of a cross-Channel invasion until 1944 contributed to a lasting Soviet perception that the burden of the land war had fallen primarily on the Red Army, a perception with some basis in the distribution of German forces and casualties but also one that became politically useful after 1945.

Material assistance was substantial. Under the American Lend-Lease program, which the United States extended to the USSR in late 1941, the Soviet Union received goods whose value is commonly estimated at about eleven billion dollars at the time, including several hundred thousand trucks and other vehicles, locomotives, rails, food, aluminum, explosives, radio equipment, and aviation fuel, delivered by Arctic convoys, through Iran, and across the Pacific to Soviet Far Eastern ports. Soviet historiography long minimized this assistance; Western accounts sometimes overstated it. The balanced view is that it complemented rather than replaced Soviet production but made the mobile offensives of 1944–1945 more feasible. A different problem in relations arose in April 1943, when German authorities announced the discovery of graves of Polish officers at Katyn near Smolensk. The Soviet government denied responsibility and broke relations with the Polish government-in-exile after it requested an international investigation. Soviet responsibility for the killings of 1940 was acknowledged by the Soviet leadership in 1990 and documented in archival records released in 1992.

The Tehran Conference, 28 November–1 December 1943, was the first meeting of Stalin, Roosevelt, and Churchill. The Allies confirmed that a cross-Channel invasion (Operation Overlord) would occur in May 1944, and Stalin reaffirmed an earlier indication that the Soviet Union would enter the war against Japan after Germany's defeat. The leaders discussed in general terms the future of Poland, whose eastern border would approximate the Curzon Line with compensation to the west at German expense, and the treatment of Germany. Detailed decisions were deferred, which left important issues open. The conference reflected cooperation but also the asymmetry of power, as Soviet armies advanced while the Western Allies prepared to land in France. Students should avoid treating Tehran either as the origin of the Cold War or as evidence of a harmonious alliance; it illustrates how coalition diplomacy managed disagreements by postponing them.

Division II — Liberation, Soviet Restoration, and the Campaigns of 1944

Section 60.6

The Breaking and Final Lifting of the Siege of Leningrad

Chapter 59 described the blockade of Leningrad, which began in September 1941 and produced mass starvation in the winter of 1941–1942. Supplies reached the city across Lake Ladoga, by ice road in winter and by barge in summer, but this route was inadequate to feed a population of more than two million. Operation Iskra (Spark), launched on 12 January 1943 by the Leningrad and Volkhov Fronts, broke the German blockade on 18 January by opening a narrow corridor, perhaps eight to eleven kilometers wide, along the southern shore of Lake Ladoga. The corridor was exposed to artillery fire, but Soviet railway troops built a rail line through it in a matter of weeks, which improved supply and allowed some reinforcement and evacuation. The blockade, in other words, was breached in January 1943 but not ended.

The final lifting occurred a year later. The Leningrad–Novgorod strategic offensive, begun in mid-January 1944 by the Leningrad, Volkhov, and 2nd Baltic Fronts, pushed Army Group North back from the city's outskirts; Novgorod was liberated on 20 January, and on 27 January 1944 the Soviet command announced the end of the siege, a date commemorated with a salute and, since, as a Day of Military Glory. The siege is conventionally said to have lasted 872 days. The human cost was immense. Estimates of deaths among Leningrad's civilian population, most from starvation and cold in 1941–1942, are generally given in a range from roughly 600,000 to 800,000, and some estimates are higher, the uncertainty arising from incomplete registration, evacuation, military deaths, and the fate of refugees. The figure of 632,000 submitted by Soviet authorities to the Nuremberg tribunal is often cited as a minimum.

The siege has had a complex history of memory. During the war it was a symbol of civilian endurance, and the diaries, poems, and radio broadcasts of the besieged city have become important sources. After 1949, in the course of the Leningrad Affair examined in Section 60.31, the city's siege museum was closed and its leadership purged, an illustration of how the same events could be incorporated into or removed from official memory depending on political circumstances. Historians also continue to debate the German strategy of starving rather than storming the city, the Soviet leadership's evacuation and rationing decisions, and the question of whether the city could have been relieved earlier, all of which belong to a continuing scholarly conversation.

Section 60.7

The Liberation of Ukraine and Crimea, 1943–1944

After Kyiv, the Red Army fought through the winter of 1943–1944 to clear right-bank Ukraine. The Dnieper–Carpathian strategic offensive, from late December 1943 to April 1944, involved several fronts and included the Zhytomyr–Berdychiv operation, the encirclement of German forces at Korsun–Shevchenkivskyi (Cherkasy) in January–February 1944, and the Uman–Botoshani and Proskurov–Chernivtsi operations that carried Soviet forces to the Dniester and the Carpathian foothills. The spring mud again limited mobility, but by April 1944 Soviet armies had reached the prewar Romanian border. Odesa was liberated on 10 April 1944. The campaign also placed Soviet forces on the edge of Romania, Hungary, and Czechoslovakia.

The Crimean operation, from 8 April to mid-May 1944, expelled German and Romanian forces from the peninsula. Soviet troops broke through at Perekop and Sivash and crossed the Kerch Strait; Sevastopol, the city whose defense in 1854–1855 and 1941–1942 held a central place in military memory (see Chapter 41), was recaptured on 9 May. German and Romanian losses in the evacuation attempts across the Black Sea were severe. The region's population had been transformed by occupation: Crimea's Jewish and Krymchak communities had been largely murdered, thousands of civilians had been deported to Germany for labor, and a minority of Crimean Tatars had served in German-organized auxiliary units, as discussed in Section 60.10.

In liberated areas, Soviet institutions were restored: party and state organs returned, the NKVD began to investigate collaboration, collective farms were reconstituted, and the Extraordinary State Commission recorded destruction. The material devastation was extensive. Retreating forces had destroyed the Dnieper hydroelectric station, mines in the Donbas had been flooded, and rural communities lacked livestock, tools, and men. Residents of liberated territory, in addition, faced Soviet suspicion because they had lived under occupation, and mobilization into the army was rapid; in some regions men conscripted immediately served in units with minimal training. For the populations of western Ukraine, which Soviet forces entered in 1944 after annexation in 1939, the return of Soviet power raised different questions, taken up in Sections 60.11 and 60.27.

Section 60.8

Operation Bagration and the Destruction of Army Group Centre

The Soviet summer offensive of 1944 was planned to coincide with the Allied landings in Normandy, which began on 6 June. Named Operation Bagration after the Russian commander of 1812, it was directed against Army Group Centre in Belarus, which the German command believed would not be the main Soviet target. Soviet planners, with Zhukov and Vasilevsky coordinating, used maskirovka (deception) on a large scale, making visible preparations in Ukraine to draw German reserves southward while concentrating forces for the Belarusian attack in secrecy. Four fronts took part: the 1st Baltic under Ivan Bagramyan, the 3rd Belorussian under Ivan Chernyakhovsky, the 2nd Belorussian under Georgy Zakharov, and the 1st Belorussian under Konstantin Rokossovsky. Partisan units, which in Belarus numbered in the tens of thousands, conducted a coordinated "rail war" in the nights before the offensive, blowing up railway lines behind German lines.

The offensive began on 22 June 1944, the third anniversary of the German invasion. Massive artillery barrages and air support supported breakthroughs on multiple axes; the Germans, weakened by the transfer of units elsewhere, and constrained by Hitler's insistence on holding fortified cities as "fortresses," were encircled at Vitebsk, Bobruisk, and east of Minsk. Minsk was liberated on 3 July. The German Fourth Army was largely destroyed, and Army Group Centre lost the equivalent of dozens of divisions; German losses are variously estimated at roughly 350,000 to well over 400,000 killed, missing, and captured, with the range reflecting differences in sources and categories. On 17 July some 57,000 German prisoners were marched through Moscow, a spectacle staged to demonstrate victory. Soviet losses were also heavy, in the hundreds of thousands killed, wounded, and missing.

Bagration continued through August: Soviet forces liberated most of Belarus, entered Lithuania, reached the Vistula, and advanced on the outskirts of Warsaw. The campaign illustrated the Red Army's maturity: it combined deception, concentration of artillery and armor, rapid exploitation by tank armies, and logistical support, including motor transport partly supplied by Lend-Lease. It also marked the point at which the German army's eastern forces ceased to be able to prevent a Soviet advance into Central Europe. Belarus, however, bore enormous costs. Occupation, anti-partisan warfare, and the destruction of hundreds of villages had killed roughly a quarter of its prewar population by commonly cited estimates, and the republic's cities lay largely in ruins.

Section 60.9

Partisans, Collaboration, and Liberation under Soviet Rule

The Soviet partisan movement, organized under the Central Staff of the Partisan Movement from May 1942, was strongest in Belarus, the Bryansk forests, and parts of northern and western Ukraine. Partisans disrupted railways, attacked garrisons, gathered intelligence, and tied down German security forces, but their activity also provoked reprisals against civilians, including the destruction of hundreds of Belarusian villages and the killing of their inhabitants. Partisan groups varied: some were led by NKVD officers and party officials parachuted in from the "mainland," others formed from encircled soldiers and local volunteers. Jewish partisans and family camps, such as the one led by the Bielski brothers in western Belarus, saved lives and fought in difficult conditions, often with reluctant support from other units. Many Jews also survived through concealment, assistance from rescuers, or flight into forests.

Collaboration with the German occupation took many forms, from forced labor and administrative cooperation under duress, to service in auxiliary police units, to voluntary participation in the persecution of Jews, to political collaboration by nationalist organizations hoping to obtain autonomy. Historians distinguish these categories because the moral and legal questions they raise differ. Several hundred thousand former Soviet citizens served in German auxiliary formations, including the so-called Russian Liberation Army associated with General Andrei Vlasov, whose forces were formed late and played a limited military role. Alongside them, Ukrainian nationalist groups (the Organization of Ukrainian Nationalists and its Ukrainian Insurgent Army, UPA) fought at various times against Germans, Soviets, and Poles; in Volhynia in 1943–1944 UPA units perpetrated mass killings of Polish civilians, which prompted Polish reprisals.

When the Red Army returned, the security organs set about identifying collaborators and others deemed unreliable. Procedures of "filtration" examined soldiers who had been encircled or captured, civilians who had worked for the occupiers, and men of military age in liberated areas. Smersh, the military counterintelligence organization, and the NKVD detained suspects, and a decree of 1943 provided for harsh penalties for traitors. Many people were executed or imprisoned; many others, after screening, were conscripted into penal or regular units. Wartime Order No. 270 of 1941, which had described soldiers who surrendered as traitors, shaped attitudes toward returning prisoners. Liberation for many civilians meant relief at the end of occupation, but also fear: the Soviet state treated survival under occupation as potentially suspect, and local accounts of who had cooperated, and why, were used to settle personal and political scores.

Section 60.10

Wartime Nationality Deportations and Collective Punishment, 1943–1944

Between 1943 and 1944 the Soviet government deported several entire peoples from their homelands in the North Caucasus, the Kalmyk steppe, and Crimea to Central Asia, Kazakhstan, Siberia, and the Urals. The sequence began in November 1943 with the Karachai of the northwestern Caucasus (about 70,000 people), followed in late December by the Kalmyks (roughly 90,000 or more), then, in late February 1944, by the Chechens and Ingush (approximately 480,000 together) in an operation code-named "Lentil" (Chechevitsa). In March 1944 the Balkars (about 37,000) were deported, and from 18 May 1944 the Crimean Tatars (on the order of 180,000–190,000) were removed from Crimea in a few days. Later in 1944, Bulgarians, Greeks, and Armenians were deported from Crimea, and Meskhetian Turks, Kurds, and Khemshils from southern Georgia. These were not the first Soviet deportations of nationalities, which had begun with Koreans in 1937 and Volga Germans in 1941 (see Chapter 58), but they were the largest wartime operations of this kind.

The official justification was collective treason: the decrees accused the peoples, as populations, of collaboration with German forces, desertion, banditry, or hostility to Soviet power. Historians do not dispute that some individuals from each group collaborated, as did people from many other nationalities, including Russians and Ukrainians; nor that, in the Caucasus, armed bands and insurgencies existed against Soviet authority, some linked to resistance to collectivization and prewar repression. But the collective accusation was not an established finding of guilt of whole peoples, and it was applied indiscriminately to men serving in the Red Army, to party members, to children, and to the elderly. Some deported peoples, such as the Kalmyks, had been under German occupation only briefly, and the Karachai and Balkars had been occupied for a few months; the Chechens and Ingush had not been occupied at all. Recent scholarship generally interprets the deportations as products of a combination of security anxieties, the Stalinist tendency to treat nationalities as collectively reliable or unreliable, and the opportunity presented by the Red Army's return to territories; some historians also stress local and economic motives and the opportunities that deportation gave to neighboring populations and officials. The question of how much weight each factor deserves remains an area of historical debate.

The operations were conducted by NKVD troops under Lavrenty Beria and his deputies. Families were given a short time, sometimes a few hours, to gather a limited quantity of belongings, were loaded onto freight cars, and endured journeys lasting weeks in conditions of overcrowding, cold or heat, and hunger. On arrival they were registered as "special settlers" (spetspereselentsy), subject to commandant supervision and forbidden to leave their assigned districts. Mortality was high during transport and especially during the first years of settlement, when housing, food, and medical care were scarce; estimates vary by group and source, from perhaps a fifth to considerably more of the deported population in some cases, with the difference depending on the time period counted and on whether Soviet records or community estimates are used. Deaths in transit and deaths in the settlements should be distinguished, as should deaths from deportation and deaths from general wartime conditions. Autonomous republics and regions of the deported peoples were abolished, and their territory transferred to neighboring units; place names were changed, and cultural monuments and graves were destroyed.

The special-settlement regime lasted until the mid-1950s. Restrictions on residence were lifted for most of the deported peoples in 1956, and in 1957 the Chechen-Ingush, Karachai, Balkar, and Kalmyk autonomous territories were restored, although many returnees found their homes occupied. Crimean Tatars were granted only partial rehabilitation in 1967, and they were not permitted to return in large numbers until the late 1980s, when a mass return began; the Soviet Supreme Soviet condemned the deportations in 1989, and the Russian Federation passed a law on the rehabilitation of repressed peoples in 1991. Memory of the deportation is central to the national identities of the groups concerned, and legislatures in several countries, including Ukraine, have characterized the Crimean Tatar deportation as genocide, while scholars debate the term's application and the evidence regarding intent. The issues remained politically sensitive after 2014 (see Chapter 70).

Historical Vocabulary
Special Settlement (spetsposelenie)

Definition: A Soviet legal and administrative status under which deported people, "special settlers," were required to live in a designated place under the supervision of NKVD commandants, without freedom of movement, but not confined in a camp.

Historical context: The system originated with the "kulak" deportations of 1930–1931 (see Chapter 56) and was extended to deported nationalities in 1937–1944. Special settlers worked in agriculture, construction, mining, and forestry, and were subject to registration and sanctions for leaving their district.

Why it matters: Special settlement was distinct from Gulag imprisonment, yet constituted forced displacement, and the distinction helps students analyze the range of coercive institutions in the Stalinist system without treating them as identical.

Section 60.11

The Red Army's Return to the Baltic Region and the Western Borderlands

The territories annexed or occupied by the USSR in 1939–1940 (see Chapter 59) were reconquered in 1944. Soviet forces entered Lithuania in the summer of 1944 during Bagration; Tallinn fell on 22 September and Riga on 13 October 1944, while a large German force held out in the Courland pocket in western Latvia until the capitulation of May 1945. Western Ukraine, including Lviv (27 July 1944), and Western Belarus, formerly eastern Poland, returned to Soviet control, as did Bessarabia and northern Bukovina, and in 1945 Soviet agreements with Czechoslovakia transferred Subcarpathian Rus to the Ukrainian SSR. In the north, Finland signed an armistice in September 1944 that ceded Petsamo (Pechenga) and committed it to expel German forces from Finnish Lapland. These territorial changes, most of which Soviet leaders had negotiated with Germany in 1939 and demanded from the Allies thereafter, became part of the postwar European order.

For the Baltic peoples, the German occupation had been a time of mass killing of Jews, with extensive local participation in some places, and of forced labor and conscription; it followed a year of Soviet rule in which tens of thousands of Estonians, Latvians, and Lithuanians were arrested or deported in June 1941. In 1944 many Baltic residents fled westward, some 100,000 or more from each country by commonly cited estimates, to avoid the returning Soviet authorities; others joined or supported armed resistance. The Western Allies had not recognized the annexations of 1940, but did not contest the Soviet reconquest. Soviet authorities described the Red Army's arrival as liberation and the restoration of Soviet republics, while many residents experienced it as a return of an occupation that had been interrupted, a distinction which was, and in the region still is, central to historical memory (see Chapter 66).

Resistance, conscription, and repression defined the next years. Armed partisan groups, known in Estonia and Latvia as the "forest brothers" and in Lithuania as partisans of the Lithuanian Freedom Fighters' movement, fought Soviet forces and officials, and Soviet security organs fought them with mass arrests and deportations. In western Ukraine, the UPA and the nationalist underground continued their struggle into the early 1950s. Population exchanges between Poland and the Soviet Union in 1944–1946 moved hundreds of thousands of Poles westward and Ukrainians eastward, and the Polish authorities' Operation Vistula of 1947 dispersed Ukrainians remaining in southeastern Poland. These developments are examined further in Section 60.27.

Division III — The Defeat of Nazi Germany and the End of the World War

Section 60.12

The Red Army in Southeastern Europe, 1944–1945

The Iași–Chișinău operation, launched on 20 August 1944, enveloped German and Romanian forces in Moldavia and led, on 23 August, to a coup in which King Michael and a coalition of political leaders dismissed the pro-German dictator Ion Antonescu and withdrew Romania from the Axis. Romania declared war on Germany on 25 August and signed an armistice with the Allies, chiefly the Soviet Union, on 12 September; the armistice obliged Romania to pay reparations, accept an Allied Control Commission under Soviet chairmanship, and commit its army to the war against Germany. Romania's army thereafter fought on the Soviet side. Soviet forces entered Bucharest at the end of August and then advanced through the Carpathians into Hungary and Yugoslavia.

Bulgaria, which had been allied to Germany but had not declared war on the USSR, was invaded by Soviet forces after Moscow declared war on 5 September 1944; a coup on 9 September installed a Fatherland Front government dominated by communists and their allies. In Yugoslavia, the Belgrade operation of September–October 1944, conducted by Soviet forces together with Tito's partisans, liberated the capital on 20 October; Soviet forces then moved on into Hungary, while Tito's forces, which had liberated much of the country themselves, retained their own political command. The Soviet military role in Yugoslavia was thus coordinated with, but distinct from, the activities of an indigenous resistance movement with its own aims. The Moscow talks of October 1944 between Stalin and Churchill produced an informal "percentages" arrangement concerning influence in Balkan states; historians debate its significance, since it was a wartime bargain, not a treaty, and reflected realities on the ground as much as it shaped them.

The campaign for Hungary was costly. Fighting for Debrecen in October 1944 was followed by the encirclement of Budapest in December. The siege of the city, which lasted from late December 1944 to 13 February 1945, destroyed much of the capital and caused heavy civilian suffering; the Arrow Cross regime, installed with German backing in October, conducted massacres of Jews in the city even as the front approached. A German relief offensive at Lake Balaton in March 1945 (Operation Spring Awakening) failed, and Soviet forces advanced into Austria, taking Vienna on 13 April. Soviet units also fought in Czechoslovakia, where the Slovak National Uprising of August–October 1944 had been put down by German forces, and later in the Prague operation. Everywhere the Red Army's arrival ended German domination, but it also meant a military occupation, requisitioning, and sexual violence against local women, topics taken up in Section 60.16.

Section 60.13

Poland, the Warsaw Uprising, and the Politics of Liberation

Poland occupied a central place in the wartime and postwar diplomacy of the coalition. The Polish government-in-exile in London, recognized by Britain and the United States, commanded the Home Army (Armia Krajowa), the largest underground force in occupied Europe. Soviet authorities, who had broken relations with that government after Katyn, supported a rival Polish organization, the Union of Polish Patriots, and the First Polish Army formed in the USSR. When Soviet forces crossed the prewar Polish border in July 1944, the Home Army's "Operation Tempest" sought to seize cities from the Germans before the Red Army arrived so that Polish authorities loyal to London would be in place. Soviet commanders, however, treated Home Army units as unauthorized, and NKVD officers disarmed and arrested many of them. On 22 July 1944 a Polish Committee of National Liberation (the "Lublin Committee") was proclaimed in Chełm and Lublin with Soviet backing; it became a Provisional Government at the end of 1944 and was recognized by the Soviet Union in January 1945.

The Warsaw Uprising began on 1 August 1944, ordered by the Home Army's commanders as Soviet forces approached the city's eastern suburb of Praga. The insurgents, some 40,000 or more with limited weapons, held large parts of the city for weeks. The Germans, reinforced with SS and police units known for brutality, suppressed the revolt street by street, killing large numbers of civilians; the uprising ended on 2–3 October. Estimates of Polish civilian deaths are generally in the range of 150,000 to 200,000, and of insurgent deaths in the neighborhood of 16,000, and German forces systematically destroyed much of the city afterward. The Soviet Army halted on the east bank of the Vistula. Soviet commanders had suffered heavy losses and faced strong German counterattacks in the preceding weeks, a military explanation advanced by Soviet and some Western historians. Polish historians and many Western scholars have emphasized that Stalin had political reasons for not rescuing a force loyal to the London government, as evidenced by his dismissal of the revolt as an "adventure" in correspondence with Churchill and by delays in granting Western aircraft landing rights for supply flights until mid-September. The two explanations are not mutually exclusive, and the extent to which each shaped decisions remains debated.

The politics of liberation meant that the Soviet Union in effect determined who would govern Poland. In March 1945 NKVD officers arrested sixteen leaders of the Polish underground who had accepted an invitation to talks, and they were tried in Moscow in June. Stanisław Mikołajczyk, the exile prime minister who returned to Poland, joined a reorganized government of national unity in June 1945, but he was marginalized and fled in 1947. Poland's borders moved westward: the eastern territory including Lviv and Vilnius went to the USSR, and Poland received German lands up to the Oder and Neisse rivers. These changes provided the postwar territorial order, but also created grievances and population movements affecting millions.

Multiple Perspectives

The Warsaw Uprising and Soviet-Polish Relations

Home Army leaders and the London government: For the leadership of the underground, the uprising was meant to assert Polish sovereignty and to take Warsaw before the Red Army arrived. They expected Soviet or Western assistance and acted on the basis of military assessments that later proved overoptimistic, and on a political calculation that the Soviets would impose a rival government.
Soviet leaders and commanders: Stalin's government regarded the Home Army as an anti-Soviet force and the uprising as an unauthorized undertaking. Soviet commanders stressed the exhaustion of their forces after Bagration and the German counterattacks. Documentary evidence shows that political distrust of the London Poles influenced Soviet decisions about aid and air support.
Warsaw's civilians: The city's inhabitants endured bombardment, summary executions, expulsion, and starvation. Many supported the uprising at first; others, after weeks of destruction, questioned the decision. Hundreds of thousands were expelled to camps or forced labor after its collapse.
Western Allies: British and American leaders sympathized with the Poles but were reluctant to antagonize Stalin; airdrops from Italy had limited effect and were costly for the crews.

The perspectives show that a single event could be a national uprising, a military operation, a political problem, and a civilian catastrophe. Power was unequal: the Germans committed the atrocities, and the Soviet decision not to advance or to facilitate aid was a decision with political consequences.

Section 60.14

The Vistula–Oder Offensive and the Invasion of Germany, 1945

The Soviet winter offensive of 1945 began on 12 January, somewhat earlier than planned, partly in response to Western Allied requests after the German Ardennes offensive in December 1944. Konev's 1st Ukrainian Front attacked from the Sandomierz bridgehead and Zhukov's 1st Belorussian Front from bridgeheads south of Warsaw. Soviet forces advanced several hundred kilometers in about two weeks, liberated Warsaw (17 January), Łódź, and Kraków (19 January), where the city was largely preserved, and reached the Oder River by the end of January, establishing bridgeheads about sixty to seventy kilometers from Berlin. On 27 January, units of the 1st Ukrainian Front entered the Auschwitz complex, where they found several thousand prisoners too ill to be evacuated on the death marches that the SS had begun days earlier.

In the north, the 2nd and 3rd Belorussian Fronts attacked East Prussia, which German propaganda and Soviet commentators both regarded as the home of Prussian militarism. Fighting there was ferocious; Königsberg fell on 9 April after a siege. The offensive provoked mass flight of the German civilian population, whose exodus by road and across the frozen lagoon and the Baltic Sea was accompanied by atrocities and drownings. The sinking of the refugee ship Wilhelm Gustloff by a Soviet submarine on 30 January 1945 caused thousands of deaths, with estimates around nine thousand. Soviet propaganda had long urged revenge for German crimes in the USSR, and incidents of killings and rape in East Prussian villages, such as Nemmersdorf in October 1944, which the Nazi press exploited, were followed by more widespread violence once the Red Army entered Germany.

Stavka planners debated whether to continue directly to Berlin from the Oder. Zhukov initially urged an immediate advance; the exposed flanks in Pomerania and a German counterattack there, along with supply problems and Soviet exhaustion, led to a pause during which the German defenders improvised a defense. Soviet forces in February–March reduced Pomerania and Silesia and besieged fortress cities. This period also prepared the political landscape: Soviet occupation authorities in the German territories east of the Oder transferred administration to Polish officials, and the expulsion of Germans from these territories began, subsequently confirmed by Allied agreement at Potsdam.

Section 60.15

The Battle of Berlin and the Collapse of Nazi Germany

The Berlin operation began on 16 April 1945, when the 1st Belorussian Front under Zhukov attacked the German positions on the Seelow Heights west of the Oder in a pre-dawn artillery barrage, while Konev's 1st Ukrainian Front crossed the Neisse to the south and Rokossovsky's 2nd Belorussian Front attacked to the north. The Soviet attack, involving roughly 2.5 million soldiers, met strong resistance at Seelow and cost Zhukov's front heavy casualties before breaking through on 17–19 April. The rivalry between Zhukov and Konev, which Stalin encouraged by assigning boundaries and objectives, contributed to haste. By 25 April the city was encircled, and on the same day Soviet and American units met on the Elbe at Torgau, splitting German-held territory.

Street fighting in Berlin lasted from 22 April to 2 May. Defenders included regular army units, SS, Hitler Youth, and the Volkssturm, a militia of older men and boys. Soviet forces used artillery fired at point-blank range, tanks accompanied by infantry, and assaults through cellars and subway tunnels. The Reichstag, a symbolic objective, was stormed on 30 April; the famous photograph of Soviet soldiers raising the flag, taken on 2 May, was a staged recreation. Hitler committed suicide in his bunker on 30 April; the German commander of Berlin, General Helmuth Weidling, surrendered the city on 2 May. Soviet losses in the operation, according to the compilation edited by Grigory Krivosheev, exceeded 80,000 killed or missing and more than 350,000 casualties overall; German losses and civilian deaths were large but are imprecisely known.

Germany's capitulation was signed at Reims on 7 May before Western Allied officers, and ratified in Berlin-Karlshorst on the night of 8–9 May. The Soviet Union demanded a Soviet-presided ceremony because Soviet armies had fought the largest share of the land war and had taken Berlin. The date 9 May became the Soviet holiday of Victory Day, though it was not an official day off between 1948 and 1965; its commemoration grew over time into a central ritual of Soviet and Russian state memory. Remnants of German forces fought on in Czechoslovakia until 11 May, as discussed in the next section.

Section 60.16

Victory, Liberation, and Violence in Defeated Europe

The advance of the Red Army uncovered the machinery of Nazi killing. Soviet troops reached Majdanek, near Lublin, in July 1944, the first major camp to be overrun, and later liberated Auschwitz-Birkenau, Stutthof, Sachsenhausen, and Ravensbrück, among others. Soviet reporters, including Vasily Grossman and Ilya Ehrenburg, wrote about the killings, and the Extraordinary State Commission collected testimony. Yet Soviet public accounts generally described victims as "peaceful Soviet citizens," giving less emphasis to the specific targeting of Jews, an omission that formed part of postwar Soviet memory and became controversial (see Section 60.30). Soldiers encountered prisoners of many nationalities, among them Soviet POWs and forced laborers, whom they regarded with mixed feelings, because Soviet policy treated captivity with suspicion.

The Red Army's conduct in defeated Europe was marked by violence against civilians. Looting was widespread, and organized "trophy brigades" dismantled factories, shipped machinery, and collected art and books for transport to the USSR. Sexual violence was extensive in Germany, Austria, Hungary, and elsewhere. Estimates of the number of women raped in Germany range from the hundreds of thousands to as many as two million, with the very wide range reflecting scarce documentation; local hospital records in Berlin suggest on the order of tens of thousands to over a hundred thousand victims in that city. Soviet commanders issued orders to restrain troops, and some offenders were punished, but enforcement was inconsistent, and the violence continued for months. Many Soviet soldiers also treated civilians humanely, shared rations, and distributed food in Berlin. Violence by Soviet forces should be understood in the context of German atrocities in the USSR, Soviet propaganda that encouraged hatred of Germans, the experience of the soldiers themselves, and the fact that sexual violence has accompanied armies of many states, including, on a smaller scale, those of the Western Allies.

The war's last campaign, the Prague operation of 6–11 May 1945, was launched after the city's inhabitants rose against German occupation on 5 May; units of Vlasov's Russian Liberation Army briefly aided the uprising, a complicated episode, before withdrawing. Soviet tank units reached Prague on 9 May. Victory celebrations in Moscow on 24 June 1945 included a parade on Red Square at which Soviet soldiers threw captured German standards at the foot of the Lenin Mausoleum. As the war ended, millions of people were displaced: surviving camp inmates, forced laborers, POWs, German refugees, and expellees. The distinction between liberation and domination is critical. The Red Army destroyed Nazi power in Central and Eastern Europe, an outcome that had immense moral and political significance, but its presence also served as the foundation for communist-dominated governments imposed in many countries, an issue developed in Section 60.21.

Section 60.17

The Soviet War against Japan and the End of World War II, August–September 1945

The Soviet Union and Japan had maintained a neutrality pact since April 1941, which the Soviet government announced in April 1945 it would not renew. At the Yalta Conference Stalin had agreed secretly to enter the war against Japan within two to three months after Germany's surrender, in return for southern Sakhalin, the Kuril Islands, and restored Russian rights in Manchuria, including Port Arthur and the Manchurian railways. At Potsdam the Soviet Union repeated its commitment. After the atomic bombing of Hiroshima on 6 August 1945, the USSR declared war on Japan on 8 August Moscow time, and its forces attacked at the start of 9 August (the day of the Nagasaki bombing).

The Manchurian Strategic Offensive involved around 1.5 million Soviet troops under Marshal Aleksandr Vasilevsky in three fronts (the Transbaikal, 1st Far Eastern, and 2nd Far Eastern), with units from the Mongolian People's Republic. They faced the Japanese Kwantung Army, which was large but lacked modern armor and aircraft and had been drained of its best formations. Soviet columns advanced across the Gobi and the Khingan mountains, and the Kwantung Army collapsed within days, though sporadic fighting continued after Japan's announcement of surrender on 15 August. Soviet casualties are given as about twelve thousand dead in official compilations, while Japanese military deaths are estimated at tens of thousands; Japanese civilians in Manchuria, many of them settlers, suffered heavily from violence, flight, and disease. About 600,000 Japanese prisoners of war were interned in the Soviet Union for forced labor, and tens of thousands died.

In the Pacific, Soviet forces invaded southern Sakhalin on 11 August and occupied it by late August; amphibious operations against the Kuril Islands began on 18 August with heavy fighting on Shumshu and continued into early September, with the southernmost islands (Kunashir, Iturup, Shikotan, and the Habomai group) occupied by about 5 September. Soviet forces also entered northern Korea, and the peninsula was divided at the 38th parallel by agreement with the United States. Japan signed the formal surrender on 2 September 1945, which the Soviet Union treated as the end of the war.

Russia and the World

Soviet Entry into the War against Japan

The Soviet attack linked the European war to the Pacific. Historians disagree about its importance in Japan's decision to surrender: some, such as Tsuyoshi Hasegawa, have argued that the Soviet declaration of war was at least as significant as the atomic bombs in shaping the decision of Japan's leaders, who had hoped for Soviet mediation; others emphasize the bombs and the effect of the blockade and bombing. The question is part of a wider debate about the end of the war, and neither explanation is established beyond dispute.

The Soviet role had lasting consequences. Soviet control of southern Sakhalin and the Kurils, confirmed in the Soviet view by the Yalta agreement, has remained disputed by Japan, which claims the four southernmost islands as its "Northern Territories." The Soviet Union did not sign the 1951 San Francisco peace treaty with Japan; the two states restored diplomatic relations in a 1956 declaration but have never signed a peace treaty. The division of Korea contributed to the creation of two states in 1948 and to the Korean War examined in Section 60.23, and Soviet support for Chinese communist forces in Manchuria after 1945 influenced the Chinese civil war. The Soviet entry in 1945 thus illustrates how the end of the war created new regional systems, rather than closing a chapter.

Division IV — The Human Cost of Victory and the New International Order

Section 60.18

Soviet Human Losses and Material Devastation

The Soviet Union suffered greater human losses in the Second World War than any other state. The figure most commonly cited for total Soviet wartime deaths, including military and civilian, is roughly 26 to 27 million, a number publicized in the late Soviet period after demographic work by Soviet researchers and then repeated in post-Soviet Russian official statements. It is not a precise count but a demographic estimate of the "excess mortality" attributable to the war, calculated by comparing prewar and postwar populations and accounting for births, normal mortality, and migration. Historians who have reexamined it have proposed somewhat lower or higher numbers; the estimate depends on assumptions about the prewar population, the changing borders of the USSR, the population of annexed territories, emigration, and the treatment of people who did not return.

The total encompasses several distinct categories that should not be conflated. Military losses, according to the compilation edited by Grigory Krivosheev (1993, expanded 2001), are in the range of roughly 8.7 million deaths in the armed forces by the original calculation, and about 11 million "irrecoverable losses," including those missing or captured, by the later count; differences arise from the treatment of prisoners and missing soldiers. Among about 5.7 million Soviet prisoners of war in German hands, a commonly cited estimate is that roughly 3.3 million died in captivity, many in the first winter of the war from deliberate starvation, exposure, and shootings. Civilian deaths, which constitute the majority of the total in most estimates, resulted from the Holocaust in Soviet territories, in which approximately two million Soviet Jews (the exact figure depends on the borders used) were murdered; from German reprisals and anti-partisan warfare; from siege and famine, notably in Leningrad and in occupied territories; from forced labor, in which millions of Soviet civilians were taken to the Reich; and from bombing, disease, and deprivation behind the lines, including in the evacuation and in the unoccupied home front. The burden was distributed unevenly: Belarus lost roughly a quarter of its population by commonly cited estimates, Ukraine lost millions, and the Baltic and other regions also suffered heavily; Russian, Ukrainian, Belarusian, Jewish, Tatar, Kazakh, Georgian, Armenian, and other soldiers and civilians were among the dead.

Material devastation was comparably vast. Soviet official statements, based on the Extraordinary State Commission, reported the destruction of some 1,700 towns, around 70,000 villages, and tens of thousands of industrial enterprises, along with tens of thousands of kilometers of railway, and claimed that around 25 million people had been left homeless. These figures were compiled for political purposes, including reparations claims, and they should be treated as indicative of scale rather than as exact. The loss of livestock, tractors, and horses crippled agriculture, and the loss of male labor affected every sector. Official estimates attributed to the war roughly a third of the country's prewar national wealth. The population itself was altered: the 1959 census showed that women outnumbered men by roughly twenty million, with an especially severe imbalance in the generations that had been of fighting age in 1941–1945. The social and economic consequences, examined in the following sections, continued for decades.

Historical Debate

Estimating Soviet Wartime Losses

The question: How many Soviet citizens died as a result of the war, and what methods can produce reliable estimates?

Interpretation A — Demographic reconstruction: Demographers compare population before and after the war and estimate excess deaths, producing totals of about 26–27 million. Their calculations are intended to capture all war-related deaths, including those that records of military and civilian casualties cannot, and they emphasize that the Soviet registration system could not document many deaths in occupied territories, prisoner camps, or the chaos of 1941–1942.
Interpretation B — Record-based compilation and revision: Other scholars, working from military records, personnel files, and local registers, argue that such records are indispensable for establishing categories, and that demographic totals depend on assumptions about prewar population, especially in annexed areas, and about births that did not occur. Some argue for somewhat lower or higher totals depending on assumptions, and some stress that estimates including the 1939–1941 annexed territories raise problems of boundary comparability. A related controversy concerns whether certain deaths, such as those of persons killed in Soviet repression during the war, should be counted as war losses.

Evidence: Censuses of 1926, 1937, 1939, and 1959; military personnel records and unit reports; registers of the Extraordinary State Commission; German records of prisoners and killings; local memorial books and lists of names; and oral testimony.

Points of agreement: Soviet losses were enormous, far larger than those of any other combatant; civilian deaths were at least as numerous as military ones; and the burden fell unevenly on regions and peoples, with Jews, Belarusians, and POWs suffering particular proportions of loss.

Points of disagreement: The exact total, the precise military fraction, the numbers of those missing and those who died in captivity, and the treatment of victims of Soviet repression remain debated.

Why it matters: The numbers shape national memory, reparations claims, comparisons with other states, and assessments of the war's costs. Students should be wary of sources that offer precision where none is possible.

Section 60.19

Demobilization, Repatriation, Displaced Persons, and the Aftermath of War

The Soviet armed forces, which numbered about eleven million at the end of the war, were reduced to fewer than three million by 1948 in a staged demobilization, beginning with older age groups and the wounded. Millions of soldiers returned to cities and villages that had been devastated, and many found families dead, scattered, or changed. Large numbers were disabled; veterans with amputations or severe injuries, visible in public spaces, received pensions that often were modest, and by the late 1940s officials sought to remove some from city streets and markets in campaigns against begging, a policy that scholars of postwar disability have criticized as an indication of the limits of the state's gratitude. Widows and orphans, many of them unaccompanied children, were cared for in state children's homes or by relatives, while others lived on the streets.

Repatriation involved millions of people. At the end of the war about five million Soviet citizens were in Germany and Western Europe as prisoners of war, forced laborers (Ostarbeiter), or others displaced; the Allied agreements at Yalta provided for their return. The Western Allies, and in particular the Americans and British, repatriated many of them, in some cases by force, and some persons resisted return out of fear. Soviet authorities placed repatriates, especially former prisoners and those who had worked for the Germans, through "filtration" at assembly and transit points: most were returned to their homes or conscripted, but substantial numbers were sentenced to forced labor, sent into special settlement, or imprisoned, and many carried a stigma that affected their employment and housing for years. Prisoners were commonly regarded under the logic of Order No. 270 as potential traitors, though policy in practice varied; the contrast between the status of those who fought on and those who were captured contributed to a bitter legacy.

Other displacement patterns shaped the period. Millions of Germans fled or were expelled from territories east of the Oder–Neisse line and from Czechoslovakia and other states, in a population movement of roughly twelve million people that caused hundreds of thousands of deaths. Poles, Ukrainians, Lithuanians, and Belarusians were moved across new borders. Some 150,000 or more Jewish survivors in Poland and the western USSR moved west into displaced persons camps, many hoping to reach Palestine or the United States; the postwar pogrom at Kielce in July 1946 reinforced their fear. Within the Soviet Union, evacuees returned slowly to reconstructed cities, and housing shortages meant that returning families often lived in basements, barracks, or crowded communal apartments for years.

Everyday Life and Social History

The Return: A Soldier, a Widow, a Displaced Family

A demobilized soldier returning in 1945–1946 to a Russian or Ukrainian village might find that farm work had been done for four years by women, adolescents, and the elderly, that houses had been dismantled for fuel or destroyed, and that the collective farm had few horses and little equipment. Many soldiers expected that victory would bring relaxation of wartime discipline and perhaps changes to the collective farm system; instead the state reasserted control over agricultural production, and the veteran's authority within the household was complicated by the fact that a wife and mother had managed alone. In cities, veterans competed for housing and jobs with workers who had stayed, and some officers entered the party apparatus or studied in universities under the government's veterans' benefits.

A war widow typically received a small pension, was expected to continue to work, and might find herself raising children in a shared apartment. Women constituted a majority of the adult population in many areas, which made marriage opportunities scarce and increased the number of children born outside marriage; a family law of July 1944 withdrew legal recognition of unregistered marriages and ended the right of unmarried mothers to seek paternity claims, while offering benefits and honors to mothers of many children. A displaced family returning from evacuation in Central Asia or Siberia, or repatriated from Germany, might find its house occupied, and a repatriated forced laborer might be required to explain years abroad to the NKVD. Evidence for these experiences comes from letters, memoirs, oral histories, petitions to authorities, and police reports; officers' memoirs and official publications offer a more optimistic view. Historians caution against generalizing from urban or elite evidence to the rural majority, and against assuming that experiences were similar across regions or nationalities.

Section 60.20

Yalta, Potsdam, Occupied Germany, and the Postwar Settlement

The Yalta Conference of 4–11 February 1945, held in Crimea, brought Stalin, Roosevelt, and Churchill together as Soviet armies stood on the Oder. The leaders agreed on the division of Germany into occupation zones (with a French zone later added), on the formation of the United Nations with a Security Council in which the great powers held vetoes, and on the principle of reparations, with a figure of twenty billion dollars, half to the Soviet Union, adopted only as a basis for discussion. They agreed that the Soviet Union's eastern Polish border would follow approximately the Curzon Line, that Poland would receive compensation in the west, and that the Polish Provisional Government would be reorganized to include democratic leaders from within and outside Poland. A Declaration on Liberated Europe pledged the powers to assist liberated peoples to form governments through free elections. A secret agreement set the terms of Soviet entry into the war against Japan. Ukraine and Belarus, as Soviet republics, were granted separate seats in the future United Nations.

The Potsdam Conference, 17 July to 2 August 1945, took place after the German surrender and included a change of Western leadership: Harry Truman replaced Roosevelt, who had died in April, and Clement Attlee replaced Churchill after the British election. The participants confirmed the occupation of Germany and agreed to treat it as an economic unit in principle, to disarm and denazify the country, and to allow each occupying power to take reparations from its own zone, with additional transfers from the western zones to the USSR in return for food and raw materials. They provisionally placed the Polish western frontier at the Oder and Neisse rivers pending a final peace settlement and authorized the "orderly and humane" transfer of German populations from Poland, Czechoslovakia, and Hungary. They also established a Council of Foreign Ministers. Stalin's government obtained Allied acceptance in principle of the transfer of the northern part of East Prussia, including Königsberg, to the USSR, and the city became Kaliningrad in 1946.

The agreements must be distinguished from their implementation. Yalta's formulas on Polish government and elections proved ambiguous, and each side accused the other of violating them. Soviet reparations policy in the eastern zone combined dismantling of factories, extraction of current production, and the creation of Soviet-owned companies, and it was criticized by Germans and Western governments. The occupation of Germany evolved into division: the Western powers merged their zones, and the Soviet zone became the German Democratic Republic in 1949. The Allied powers' differing interpretations of Potsdam's principle of economic unity, the Soviet emphasis on reparations, and Western concern about economic recovery all contributed to the outcome. Austria was also divided into zones, and remained occupied until the State Treaty of 1955. Students should avoid reading these conferences as a "betrayal" or as an "agreement to divide the world"; they were negotiations among powers with armies on the ground, and the outcomes reflected military realities and the objectives of each government.

Section 60.21

The Establishment of Soviet Power in Eastern Europe

Between 1944 and 1949 communist parties, backed by the Soviet occupation or by the presence of the Red Army, came to dominate the governments of Poland, Romania, Bulgaria, Hungary, Czechoslovakia, and the Soviet zone of Germany. Historians today emphasize that this was not a single plan implemented uniformly from Moscow; it was a process shaped by local political conditions, the strength of indigenous communist movements, wartime experience, Soviet security concerns, and changing international circumstances, including the deterioration of relations with the West. Soviet leaders in 1944–1945 spoke of "people's democracy," a stage in which broad coalitions including noncommunist parties would govern, and Stalin reportedly told communist leaders that the postwar order would differ from the Soviet model. Yet the Red Army, the Soviet-controlled control commissions, and the security services supported communist control of ministries of the interior, justice, and information, and used coercion against rivals.

The methods and pace varied. In Romania, Soviet deputy foreign minister Andrei Vyshinsky intervened directly in February 1945 to demand a government headed by Petru Groza; elections in November 1946 were manipulated, and King Michael was forced to abdicate in December 1947. In Poland, the communist-led government rigged a 1946 referendum and the January 1947 elections, while the underground resistance was crushed and Mikołajczyk's Polish Peasant Party was suppressed. In Bulgaria, the Fatherland Front eliminated opponents, and the agrarian leader Nikola Petkov was executed in 1947. In Hungary, the Smallholders Party won about 57 percent of the vote in free elections in November 1945, but communists, using control of the police and a strategy of gradual pressure that Mátyás Rákosi called "salami tactics," forced out leaders and manipulated the 1947 election. In Czechoslovakia, relatively free elections in May 1946 gave the Communist Party about 38 percent of the national vote and the largest share; in February 1948, after noncommunist ministers resigned, the communists, with the help of mobilized workers' militias and security forces, took power in a coup, and Foreign Minister Jan Masaryk was found dead in March. In the Soviet zone of Germany, the Communist and Social Democratic parties were merged into the Socialist Unity Party in April 1946 under Soviet pressure. In Yugoslavia and Albania, communist movements had come to power largely through their own wartime resistance.

The transformation involved economic and social change as well as political control: land reforms redistributed large estates, key industries were nationalized, and political opponents, clergy, and prewar elites were arrested or sidelined. The extent of popular support for communist parties varied by country and class, and no single assertion of either "enthusiastic acceptance" or "total imposition" fits the evidence. Many people, particularly among workers, intellectuals, and the young, supported social change and anti-fascism; many others, especially peasants, clergy, and noncommunist politicians, resisted. By 1948 the process was accelerating in response to the Marshall Plan and the hardening of blocs, and show trials followed in Hungary (Rajk, 1949), Bulgaria, and Czechoslovakia (Slánský, 1952).

Why Soviet-Backed Political Systems Took Hold in Eastern Europe

Long-Term Conditions

Interwar instability and authoritarian regimes in the region; the Nazi-Soviet pact and German occupation, which discredited many prewar elites; Soviet security concerns shaped by the experience of 1941 and the earlier "cordon sanitaire."

Intermediate Developments

Military victories placing the Red Army across the region; wartime Allied agreements granting the USSR predominant influence in the Balkans and Poland's eastern frontier; formation of communist-led governments of national fronts.

Short-Term Triggers

Disputes over elections and governments in Poland and Romania; the Marshall Plan offer of 1947; Western policies in Germany; the formation of the Cominform and the Soviet decision to consolidate loyal regimes.

Effects

Section 60.22

From Wartime Alliance to Cold War, 1945–1949

The breakdown of the Grand Alliance was gradual and had multiple causes. Disagreements over Poland, Romania, Germany's reparations, the control of Japan, Iran, and the Turkish Straits unfolded in 1945–1946. In Iran, Soviet troops remained in the northern provinces beyond the agreed March 1946 withdrawal date and supported separatist movements until diplomatic pressure and a Soviet-Iranian arrangement led to withdrawal in May. In February 1946 Stalin gave a speech emphasizing the incompatibility of capitalism and socialism; George Kennan, the American chargé in Moscow, sent his "Long Telegram" interpreting Soviet conduct as rooted in insecurity and ideology; in March Churchill, in Fulton, Missouri, spoke of an "iron curtain" descending across Europe. The Soviet ambassador in Washington, Nikolai Novikov, reported to Moscow in September 1946 that the United States sought world domination. Each side interpreted the other's actions in the light of its own fears.

The U.S. policy of containment took institutional form in the Truman Doctrine of March 1947, which offered aid to Greece and Turkey, and in the Marshall Plan announced in June 1947, offering economic assistance to European states. Soviet leaders, who initially considered participation, rejected the plan as an instrument of American domination, and pressured Czechoslovakia and Poland to withdraw their acceptance. In September 1947 the Soviet Union created the Cominform, and Andrei Zhdanov's speech at its founding described a world divided into two camps. The Soviet response in Eastern Europe was consolidation; in January 1949 the Council for Mutual Economic Assistance (Comecon) was established.

Germany was the principal arena. After the Western powers introduced a new currency in their zones in June 1948, the Soviet authorities blockaded land and water access to the Western sectors of Berlin from 24 June 1948. The Western Allies supplied the city by air in the Berlin Airlift until the blockade ended in May 1949. The Federal Republic of Germany was founded in May 1949 and the German Democratic Republic in October; NATO was established in April 1949. A different crisis arose in the communist world: the Soviet-Yugoslav split of 1948, in which the Cominform expelled Yugoslavia's party in June after disputes over Tito's independence and ambitions in the Balkans, showed that Soviet leadership did not command uniform obedience. Stalin responded with purges and show trials against alleged "Titoists" in other states. Historians disagree about whether the Cold War's origins lay primarily in Soviet ideology and expansion, in American policy, or in a security dilemma in which defensive moves seemed threatening, and most contemporary scholarship, drawing on archives from several countries, favors multi-causal explanations. The Khrushchev-era evolution of the conflict is the subject of Chapter 62.

Section 60.23

The Soviet Union as a Global Superpower, 1949–1953

The Soviet atomic project, organized from 1943 under Igor Kurchatov and accelerated by the Special Committee established after Hiroshima under Beria's supervision, produced the first Soviet nuclear explosion on 29 August 1949 at the Semipalatinsk test site in Kazakhstan. The project drew on espionage that provided information about American designs (including from Klaus Fuchs), but also on the work of Soviet physicists and engineers and a vast mobilization of resources, including the forced labor of Gulag prisoners in uranium mining and construction and the use of German specialists and uranium from East Germany. The test ended the American monopoly and changed Soviet security. Local populations near the test site experienced radiation exposure, an issue that emerged in later decades.

The victory of the Chinese Communist Party in 1949 created a second major communist power. Mao Zedong visited Moscow from December 1949 to February 1950, and the two states signed a Treaty of Friendship, Alliance, and Mutual Assistance on 14 February 1950, accompanied by Soviet credits and technical assistance, and by agreements on Soviet interests in Manchuria. The relationship combined cooperation and mutual suspicion, as later chapters will show. In Korea, the division at the 38th parallel was followed by the establishment of rival states in 1948. North Korean leader Kim Il Sung secured Stalin's approval in 1950, after Stalin consulted Mao, for an attack on South Korea, which began on 25 June 1950. The USSR had been boycotting the UN Security Council over the Chinese seat, which allowed the council to authorize a U.S.-led defense; China intervened in October 1950; Soviet pilots, flying aircraft marked as Chinese or Korean, participated in air combat from late 1950. The armistice came after Stalin's death, in July 1953.

By 1953 the Soviet Union had a military and political position unmatched in its history, in command of an extensive bloc in Eastern Europe and allied with China, but it remained far weaker than the United States economically and in nuclear capability. The costs were substantial: military spending and heavy industry took priority over consumption, and Soviet leaders viewed their position as vulnerable. Historians stress that the "superpower" status that Soviet leaders achieved coexisted with insecurity, and they continue to debate how far Stalin's postwar foreign policy was driven by caution or by opportunism; there is broad agreement that he sought to consolidate gains and avoid direct war with the West.

Division V — Reconstruction, Famine, and Postwar Soviet Society

Section 60.24

The Fourth Five-Year Plan and Industrial Reconstruction, 1946–1950

The Fourth Five-Year Plan, adopted by the Supreme Soviet in March 1946, set the goal of restoring the economy of the regions that had been occupied and exceeding prewar levels of industrial output by 1950. Planning remained under Gosplan, headed by Nikolai Voznesensky, who had directed wartime economic coordination. The plan gave priority to heavy industry, energy, and transport, including coal, steel, electric power, machine-building, and railways, and to the rebuilding of devastated cities such as Stalingrad, Kyiv, Minsk, Kharkiv, and Sevastopol. The Dnieper hydroelectric station, destroyed in 1941, was restored by 1947. Investment in the western regions was complemented by continued industrial expansion in the Urals, Siberia, and Central Asia, where wartime evacuation had created new capacity.

Resources came from a variety of sources. State investment was financed by taxation, compulsory loans, and the low prices paid to collective farms for their produce. Reparations and trophies from Germany, Romania, Hungary, and Finland, and the dismantling of factories in the occupied zones, provided equipment and materials; in eastern Germany, the Soviet Union also operated joint companies, among them the Wismut uranium enterprise. Labor came from demobilized soldiers, workers recruited or directed into industry, the evacuated, and large numbers of coerced workers, including Gulag prisoners, whose numbers in camps and colonies were roughly 2.5 million in the early 1950s, German and Japanese prisoners of war, and special settlers. Labor discipline laws from 1940 remained in force, making unauthorized job changes a criminal offense. The government announced that the plan had been fulfilled ahead of schedule, in about four years and three months, and that industrial output in 1950 exceeded the 1940 level by around 70 percent. Historians recognize substantial real recovery, particularly in heavy industry and energy, but note that official statistics were affected by pricing practices, the inclusion of newly annexed territories, and the political incentives to report success.

The distinction between industrial reconstruction and living standards is crucial. Heavy industry recovered faster than agriculture, housing, and consumer goods, and real wages remained below the 1940 level for several years, with some recovery in the early 1950s through annual price reductions after 1947. The state's emphasis on investment goods and military production, intensified from 1948–1950 as the Cold War deepened, limited resources for housing and consumption. The reconstruction thus demonstrated the capacity of the Stalinist planned economy to mobilize resources for chosen priorities, while also illustrating its costs to consumers and agricultural producers.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Industrialization Before and After the War

What Stayed the Same

  • The prioritization of heavy industry and defense over consumer goods, as in the prewar Five-Year Plans (Chapter 55).
  • Central planning through Gosplan and the economic ministries, with campaign methods and labor discipline.
  • The extraction of resources from agriculture and the use of coerced labor.

What Changed

  • The geography of industry: the war had created large industrial centers in the east, while the west had to be rebuilt.
  • Technology and science: nuclear, aviation, and radar sectors expanded, using large research institutions.
  • External resources: reparations and the economies of Eastern Europe supplemented Soviet resources.

Why the pattern matters: Late Stalinist reconstruction shows that the Soviet economic model survived the war essentially intact, but in a transformed international and geographic setting. Section 60.25

Agriculture, State Procurement, and the Famine of 1946–1947

Soviet agriculture emerged from the war in severe condition. The occupied regions had lost livestock, equipment, and buildings; the countryside had given up its young men for the army; horses had been requisitioned; and collective farms depended on women, older men, and adolescents. Tractors and machinery were in short supply, and fertilizer and fuel were scarce. In 1946, a severe drought struck Ukraine, Moldova, the central Black Earth region, the Lower Volga, and parts of the North Caucasus, reducing the grain harvest to well under half of the 1940 level by commonly cited official figures, around forty million tons.

The state, however, kept up demands for procurement from collective farms, and in many districts took grain needed for peasants' own consumption; it also maintained exports of grain to Eastern European states, France, and other countries, and kept strategic reserves. Rationing was tightened for some urban groups in 1946, and rural residents, who were not rationed, received little relief. Scholars of the famine, among them Michael Ellman, Elena Zubkova, and Vladimir Zima, agree that drought and war damage were root causes, and that official policies on procurement, price, and distribution intensified the disaster; they differ on how much weight to assign to each, on how well the leadership understood the scale of the crisis, and on how much could have been done. Peasants ate substitutes, such as weeds and bark, sold possessions, and migrated to cities; cases of cannibalism are recorded in official reports. Mortality estimates, which vary with the sources and method, are commonly given in a range from roughly one to two million excess deaths, and no precise figure is established. The famine was less well documented than that of 1932–1933 (see Chapter 56), and it received little public acknowledgment in the Soviet period. Unlike the earlier famine, no historian of note has argued that it was a deliberate attempt to eliminate a particular national group, and scholars generally treat it as a consequence of war, drought, and policy choices.

The state responded partially. In 1947 the government allocated grain for seed and food relief, and it adopted measures to reduce procurement pressure in some regions; in September 1946 a decree had ordered the return of land illegally taken from collective farms, in an attempt to restore the collective farm charter. The Ukrainian party leader Nikita Khrushchev, who had asked for relief in 1946, was temporarily replaced by Lazar Kaganovich in March 1947, and he returned in December. A good harvest in 1947 and the end of rationing in December 1947 marked the transition from famine to recovery. The famine also illustrated the persistent subordination of the countryside to state priorities, a pattern that persisted into the Khrushchev years.

Regional Context

Ukraine and Moldova during the Famine of 1946–1947

In the Ukrainian SSR, which had suffered devastation by war and occupation, the famine affected the southern and eastern provinces and parts of the right bank. In the Moldavian SSR, which Soviet forces had annexed in 1940 and retaken in 1944, the combined effects of drought, wartime destruction, high procurement quotas on a recently incorporated peasantry, and the pressure to begin collectivization produced exceptionally severe conditions; modern estimates of famine deaths there range from about 100,000 to 200,000, a very large share of a small population, although estimates differ. Local officials sent appeals to Moscow, and reports from the security organs described starvation, mass migration, and desperation. Moldovan and Ukrainian experiences differed in timing and character from those of Russian provinces, and regional memories of the famine are shaped by the later political histories of the two republics, including its commemoration after 1991.

Viewed from outside Moscow, then, the famine was not simply an extension of reconstruction; it was an experience of acute hardship whose severity depended on region, local administration, and the degree of devastation. This regional view also complicates the treatment of the "postwar" period as uniformly one of victory and recovery.

Section 60.26

Housing, Consumer Goods, Women, Veterans, and Everyday Postwar Life

For most Soviet citizens, postwar life was defined by scarcity. Housing was the most acute problem: the war had destroyed millions of dwellings, and urban populations were growing as people returned and migrated. Urban families commonly lived in communal apartments (kommunalki), in which several households shared a kitchen and bathroom; many lived in barracks, dugouts, or self-built huts on city outskirts. Rural families rebuilt houses with local materials, often without state help. Food was monotonous, with bread and potatoes as the basis, supplemented by garden produce; collective farmers depended heavily on household plots, which the state taxed heavily. Clothing and household goods were scarce and often repaired, and trophy goods from Germany, such as sewing machines and watches, were sought after.

The government abolished rationing and reformed the currency in December 1947. The reform, announced on 14 December, exchanged old money for new at a rate of ten to one for cash and at more favorable rates for small savings, which reduced the money held by peasants who had profited from wartime market sales and by others who held cash; it was accompanied by the end of the ration-card system, and by the introduction of unified prices at levels above prewar rationed prices but below the wartime market prices. Annual price reductions from 1948 or 1949, announced each spring, raised real incomes modestly and became a tool of propaganda. Wages for many workers remained low, and the proportion of income spent on food was high. Expectations that victory would bring a better life, encouraged by wartime propaganda and by the experience of seeing European standards in occupied countries, were partly disappointed.

Women played central roles. They formed a majority of the postwar adult population, worked in industry, construction, and agriculture in numbers well above prewar levels, and carried most domestic burdens. After the war many women were displaced from skilled industrial positions by returning male workers, although they continued in heavy labor in many sectors. State family policy in 1944 provided benefits for large families, honored "mother-heroines," and taxed single and childless adults, while limiting recognition of de facto marriages and making divorce harder; the ban on abortion, introduced in 1936, remained in force until 1955. Schools were separated by sex in cities from 1943 until 1954. Veterans' experience differed by rank, wound, and region, and postwar culture on the one hand celebrated them as heroes and on the other offered limited material support. Popular culture, with its films, songs, and literature, often focused on the war's memory and on family reunion, while the state encouraged discipline, productivity, and vigilance.

Section 60.27

Repression and Resistance in the Reconstructed Borderlands

In the territories annexed in 1939–1940 and reconquered in 1944, Soviet authorities faced both armed resistance and the task of political and economic integration. In western Ukraine, the UPA and the nationalist underground, which fought until the early 1950s, were opposed by NKVD and MVD troops, "destruction battalions" of local pro-Soviet recruits, and a policy of mass arrests, executions, and deportations. The underground in turn killed Soviet officials, teachers, collective farm activists, and suspected informers, including civilians, and the conflict assumed the character of a civil war with terrible violence on both sides. The Soviet state also pursued a religious policy: in March 1946 the Ukrainian Greek Catholic (Uniate) Church was forced at the Lviv Council to "reunite" with the Russian Orthodox Church under Moscow, and its bishops were arrested. Tens of thousands of western Ukrainians were deported in operations of 1944–1952, with estimates that range up to the hundreds of thousands depending on the categories counted.

In the Baltic republics, Soviet policy included the reimposition of Soviet institutions, the nationalization of industry, the distribution of land and its subsequent collectivization, and mass deportations. In March 1949, Operation Priboi (Surf) deported roughly 90,000 people, around 20,000 from Estonia, 42,000 from Latvia, and 32,000 from Lithuania, to Siberia and other regions of the USSR, targeting "kulaks," families of partisans, and others, and it hastened the collectivization of agriculture, which was largely complete by 1950–1951. Lithuanian armed resistance was especially strong and prolonged, with tens of thousands of partisans and a larger network of supporters; the last organized units were destroyed in the early 1950s, although individuals hid for years. Moldova faced a deportation of some 35,000 people in July 1949 (Operation South) and collectivization. Industrial and administrative integration brought migrants from other Soviet republics, especially Russians, into the Baltic republics, changing their demographic composition, a development that became politically important in the perestroika period (see Chapter 66).

These territories also illustrate how the Gulag and related institutions operated after the war. The camps and colonies held about 2.5 million prisoners around 1950, including large numbers of people convicted in the immediate postwar years for collaboration, desertion, and political offenses, together with common criminals, and the special settlement system held more than two million additional people, including the deported nationalities. Forced labor was used in construction, mining, forestry, and the atomic project. The postwar camp population also included many women and, after 1947 laws that sharply increased penalties for theft of state and personal property, large numbers of people imprisoned for petty offenses. Capital punishment, abolished for peacetime in May 1947, was restored for treason, espionage, and sabotage in January 1950. Historians continue to study how this repression was experienced differently by Ukrainians, Balts, Poles, Moldovans, and others, and how historical memory of it shaped the politics of the post-Soviet republics.

Division VI — Late Stalinism and the End of an Era, 1946–1953

Section 60.28

Stalin's Postwar Government and the Restoration of Political Control

Stalin's formal titles changed with the war's end: the State Defense Committee, the wartime emergency body, was abolished in September 1945, and in March 1946 the People's Commissariats were renamed ministries and Stalin became Chairman of the Council of Ministers, with the title of Generalissimo conferred in June 1945. The Politburo met rarely as a body; decisions were taken in small groups of senior figures, including Molotov, Beria, Malenkov, Mikoyan, Kaganovich, Voroshilov, and, in the late 1940s, Khrushchev and Bulganin. Stalin was seventy in December 1949, and he spent long periods at his southern residences. Historians stress that the system remained strongly personalized but also depended on bureaucratic institutions: the party apparatus under the Secretariat, the economic ministries, the security organs, and the military, among which rivalry was common and sometimes manipulated by Stalin.

The security organs were reorganized in 1946 into the Ministry of Internal Affairs (MVD) and the Ministry of State Security (MGB), the latter headed by Viktor Abakumov until his arrest in 1951. The state moved to reassert discipline over the army, whose wartime prestige had given its leaders independent standing. Marshal Zhukov was removed from his central position in 1946 and sent to command the Odesa military district and later the Urals, and the "Trophy Case" and "Aviation Case" investigations targeted generals and officials accused of misappropriation and other offenses. Party institutions were revived: the party, whose membership had grown substantially during the war, resumed regular ideological education, and the Central Committee's departments tightened control over appointments. The party congress, absent since 1939, did not meet until October 1952.

Late Stalinism was not simply a continuation of the prewar system. The state governed a much larger and more complex empire, including the new external bloc; it needed to rebuild a devastated economy; it faced new international threats and a new population of veterans; and it dealt with societal expectations of relaxation. Historians such as Elena Zubkova have argued that postwar society hoped for reform and that the regime responded by reasserting control, while Yoram Gorlizki, Oleg Khlevniuk, and others have shown that bureaucratic institutions and elite politics continued to evolve. The late 1940s thus combined consolidation, institutional change, and renewed purges.

Section 60.29

Culture, Science, and Ideological Campaigns after Victory

From August 1946 the Central Committee issued a series of resolutions directing cultural life under the supervision of Andrei Zhdanov, a campaign known as the Zhdanovshchina. The first, on 14 August 1946, "On the journals Zvezda and Leningrad," condemned the satirist Mikhail Zoshchenko and the poet Anna Akhmatova, who were attacked as apolitical or alien to Soviet life; both were expelled from the Writers' Union, and the Leningrad journals were reorganized. Further resolutions addressed theater repertoire (August 1946), cinema (September 1946, including a ban on the second part of Sergei Eisenstein's Ivan the Terrible), and, in February 1948, music: the resolution on Vano Muradeli's opera The Great Friendship condemned "formalism" and criticized leading composers, including Dmitri Shostakovich, Sergei Prokofiev, and Aram Khachaturian. The campaigns demanded art that was accessible, optimistic, patriotic, and loyal to party ideas; they also used the rhetoric of resisting "servility before the West."

The campaigns extended to science and scholarship. In August 1948, at a session of the Lenin All-Union Academy of Agricultural Sciences, Trofim Lysenko announced that the Central Committee had approved his rejection of Mendelian genetics, and geneticists were dismissed, laboratories closed, and Soviet biology was dominated by Lysenko's doctrines for years, with severe damage to research and teaching; Lysenko's authority continued until the mid-1960s. Other fields experienced similar pressure: philosophy, history, economics, physiology, and linguistics. A campaign against "kowtowing to the West" and "rootless cosmopolitanism," which began in 1946–1947 and intensified in 1949, assaulted Western influence and Jewish writers and critics. In physics, an attempted ideological campaign against relativity and quantum mechanics was restrained because of the needs of the atomic project, an illustration of the limits imposed by state priorities. In 1950 Stalin himself intervened in linguistics, rejecting the theories of Nikolai Marr, and in 1952 he published Economic Problems of Socialism in the USSR.

Yet culture in late Stalinism was not simply a wasteland. Soviet audiences read and watched works that dealt with the war, such as Konstantin Simonov's and Viktor Nekrasov's writings; the film industry produced popular comedies and epics; scientists, protected by the atomic and military programs, achieved major advances; and an expanding school and university system trained millions. Some works created in these years, such as poetry and music written "for the drawer," circulated privately and appeared only after Stalin's death. Historians argue over how much creative autonomy persisted and how far the campaigns reflected Stalin's own preferences, Zhdanov's initiatives, or institutional competition.

Historical Vocabulary
Zhdanovshchina

Definition: The name, derived from Andrei Zhdanov, for the campaign of ideological and cultural discipline launched in 1946, often extended in scholarship to the wider campaigns against "cosmopolitanism" and Western influence in the late 1940s.

Historical context: The term originated later, in the post-Stalin period. Zhdanov died in August 1948, but the campaigns continued after his death under other leaders, so the label is imperfect.

Why it matters: It shows how culture and science became instruments of state policy in the early Cold War, while reminding students that a label tied to one person may obscure institutional and political causes.

Section 60.30

State Antisemitism, the Jewish Anti-Fascist Committee, and the Doctors' Plot

Soviet policy toward Jews was shaped by the experience of the Holocaust, the creation of Israel, and Stalin's suspicions of foreign connections. The Jewish Anti-Fascist Committee (JAC), founded in 1942 under the chairmanship of the actor Solomon Mikhoels, had gathered international Jewish support for the war effort. After 1945, the Committee gathered materials on the Nazi murder of Soviet Jews for the Black Book, edited by Ilya Ehrenburg and Vasily Grossman, whose publication was halted in 1947–1948. Mikhoels was murdered in Minsk on 13 January 1948 by MGB agents on Stalin's orders, in an operation disguised as a traffic accident. In November 1948 the Committee was dissolved; in the following months, its members and other Jewish cultural figures were arrested. Jewish theaters, schools, and publications were closed. The Soviet Union recognized Israel in May 1948, and enthusiastic crowds greeted Israeli envoy Golda Meir in Moscow that autumn, which may have heightened official suspicion.

The campaign against "rootless cosmopolitans," which grew in 1949, targeted writers, critics, scientists, and officials, many of them Jewish, and dismissed thousands from posts. The JAC trial, held in secret in May–July 1952, resulted in the executions on 12 August 1952 of thirteen defendants, including the Yiddish poets Perets Markish, David Bergelson, and Leib Kvitko, and Solomon Lozovsky, former head of the Soviet Information Bureau; the biologist Lina Stern was among those who received prison or exile sentences. In the neighboring bloc, the Slánský trial in Prague in November 1952 resulted in the execution of eleven defendants, most of them of Jewish origin, on charges of "Zionist" conspiracy. These campaigns involved distinct but related processes: the suppression of Jewish institutions, a purge of cultural and professional elites, and a security trial framed as treason.

On 13 January 1953 the Soviet press announced the arrest of a group of "saboteur-doctors," mostly prominent physicians at the Kremlin clinic, accused of killing Politburo members Andrei Zhdanov and Aleksandr Shcherbakov through deliberate medical malpractice and of planning to kill military leaders, at the instigation of American and British intelligence and the Jewish charity, the American Jewish Joint Distribution Committee. Six of the nine named doctors were Jewish. The announcement began a campaign of denunciations in the press, accompanied by dismissals and by public fear. The case drew on a letter by the physician Lydia Timashuk, written in 1948, and it was directed by MGB investigators under pressure from Stalin, who personally demanded confessions. After Stalin's death the accused were released, and the government announced in April 1953 that the charges had been fabricated.

Historians distinguish what is documented from what is hypothesized. The arrests, interrogations, press campaign, and trials of the JAC are documented in archival records. A related belief, that Stalin planned mass deportation of Soviet Jews to Siberia or the Far East in early 1953, rests on later testimony and on a draft letter of "prominent Jews" condemning the doctors, but no archival order for mass deportation has been found; some scholars consider the plan plausible, while others regard the evidence as inadequate. Motives are likely to have been mixed: Stalin's suspicion of cosmopolitan and Western contacts, the context of the Cold War and the creation of Israel, anxiety about his health and his entourage, and the logic of purges against the security leadership, since the arrest of the doctors was linked to the removal of senior MGB officials. No single motive is established.

Section 60.31

The Leningrad Affair, Late Purges, and the Crisis of the Stalinist Leadership

The Leningrad Affair of 1949–1950 was a purge of leading officials who had risen through the Leningrad party organization, a group associated with Zhdanov, who had led Leningrad before and during the war. After Zhdanov's death in 1948, his rivals, principally Malenkov and Beria, moved against his associates, including Aleksei Kuznetsov, who had been a Central Committee secretary, Mikhail Rodionov, chairman of the Council of Ministers of the Russian Republic, Pyotr Popkov, party head in Leningrad, and Nikolai Voznesensky, the head of Gosplan and a Politburo member. They were accused of anti-party activity, of creating a Leningrad "center" opposed to the party, and of other offenses. Voznesensky was removed in March 1949, arrested later that year, and, with Kuznetsov, Rodionov, Popkov, and others, tried in secret in Leningrad and shot on 1 October 1950. Hundreds of officials in Leningrad and elsewhere lost their positions or were arrested, and the Leningrad siege museum was closed.

Other purges followed. The "Mingrelian Affair" of 1951–1952, directed at officials in Georgia including Mingrelians connected to Beria, weakened his networks; Abakumov, the head of the MGB, was arrested in July 1951; and in 1952–1953 Stalin's closest staff were removed, among them his longtime secretary Aleksandr Poskrebyshev and security chief Nikolai Vlasik. The Nineteenth Party Congress, held in October 1952, the first since 1939, renamed the party the Communist Party of the Soviet Union (CPSU), replaced the Politburo with a larger Presidium of twenty-five members and eleven candidates, and approved a new structure. Malenkov gave the main report, and Stalin spoke briefly at the end. At a Central Committee plenum immediately afterward, Stalin attacked Molotov and Mikoyan, two of his oldest associates, in language that suggested they might be removed.

Historians interpret these events in different ways. Some, such as Khlevniuk, see Stalin as preparing a further purge of his entourage and as intending to renew the instruments of terror, in a pattern like that of 1936–1938, while others emphasize his declining health, the intensification of factional rivalry, and the logic of institutional change, including the new Presidium, which may have been intended to dilute the old leaders' power. There is general agreement that the leadership was in a state of uncertainty and fear, and that no clear succession arrangement existed. The documentary record remains incomplete, and attempts to infer Stalin's intentions require caution.

Section 60.32

Stalin's Death, March 1953, and the End of High Stalinism

Stalin, aged seventy-four, spent the evening of 28 February 1953 at his dacha at Kuntsevo with Beria, Malenkov, Khrushchev, and Bulganin. He was found by staff in the early hours of 2 March, or the late evening of 1 March, lying on the floor, having suffered a stroke; doctors were called only after a delay of many hours, and the physicians who treated him were not those who had been arrested in the Doctors' Plot, since many of the leading specialists were in custody. The government announced his illness on 4 March, and he died on the evening of 5 March 1953. Some writers have suggested that he was poisoned, or that Beria or others hastened his death, but these hypotheses rest on circumstantial evidence and no documentation has established them; most historians accept a cerebral hemorrhage as the cause.

The funeral on 9 March was accompanied by an outpouring of public mourning, and large crowds converged on the center of Moscow; a crush on the approaches to the Hall of Columns killed an unknown number of people, estimates varying from dozens to many hundreds. Stalin's body was placed beside Lenin's in the Mausoleum. Public reaction was varied: grief, sincere or conformist, among many, shock and anxiety about the future, and, privately and in some places in the camps and special settlements, hope for change. Soviet leaders rapidly announced a new government, in which Malenkov became chairman of the Council of Ministers, Beria headed a combined MVD, and a reduced Presidium was formed; what followed, the struggle for power and the dismantling of parts of the repressive system, is the subject of Chapter 61.

The state that Stalin left was formidable and deeply scarred. It had won a world war, rebuilt industry, developed nuclear weapons, and led a bloc of states. It also held millions in camps, colonies, and special settlements, had recently experienced famine, and operated a political system built on fear and the personal authority of one man. The extent to which the system could continue without him was uncertain to contemporaries.

Historical Debate

Assessing Stalin's Legacy and the Nature of Late Stalinism

The question: How should historians characterize the Soviet system in its final Stalinist years, and how should they assess Stalin's role and legacy?

Interpretation A — A dictatorship driven by Stalin and the security state: Scholars in the "totalitarian" tradition and many others stress the centrality of Stalin's decisions, the role of ideology and terror, and the continuity of coercive institutions from the 1930s through 1953. Archival evidence on the Leningrad Affair, the Doctors' Plot, and Stalin's conduct in 1952 is used to argue that he retained decisive control and was preparing new purges.
Interpretation B — A complex system with institutions, social participation, and limits: Other historians, including those in the "revisionist" and social-history traditions, emphasize bureaucratic politics, popular participation, social mobility, wartime legitimacy, and the system's improvisation and inefficiency. They stress that the state depended on administrators, specialists, and a population that in various ways accepted, adapted to, or benefited from it, and that policies often resulted from institutional competition and local initiative. Both traditions have been refined by post-1991 archival research.

Evidence: Politburo and Council of Ministers records, security-service files, party and Gosplan archives, correspondence and diaries, oral histories, memoirs, demographic data, and the statistics of the Gulag system.

Points of agreement: Stalin held ultimate authority; the system relied on coercion, including mass imprisonment, deportation, and executions; it also mobilized resources effectively for industrialization and war; and the documented scale of repression is large.

Points of disagreement: The relative weight of Stalin's personal role versus institutions; the degree of popular support, and how to interpret surveys and memory; the extent to which later Soviet and Russian developments were shaped by Stalinist institutions; and the question of what Stalin planned in 1952–1953.

Why it matters: Assessments of Stalin and of the system he led also inform contemporary memory politics, in which his role in winning the war has been emphasized by some political figures and surveys have recorded significant approval of his "role" in Russia, while historians and organizations such as Memorial have documented the repression (see Chapter 72).

Section 60.33

Chapter Review and Historical Thinking Checkpoint

This chapter has traced the Soviet Union from the destruction of the German Sixth Army to the death of Stalin, and it has argued that victory, coercion, and reconstruction must be understood together. The Red Army's improvement between 1943 and 1944 resulted from learning, from the relocation of industry and the supply of trucks and raw materials from abroad, from superior planning in operations such as Kursk and Bagration, and from the strategic errors of the German command; it was also paid for in enormous casualties. Students should be able to explain the shift in initiative in 1943 as a process with multiple causes, not as the result of a single battle or of numbers alone.

The chapter has stressed that liberation and domination coincided. The Red Army ended German occupation in Ukraine, Belarus, the Baltic, Poland, and Southeastern Europe, but the Soviet state also deported nationalities, filtered returning citizens, suppressed resistance in the borderlands, and supported communist control in neighboring countries. The Soviet experience of war included the deaths of some 26 to 27 million people by the usual estimate, a figure whose uncertainty must be understood, and unequal distributions of suffering among republics and peoples. Evidence concerning these developments comes from military records, NKVD and party archives, decrees, diplomatic documents, memoirs, oral histories, and demographic data, and each has strengths and limits.

After 1945, Soviet power rested on military strength and on a political system that reasserted control over society, culture, and the elite. The Fourth Five-Year Plan restored industry but did not quickly improve living standards; the famine of 1946–1947 showed the vulnerability of the countryside; ideological campaigns, anti-Jewish measures, and the Leningrad Affair showed the persistence of repression; and international developments from the Marshall Plan to the Korean War shaped the transition from alliance to Cold War. Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the Soviet Union between the destruction of the German Sixth Army at Stalingrad in February 1943 and the death of Stalin in March 1953. Its central problem was how the USSR achieved military victory, converted that victory into international power, and then confronted reconstruction, hardship, and renewed Stalinist control. The answer required attention to military operations, to the Soviet and non-Russian peoples who fought and suffered, to the coercive institutions of the state, and to the international system that emerged from the war.

The strategic turning point of 1943 followed Stalingrad, the failed Soviet overreach in the winter, and the German defeat at Kursk, after which the Red Army held the initiative. Its improved operational practice, the evacuated industrial base, Allied deliveries of trucks and materials, and German strategic errors all contributed. The advance to the Dnieper, the recapture of Kyiv, the lifting of the siege of Leningrad, the liberation of Ukraine and Crimea, and Operation Bagration extended these advantages, and the destruction of Army Group Centre opened the path into Poland.

The liberation of occupied territories was also the return of Soviet power. Partisans, collaborators, and ordinary civilians had experienced occupation differently, and the returning authorities screened, punished, and mobilized them. In 1943–1944 the government deported the Karachai, Kalmyks, Chechens, Ingush, Balkars, Crimean Tatars, and other peoples on charges of collective treason, a policy of collective punishment with grave demographic consequences and a long afterlife in memory. In the Baltic republics and western Ukraine, renewed Soviet rule met armed resistance and brought deportations and collectivization.

The defeat of Germany in 1945 involved campaigns in Southeastern Europe, the Warsaw Uprising and the politics of Polish liberation, the Vistula–Oder offensive, and the Battle of Berlin. The Red Army liberated camps and defeated Nazi Germany, but its soldiers also committed widespread looting and sexual violence, and Soviet policies laid foundations for communist control. In August 1945 the USSR entered the war against Japan, conquering Manchuria, southern Sakhalin, and the Kurils, which produced territorial questions that remain unresolved.

The human cost was immense. Estimates of about 26 to 27 million Soviet deaths, though uncertain and dependent on method, indicate the scale; losses and destruction were distributed unequally among regions and peoples, and left a society with a severe imbalance between women and men, millions of disabled veterans and orphans, and devastated cities and villages. Demobilization, repatriation, and filtration produced varied experiences for returning soldiers, prisoners, and displaced civilians.

Internationally, the Yalta and Potsdam conferences set frameworks that the powers interpreted differently. Soviet-backed communist-led governments were established across Eastern Europe through processes that varied by country, combining local support, coercion, and Soviet security interests. The wartime alliance gave way to the Cold War through a series of confrontations over Germany, Iran, economic aid, and security, and the Soviet atomic test of 1949, the alliance with China, and the Korean War confirmed a bipolar system. The Soviet-Yugoslav split showed that Soviet authority within the communist world had limits.

Domestically, the Fourth Five-Year Plan restored industry, especially heavy industry and energy, through planning, reparations, and coerced labor, while consumption and agriculture lagged. The 1946–1947 famine, produced by drought, wartime damage, and procurement and distribution policies, killed an estimated one to two million people. The currency reform of 1947 and the end of rationing began a slow improvement in everyday conditions, but housing and consumer goods remained scarce, and women and veterans bore the burdens of reconstruction.

Late Stalinism combined consolidation and repression. Ideological campaigns reshaped literature, music, and science; the suppression of Jewish institutions and the Doctors' Plot revealed state antisemitism, even as historians distinguish documented actions from disputed hypotheses; and the Leningrad Affair and other purges revealed instability among the leadership. Stalin's death on 5 March 1953 ended an era but left open the question of what would follow.

The chapter's historiographical lessons are that victory should be neither reduced to a heroic myth nor to a story of repression alone, that statistics require attention to categories and methods, and that the postwar order resulted from interacting decisions and circumstances rather than a single plan. The developments here lead directly to Chapter 61, which treats the succession struggle and de-Stalinization, and to Chapter 62, which examines Khrushchev-era Cold War confrontation.

Key Terms

Great Patriotic War
The Soviet and Russian name for the war against Germany and its allies, 1941–1945; it excludes the 1939–1941 period and the war against Japan.
Strategic initiative
The capacity to determine where and when major offensives occur; held by the Red Army from mid-1943.
Operational depth
The depth behind enemy lines at which an operation is designed to have effect, using successive echelons and mobile forces.
Maskirovka
Soviet military deception, including concealment, feints, and disinformation, used notably before Bagration.
Operation Citadel
The German offensive of July 1943 against the Kursk salient, halted by Soviet defenses.
Operation Bagration
The Soviet offensive beginning 22 June 1944 that destroyed Army Group Centre in Belarus.
Lend-Lease
U.S. program supplying war matériel and other goods to Allies, including the USSR, valued at roughly eleven billion dollars for the Soviet Union.
Second front
The Soviet term for a Western Allied invasion of northwestern Europe, realized in June 1944.
Partisan movement
Armed resistance behind German lines, organized in part by Soviet authorities and strongest in Belarus.
Collaboration
A broad term for cooperation with an occupier, from coerced labor to voluntary participation in persecution; historians distinguish its forms.
Filtration
Security screening of returning soldiers, prisoners, and civilians, to identify suspected collaborators.
Collective punishment
Punishment of a group for alleged acts of some members, as in the 1943–1944 deportations of peoples.
Special settlement
Compulsory residence under NKVD supervision without confinement in camps, applied to deported peoples and others.
Extraordinary State Commission
Soviet body documenting German crimes and wartime damage; its figures were produced for political as well as evidentiary use.
Curzon Line
A proposed Polish-Soviet boundary of 1919–1920, roughly followed in 1944–1945 for the USSR's western border.
Reparations
Compensation extracted from defeated states, through payments, deliveries, and dismantling of industrial plant.
People's democracy
Official label for postwar regimes in Eastern Europe, initially coalitions, later communist-dominated.
Containment
U.S. policy of resisting the expansion of Soviet influence, associated with Kennan and the Truman Doctrine.
Cominform
The Communist Information Bureau (1947–1956), a coordinating organization of selected communist parties.
Comecon
Council for Mutual Economic Assistance, established in January 1949 to coordinate economies of the Soviet bloc.
Berlin Blockade
Soviet closure of land access to West Berlin, 1948–1949, answered by the Western airlift.
Fourth Five-Year Plan
The 1946–1950 plan for reconstruction and expansion, emphasizing heavy industry.
Communal apartment (kommunalka)
An apartment shared by several households with common kitchen and bathroom.
Currency reform of 1947
Exchange of old rubles for new on 14 December 1947 that cut cash holdings and ended rationing.
Zhdanovshchina
The postwar campaign of cultural and ideological discipline associated with Andrei Zhdanov.
Lysenkoism
The dominance of Trofim Lysenko's anti-Mendelian doctrines in Soviet biology, confirmed in 1948.
Rootless cosmopolitanism
Label used in a campaign from 1948–1949 against writers and others accused of Western orientation; in practice often directed against Jews.
Leningrad Affair
The purge of 1949–1950 of officials linked to the Leningrad party organization, including Voznesensky and Kuznetsov.
Doctors' Plot
The fabricated case announced in January 1953 accusing Kremlin physicians of murder; repudiated in April 1953.
High Stalinism
A scholarly label for the late 1940s and early 1950s, when Stalin's personal authority and ideological controls were at their peak.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it joins two histories that are often separated: the history of a great military victory and the history of the coercive state that won it. Between 1943 and 1953 the Soviet Union destroyed the main land forces of Nazi Germany, shared in the defeat of Japan, rebuilt its industry, acquired nuclear weapons, and led a bloc of states. In the same years, it deported peoples, imprisoned millions, endured famine, disciplined culture and science, and prepared further purges. Each statement is supported by evidence, and the historian's task is to explain how they coexisted.

The period changed the map and the political order of Europe and Asia, and it changed Soviet society. The demographic loss, the imbalance between the sexes, the widespread disability, the new borders, and the experience of life abroad shaped the generations that followed. The deportations created communities whose return and rehabilitation became political questions for decades. The establishment of the Eastern bloc and the early Cold War created institutions and rivalries that structured Soviet foreign policy until 1991.

The chapter also shows the importance of memory. The Great Patriotic War became the Soviet state's central legitimating narrative, and its commemoration, centered on Victory Day, has continued in the Russian Federation, while other societies, among them Ukrainians, Poles, Balts, Crimean Tatars, and Jews, remember the war and its aftermath differently. Chapters 66, 70, and 72 return to these questions. Students should keep in mind that memory is evidence about the societies that preserve and shape it, not a substitute for the evidence of the events themselves.

Stalin's death in March 1953 did not determine what would follow. Chapter 61 examines how his successors competed, how Beria fell, how Khrushchev rose, and how the Secret Speech and de-Stalinization began to dismantle parts of the system. Unresolved questions remain: how to estimate losses with greater accuracy, how to explain Stalin's intentions in his last years, how to compare the experiences of different nationalities, and how to judge the relationship between victory and repression. These questions continue to guide historical research.
