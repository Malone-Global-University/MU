Part VI — Peter the Great and the Eighteenth-Century Empire
31

Peter the Great and the Great Northern War

A History of Russia: From the Eurasian World to the Russian Federation

Chapter Opening

In November 1700, a Russian army of roughly forty thousand men, besieging the small Swedish fortress town of Narva on the Gulf of Finland, was routed in a matter of hours by a Swedish relief force perhaps a quarter its size. Twenty-one years later, in 1721, Russian diplomats sat across the negotiating table at Nystad in Finland and secured a highly favorable peace settlement from an exhausted Sweden, terms that gave the Russian state a permanent Baltic coastline, a fleet capable of contesting northern waters, and a ruler who now styled himself not tsar but emperor.

The distance between these two moments defines this chapter. Narva was not a minor skirmish; it was a humiliation witnessed across Europe, in which Russian infantry broke and fled, senior commanders including several foreign generals in Russian service surrendered or were captured, and the surviving field artillery was lost almost in its entirety. European newspapers treated the battle as confirmation of what many educated Europeans already assumed: that Muscovy, however large and populous, remained a military and political backwater, incapable of sustained warfare against a first-rank European power. Sweden under its young king, Charles XII, had in a single afternoon apparently settled the question of which state would dominate the eastern Baltic.

That assumption proved wrong, but not because of any single dramatic transformation. The two decades separating Narva from Nystad were filled with exhausting, uneven, often improvised work: the raising and training of new regiments, the casting of new artillery from melted church bells, the recruitment of foreign officers and the promotion of Russian ones, the founding of a new fortress city on marshy ground at the mouth of the Neva River, the building of a fleet where none of consequence had existed, and the extraction of soldiers, labor, and taxes from a population that had little say in the matter. Peter I, who had ruled jointly with his half-brother Ivan V since 1682 and become sole ruler in fact after 1696, presided over this transformation, but he did not invent it from nothing, and he did not accomplish it alone.

This chapter asks a specific question: how did a state that suffered a catastrophic defeat in 1700 become, by 1721, a major military power in northern and eastern Europe? Answering that question requires resisting two tempting but misleading stories. The first misleading story treats Peter as a solitary genius who single-handedly discovered Western civilization during his travels abroad and then imposed it on a passive, backward Russia. The second misleading story treats Russian military success as inevitable, the natural result of Russia's size and population, as though Sweden's defeat required no explanation at all. Neither story survives close examination. Muscovy before Peter had already been absorbing European military technique for decades, as earlier chapters on the seventeenth century have shown; and Sweden, far from a minor power destined to lose, was one of the most formidable military states in Europe, whose king Charles XII had already humbled far larger coalitions before he ever faced Peter's rebuilt army at Poltava.

The real story is one of strategic ambition colliding with military inadequacy, followed by a long, costly process of adaptation under the pressure of continued war. Peter's Baltic ambitions were neither arbitrary nor primarily cultural; they reflected calculations about trade routes, commercially advantageous ports closer to Russia's core, fortified frontiers, and the balance of power among Sweden, Denmark-Norway, and the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth that had structured northern European politics for generations. The war that resulted, the Great Northern War, lasted twenty-one years, ranged from the Gulf of Bothnia to the Ukrainian steppe, and reshaped the political map of northern and eastern Europe.

This chapter also introduces a theme that recurs across the remainder of Part VI: that Peter's reign should be understood as an intensification and acceleration of processes already under way in seventeenth-century Muscovy, examined in Chapter 26, rather than a sudden rupture with an otherwise unchanging past. At the same time, the scale of what the Great Northern War demanded—new taxes, new conscription systems, new industries, a new capital, a new fleet, a permanently expanded standing army—went well beyond anything the seventeenth-century Muscovite state had attempted, and it is this scale, sustained across two decades of war, that this chapter treats as the central transformation to be explained.

Finally, this chapter deliberately limits its scope. The broader institutional apparatus that Peter constructed and that outlasted the war itself—the Senate, the administrative colleges, the Table of Ranks, the poll tax, the subordination of the church to state administration, and the cultural reorientation symbolized by St. Petersburg—receives full treatment in Chapter 32. Here, those institutions appear only where necessary to explain how Russia won the war. The question this chapter answers is military and strategic: how war made a state, and at what cost.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Describe the political, military, and social condition of Muscovy on the eve of Peter's sole rule, and identify continuities with the seventeenth-century developments examined in Chapter 26.
  2. Explain the succession crisis of 1682, the Streltsy uprising, and the regency of Sophia as the political environment that shaped Peter's early life and rule.
  3. Analyze the Azov campaigns as a formative military experience that revealed the strategic importance of naval and logistical capacity.
  4. Evaluate the diplomatic and technical purposes of the Grand Embassy, distinguishing genuine innovation from continuity with earlier Muscovite contact with Europe.
  5. Explain the strategic origins of the Great Northern War in Baltic power politics among Russia, Sweden, Denmark-Norway, and Augustus II as Elector of Saxony.
  6. Analyze the Battle of Narva as a military and political crisis, identifying the specific weaknesses it exposed in Russian military organization.
  7. Trace the reconstruction of the Russian army and the creation of a Baltic fleet as a sustained, resource-intensive wartime mobilization.
  8. Explain the political choices of Ivan Mazepa and the Hetmanate during the Swedish invasion of Ukraine without projecting later national narratives backward.
  9. Assess the military and strategic significance of the Battle of Poltava within the broader course of the war.
  10. Explain the terms and consequences of the Treaty of Nystad and the significance of Peter's assumption of the imperial title.
  11. Distinguish the wartime military and fiscal mobilization examined in this chapter from the broader administrative, economic, and cultural reforms examined in Chapter 32.
  12. Evaluate competing historiographical interpretations of whether Peter's reign represented transformation or acceleration of existing trends.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. What military, administrative, and diplomatic foundations did Peter inherit from seventeenth-century Muscovy, and what remained genuinely underdeveloped?
  2. Why did Peter pursue Baltic access as a strategic objective, and how did that objective connect to broader patterns of European great-power competition?
  3. What specifically went wrong for the Russian army at Narva in 1700, and why did the defeat not destroy the Russian war effort?
  4. How did two decades of war reshape Russian military organization, taxation, and industry, and what did that reshaping cost ordinary subjects?
  5. Why did Ivan Mazepa align with Charles XII in 1708, and what does his decision reveal about the political position of the Hetmanate within the Muscovite/Russian state?
  6. Why was the Battle of Poltava decisive, and why did the war nonetheless continue for twelve more years afterward?
  7. What changed, concretely, between the Treaty of Nystad in 1721 and the situation Russia faced at Narva in 1700?
  8. To what extent should Peter's reign be understood as a transformation of Russia and to what extent as an acceleration of trends already under way?

Historical Context

Chapter 26 traced the reconstruction of the Muscovite state after the Time of Troubles and showed that, well before Peter's birth in 1672, Muscovy had already begun recruiting foreign military specialists, organizing "new formation" infantry and cavalry regiments trained along contemporary European lines, expanding its bureaucratic apparatus, and engaging diplomatically and commercially with a widening circle of European states. Tsar Alexei Mikhailovich's reign had seen substantial military reform prompted by wars with the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth and by the incorporation of the Left-Bank Hetmanate examined in Chapter 30. Foreign merchants, officers, and technical specialists already resided in Moscow's foreign quarter, the Nemetskaya Sloboda, where the young Peter would spend a great deal of time and form lasting friendships and professional relationships.

At the same time, real limitations persisted. Muscovy possessed no navy of consequence and no direct and strategically useful access to the Baltic or Black Sea; its coastlines on those seas were controlled respectively by Sweden and the Ottoman Empire and its Crimean Tatar vassals. Its army, though it included modernized regiments, still relied heavily on the traditional cavalry service of the landed service class and on the Streltsy, semi-professional musketeer garrison troops concentrated in Moscow and other towns, whose loyalty was frequently as much political as military. Artillery production, shipbuilding capacity, and the manufacture of firearms remained limited relative to leading European states. State finances depended on a tax and service system still recovering from the disruptions of the seventeenth century, examined in Chapters 26 and 27.

Internationally, the northern European balance of power in the late seventeenth century rested on Swedish dominance of the Baltic. Sweden, though smaller in population than Russia, Poland-Lithuania, or the Habsburg lands, had emerged from the Thirty Years' War and subsequent conflicts as a first-rank military power, controlling substantial territories on both shores of the Baltic Sea, including Livonia, Estonia, Ingria, and parts of northern Germany, and fielding an army and navy that had repeatedly defeated larger coalitions. This dominance constrained Russian, Danish-Norwegian, and Polish-Lithuanian ambitions alike, and by the 1690s each of these states had reason to seek an opportunity to challenge it.

It was within this inherited institutional and international context—a Muscovy already partially modernized but still militarily constrained, confronting a dominant Swedish Baltic power—that Peter's personal rule began. Understanding the war that followed requires holding both halves of this context in view: real continuity with seventeenth-century developments, and real, unresolved weaknesses that a prolonged war would expose and, over two decades, force the state to address.

Section 31.1

Russia Before Peter

By the 1680s, Muscovy was neither isolated from Europe nor institutionally static, despite a persistent later stereotype to the contrary. Chapter 26 has already shown that the reigns of Mikhail and, especially, Alexei Mikhailovich brought substantial administrative reconstruction after the Time of Troubles: an expanding network of central chancelleries (prikazy), a growing service nobility bound to the state through landholding obligations, and, from the 1630s onward, the recruitment of foreign officers to organize infantry and cavalry regiments—the so-called new formation regiments—trained and equipped along contemporary European lines. These regiments fought in the wars against the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth examined in Chapter 30, including the prolonged conflict over Ukraine that brought the Left-Bank Hetmanate under Muscovite suzerainty.

Muscovy's engagement with the wider world had likewise deepened across the seventeenth century. Foreign merchants and specialists—Dutch, English, German, and others—resided in Moscow's foreign quarter, the Nemetskaya Sloboda, working as officers, physicians, engineers, and traders. Muscovite diplomacy maintained regular contact with Poland-Lithuania, Sweden, the Ottoman Empire, and, more distantly, western European courts. Siberian expansion, examined in Chapter 28, had extended Muscovite authority to the Pacific and generated a valuable fur trade integrated into wider Eurasian commercial networks.

Yet significant limitations remained, and they matter directly for this chapter's subject. Muscovy possessed no standing navy of any consequence. Its only reliable maritime outlet, the port of Arkhangelsk on the White Sea, was frozen for much of the year and distant from the main centers of population and trade. Both the Baltic coast, controlled by Sweden since the seventeenth century's wars, and the Black Sea coast, controlled by the Ottoman Empire and its Crimean Tatar vassal state, lay beyond Muscovite reach. Artillery production, small-arms manufacture, and shipbuilding capacity remained modest by comparison with leading European states. The army's core still included the traditional cavalry service of the landed service class alongside the new formation regiments, and a substantial share of urban garrison duty fell to the Streltsy, whose loyalty, as Section 31.2 will show, could not always be relied upon. State revenue depended on a tax base still recovering from seventeenth-century disruption, examined in Chapter 27, and on a service and taxation system not yet organized for the scale of sustained warfare that the years after 1700 would demand.

This combination—real institutional development alongside real strategic and military limitation—is the essential starting point for this chapter. Peter did not inherit an unchanging medieval state that he single-handedly modernized. He inherited a state already in the middle of a slow process of military and administrative adaptation, a state that nonetheless lacked the naval power, the artillery and small-arms production, the trained officer corps, and the fiscal capacity to contest a first-rank European military power such as Sweden. The Great Northern War would expose exactly these gaps, and the response to that exposure is this chapter's central story.

Section 31.2

Peter's Childhood and the Streltsy

Peter was born in 1672, the son of Tsar Alexei Mikhailovich by his second wife, Natalia Naryshkina. Alexei's death in 1676 and the brief reign of Peter's half-brother Fyodor III, who died without an heir in 1682, opened a succession crisis rooted in rivalry between two branches of the royal family connected through Alexei's two marriages: the Miloslavsky relatives of Alexei's first wife and the Naryshkin relatives of his second. When Fyodor died, a group of boyars and churchmen proclaimed the ten-year-old Peter tsar, bypassing his elder but chronically ill half-brother Ivan, a decision that favored the Naryshkin faction.

This arrangement provoked a violent response. In May 1682, the Streltsy—the semi-professional musketeer garrison troops stationed in Moscow, whose grievances over pay, working conditions, and officer corruption had been building independently of dynastic politics—rose in revolt, encouraged by Miloslavsky partisans who spread rumors, some accurate and some fabricated, that the Naryshkins had murdered Ivan. Streltsy units stormed the Kremlin and killed a number of officials and Naryshkin relatives before the young Peter's eyes, an experience later historians have often linked to his lifelong wariness of the Streltsy and of Moscow's political establishment more broadly, though caution is warranted against treating this single episode as a complete psychological explanation for the whole of Peter's later conduct. The revolt's immediate political result was a compromise: Ivan V and Peter were proclaimed joint tsars, with Ivan's elder full sister Sophia Alekseyevna installed as regent, governing in the name of both boy-tsars.

Sophia's regency, which lasted from 1682 to 1689, was not simply a placeholder period. Her government, closely associated with her favorite, Prince Vasily Golitsyn, pursued an active foreign policy, including a treaty with Poland-Lithuania that formally recognized Muscovite possession of Kyiv and the Left-Bank Hetmanate, and two costly campaigns against the Crimean Khanate that achieved little militarily but reflected continuing Muscovite ambitions toward the Black Sea steppe frontier examined in Chapters 28 and 30. During these years, the young Peter was largely kept apart from formal governance and spent much of his time at country estates and in the Nemetskaya Sloboda, where he pursued a passionate interest in military drill, fortification, and shipbuilding, organizing play regiments of boyar and commoner youths that gradually evolved into serious, well-drilled units—the origin of what would become the Preobrazhensky and Semyonovsky Guards regiments, core formations of the later reformed army.

The regency ended in 1689 when Sophia's attempt to retain power, amid renewed Streltsy unrest, collapsed; she was confined to a convent, and effective authority passed to Peter's mother's family and, increasingly, to Peter himself, although Ivan V remained nominal co-tsar until his death in 1696. The Streltsy remained a recurring source of instability. A further, larger Streltsy revolt in 1698, occurring while Peter was abroad on the Grand Embassy examined in Section 31.4, was suppressed with considerable severity upon his return, including mass executions and the effective disbandment of much of the Streltsy establishment—an episode that anticipated the more thoroughgoing military reorganization the Great Northern War would soon demand.

Section 31.3

The Azov Campaigns

Peter's first major independent military undertaking targeted Azov, an Ottoman fortress commanding the mouth of the Don River and access to the Sea of Azov and, beyond it, the Black Sea. The objective fit within a long Muscovite and, more broadly, European pattern: Sophia's regency had already committed Muscovy, as part of the broader anti-Ottoman Holy League that included the Habsburg monarchy, Poland-Lithuania, and Venice, to campaigns against Ottoman and Crimean Tatar power on the steppe frontier. Peter's Azov campaigns continued this commitment rather than beginning a wholly new policy.

The first campaign, in 1695, ended in failure. A Russian army besieged Azov by land but lacked any naval capacity to blockade the fortress from the sea, allowing the Ottoman garrison to be resupplied and reinforced at will; after a costly siege, Peter withdrew his forces. The lesson Peter and his commanders drew was specific and consequential: without a fleet, even a substantial land army could not reduce a fortress accessible by water. Over the following winter, Peter organized a large-scale, hastily improvised shipbuilding effort at Voronezh, on a tributary of the Don, mobilizing labor and timber on a considerable scale to construct a flotilla of galleys and smaller vessels within a few months.

The second campaign, in 1696, applied this new naval capacity. The Russian flotilla blockaded Azov from the sea while land forces renewed the siege, cutting off the Ottoman garrison's resupply and forcing its surrender after a shorter siege than in 1695. The capture of Azov was a genuine, if strategically modest, achievement: it gave Muscovy a foothold on the Sea of Azov and a claim to Black Sea access, though the fortress and the surrounding gains would later be returned to the Ottomans as part of the settlement following the Russo-Ottoman war of 1710–1713, discussed in Section 31.9.

The lasting significance of the Azov campaigns lies less in the fortress itself than in what the experience taught Peter about the relationship between naval power, logistics, and strategic objectives. A land army alone, however large, could not achieve decisive results against a fortified position with water access unless it could also control the water. This lesson, learned against a secondary Ottoman objective in the south, would inform Peter's approach to the much larger and more consequential Baltic struggle that followed, and it helps explain why, even as war with Sweden loomed, Peter continued to invest heavily in naval construction throughout the 1690s and beyond.

Section 31.4

The Grand Embassy

In 1697, Peter dispatched a large diplomatic mission—the Grand Embassy—to several western European states, traveling incognito for part of the journey under the name Peter Mikhailov, though his identity was widely known and often an open secret among the courts he visited. The Embassy's official diplomatic purpose was to strengthen and expand the anti-Ottoman Holy League, seeking new allies for continued war against the Ottoman Empire following the Azov campaigns. In this narrow diplomatic aim, the mission achieved little; European powers, increasingly preoccupied with the looming War of the Spanish Succession over the Habsburg succession, showed limited interest in a renewed anti-Ottoman crusade.

The Embassy's lasting significance lay instead in its technical and recruiting dimension. Peter and members of his entourage spent extended periods working directly in Dutch and English shipyards, studying shipbuilding techniques, navigation, artillery production, and administrative and military organization. Peter recruited substantial numbers of foreign specialists—shipwrights, artillery officers, engineers, and administrators—for service in Russia, and he purchased equipment, books, and instruments to bring home. This should not be mistaken for Russia's first contact with European technical knowledge; as Section 31.1 emphasized, Muscovy had been recruiting foreign officers and specialists for decades before 1697. What the Grand Embassy accomplished was an intensification and acceleration of this existing pattern, conducted at an unusually high level, with the ruler himself participating directly in technical work rather than merely commissioning it from a distance.

The Embassy was cut short in 1698 by news of a major Streltsy revolt in Moscow, and Peter returned to oversee its suppression, described in Section 31.2. Although the rebellion had already been defeated by loyal forces before his arrival, Peter's subsequent investigation and reprisals were extensive and brutal, reflecting both genuine concern about the political threat the Streltsy represented and a broader determination to reorganize Russia's military establishment on new foundations. The years immediately following the Grand Embassy saw the coalition-building that would produce the Northern Alliance and, within two years, the outbreak of the Great Northern War—a conflict for which the technical knowledge, foreign recruits, and shipbuilding expertise gathered during the Embassy would prove directly useful, even though the Embassy itself had been organized around a different strategic objective.

Section 31.5

The Opening of the Great Northern War

By the late 1690s, Swedish dominance of the Baltic had become a source of grievance for several neighboring states. Sweden, since its victories in the wars of the mid-seventeenth century, controlled Livonia, Estonia, Ingria (the territory at the eastern end of the Gulf of Finland, including the mouth of the Neva River), and substantial territories in northern Germany, giving it command of major trade routes, river mouths, and ports along both shores of the Baltic. Denmark-Norway resented Swedish territorial gains at its expense in earlier conflicts and Swedish support for the neighboring Duchy of Holstein-Gottorp. Augustus II, Elector of Saxony and, since 1697, elected King of Poland-Lithuania, sought Livonia as a prize that would strengthen his position within the fractious Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth. Peter sought secure Baltic access for Russia, both for direct commercial reasons—ports offering far more direct access to European trade than distant Arkhangelsk—and as a matter of strategic position, fortification, and prestige among European powers.

These converging interests produced the Northern Alliance of 1699, a set of agreements linking Russia, Denmark-Norway, and Augustus II in his capacity as Elector of Saxony against Sweden. Although Augustus also held the Polish-Lithuanian crown, the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth itself was not initially a formal party to the alliance and did not enter the war on Russia's side until 1704, when a pro-Swedish faction within the Commonwealth had already forced Augustus's temporary replacement as king; this distinction between Augustus's personal Saxon commitments and the Commonwealth's own, more divided and delayed involvement mattered a great deal for how the war's early campaigns unfolded. The allies calculated that Sweden's throne, recently passed to the eighteen-year-old Charles XII in 1697, offered a moment of vulnerability; a young, untested king seemed to present an opportunity that a more experienced ruler might not. This calculation proved badly mistaken. Charles XII, whatever his youth, displayed exceptional energy, tactical skill, and personal courage as a battlefield commander, and Sweden's professional, well-drilled army and substantial fleet remained formidable regardless of who occupied the throne.

The war opened in early 1700 with a rapid Danish-Norwegian attack on Holstein-Gottorp, Sweden's ally, but Charles XII responded with startling speed, landing forces near Copenhagen with the support of an Anglo-Dutch fleet and forcing Denmark-Norway out of the war within weeks through the Treaty of Travendal. With Denmark-Norway neutralized, Charles turned his attention toward the Baltic provinces, where Augustus II's Saxon-Polish forces had already begun besieging the Swedish-held port of Riga, and where Peter's own forces, only weeks after formally declaring war on Sweden in August 1700, had begun besieging the fortress town of Narva in Estonia. It was against this Russian siege that Charles XII directed his next, decisive intervention, discussed in Section 31.6.

Russia and the World

The Baltic Power System

The Great Northern War should be understood as part of a wider European system of competing dynastic and commercial states rather than as a purely Russian story. Sweden's Baltic empire, assembled across the seventeenth century, rested on control of key river mouths and ports that connected the agricultural and mineral wealth of the eastern Baltic hinterland—grain from Livonia and Estonia, iron and timber from Sweden itself—to markets in western Europe, particularly the Dutch Republic and England, both of which depended on Baltic grain, timber, and naval stores (tar, hemp, and masts essential for shipbuilding).

This commercial dimension explains why Western European powers, especially the Dutch Republic and England, watched the war closely and periodically intervened diplomatically, even while remaining largely preoccupied after 1701 with the War of the Spanish Succession. Access to Baltic naval stores mattered directly to their own naval power, giving them a stake in who controlled the region's ports without drawing them into direct, sustained military involvement in the Baltic conflict itself. Russia's emergence as a Baltic power after 1721 would gradually change these commercial relationships, integrating St. Petersburg into the same trading networks that had previously run chiefly through Swedish-controlled ports such as Riga and Reval (Tallinn).

Section 31.6

Narva and Military Crisis

Peter's siege of Narva began in October 1700 with a Russian force of roughly thirty-five to forty thousand men, a substantial artillery train, and high expectations following the relatively favorable outcome of the Azov campaigns four years earlier. The siege, however, proceeded slowly and badly: autumn rains turned roads to mud, supply lines from the Russian interior proved inadequate to sustain such a large force through a prolonged siege, and much of the Russian artillery, cast in haste, proved to be of poor quality, with a significant number of guns malfunctioning or bursting during use.

Charles XII, having neutralized Denmark-Norway within weeks, moved his army by sea to relieve Narva with remarkable speed, landing near the town in November with a force considerably smaller than the besieging Russian army—modern estimates generally place Swedish numbers at somewhere between eight and twelve thousand men against a Russian force several times larger. Despite this numerical disadvantage, the Swedish attack on 19 November O.S. (30 November N.S.) 1700, launched under cover of a sudden snowstorm that reduced visibility and disoriented Russian defenders, broke through the extended and thinly held Russian siege lines at several points. The Russian army, strung out along fortified lines many kilometers in length and commanded in its critical early moments by a corps of foreign officers whose authority Russian troops did not always fully accept, collapsed into disorganized retreat. A bridge across the Narva River gave way under the weight of fleeing soldiers, drowning many; numerous senior officers, including several of the foreign generals Peter had placed in overall command, were captured or surrendered; and nearly the entire Russian field artillery, along with substantial stores of equipment and supplies, was lost or abandoned.

The scale of the disaster should not be understated, but neither should its long-term consequences be overstated in the opposite direction. Narva exposed specific, identifiable weaknesses: inadequate supply and logistics for a prolonged campaign; artillery and small-arms of uneven and often poor quality; insufficiently trained infantry, particularly among newly raised regiments that had not yet developed cohesion or confidence under fire; and a command structure that mixed foreign professional officers with a Russian officer corps still in the early stages of systematic training, producing friction and uncertain authority at critical moments. These were, in principle, correctable problems rather than evidence of any permanent Russian incapacity for modern warfare.

Equally important to the war's ultimate outcome was a strategic decision made by Charles XII himself. Rather than pursuing the shattered Russian army deeper into Russian territory to press his advantage while Peter's forces remained disorganized, Charles turned south to confront Augustus II's Saxon-Polish forces, judging Poland-Lithuania the more urgent and more prestigious target and apparently regarding Russia as sufficiently humbled to pose no immediate further threat. This decision gave Peter several years of relatively unmolested opportunity to rebuild his army, reorganize his artillery production, and begin constructing a Baltic fleet—the subject of Section 31.7. Historians continue to debate how much weight to assign to Charles's strategic choice versus Russia's own capacity for adaptation in explaining the war's eventual outcome, a question this chapter's Cause and Effect feature examines directly.

Section 31.7

Building a New Army and Navy

The years following Narva saw a sustained, resource-intensive mobilization that touched nearly every part of Russian society and state administration. This section examines that mobilization only insofar as it was necessary to explain Russian military recovery and eventual victory; the fuller administrative, fiscal, and social apparatus that Peter constructed to sustain the war—and that persisted well beyond it—is examined systematically in Chapter 32.

Peter's government responded to the loss of the Narva artillery train with immediate, drastic measures, including the requisitioning and melting down of church bells to cast new cannon, a step that produced significant quantities of new artillery within roughly a year but that also signaled the extraordinary demands the war would place on Russian institutions, including the church. Artillery production was reorganized and expanded at foundries in the Urals and elsewhere, gradually improving both the quantity and the reliability of Russian guns.

Military manpower was raised through an expanded and more systematic recruit levy (rekrutstvo), under which peasant communities and townspeople were required to furnish a set quota of men for lifetime military service, a system that fell overwhelmingly on the peasant and lower urban population and that, over the course of the war, drew hundreds of thousands of men into permanent military service, with substantial losses to combat, disease, and the harsh conditions of campaign life. This recruit levy, formalized and expanded during the war years, became the basic mechanism of Russian military manpower for the remainder of the imperial period, discussed further in Chapter 32.

Officer training and promotion were reorganized to draw more systematically on Russian personnel rather than relying primarily on foreign officers, though foreign specialists continued to serve throughout the war in important technical and command roles. The Preobrazhensky and Semyonovsky Guards regiments, whose origins lay in Peter's youthful play regiments described in Section 31.2, served as elite formations and as a training ground for officers who were subsequently distributed throughout the expanding army.

Naval construction proceeded on two fronts. In the south, shipbuilding at Voronezh, begun during the Azov campaigns, continued intermittently, though the southern fleet's usefulness diminished after the loss of Azov following the 1710–1713 war with the Ottomans, discussed in Section 31.9. In the north, following the capture of the mouth of the Neva River in 1703 and the founding of the fortress city of St. Petersburg on the marshy delta that year—built under harsh conditions that caused significant illness and mortality among the laborers, many of them conscripted peasants and prisoners, employed in its construction, though the precise scale of loss of life remains disputed—Peter established shipyards that grew, over the following decade, into a substantial Baltic fleet, including both galleys suited to the shallow, island-studded waters of the Gulf of Finland and larger sailing warships modeled on Dutch and English designs.

Financing this sustained effort required a substantial expansion of taxation, including new and increased taxes on a wide range of goods and activities, monopolies on certain commodities, and debasement of the coinage, measures that placed severe strain on the population and that anticipated the more systematic fiscal reorganization, including the poll tax, examined in Chapter 32. By 1708, when Charles XII finally turned his attention back toward Russia after years of campaigning against Poland-Lithuania and Saxony, he confronted an army, an artillery establishment, and a fledgling navy substantially different from the force he had routed at Narva eight years earlier.

Historical Vocabulary
Streltsy

Streltsy (literally, "shooters") were semi-professional musketeer units first organized in the sixteenth century to garrison Moscow and other towns and to provide infantry firepower alongside the traditional cavalry service of the landed nobility. By the late seventeenth century, Streltsy units combined military duties with civilian trades and family life in a manner that gave them a stake in urban political and economic conditions, and they had repeatedly shown a willingness to intervene in dynastic politics, most dramatically during the 1682 succession crisis examined in Section 31.2.

The Streltsy matter to this chapter because their political unreliability and, in Peter's assessment, their military inadequacy relative to disciplined, uniformly trained regular infantry, helped motivate the reorganization of the Russian army around new formation regiments and, eventually, the systematic recruit-levy system described in Section 31.7. Following the 1698 revolt suppressed after Peter's return from the Grand Embassy, the Streltsy establishment was drastically reduced, though remnants continued to exist in various garrison roles for some years afterward.

Section 31.8

Poltava

Charles XII's campaigns against Augustus II between 1701 and 1706 were largely successful, culminating in Augustus's forced abdication of the Polish throne in favor of a Swedish-backed candidate and Saxony's withdrawal from the war. With his western flank secured, Charles turned toward Russia in 1707–1708, launching an invasion intended to strike at the heart of Peter's territories, reportedly with Moscow itself as an eventual objective, though the immediate campaign plan and its precise objectives remain a matter of some historical debate.

The invasion did not go as Charles had planned. Russian forces avoided decisive battle while harassing Swedish supply lines and employing a scorched-earth strategy that denied the advancing Swedish army local provisions, a strategy whose costs fell heavily on the peasant population of the affected territories. A Swedish supply column moving from the Baltic provinces to reinforce the main army was intercepted and destroyed at the Battle of Lesnaya in September 1708, depriving Charles of crucial reinforcements, artillery, and supplies at a critical juncture. Facing a harsh winter and dwindling provisions, Charles diverted his army southward into Ukraine, in part drawn by the prospect of support from Ivan Mazepa, hetman of the Left-Bank Hetmanate.

Mazepa's decision to abandon his allegiance to Peter and align with Charles XII in the autumn of 1708 requires careful historical explanation rather than simple moral judgment in either direction. Mazepa had served as hetman since 1687 and had generally cooperated closely with Peter's government, including supplying Cossack forces for Russian campaigns. By 1708, however, Hetmanate autonomy faced growing pressure from wartime demands: Cossack forces were required for campaigns far from Ukraine, Russian garrisons and fortifications were being established within Hetmanate territory, and the burdens of the war fell increasingly heavily on the Hetmanate's population and resources. Mazepa appears to have calculated that a Swedish victory, or at least a negotiated settlement following Swedish battlefield success, offered a better prospect for preserving Hetmanate autonomy than continued subordination to an increasingly demanding wartime Russian state. His alignment with Charles should be understood within this context of contemporary political calculation about autonomy and survival rather than framed, as later national narratives on different sides have sometimes framed it, as either straightforward treason against a single unified Russian nation or as an act of essential Ukrainian national liberation; both framings import later categories that did not correspond to the political vocabulary or self-understanding of 1708.

Mazepa's support, in the event, proved far more limited than either he or Charles had hoped. Most of the Hetmanate's population and a substantial portion of its Cossack officer corps did not follow Mazepa's lead, and Peter's forces moved quickly to secure the Hetmanate, destroying Mazepa's fortified capital at Baturyn in November 1708 with severe loss of life among its garrison and inhabitants, an action intended both to eliminate a potential Swedish base of supply and to deter further defection. A new hetman loyal to Peter, Ivan Skoropadsky, was installed, and the Hetmanate's institutional autonomy was subsequently curtailed further in the years following the war, a process examined in later chapters covering eighteenth-century imperial administration.

Denied the substantial Cossack support and secure winter quarters he had anticipated, and having spent a harsh winter in Ukraine with a shrinking, poorly supplied army, Charles XII besieged the town of Poltava in the spring of 1709. Peter, commanding a relieving force that now substantially outnumbered the depleted Swedish army and that fielded artillery and infantry rebuilt and retrained since 1700, engaged Charles's forces outside Poltava on 27 June (8 July, N.S.) 1709. The battle, following a series of Russian field fortifications (redoubts) that disrupted the initial Swedish advance and inflicted heavy casualties before the main engagement, ended in a decisive Russian victory. The Swedish army was effectively destroyed as a fighting force; a large portion of the surviving Swedes, including senior commanders, surrendered a few days later at Perevolochna on the Dnieper, while Charles XII himself escaped southward into Ottoman territory, where he would remain for several years lobbying the Ottoman court against Russia; Ottoman-Russian tensions, to which this lobbying contributed, culminated in the Ottoman declaration of war on Russia in 1710, examined in Section 31.9.

Poltava's significance lay above all in the destruction of Sweden's principal field army, the force on which Swedish dominance of the Baltic had ultimately rested. It should not, however, be understood as the war's endpoint. Sweden retained substantial naval power, fortified positions in the Baltic provinces and Finland, and the political will to continue fighting for over a decade more, as Section 31.9 explains. The battle's importance was strategic rather than final: it removed, in a single engagement, the field army capable of directly threatening Russian territory and shifted decisive initiative in the war to Peter's forces for its remaining course.

Section 31.9

Victory, Empire, and the Treaty of Nystad

The years following Poltava saw the war's center of gravity shift decisively to the Baltic and to naval operations, even as complications elsewhere required attention. In 1710, the Ottoman Empire, pressed by Charles XII's continued presence and diplomacy from his refuge at Bender, declared war on Russia, leading to a difficult campaign in 1711 along the Pruth River in which Peter's army was nearly encircled; the resulting Treaty of the Pruth required Russia to return Azov to Ottoman control, reversing the gain of the 1696 campaign examined in Section 31.3 and illustrating that Russian military success against Sweden did not translate automatically into strength on every frontier.

In the Baltic theater, Russian forces steadily captured the major fortified ports and provinces that had constituted Sweden's eastern Baltic empire: Riga fell in 1710, along with Reval (Tallinn), Vyborg, and other Livonian, Estonian, and Finnish positions over the following years. The Russian Baltic fleet, built up over the preceding decade as described in Section 31.7, came into its own during this phase, winning a significant naval victory over a Swedish squadron at Gangut (Hanko) in 1714, a battle Peter himself regarded as comparable in significance to Poltava for demonstrating Russia's arrival as a genuine naval power. Russian forces occupied much of Finland by 1714, and subsequent years saw continued naval operations and diplomatic maneuvering among Russia, Sweden, and other interested powers, including Britain, Denmark-Norway, and various German states affected by Sweden's weakening grip on its remaining continental territories.

Charles XII returned to Sweden in 1714 and continued to resist a negotiated peace, pursuing instead renewed campaigns, including an invasion of Norway, until his death from a wound sustained during a siege in Norway in late 1718. His death removed the principal obstacle to a negotiated settlement, and after further negotiation, Russia and Sweden concluded the Treaty of Nystad in August 1721.

Under the treaty's terms, Sweden formally ceded to Russia the territories of Livonia, Estonia, Ingria, and a portion of Karelia including Vyborg, in exchange for a substantial monetary payment from Russia and the return of most of occupied Finland to Swedish control. These territorial gains gave Russia secure, permanent possession of the eastern Baltic coastline, including the ports of Riga and Reval and the mouth of the Neva, where St. Petersburg, founded in 1703, could now develop without immediate military threat as a genuine commercial and administrative center rather than merely a frontier fortress.

In the aftermath of Nystad, the Senate, acting together with the Holy Synod, formally petitioned Peter to accept the titles Father of the Fatherland, Emperor of All Russia, and "the Great," a set of titles that, whatever their ceremonial character, marked a genuine shift in how the Russian state described itself and sought to be regarded internationally. The adoption of the imperial title placed the Russian ruler, in formal diplomatic protocol, on a rank equivalent to the Holy Roman Emperor, a claim several European powers were initially slow to formally recognize but that gradually became accepted over the following decades. The change from tsar to emperor was not merely semantic; it reflected the state's altered self-understanding following two decades of war that had transformed Russia's military capacity, territorial reach, and international standing.

Comparing the situation at Nystad in 1721 to the situation at Narva in 1700 makes the scale of change concrete. In 1700, Russia possessed no secure Baltic coastline, no navy capable of contesting Swedish sea power, an army recently routed in its first major engagement of the war, and an international reputation, following that defeat, as a marginal military power. In 1721, Russia possessed a permanent Baltic coastline stretching from the Gulf of Finland to Riga, a fleet capable of defeating Swedish naval forces, a rebuilt and battle-tested army, a new capital under construction at St. Petersburg, and a ruler recognized, however grudgingly by some courts, as an emperor. This transformation had been purchased at severe cost—in the lives of conscripted soldiers and laborers, in the tax burdens placed on the peasantry and townspeople, and in the curtailed autonomy of regions such as the Hetmanate—costs that Chapter 32 examines as part of its fuller assessment of the broader Petrine transformation of Russian government, economy, and society.

Primary Source Window

The Treaty of Nystad (1721)

Creator: Negotiated by Russian and Swedish plenipotentiaries · Date: 30 August (10 September, N.S.) 1721 · Type: Diplomatic treaty

Context: The treaty concluded twenty-one years of warfare between Russia and Sweden. It was negotiated at Nystad (Uusikaupunki), a town on Finland's western coast, following the death of Charles XII in 1718 removed the principal obstacle to a settlement and after several years of continued Russian naval pressure on Sweden's remaining Baltic and Finnish positions.

Paraphrase of key provisions (not a verbatim translated quotation):

The treaty establishes a lasting, sincere, and inviolable peace between the two crowns. Sweden cedes to Russia, in full and perpetual possession, the provinces of Livonia, Estonia, and Ingria, together with a portion of Karelia and the town and district of Vyborg. Russia, in turn, undertakes to pay Sweden a substantial sum in compensation and returns to Swedish control the greater part of Finland that Russian forces had occupied during the war. Free trade is to be restored between the two states, and subjects of each are guaranteed certain religious and property protections in the ceded territories.

Analysis: The treaty's territorial provisions should be read alongside its commercial and religious clauses, which reveal that both sides anticipated an ongoing, not merely a concluded, relationship: restored trade mattered to a Sweden whose economy depended substantially on Baltic commerce, and guarantees for the German-Baltic nobility and Lutheran church institutions in the ceded provinces reflect Peter's calculation that stable administration of newly acquired territories required accommodating existing local elites rather than displacing them outright. The treaty's brevity relative to the war's duration and cost is itself notable: two decades of mobilization, the destruction of Sweden's field army at Poltava, and a Russian naval victory at Gangut are compressed into a relatively small number of formal clauses concerning territory, payment, and trade.

Questions for students:

  1. Why might Russia have agreed to pay Sweden a monetary sum despite having won the war?
  2. What do the treaty's religious and property guarantees for the ceded provinces suggest about Peter's approach to governing newly acquired, non-Russian, non-Orthodox territories?
  3. Why does the treaty restore most of occupied Finland to Sweden rather than Russia retaining all territory its forces controlled at the war's end?
  4. How does the treaty's language of "lasting, sincere, and inviolable peace" compare with the war's actual twenty-one-year duration and its several years of intermittent, inconclusive negotiation?

Historical Debate

Did Peter Transform Russia, or Accelerate Changes Already Under Way?

The question: Was Peter's wartime military transformation a genuine break with Russia's past, or an intensification, under the extraordinary pressure of prolonged war, of processes already visible in seventeenth-century Muscovy?

The transformation interpretation: Some historians emphasize the scale and speed of change after 1700: a permanent standing army raised through systematic conscription, a genuine Baltic navy built essentially from nothing, a new capital established in the marshy, recently conquered Neva delta, and a ruler who assumed a new imperial title symbolizing Russia's altered international position. On this view, the sheer intensity of wartime mobilization—unmatched by anything in the seventeenth century—marks a genuine discontinuity.
The acceleration interpretation: Other historians point to Chapter 26's evidence of seventeenth-century new formation regiments, foreign officer recruitment, expanding bureaucracy, and growing European contact, arguing that Peter's wartime program extended and intensified existing trends rather than introducing them. On this view, what changed after 1700 was less the direction of development than its pace and scale, driven by the specific pressure of a prolonged, existential war against a first-rank power.

Evidence: Historians weigh comparative evidence on army organization, officer recruitment, and foreign specialist employment before and after 1700; the scale of new taxation and conscription relative to seventeenth-century precedents; and the degree to which institutions created during the war, such as the recruit levy, persisted afterward in modified form.

Points of agreement: Most historians agree that seventeenth-century Muscovy was neither static nor isolated, and that Peter's wartime program built upon existing foundations rather than starting from nothing.

Points of disagreement: Historians differ over how much weight to give the sheer scale and pace of wartime change—whether a sufficiently large quantitative change (a permanent standing army several times larger than before, a navy where none existed, a new capital) amounts to a qualitative transformation.

Student task: Using evidence from Sections 31.1 and 31.7, identify one specific military development that represents clear continuity with seventeenth-century Muscovy and one that represents a genuine departure, explaining your reasoning for each classification.

Multiple Perspectives

Living Through the War

A peasant household subject to the recruit levy: For a peasant community required to furnish recruits for lifetime military service, the war meant the permanent loss of able-bodied men, increased tax burdens to fund the army and navy, and, in regions near St. Petersburg or major fortress-building projects, potential conscription into grueling and often deadly construction labor.
A Cossack officer within the Hetmanate: For a member of the Cossack officer elite, the war years after 1700 brought escalating demands for military service far from Ukraine, growing Russian garrison presence within Hetmanate territory, and, following Mazepa's 1708 alignment with Sweden, a difficult choice between loyalty to a hetman whose cause soon collapsed and accommodation with a Russian state determined to curtail Hetmanate autonomy afterward.
A foreign officer or technical specialist in Russian service: For a Dutch, Scottish, or German officer recruited during or after the Grand Embassy, Russian service offered rapid advancement and significant responsibility, particularly as the army expanded after Narva, but also required navigating friction with an increasingly capable and numerous Russian-born officer corps and adapting to command conditions—climate, supply, distance—very different from western European warfare.

These perspectives illustrate that the Great Northern War's costs and opportunities were distributed very unevenly across Russian and Hetmanate society, even as the war's outcome is often narrated primarily through the decisions of Peter and Charles XII.

Cause and Effect

From Narva to Poltava

Long-Term Conditions

Seventeenth-century Muscovite military and administrative development (Chapter 26); Sweden's established dominance of the Baltic and formidable professional army.

Intermediate Developments

The Azov campaigns' lesson about naval power; the Grand Embassy's technical recruitment; formation of the Northern Alliance; post-Narva reconstruction of artillery, army, and navy across 1701–1708.

Short-Term Triggers

Charles XII's strategic decision to turn against Poland-Lithuania and Saxony rather than pursue Russia after Narva; the destruction of Swedish supply columns at Lesnaya in 1708; Mazepa's failed bid for broader Hetmanate support.

Effects

A depleted, poorly supplied Swedish army facing a rebuilt, numerically superior Russian force at Poltava in 1709; destruction of Sweden's field army; a decisive but not final shift in strategic initiative toward Russia.

Historical complexity: No single factor—not Russian reform alone, not Charles XII's strategic choices alone, not Mazepa's failed defection alone—fully explains the outcome at Poltava; each factor shaped the others' consequences.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Muscovite to Imperial Army

What Stayed the Same

  • Reliance on foreign officers and technical specialists in key roles continued from the seventeenth century through the war years and beyond.
  • The basic reliance on peasant and lower-class manpower for the bulk of military service continued, though the specific mechanism (recruit levy) was new.
  • The landed service elite's obligation to serve the state, established in earlier centuries and examined in Chapter 22, persisted as the framework for officer recruitment even as its scale expanded.

What Changed

  • A large, permanent standing army raised through systematic lifetime conscription replaced the seventeenth-century mixture of new formation regiments, traditional cavalry service, and semi-professional Streltsy garrisons.
  • A substantial Baltic navy was created essentially from nothing, giving Russia sustained naval capacity it had previously lacked.
  • Domestic artillery and small-arms production expanded considerably in scale and reliability, reducing dependence on imported equipment.
  • Russia's international position shifted from a regional land power with limited sea access to a recognized Baltic and, with the assumption of the imperial title, self-declared great power.

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both continuity and change equips students to evaluate the Historical Debate feature above without collapsing into either extreme position.

Everyday Life and Social History

A Conscripted Recruit

For the peasant men swept up in the recruit levy after 1705, entry into the army meant, in practice, permanent separation from family, village, and agricultural life; conscription was typically for life, and desertion was punished severely. Communities selected recruits through a mixture of local decision-making and lot-drawing, and the departure of a conscripted man was often marked by rituals resembling mourning, since the likelihood of his return was low, whether from combat, disease, or the harsh conditions of garrison and campaign life.

New recruits underwent basic training intended to instill the coordinated volley fire, formation drill, and discipline that distinguished the new regular regiments from the older, more loosely organized forces they replaced. Life in the ranks involved long marches, frequently inadequate supply, exposure to disease in crowded camps and garrisons, and, for those assigned to construction projects such as the building of St. Petersburg or Baltic fortifications, brutal physical labor under harsh conditions that caused significant illness and mortality, although the total death toll remains uncertain and traditional very large figures should be treated with caution. The recruit levy's human cost, borne overwhelmingly by the peasantry, represented one of the heaviest burdens of the entire Petrine military transformation, a burden this chapter's Multiple Perspectives feature situates alongside the very different wartime experiences of Cossack officers and foreign specialists.

Regional Context

The Hetmanate at War

From the perspective of the Left-Bank Hetmanate, examined as a distinct political community in Chapter 30, the Great Northern War looked very different than it did from Moscow or St. Petersburg. The Hetmanate had entered Muscovite suzerainty in the mid-seventeenth century on terms that preserved substantial internal autonomy, including its own administrative, judicial, and military institutions under the hetman's authority. The demands of a prolonged Baltic war, fought far from Ukrainian territory, strained this arrangement: Cossack regiments were required for campaigns and construction projects distant from their home territory, and the stationing of Russian garrisons within the Hetmanate signaled a tightening, rather than a loosening, of central control.

Mazepa's 1708 alignment with Charles XII, examined in Section 31.8, should be read against this regional background of eroding autonomy rather than as an isolated personal decision. Its failure, and the harsh Russian response at Baturyn, accelerated exactly the process Mazepa had apparently hoped to forestall: over the following decades, examined in later chapters, the Hetmanate's institutional autonomy was progressively curtailed, a long process that culminated in its formal abolition later in the eighteenth century.

Historical Vocabulary
Hetmanate

Hetmanate refers to the autonomous Cossack polity established on the Left Bank of the Dnieper River following the 1648 Khmelnytsky uprising and the subsequent 1654 Pereiaslav Agreement examined in Chapter 30, under which the Hetmanate accepted Muscovite suzerainty while retaining substantial internal self-government under an elected hetman. The term should not be treated as synonymous with a modern nation-state; it designated a specific military-administrative structure combining Cossack self-governance with a tributary relationship to Muscovy, later Russia.

The Hetmanate matters to this chapter because its resources, manpower, and internal politics became directly entangled in the Great Northern War, most dramatically through Ivan Mazepa's 1708 defection, examined in Section 31.8, and because the war years marked an important stage in the longer process by which Hetmanate autonomy was progressively curtailed by the Russian state, a process this chapter's Regional Context feature situates within the war's broader demands.

Chapter Summary

This chapter examined Peter I's rise to sole rule and the twenty-one-year Great Northern War through which Russia became a major Baltic and European military power. It began by establishing that Muscovy on the eve of Peter's personal rule was neither isolated nor institutionally static, having already absorbed decades of seventeenth-century military and administrative development, examined in Chapter 26, even as significant weaknesses remained, particularly the absence of any substantial navy and limited artillery and small-arms production.

The chapter traced Peter's turbulent childhood, including the 1682 Streltsy uprising and Sophia's regency, as the political environment from which his later rule emerged, while cautioning against treating any single childhood episode as a complete explanation for his subsequent conduct. It examined the Azov campaigns of 1695–1696 as a formative experience that taught Peter the strategic necessity of naval power, and the Grand Embassy of 1697–1698 as an intensification, rather than a wholly new beginning, of Muscovy's existing engagement with European technical and military knowledge.

The chapter then turned to the Great Northern War itself, explaining its origins in Baltic power politics among Russia, Sweden, Denmark-Norway, and Augustus II as Elector of Saxony, and examining the catastrophic Russian defeat at Narva in 1700 as a crisis that exposed specific, correctable military weaknesses rather than any permanent incapacity. It traced the sustained, costly process of military reconstruction across the following years—new artillery production, an expanded recruit levy, officer reorganization, and the creation of a Baltic fleet—while noting that the fuller administrative and fiscal apparatus behind this mobilization receives full treatment in Chapter 32.

The chapter examined Charles XII's 1708 invasion of Russia, Ivan Mazepa's alignment with Sweden and its limited support within the Hetmanate, and the decisive Battle of Poltava in 1709, which destroyed Sweden's invading field army without immediately ending the war. It then followed the conflict's long aftermath through continued Baltic campaigns, the Russo-Ottoman war that cost Russia Azov, the naval victory at Gangut, and the eventual Treaty of Nystad in 1721, under which Russia secured permanent possession of Livonia, Estonia, Ingria, and part of Karelia, and Peter assumed the title of emperor.

Throughout, the chapter emphasized that Russia's transformation from the defeated army at Narva to the dominant victor at Nystad resulted from a combination of factors—Russian institutional adaptation, Charles XII's own strategic choices, and contingent events such as the destruction of Swedish supply columns at Lesnaya—rather than any single cause, and that this transformation was purchased at severe human cost, borne overwhelmingly by conscripted peasants, laborers, and the population of the Hetmanate.

These lessons—distinguishing continuity from genuine institutional change, weighing multiple causal factors in a complex military and diplomatic outcome, and resisting anachronistic political framing of contested historical loyalties—prepare students directly for Chapter 32's systematic examination of the government, military, economic, and social institutions of the Petrine state.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Streltsy
Semi-professional musketeer garrison troops central to the 1682 succession crisis and later reorganized out of existence after 1698.
Regency
Government exercised on behalf of a minor or incapacitated ruler; Sophia Alekseyevna's regency governed in the name of Ivan V and Peter from 1682 to 1689.
Nemetskaya Sloboda
Moscow's foreign quarter, home to European merchants and specialists, where the young Peter spent formative time.
Azov campaigns
Peter's 1695–1696 campaigns against the Ottoman fortress of Azov, which demonstrated the strategic necessity of naval power.
Grand Embassy
Peter's 1697–1698 diplomatic and technical mission to western Europe.
Northern Alliance
The 1699 coalition of Russia, Denmark-Norway, and Augustus II as Elector of Saxony against Sweden; the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth itself joined only in 1704.
Great Northern War
The 1700–1721 war between Russia and its allies and Sweden, the chapter's central subject.
Narva
Site of the catastrophic Russian defeat of November 1700.
New formation regiments
Infantry and cavalry units organized along European lines, developed in seventeenth-century Muscovy and expanded under Peter.
Recruit levy (rekrutstvo)
The systematic conscription of peasant and townsman recruits for lifetime military service, formalized during the war.
Baltic fleet
The Russian navy constructed after 1703, based on shipyards near St. Petersburg.
Hetmanate
The autonomous Left-Bank Cossack polity whose internal politics became entangled in the war through Mazepa's 1708 defection.
Poltava
Site of the 1709 battle that destroyed Sweden's invading field army.
Treaty of Nystad
The 1721 treaty concluding the Great Northern War and confirming Russia's Baltic territorial gains.
Livonia
Baltic province ceded to Russia by the Treaty of Nystad.
Ingria
Territory at the eastern end of the Gulf of Finland, including the mouth of the Neva, ceded to Russia in 1721.
Emperor (imperial title)
Title assumed by Peter following Nystad, signaling Russia's changed international standing.
Old Style/New Style dating
The distinction between the Julian calendar used in Russia and the Gregorian calendar used in much of Western Europe during this period; several dates in this chapter are given with both designations where useful.
Field artillery
Mobile cannon used in open battle; its loss at Narva and reconstruction afterward is a recurring theme of this chapter.
Battle of Gangut
1714 naval battle demonstrating the effectiveness of the new Russian Baltic fleet.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has carried students from the defeated Russian army fleeing across a collapsing bridge at Narva in 1700 to the emperor whose state secured a highly favorable Baltic peace settlement at Nystad in 1721, marking one of this textbook's clearest demonstrations of how sustained institutional adaptation under wartime pressure can transform a state's military capacity and international standing within a single generation. Peter's role in this transformation was substantial and often personally direct, but the chapter has resisted reducing the story to his individual genius; the war was won by conscripted soldiers, foreign specialists, Cossack officers who remained aligned with Peter, artisans casting new cannon from church bells, and laborers who died building a new capital on unfavorable ground.

The chapter's central methodological lesson—that a defeat as severe as Narva reflects specific, identifiable weaknesses rather than permanent incapacity, and that recovery depends on a combination of institutional adaptation and contingent strategic choices by adversaries—will recur throughout this textbook's later treatment of Russian and Soviet military history, including the crises examined in Chapters 36, 48, and 59.

As the textbook proceeds to Chapter 32's systematic examination of the Senate, the administrative colleges, the Table of Ranks, taxation, church reform, and the cultural reorientation symbolized by St. Petersburg, students should carry forward this chapter's central question in a new form: not merely how Russia won a war, but what kind of state, government, and society that victory required Peter to build, and at what lasting cost to Russian society.
