Law, Serfdom, and the Social Order

A History of Russia: From the Eurasian World to the Russian Federation

Chapter Opening

A government that can compile, print, and distribute a comprehensive law code has already declared something important about itself: it believes it now possesses the administrative reach to make that code matter.

In January 1649, in the assembly hall of the Kremlin, representatives of the Muscovite service elite, clergy, townspeople, and other groups gathered in a Zemsky Sobor, or Assembly of the Land, to approve a new law code commissioned the previous year by Tsar Alexei Mikhailovich. The resulting text, the Sobornoe Ulozhenie, ran to nearly a thousand articles organized into twenty-five chapters, covering subjects as varied as treason, taxation, land tenure, the organization of the church, criminal procedure, inheritance, and the status of peasants, townspeople, slaves, and servitors. It was printed rather than merely copied by hand, distributed to provincial offices across the realm, and remained the formal foundation of Russian law for roughly two centuries afterward, revised and supplemented many times but never entirely replaced until the nineteenth century. No previous Muscovite law code had approached its scope, its systematic organization, or its administrative reach.

Chapter 26 examined how the first Romanov rulers, confronting the devastation of the Time of Troubles, rebuilt the fiscal system, reconstituted the army, restored central administrative offices, and gradually reasserted the practical authority of the Muscovite state after decades of civil war, foreign intervention, and institutional collapse. That chapter's central argument was that the Romanov state recovered not by returning to an unchanged status quo but by building new administrative capacity, often improvised under pressure, that by the 1640s allowed the government to compile detailed information about population, land, and resources with an effectiveness it had not possessed a generation earlier.

This chapter asks what that recovered and expanded capacity allowed the state to do. A government that can count its taxpayers, muster its servitors, and collect its revenues has also acquired the practical ability to define, in far more specific and enforceable terms than before, who belongs to which social category, what each category owes, and where its members must remain. The Sobornoe Ulozhenie should be understood in exactly this light: not as a bolt from nowhere that instantly created a new society, but as the most comprehensive expression to date of a long process by which Muscovite law had been narrowing the movement, defining the obligations, and hardening the boundaries of major social groups. Restrictions on peasant departure had been accumulating since at least the late sixteenth century, discussed in Chapter 24; the state's fiscal reconstruction after the Troubles, discussed in Chapter 26, depended on knowing precisely who owed what, and to whom. The 1649 code drew these separate threads together into a single, systematic, and far more enforceable legal fabric.

The chapter's central historical question, then, is not simply "what did the law require?" but "why did a recovering state, at this particular moment, find it necessary and possible to regulate social relationships this comprehensively?" The answer touches nearly every group examined in this chapter: the service nobility, whose landholding and status depended on a reliable and locatable peasant labor force; the peasants themselves, whose mobility had narrowed even as many continued to migrate, flee, petition, and negotiate within an increasingly restrictive framework; the townspeople, bound by law to registered urban communities and their tax burdens; the merchants, who occupied a distinctive and partially privileged position within a hierarchical commercial order; the enslaved and other dependent laborers, whose legal status the code addressed separately from that of serfs; and the households in which all these legal categories were, in practice, lived out day by day.

It is equally important to state at the outset what this chapter does not argue. It does not claim that Russian serfdom was invented in a single year, by a single decree, or through a single legislative act. Historians who have studied the long documentary record of peasant mobility restrictions, landlord petitions, and fiscal administration generally describe the consolidation of serfdom as a gradual, cumulative, and regionally uneven process extending across more than a century, of which 1649 marks a major legal milestone rather than an isolated beginning. Nor does this chapter suggest that Muscovite society, once codified, became static or perfectly controlled. Legal categories overlapped in practice, and the enormous frontier examined in Chapter 28 offered possibilities of flight, resettlement, and renegotiation that the printed code could specify but not fully enforce across so vast a territory.

By the chapter's end, students should be able to explain how the Sobornoe Ulozhenie of 1649 related to developments already traced in earlier chapters, describe the legal position and lived experience of service elites, peasants, townspeople, merchants, and dependent laborers under the new code, analyze family and household authority as both a legal and a social institution, and evaluate the tension between the state's growing capacity to classify and regulate its population and the practical limits of that control.

Historical Context

Chapter 26 traced the reconstruction of the Muscovite state following the Time of Troubles: the election of Mikhail Romanov by a Zemsky Sobor in 1613, the slow restoration of central administrative offices (prikazy), the rebuilding of the tax base through renewed land surveys and household counts, the reconstitution of the army with growing reliance on foreign-trained "new formation" regiments, and the gradual strengthening of autocratic authority under Tsar Alexei Mikhailovich after 1645. That chapter emphasized that this recovery was neither automatic nor a simple return to a pre-1598 status quo; it required improvisation, negotiation with the service elite and provincial gentry through repeated Zemsky Sobor assemblies, and the painstaking rebuilding of the documentary and fiscal apparatus needed to govern at all.

That documentary apparatus is essential background for this chapter. A government that can conduct household censuses, compile land registers, and track who owes taxes, military service, or labor obligations to whom has also acquired the practical means to enforce far more specific and comprehensive social regulation than a government lacking such tools. The restrictions on peasant departure examined briefly in Chapter 24 — including the introduction of "forbidden years" during which peasants could not exercise a traditional right to move between landlords around St. George's Day, and subsequent extensions of the period during which a fled peasant could be legally pursued and returned — had accumulated piecemeal across the late sixteenth and early seventeenth centuries, often as emergency or temporary fiscal and military measures rather than as a single coherent program. By the 1630s and 1640s, provincial servitors were petitioning the government repeatedly for a permanent, unlimited right to recover fled peasants, since without a secure and locatable labor force their own capacity to render military service, which depended on estate income, was placed in jeopardy.

The service elite's petitions for firmer peasant attachment must be understood alongside the government's own fiscal interests. Taxation in Muscovy fell heavily on households and communities rather than only on individuals, and the state's ability to collect revenue, muster soldiers, and maintain order depended on knowing where taxable and service-rendering populations actually lived. Peasant flight, whether toward the southern frontier, into Siberia, or onto the lands of wealthier and more tax-privileged monasteries and magnates, threatened both landlord income and state revenue simultaneously. These two pressures, service-elite demand for labor security and state demand for fiscal predictability, reinforced one another and together drove the legal developments this chapter examines.

Alexei Mikhailovich's decision to convene a Zemsky Sobor in 1648 and commission a new law code also followed a moment of acute crisis: the Salt Riot of June 1648, an urban uprising in Moscow provoked by unpopular taxation and elite corruption, in which rioters demanded the punishment of unpopular officials and pressed for legal reform. The disorder of 1648, alongside broader grievances from townspeople and provincial servitors alike, gave the new code an additional urgency: it needed to address, and where possible defuse, accumulated social tension by setting out, in a single authoritative and printed text, the rights, obligations, and legal standing of the realm's major social groups. The resulting Ulozhenie should therefore be read as a response to multiple, overlapping pressures — service-elite demands for labor security, urban unrest over taxation, and the state's own interest in a stable, well-documented, and enforceable social order — rather than as the expression of a single motive.

Section 27.1

The Sobornoe Ulozhenie of 1649

The Sobornoe Ulozhenie, literally the "Conciliar Code" or "Assembly Code," takes its name from its approval by the Zemsky Sobor convened in 1648. Tsar Alexei Mikhailovich appointed a small drafting commission, headed by the boyar Prince Nikita Odoevsky, to compile the new code by gathering and revising earlier Muscovite legislation, including the Sudebniki of 1497 and 1550, subsequent decrees issued by the Boyar Duma and various administrative offices, and, in places, provisions drawn from Byzantine ecclesiastical law and Lithuanian statute. The commission worked quickly, and the resulting text, debated and approved by the assembled Sobor delegates in January 1649, was printed, an unusual step for Muscovite legislation that reflected the government's determination to distribute a single, authoritative, and widely accessible text to provincial administrators across the realm.

The code's scope was extraordinary by earlier Muscovite standards. Its twenty-five chapters addressed, among other subjects, crimes against the tsar and the church, the organization and privileges of the church itself, judicial procedure, the status of pomestie and votchina landholding, taxation and the position of townspeople, the treatment of fled peasants, the legal status of slaves and other dependents, family and inheritance matters, and military service obligations. No previous Muscovite code had attempted anything approaching this range, and the Ulozhenie's systematic character, moving deliberately from crimes against the state and church through property, procedure, and social status, reflected a government confident enough in its administrative reach to attempt comprehensive regulation rather than isolated decrees addressing individual problems as they arose.

It is essential to understand the Ulozhenie primarily as an act of codification rather than of pure invention. Historians examining the code's provisions in detail find that a large majority of its content restates, clarifies, systematizes, or extends existing law and administrative practice rather than introducing entirely novel principles. The provisions concerning fled peasants, examined in detail in Section 27.4, built directly on decades of accumulated decrees regarding "forbidden years" and limited periods for the recovery of fugitives; the provisions concerning townspeople responded to specific, repeatedly voiced petitions from urban communities. What the Ulozhenie contributed was not so much new social policy as new legal comprehensiveness, consistency, and, crucially, enforceability: a printed, widely distributed, single authoritative text that provincial officials, landlords, and litigants could all consult and cite, replacing a fragmented patchwork of individual decrees whose cumulative effect had been difficult even for the government itself to track with precision.

Sobornoe Ulozhenie

Sobornoe Ulozhenie, the "Conciliar Code" or "Assembly Code," is the comprehensive Muscovite law code approved by the Zemsky Sobor and Tsar Alexei Mikhailovich in January 1649. Its twenty-five chapters, drafted by a commission headed by Prince Nikita Odoevsky, drew together and systematized earlier Sudebniki, subsequent decrees, and, in places, Byzantine ecclesiastical and Lithuanian statute material, addressing subjects from treason and church organization to landholding, taxation, and the legal status of peasants, townspeople, and dependent laborers.

The Ulozhenie mattered historically not because it introduced an unprecedented body of social policy but because it was printed, comprehensive, and durable: a single, widely distributed, authoritative text that provincial officials and litigants could consult directly, and that remained the formal foundation of Russian law for roughly two centuries. Its provisions on fugitive peasants relied explicitly on existing administrative records, especially the household census books compiled in 1645/46–1646/47, to determine to whom a given peasant belonged — a reminder that the code's practical force depended on the documentary and administrative capacity described in Chapter 26 as much as on the text of the law itself.

This distinction between codification and creation matters for how students should interpret every subsequent section of this chapter. When Section 27.4 explains that the Ulozhenie removed the previous time limits on recovering fled peasants, it is describing the final, decisive step in a legal process that had been building for roughly sixty years, not the sudden invention of unfree peasant status. When Section 27.5 explains that townspeople were bound to their registered urban communities, it is describing the extension, to a different social group, of a broader seventeenth-century pattern of legally fixing populations in place for purposes of taxation and administrative predictability. The Ulozhenie's true historical significance lies in its comprehensiveness and its durability — it remained the formal foundation of Russian law for roughly two centuries, revised, supplemented, and eventually superseded only in the nineteenth century by later law codes examined in later chapters of this textbook — rather than in any single novel provision.

Section 27.2

The Service Elite

At the top of the Muscovite social hierarchy stood a service elite whose status, income, and obligations were bound together by a single reciprocal logic: land in exchange for service. The highest tier consisted of boyars and other court dignitaries, many drawn from ancient aristocratic families who sat in the Boyar Duma and held the most senior military and administrative posts. Beneath them stood the broader service nobility, commonly described in Muscovite sources as dvoriane and deti boiarskie, provincial landholders who formed the backbone of the cavalry army and staffed local administrative posts. This chapter's focus is less on the highest court elite, examined in Chapter 26 in connection with central government, than on the much larger stratum of service landholders whose economic security depended directly on the peasant labor examined in Sections 27.3 and 27.4.

Two forms of landholding structured this elite's material position. The pomestie, a conditional grant of land traditionally tied to continued military or administrative service, had been the dominant form since the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries and could, in principle, be withdrawn if a servitor failed to perform the obligations attached to it. The votchina, by contrast, was a hereditary patrimonial estate carrying substantially stronger inheritance and alienation rights than a pomestie, since a votchina could be sold, mortgaged, or bequeathed with far fewer restrictions. This inheritance distinction should not, however, be mistaken for a categorical exemption from service: land tenure and personal service obligations overlapped in complex ways across the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, and in practice many votchina holders also rendered military or administrative service as a matter of course, even though their landholding itself was not formally conditioned on it. The Sobornoe Ulozhenie treated property rights and service obligations as related but analytically separate questions, and it also permitted certain hereditary estates to be held by women, by merchants, and by other persons outside the service ranks, a further sign that votchina tenure was not simply a service category under another name. Across the seventeenth century, the practical distinction between pomestie and votchina in terms of the labor and revenue they produced gradually narrowed, as pomestie holdings became increasingly heritable in practice even where they remained formally conditional, a trend that continued into the following century.

Whatever the precise legal form of a servitor's landholding, its economic value depended almost entirely on the peasant households settled on it. A pomestie or votchina without peasants to work the land, pay rents, or render labor obligations was, in practical terms, worthless to a servitor who needed reliable income to equip himself, his retainers, and his horses for campaign, since Muscovite military service required servitors to arm and provision themselves largely from their own estate revenues. This dependence explains why provincial servitors, rather than the highest court boyars, were often the most insistent petitioners for stronger legal protection against peasant flight: a modest provincial landholder whose peasants fled to a wealthier magnate's estate, a monastery, or the southern frontier could lose the very foundation of his capacity to serve, threatening both his personal status and the state's military readiness.

The service elite's relationship to the state was therefore genuinely reciprocal rather than one of simple subordination. The state needed servitors capable of self-equipped military service and reliable local administration; servitors needed secure landholding and, above all, a stable and locatable peasant labor force to make that landholding productive. The legal consolidation of peasant attachment examined in Section 27.4 should be understood as the state's response to this structural dependency: by tying peasants ever more firmly to specific landholdings, the government secured the economic foundation of the service class on which its own military and administrative capacity rested. This reciprocal bargain, land and labor security in exchange for service, forms the deep structural logic connecting the state-building of Chapter 26 to the social consolidation examined throughout this chapter.

Section 27.3

Peasants and Landlords

The great majority of the Muscovite population in the seventeenth century lived as peasants, working the land within village communities organized around households, extended kin groups, and communal arrangements for land use, tax assessment, and mutual obligation. Peasants fell into several broad legal categories: peasants settled on service or hereditary landlord estates (vladel'cheskie krest'iane), peasants settled on church and monastery lands, and so-called state or "black-plowing" peasants (chernososhnye krest'iane) who paid dues directly to the treasury rather than to a private landlord, concentrated especially in the northern regions and Siberia where landlord estates were less developed. This chapter concentrates primarily on peasants living under private landlord authority, since it was their legal status that the Ulozhenie most decisively transformed, while recognizing that a substantial minority of the peasantry remained outside direct landlord control.

Peasant obligations to landlords took two overlapping forms across the period examined in this textbook: barshchina, labor service performed directly on the landlord's own demesne land, and obrok, dues paid in kind or, increasingly across the century, in cash, calculated against a household's land allotment and labor capacity. The specific balance between labor service and cash or kind dues varied considerably by region, soil quality, market access, and individual landlord practice; estates near growing urban markets or navigable trade routes often favored cash obrok, since peasants could more readily convert agricultural surplus or craft production into money, while estates in less commercially developed areas more often relied on direct labor service. Village communities, organized through communal assemblies, played a significant role in allocating land among households, apportioning tax and service burdens, and mediating disputes, even as ultimate authority over the community rested with the landlord or his steward.

Peasant households were not passive recipients of landlord and state demands; they were historical actors who negotiated, petitioned, migrated, and, when conditions became sufficiently intolerable, fled. Petitions (chelobitnye) addressed to landlords, local administrators, or even the tsar himself survive in considerable numbers from this period, documenting peasant complaints about excessive exactions, disputes over land boundaries, and requests for relief following crop failure or other hardship. Migration, whether authorized or not, remained a persistent feature of peasant life throughout the seventeenth century: peasants moved toward the fertile but dangerous southern frontier, into the vast spaces of Siberia examined in Chapter 28, onto the estates of wealthier and more tax-privileged monasteries capable of offering more favorable terms to attract labor, or simply from one village to another within a landlord's own holdings.

This background of active peasant mobility and negotiation is essential for understanding why landlords and the state increasingly sought firmer legal restrictions across the late sixteenth and early seventeenth centuries. A peasant population capable of moving toward better terms, whether offered by a rival landlord, a tax-exempt monastery, or the relative freedom of the frontier, exercised real, if constrained, economic leverage, and a landlord whose peasants departed en masse could find his estate's productive capacity, and therefore his own ability to render service, severely undermined. The cumulative legal restrictions examined in the following section developed directly out of this recurring tension between peasant mobility and landlord dependence on a stable labor force, a tension the fiscal and military pressures traced in Chapter 26 only intensified.

Section 27.4

The Consolidation of Serfdom

The legal process that culminated in the Sobornoe Ulozhenie's provisions on peasant attachment did not begin in 1649; it extended across more than half a century of accumulated decrees, petitions, and administrative practice. Under the Sudebniki of 1497 and 1550, peasants possessed a recognized, if narrowly limited, legal right to change landlords during a short period surrounding St. George's Day (late November, by the Julian calendar then in use), provided they settled outstanding debts and paid an exit fee. This arrangement, while hardly a guarantee of broad peasant freedom, at least established a defined legal window for lawful departure, distinguishing authorized movement from unauthorized flight.

Beginning in the 1580s and 1590s, amid the fiscal exhaustion and demographic disruption produced by the Livonian War, the oprichnina, and widespread famine, the government began suspending the St. George's Day right in designated "forbidden years" (zapovednye leta), initially framed as temporary emergency measures rather than permanent policy. On 24 November 1597, a separate and more specific decree established a five-year limitation period (urochnye leta) within which a landlord could lawfully pursue and reclaim a peasant who had fled or been abducted; this 1597 rule addressed a distinct legal question from the zapovednye leta, since it concerned not whether a peasant could lawfully depart but how long a former landlord's claim to recover someone who had already left remained valid. That five-year period was repeatedly adjusted, and generally lengthened, across the early seventeenth century in response to continued lobbying from provincial servitors, whose petitions, especially numerous in the 1630s and 1640s, argued that any fixed search period left them unable to recover peasants who had fled to more distant or better-connected estates before the deadline expired.

The Sobornoe Ulozhenie's decisive step was to abolish the 1597 time limit altogether. Under the 1649 code, a landlord's right to search for and reclaim a fled peasant became, in principle, unlimited in time, and peasants and their descendants were declared permanently attached to the estate and proprietor to whom they had been legally assigned according to existing cadastral and census registration, above all the household census books compiled in 1645/46–1646/47, which the Ulozhenie relied upon directly in determining to whom a given fugitive should be returned. This change had several interlocking consequences. It converted what had previously been a temporary emergency suspension of an established peasant right, followed by a fixed and adjustable search period, into a permanent structural feature of Muscovite law resting on a specific, dateable body of registration records. It extended attachment not merely to individual peasants but to their households and descendants, embedding serfdom within the intergenerational structure of the peasant family itself. And it substantially strengthened landlords' practical authority, since the threat of indefinite legal pursuit, now anchored in a fixed census record rather than a moving deadline, made unauthorized flight a far riskier proposition than it had been under the earlier, time-limited search provisions, even though, as Section 27.9 will explain, flight never disappeared entirely.

Students should resist two tempting oversimplifications. The first is to treat 1649 as the single moment "serfdom began," when in fact the legal machinery of restricted mobility had been accumulating for roughly sixty years beforehand, and the social and economic dependency of peasants on landlords, rooted in debt, custom, and unequal power, predated even those restrictions. The second is to treat 1649 as the final or complete legal settlement of peasant status, when in fact later seventeenth- and eighteenth-century legislation, examined in subsequent chapters of this textbook, continued to expand landlord authority over serfs' persons, labor, and even, eventually, aspects of their family life, in ways the 1649 code itself did not yet fully specify. The most defensible historical description is that the Ulozhenie marks the decisive legal consolidation of a long process, converting cumulative, piecemeal, and originally temporary restrictions into a comprehensive, permanent, and heritable system, rather than serving as either the sole origin or the final form of Russian serfdom.

Section 27.5

Townspeople and Urban Obligations

Although rural serfdom has dominated later popular memory of the seventeenth-century social order, the Sobornoe Ulozhenie devoted substantial attention to a legally distinct but comparably significant process: the fixing of townspeople to registered urban tax communities. Muscovite towns contained a posad, the legally defined community of merchants, artisans, and other urban taxpayers obligated to render monetary and in-kind dues to the state and, in many towns, to perform various communal service obligations as well. Posad status carried real burdens, and by the early seventeenth century a persistent problem had emerged: substantial numbers of urban residents had managed to escape posad tax rolls by attaching themselves, often through informal arrangements, to tax-exempt institutions such as monasteries, boyar households, or church properties located within or near town boundaries, whose residents (sometimes called "white-settlement" or beloe mesto inhabitants) did not owe the same obligations as registered posad taxpayers.

This arrangement created a chronic grievance among posad communities, since it reduced the pool of taxpayers among whom a town's fixed obligations had to be divided, effectively increasing the burden on those who remained legally registered. Petitions from posad communities to the government, paralleling the provincial servitor petitions concerning fled peasants, repeatedly urged the abolition of these tax-exempt urban enclaves and the compulsory return of escaped taxpayers to posad rolls. The Salt Riot of 1648, discussed in the Historical Context section above, drew significant energy from exactly this kind of urban grievance over uneven and often corruptly administered taxation.

The Sobornoe Ulozhenie responded directly to these petitions. It abolished the tax-exempt "white settlements" within town boundaries, absorbing their populations into the taxable posad, and it decreed that townspeople who had left registered posad communities, whether recently or in the more distant past, should be forcibly returned and permanently bound to their town of registration, much as peasants were bound to their landholding. Like rural serfdom, this urban attachment was intended to be comprehensive and enduring rather than temporary, reflecting the same underlying state interest examined throughout this chapter: a government capable of compiling detailed population registers wished to use that capacity to fix taxable and service-obligated populations in place, reducing the uncertainty and revenue loss that mobility, whether rural or urban, had long produced.

The comparison between rural and urban attachment illuminates a broader point about the 1649 code as a whole. Peasant serfdom and posad attachment were legally distinct institutions, involving different obligations, different administrative mechanisms, and different relationships to private landlord authority, since townspeople were bound to their community and its tax obligations rather than to an individual landlord's personal authority in the way serfs were bound to a landlord's estate. Yet both institutions reflected the same underlying logic: a state with newly reconstructed administrative capacity used that capacity to convert previously fluid, negotiable, and partially evadable populations into fixed, documented, and permanently obligated legal categories. Reading Sections 27.4 and 27.5 together, rather than treating rural serfdom in isolation, shows that the Ulozhenie's social project extended well beyond the peasantry to encompass a broader pattern of legally fixing Muscovite society in place.

Section 27.6

Merchants and Trade

Within the posad community examined in the previous section, merchants occupied a distinctive and, in certain respects, privileged position, even as they remained subject to the same fundamental logic of fixed obligation and legally defined status that governed the rest of urban society. The wealthiest and most prominent merchants belonged to specially designated corporations, most notably the gosti (a small, elite group of the very wealthiest long-distance and foreign-trade merchants, granted particular privileges including exemption from certain local taxes and the right to travel abroad in pursuit of commerce), along with the somewhat larger gostinaia sotnia and sukonnaia sotnia corporations, whose members enjoyed intermediate status and privileges below the gosti but above the ordinary posad population. These graduated commercial corporations reflected the broader Muscovite tendency, evident throughout this chapter, to assign subjects to legally defined, hierarchically ranked statuses and corporate categories (chiny) rather than to treat economic activity as a domain separate from formal social status. The more aggregated, comprehensive "estate" categories later associated with imperial Russia, and often labeled sosloviia in that later context, would take fuller shape only in the eighteenth century; in 1649, Muscovite officials were still working with a developing patchwork of specific ranks and registered statuses rather than a single settled estate system.

Muscovite merchants engaged in a wide range of commercial activity: domestic trade linking agricultural regions, towns, and the growing market for grain, furs, salt, and manufactured goods; long-distance trade along the Volga and other river systems examined in earlier chapters; and foreign trade, increasingly significant across the seventeenth century, conducted with English, Dutch, and other Western European merchants operating through Arkhangelsk on the White Sea, as well as with merchants trading across the southern and eastern frontiers toward Persia, the Caucasus, and Central Asia. Foreign merchants operating within Muscovy occupied their own distinct legal position, at times resented by domestic merchants who petitioned, with some success, for restrictions on foreign trading privileges within the realm's interior, reflecting a persistent tension between the state's interest in the revenue foreign trade generated and domestic merchants' interest in protecting their own commercial position.

It is important for students to recognize that commercial wealth did not, by itself, translate into the same social and political standing enjoyed by the service elite examined in Section 27.2. Even the wealthiest gosti merchants, however substantial their fortunes, remained legally and socially distinct from, and generally subordinate in status to, the landholding service nobility, whose claim to honor and precedence rested on military service and, increasingly, on genealogical standing rather than on commercial success. Merchants could not, as a rule, hold pomestie land on the same terms as servitors, and their political voice, while real, particularly through participation in Zemsky Sobor assemblies and through direct petitioning, operated within a hierarchy that consistently privileged service and birth over accumulated wealth. This should caution students against assuming that a commercially dynamic urban sector automatically produced a politically ascendant merchant class comparable to developments in some contemporary Western European cities; Muscovite social hierarchy remained organized primarily around service, legal rank, and registered status rather than around capital.

Section 27.7

Slavery and Dependent Labor

The Sobornoe Ulozhenie addressed slavery and related forms of bonded dependency, collectively covered by the Muscovite legal category of kholopstvo, in provisions distinct from those governing peasant serfdom, and students should take care not to collapse these categories into a single undifferentiated concept of "unfreedom." Kholopy, commonly translated as slaves or bondsmen, encompassed several legally distinct subtypes with different origins and different degrees and durations of bondage. "Full" or hereditary slaves (polnye kholopy) had entered bondage, or been born into it, under terms that in principle bound them and their descendants permanently to a master's household. "Service contract" bondage (kabal'noe kholopstvo), by contrast, arose from debt, but by the seventeenth century its legal structure had already been transformed by a decree of 1597: a bonded person could no longer simply repay the original loan to end the relationship, and service instead normally continued for the lifetime of the creditor or master rather than for a fixed, negotiable term. At the master's death, the bondperson was legally to become free rather than passing automatically to the master's heirs as hereditary property, a rule that distinguished kabal'noe kholopstvo sharply from the "full" hereditary slavery described above, even though economic dependency often led freed people, lacking viable independent support, to enter another bond soon afterward.

Kholopy typically served within elite and even middling households as domestic servants, agricultural laborers on a lord's own demesne land, estate managers, or, in some cases, military retainers accompanying their master on campaign, a role with roots extending back to the armed retinues of earlier Rus' princes examined in earlier chapters. Their legal status differed from that of enserfed peasants in several important respects: kholopy were, in Muscovite legal theory, closer to their master's direct personal property than serfs were to their landlord, lacked the (admittedly minimal) independent household and land-tenure standing that peasant serf families retained, and were not taxed by the state in the same way registered peasant households were, since their master bore responsibility for them. Over the course of the seventeenth century, however, the practical distinction between the most severe forms of kholopstvo and the increasingly restrictive condition of enserfed peasants narrowed considerably, and some historians identify a longer-term trend, continuing into the following century, toward the partial convergence of these originally distinct legal categories, as later legislation increasingly extended kholop-like restrictions on personal liberty to the serf population as a whole.

This convergence should not be overstated for the mid-seventeenth century itself. The Ulozhenie continued to treat kholopstvo and peasant serfdom as separate legal institutions, governed by different chapters of the code, with different rules regarding registration, inheritance, and the conditions under which bondage could be entered or, in principle, ended. Debt bondage in particular retained, at least in legal theory, a contractual character that hereditary peasant serfdom, as reshaped by the 1649 code's abolition of time limits on recovery, increasingly lacked. The central lesson of this section is that seventeenth-century Muscovy contained multiple, overlapping, but legally distinguishable systems of unfreedom, each with its own origins, mechanisms, and trajectory, and that careful historical analysis requires keeping these systems analytically separate even while recognizing the ways in which, over time, some of their practical distinctions diminished.

Section 27.8

Family, Gender, and Household Authority

Beneath the legal categories examined in the preceding sections lay the household (dvor), the basic unit through which Muscovite law, taxation, and social organization actually operated. Households, rather than isolated individuals, were typically the units registered in censuses, assessed for taxation, and held collectively responsible for labor and service obligations, whether among peasants, townspeople, or service landholders. Understanding the household as an economic and legal unit, and not merely a private domestic space, is essential to interpreting how the broader social order examined in this chapter actually functioned in daily life.

Muscovite household authority was strongly patriarchal in both legal theory and prevailing social expectation. The head of household, typically the senior male, held broad authority over the labor, marriage arrangements, and discipline of household dependents, including wives, children, and, where applicable, household kholopy. The Domostroi, a mid-sixteenth-century text offering detailed prescriptive guidance on household management, religious observance, and domestic discipline, is frequently cited as evidence of Muscovite family ideals, and it does provide a valuable window into elite conceptions of proper household order, including expectations of wifely obedience, careful management of household resources, and religiously grounded discipline of children and servants. Students should nonetheless treat the Domostroi cautiously as a prescriptive ideal composed for an elite, likely urban and merchant, audience, rather than as a transparent description of how households at every social level actually functioned; peasant households, in particular, operated under practical economic constraints, labor requirements, and communal expectations that a text addressed to a literate urban household could not fully capture.

Women's legal position in seventeenth-century Muscovy combined significant formal subordination with meaningful, if constrained, practical agency. Women generally could not hold pomestie land in their own right on the same terms as men, and marriage arrangements, especially among elite and service families, were typically negotiated by parents or guardians with an eye to property, status, and alliance rather than by the individuals concerned. At the same time, women, particularly widows, could and did exercise real authority over household property and dependents in the absence of an adult male head of household, and evidence from court records, petitions, and property transactions indicates that women engaged directly with legal and administrative institutions, filing petitions, defending property claims, and managing estates, more actively than a purely formal reading of legal subordination might suggest. Peasant and townswomen contributed essential agricultural, craft, and household labor, including textile production, food processing, and, particularly during a husband's absence on service or campaign, direct management of household economic affairs.

Childhood in this period was shaped heavily by household economic need, religious upbringing, and, for elite children, expectations of eventual marriage and estate management or, for boys, service obligation. Formal education remained limited and unevenly available, concentrated mainly among the clergy and portions of the elite, while most peasant and many urban children acquired practical skills through household labor and apprenticeship rather than formal schooling, a pattern this textbook will trace changing only gradually in subsequent centuries. Students should recognize that surviving evidence disproportionately illuminates elite and, to a lesser degree, urban households, and that peasant family life, while shaped by the same broad patriarchal norms, must often be reconstructed more indirectly, through court records, tax documents, and communal petitions, than through prescriptive texts addressed to a literate few.

Historical Vocabulary
Serfdom (later Russian: Krepostnoe Pravo)

Serfdom is the conventional historical term for the evolving system of legal restrictions and landlord-peasant relationships consolidated in seventeenth-century Muscovy. The later Russian expression krepostnoe pravo became standard terminology much later, chiefly in official and scholarly usage in the decades immediately preceding the 1861 emancipation reforms examined in Chapter 42, and should not be mistaken for language used by the Sobornoe Ulozhenie itself. The seventeenth-century code and the decrees that preceded it spoke instead of specific peasant statuses, obligations, and registration requirements rather than naming a single, comprehensive institution by that later term. The expression krepost', a legal deed, bond, or document of attachment, nonetheless captures something genuine about the system this chapter examines: from its consolidation, peasant dependency in Muscovy was fundamentally a documented, registered legal condition rather than merely an informal social custom.

What historians call serfdom differed in important respects from Western European serfdom of the medieval period, from Atlantic chattel slavery, and from the kholopstvo examined in Section 27.7, though it shared certain features with each. Students should avoid treating "serfdom" as a single, universal, or terminologically fixed institution and should instead attend to its specifically Muscovite legal characteristics as they existed in this period: heritable attachment to a registered estate and landlord, household rather than purely individual registration, and a legal basis resting on cumulative state decree and codification rather than on a single originating act or a single settled name.

Section 27.9

Social Mobility and Social Control

Having traced, across Sections 27.1 through 27.8, the ways in which the Sobornoe Ulozhenie and the broader legal developments of the mid-seventeenth century sought to fix Muscovite society into clearly defined, documented, and largely hereditary categories, this section asks a necessary complicating question: how rigid, in practice, were these categories? The honest answer is that the Muscovite state achieved a substantial and historically significant increase in its capacity to classify and regulate society, but that this increased capacity fell well short of complete or uniform control.

Flight remained a persistent feature of Muscovite social life even after 1649. The southern frontier, examined further in Chapters 28 and 30, offered peasants and others fleeing landlord authority the possibility of resettlement among Cossack communities or in newly settled borderland territories where administrative reach remained thin and where a rough customary principle, sometimes summarized in the phrase "there is no extradition from the Don," offered fugitives a degree of practical, if legally unrecognized, protection. Siberia, whose conquest and settlement Chapter 28 examines in detail, similarly absorbed substantial numbers of peasants, some fleeing servitude and others recruited or coerced into frontier service, at a distance from which landlord search and recovery efforts became increasingly impractical. The vast scale of Muscovite territory, combined with the limits of seventeenth-century administrative and communication technology, meant that legal attachment, however comprehensively codified, could never be enforced with anything approaching total effectiveness across the entire realm.

Social control operated through mechanisms considerably broader than formal law enforcement alone. Village communal institutions, responsible for allocating tax and service burdens among households, gave peasant communities themselves a role, however constrained, in managing internal obligations and, at times, in shielding individual households from the full weight of landlord or state demands. Household patriarchal authority, examined in Section 27.8, extended the state's and landlord's reach into daily life through the disciplinary power vested in household heads. The Orthodox Church, whose institutional role Chapter 29 examines in greater depth, reinforced social hierarchy and obedience through religious teaching, while also providing, through monastery lands and ecclesiastical courts, alternative structures of authority that sometimes competed with, rather than simply reinforced, secular landlord control. Petitioning, as noted throughout this chapter, offered peasants, townspeople, and others a recognized, if unequal, channel for contesting specific abuses without directly challenging the legitimacy of the social order itself.

Mobility within the legally defined hierarchy, while constrained, was not entirely absent. Military service, particularly in the newly organized "new formation" regiments examined in Chapter 26, offered some peasants and townspeople limited avenues toward changed status, and successful commercial activity, while it did not translate automatically into elite standing as Section 27.6 explained, could nonetheless improve a family's material position within its existing legal category. Clerical careers offered another partial avenue of mobility for some, particularly literate, individuals. None of these avenues amounted to a general loosening of the hierarchical structure the Ulozhenie codified, and students should not mistake limited, individual-level mobility for evidence that the broader system of legal attachment was weak or ineffective. The most defensible conclusion is that the Muscovite state, by the mid-seventeenth century, had achieved a genuinely new level of capacity to classify, document, and legally bind its population, and that this capacity represented a durable and historically consequential achievement, even though the sheer scale of the realm and the practical limits of premodern administration ensured that classification was never fully equivalent to control.

Primary Source Window

The Sobornoe Ulozhenie of 1649, Chapter XI ("The Trial of Peasants")

Creator: Drafting commission headed by Prince Nikita Odoevsky, approved by the Zemsky Sobor and Tsar Alexei Mikhailovich · Date: January 1649 · Type: Codified state law

Context: Chapter XI of the Ulozhenie, concerning the "trial," or legal handling, of peasants, directly addresses the recovery of fled peasants and the abolition of the previous fixed time limits within which landlords could lawfully search for and reclaim them. The chapter's provisions relied explicitly on existing cadastral and census registration, above all the household census books of 1645/46–1646/47, to identify the estate and proprietor to whom a given peasant had been legally assigned.

Brief paraphrase of the provision (not a verbatim translated quotation):

The chapter directs that peasants who have fled from their registered landholding, together with their wives, children, and possessions, are to be sought out, however long ago the flight occurred and without regard to any previously established time limit, and returned to the landlord on whose land they were last legally registered, together with any children born to them after their flight; and that persons who knowingly harbored or accepted such fled peasants are to be held liable for the dues and losses the rightful landlord suffered during the period of their absence.

Analysis: This provision illustrates precisely the "decisive step" described in Section 27.4: the removal of the fixed-term limits that had structured peasant recovery since the late sixteenth century, converting what had been a time-limited legal remedy into an unlimited and heritable claim. The provision's attention to children born after flight shows the code's intent to bind not merely the individual peasant but the entire peasant household and its descendants to a specific landholding, embedding serfdom within family structure itself. The liability imposed on those who harbored fugitives also reveals the state's interest in discouraging the alternative estates, monasteries, and communities that had previously offered peasants a practical, if unauthorized, escape from landlord authority.

Historical Debate

When Was Serfdom "Created"?

The question: Should historians identify a single decisive moment, such as 1649, at which Russian serfdom was "created," or should they describe serfdom's consolidation as an extended, cumulative process without a single point of origin?

The decree-centered position: Some older historiography, and much popular understanding, has emphasized specific legislative moments, particularly the "forbidden years" of the 1580s–1590s and the Ulozhenie of 1649, as the effective creation of Russian serfdom, arguing that these decrees marked a clear, identifiable transition from a peasantry possessing at least limited legal mobility to one legally bound in place.
The gradualist position: Many historians, drawing on detailed study of estate records, petitions, and local administrative documents, argue that peasant dependency on landlords, rooted in debt, custom, communal obligation, and unequal power, substantially predated the legal restrictions on mobility, and that the legal restrictions themselves accumulated gradually and unevenly across some sixty years before 1649 rather than emerging from a single decisive enactment; on this view, 1649 marks a major consolidation and systematization of a long process rather than a true point of origin.

Evidence: Historians weigh the documentary record of sixteenth- and seventeenth-century decrees on peasant mobility, surviving landlord and peasant petitions, court records concerning fled peasants, and comparative evidence on the earlier social and economic dependency of peasants on landholding elites.

Points of agreement: Nearly all historians agree that the 1649 code represents the decisive legal consolidation of serfdom as a comprehensive, permanent, heritable institution, and that this consolidation built on decades of prior legal development rather than emerging without precedent.

Points of disagreement: Historians differ over how much explanatory weight to assign to specific legislative acts versus longer-term social and economic pressures, and over whether it is more useful to speak of serfdom's "creation" or its "consolidation."

Student task: Using evidence from Sections 27.3 and 27.4, construct a short argument for why "consolidation" is a more historically defensible term than "creation" to describe the Ulozhenie's role in the development of Russian serfdom.

Multiple Perspectives

Boyar, Peasant, Townsman, Household

A provincial service landholder: For a modest dvorianin dependent on pomestie income to equip himself for military service, the Ulozhenie's abolition of time limits on recovering fled peasants addressed a genuine and long-voiced economic anxiety, converting an uncertain and time-pressured legal remedy into a durable guarantee of labor security underpinning his own capacity to serve the tsar.
An enserfed peasant household: For a peasant family newly and permanently bound to a specific landholding, the 1649 code closed off a previously available, if always risky, avenue of escape from an oppressive landlord or unfavorable local conditions, embedding both the household's present obligations and its descendants' future status within a single, heritable legal attachment.
A registered posad taxpayer in a Muscovite town: For a townsman burdened by posad obligations while wealthier neighbors evaded taxation through tax-exempt "white settlements," the Ulozhenie's abolition of those exemptions and its compulsory return of escaped taxpayers addressed a specific, long-standing grievance, even as it simultaneously bound him and his own descendants more firmly to the town's tax rolls.

These perspectives illustrate why the 1649 code cannot be read simply as an instrument of elite oppression imposed uniformly on a passive population; different groups experienced its provisions as addressing genuine, if unequal, grievances, even as the code's cumulative effect was to narrow mobility and harden hierarchy across nearly every level of Muscovite society.

Cause and Effect

The Consolidation of Serfdom

Long-Term Conditions

Peasant economic dependency on landholding elites rooted in debt, custom, and unequal power; the pomestie service-land system's reliance on peasant labor for its economic value.

Intermediate Developments

The accumulation of "forbidden years" and fixed-term recovery periods from the 1580s onward; the demographic and fiscal exhaustion of the Time of Troubles and its aftermath, examined in Chapters 24–26; repeated provincial servitor petitions of the 1630s–1640s.

Short-Term Triggers

The Salt Riot of 1648; the convening of the Zemsky Sobor and the commissioning of a comprehensive new law code under Alexei Mikhailovich.

Effects

Abolition of time limits on peasant recovery; permanent, heritable attachment of peasants to landholdings; comparable fixing of townspeople to posad communities; strengthened landlord authority; continued, though riskier, peasant flight to the frontier.

Historical complexity: No single cause—not landlord pressure alone, not state fiscal need alone, not the 1648 crisis alone—fully explains the Ulozhenie's provisions; each factor reinforced the others across a process extending well beyond a single year.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From Custom to Code

What Stayed the Same

  • The basic reciprocal logic linking service landholding to peasant labor continued unchanged in principle, even as the legal security of that labor tightened considerably.
  • Household-based taxation and communal peasant administration persisted as the practical mechanism through which state and landlord authority reached individual families.
  • Flight to the frontier remained a real, if increasingly risky, option throughout and after this period.

What Changed

  • Peasant recovery shifted from a time-limited legal remedy to an unlimited, permanent, and heritable claim.
  • Urban tax-exempt enclaves were abolished, extending comparable legal fixing to townspeople.
  • Muscovite law moved from a fragmented patchwork of individual decrees to a single, comprehensive, printed, and widely distributed code.

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both continuity and change equips students to see 1649 as a genuine turning point in enforceability and comprehensiveness without mistaking it for the sudden invention of social relationships that had, in large part, existed in less codified form for generations.

Everyday Life and Social History

A Muscovite Household

For most Muscovites, whether peasant, townsperson, or minor servitor, daily life centered on the household economy rather than on the distant legal abstractions of the Ulozhenie itself. A peasant household's year followed the agricultural calendar: plowing, sowing, and harvest organized around a short growing season, supplemented by animal husbandry, textile production (particularly the spinning and weaving of flax and wool), food preservation for the long winter, and, in many regions, seasonal craft or trade activity to supplement agricultural income. Labor was divided along broadly gendered lines, with men typically responsible for plowing, heavier fieldwork, and dealings with landlord officials, and women responsible for dairying, textile production, food processing, and childcare, though the boundaries between these roles were far from absolute and shifted according to household need, particularly during a male household head's absence on service or labor obligation.

Housing for most peasant and many urban households consisted of simple wooden structures, often a single main room heated by a masonry stove that also served for cooking, with limited furnishings and, in colder regions, shared space between family members and, at times, young livestock during the harshest winter months. Diet centered on rye bread, porridge made from various grains, cabbage and other preserved vegetables, and, for many households, only occasional meat or fish, with fasting periods prescribed by the Orthodox liturgical calendar shaping food consumption throughout the year. Evidence for this ordinary material life comes disproportionately from tax records, court documents concerning disputes, and occasional foreign travelers' observations, since peasants themselves left few direct written records, a limitation students should bear in mind when reconstructing everyday experience from the available sources.

Regional Context

The Frontier and the Limits of Control

The social order the Sobornoe Ulozhenie sought to fix in place looked very different depending on distance from Moscow. In the densely settled central districts, landlord estates, registered posad communities, and administrative offices operated in close proximity, giving the government and service elite real practical capacity to enforce the code's provisions. Along the southern steppe frontier and in the still-expanding Siberian territories examined in Chapter 28, by contrast, administrative reach thinned dramatically, populations were more sparse and mobile, and Cossack communities operated under customary arrangements that coexisted uneasily with, rather than simply submitted to, Muscovite law.

This regional variation matters for understanding the Ulozhenie's real historical effect. The code's legal comprehensiveness represented a genuine administrative achievement, but its practical enforceability diminished sharply with distance from the state's densest administrative and military presence. Students should therefore resist imagining seventeenth-century Muscovy as a uniform legal space in which the Ulozhenie's provisions applied with equal force everywhere; the frontier, examined further in Chapters 28 and 30, functioned throughout this period as a genuine, if increasingly risky, alternative to the legally consolidated social order this chapter describes.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the consolidation of Muscovy's legal and social hierarchy during the mid-seventeenth century, centered on the Sobornoe Ulozhenie of 1649. It argued that the reconstructed administrative capacity traced in Chapter 26 gave the Romanov state, for the first time since the Time of Troubles, the practical means to classify, document, and enforceably regulate the obligations and mobility of nearly every major social group within the realm.

The chapter examined the Ulozhenie itself as primarily an act of codification: a comprehensive, printed, and systematically organized law code that drew together, clarified, and extended decades of prior legislation and administrative practice rather than inventing an entirely new social order from nothing. It traced the service elite's structural dependence on secure peasant labor, and explained how this dependence, combined with the state's own fiscal interest in a documented and locatable population, drove the legal developments examined throughout the chapter.

The chapter analyzed the long, cumulative process by which peasant mobility was restricted, from the St. George's Day framework of the 1497 and 1550 Sudebniki, through the "forbidden years" and fixed search periods of the late sixteenth and early seventeenth centuries, to the Ulozhenie's decisive abolition of time limits on the recovery of fled peasants in 1649. It examined the comparable, though legally distinct, process by which townspeople were bound to registered posad communities and tax-exempt urban enclaves were abolished, and it situated merchants within a commercial hierarchy that, however economically significant, remained subordinate in status to the landholding service elite.

The chapter distinguished carefully among slavery and bonded labor (kholopstvo), peasant serfdom (krepostnoe pravo), and other forms of dependency, cautioning against treating these related but legally distinct institutions as interchangeable. It examined the household as the basic economic, legal, and patriarchal unit through which state and landlord authority actually reached individual lives, while cautioning against treating prescriptive texts such as the Domostroi as a straightforward description of universal practice, particularly for peasant families whose experience is documented far less directly than that of the literate elite.

The chapter distinguished merchants from the service elite examined elsewhere in the chapter, showing that commercial wealth, even at the level of the privileged gosti corporation, did not translate automatically into the social and political standing enjoyed by landholding servitors, whose claim to honor rested on service and birth rather than capital. It likewise distinguished slavery and bonded labor (kholopstvo) from peasant serfdom, explaining that "full" hereditary bondage and debt-based kabal'noe kholopstvo, itself transformed by a 1597 decree so that service typically continued for a creditor's lifetime rather than ending upon repayment, remained legally separate institutions from the serfdom the Ulozhenie consolidated, even as the practical gap between them narrowed over time.

The chapter also examined family, gender, and household authority as the level at which the broader legal categories of service, serfdom, urban attachment, and bondage were actually lived. It described the household (dvor), rather than the individual, as the basic unit of Muscovite taxation and social registration, and explained patriarchal authority as both a legal and a social institution, exercised most fully by the male household head but in practice shared, especially by widows, with women who managed property and dependents in his absence. It cautioned specifically against treating a prescriptive text such as the Domostroi as a transparent description of how households at every social level actually functioned, since surviving evidence disproportionately illuminates elite and urban households and leaves peasant family life to be reconstructed more indirectly, through court records, tax documents, and communal petitions.

Finally, the chapter assessed the practical limits of legal classification, emphasizing that flight to the southern frontier and Siberia, communal negotiation, petitioning, and limited avenues of individual mobility persisted even after 1649, and concluding that the Ulozhenie represented a genuine and historically significant increase in the state's capacity to regulate society without amounting to complete or uniform control. Regional variation reinforced this conclusion: the code's provisions applied with real practical force in the densely administered central districts around Moscow, but weakened considerably with distance, so that the southern steppe frontier and the expanding spaces of Siberia functioned throughout this period as genuine, if increasingly risky, alternatives to the legally consolidated social order the rest of the chapter describes. The distinction between legal classification and practical control, rather than any single legislative provision, is therefore the chapter's most important and most transferable historical-thinking lesson.

These lessons—distinguishing codification from creation, tracing cumulative rather than sudden causation, keeping related legal categories analytically distinct, and weighing state capacity against the practical limits of premodern administration—prepare students directly for Chapter 28's examination of Siberian conquest and settlement, where many of the same populations who evaded the social order examined in this chapter reappear as frontier settlers, and for the later chapters of this textbook that trace serfdom's further expansion and, eventually, its abolition.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has shown how the reconstructed administrative capacity of the Romanov state, examined in Chapter 26, was turned toward the comprehensive legal classification of nearly every major group in Muscovite society. The Sobornoe Ulozhenie of 1649 did not create Russian serfdom, urban taxation, or household patriarchy from nothing; it drew together, systematized, and made durably enforceable a set of social relationships and legal restrictions that had been developing, often unevenly and under crisis conditions, for decades. Its lasting significance lies precisely in this comprehensiveness and durability: a single, printed, authoritative code that would remain the formal foundation of Russian law for roughly two centuries.

The chapter's central methodological lesson—that a government's growing administrative capacity can substantially increase its capacity to regulate social relationships, though never completely or automatically, and that legal codification should be read as consolidation rather than creation—will recur throughout this textbook, particularly in later chapters examining the further expansion of serfdom under Peter the Great and Catherine the Great, and its eventual abolition under Alexander II.

As the textbook proceeds to Chapter 28's examination of the conquest and settlement of Siberia, students should carry forward this chapter's attention to the gap between legal classification and practical control, since many of the peasants, fugitives, and frontier settlers who appear in the next chapter are the very people who found, in the vast spaces beyond Moscow's administrative reach, a means of evading the social order this chapter has described.
