Organized Crime: A Global History — Navigation, Review, and Research Tools
Section 1

How to Use This Guide

This back matter is a navigation companion, not a second textbook. Its purpose is to help you review what you have learned, locate information quickly, and find your way into deeper study.

The chapters of Organized Crime: A Global History build a sustained, chronological, and thematic argument about how criminal organizations have emerged, evolved, adapted, and persisted across human societies. This reference guide serves three purposes that the chapters themselves cannot efficiently serve.

First, it helps you answer the question, What did I just learn? The Chapter at a Glance table gives you a one-line summary of every chapter's central argument. The Chronological Timeline arranges the most important events and developments in sequence across the entire scope of the book. Together, these tools let you see the larger pattern that individual chapters, each absorbed in their own period or place, can sometimes obscure.

Second, it helps you answer the question, Where can I quickly find something again? The sections on Major Organizations, Major Figures, Key Laws, Criminal Markets, and Geographic Hotspots are brief reference entries, not encyclopedia articles. Each entry identifies what a person, organization, law, or place is, why it matters to the study of organized crime, and which chapters discuss it in depth. When you need to relocate a concept or check a fact during revision or research, these entries are your first stop.

Third, it helps you answer the question, Where do I go next if I want to learn more? The Connections Across the Book section shows how core themes, such as protection racketeering, corruption, money laundering, and the relationship between state weakness and criminal power, develop across multiple chapters. The Continuing Your Study section points toward the broader MGU Library collections, companion volumes, and related fields of inquiry. The Bibliography identifies the primary and secondary works that support the textbook's arguments.

A note on scope: the entries in this guide are intentionally brief. For longer biographical profiles, organizational histories, legal analyses, and statistical data, the MGU Encyclopedia, MGU Dictionary of Criminal Justice, and MGU Reference collections are the appropriate resources. This guide is the bridge that connects the textbook to those deeper collections, not a replacement for them.

29 Cybercrime Organizations IX — Organized Crime in the Information Age The internet created a vast new frontier for organized crime, enabling ransomware gangs, identity theft operations, and fraud networks to operate across borders with minimal physical infrastructure.

31 Global Criminal Networks IX — Organized Crime in the Information Age Contemporary organized crime increasingly operates as fluid, networked, and decentralized structures rather than hierarchical organizations, making them harder to infiltrate, prosecute, and dismantle.

33 Laws Against Organized Crime X — Fighting Organized Crime Anti-racketeering statutes, asset forfeiture, and witness protection programs represent the legal arsenal developed to fight organized crime, each with both successes and significant civil liberties tensions.
34 Successes and Failures X — Fighting Organized Crime The historical record of anti-organized crime efforts shows that successful prosecutions often redirect rather than eliminate criminal enterprise. Criminal adaptation has consistently outpaced enforcement innovation.
35 Why Organized Crime Persists XI — Organized Crime and Society As long as societies prohibit goods and services for which demand remains strong, generate economic inequality that leaves populations vulnerable, and sustain institutions susceptible to corruption, organized crime will find conditions for survival.
36 Organized Crime in the Twenty-First Century XI — Organized Crime and Society Globalization, technology, and shifting geopolitics have created new opportunities and new vulnerabilities for criminal organizations, producing a landscape of organized crime more complex, more varied, and more globally integrated than any previous era.
37 The Recurring Cycle XI — Organized Crime and Society The history of organized crime follows a recurring pattern: prohibition creates black markets, black markets create criminal enterprises, criminal enterprises corrupt governance, enforcement responses disrupt but rarely eliminate them, and the cycle begins again.

Major Organizations

Each entry below provides a brief identification of a major criminal organization discussed in the textbook. For extended organizational histories, consult the MGU Encyclopedia.

Sicilian Mafia (Cosa Nostra)

Origin
Sicily, mid-nineteenth century
Structure
Territorial families (cosche) led by a boss (capo), governed locally by councils
Primary Activities
Protection, extortion, political corruption, infiltration of legitimate business
Distinctive Feature
Operates as private governance in areas of state weakness; rules by omertà (code of silence)
Discussed In
Chapters 6, 13, 15, 16

Camorra

Origin
Naples, early nineteenth century (prison origins)
Structure
Loosely networked clans without a unified hierarchy
Primary Activities
Drug trafficking, extortion, construction fraud, waste disposal, counterfeiting
Distinctive Feature
Urban and fragmented; more violent internal competition than the Sicilian Mafia
Discussed In
Chapters 7, 16

'Ndrangheta

Origin
Calabria, late nineteenth century
Structure
Clans ('ndrine) organized around blood family ties; highly cohesive
Primary Activities
Cocaine trafficking (European distribution), kidnapping, extortion, public contracts
Distinctive Feature
Family-based structure makes infiltration extremely difficult; now considered Europe's most powerful criminal organization
Discussed In
Chapters 8, 16, 31

American Mafia (La Cosa Nostra)

Origin
United States, developed from Italian immigrant criminal networks, 1910s–1930s
Structure
Twenty-four families nationwide; New York Five Families dominant; governed by the Commission
Primary Activities
Labor racketeering, gambling, loan sharking, construction, narcotics
Distinctive Feature
The Commission (1931) made it the most bureaucratically rationalized criminal organization of its era
Discussed In
Chapters 9–16

Yakuza

Origin
Japan, early modern era; modern form consolidated in twentieth century
Structure
Hierarchical syndicates (the Yamaguchi-gumi the largest); semi-open membership
Primary Activities
Extortion, gambling, prostitution, financial crime, construction
Distinctive Feature
Historically tolerated by the Japanese state as a stabilizing force; operates with unusual public visibility
Discussed In
Chapter 17

Chinese Triads

Origin
China, seventeenth century (political secret societies); criminal evolution in nineteenth–twentieth centuries
Structure
Loose networks of affiliated groups; no global central command
Primary Activities
Drug trafficking, extortion, human smuggling, counterfeiting, gambling
Distinctive Feature
International reach across Chinese diaspora communities; ritual initiation and historical mythology
Discussed In
Chapter 18

Russian Organized Crime (Bratva)

Origin
Soviet Union (black market networks); explosive growth after 1991
Structure
Loosely affiliated criminal networks; often tied to oligarchic business and political structures
Primary Activities
Fraud, money laundering, cybercrime, energy-sector corruption, extortion, arms trafficking
Distinctive Feature
Deep entanglement with Russian political and business elites; operates across Europe, Israel, and North America
Discussed In
Chapter 19

Medellín Cartel

Origin
Colombia, late 1970s
Structure
Led by Pablo Escobar; vertically integrated cocaine production and distribution
Primary Activities
Cocaine production and trafficking; narco-terrorism; political assassination
Distinctive Feature
Pioneered narco-terrorism as a political strategy to fight extradition; killed thousands
Discussed In
Chapters 22, 24

Cali Cartel

Origin
Colombia, 1970s
Structure
More corporate and less violent than Medellín; sophisticated financial networks
Primary Activities
Cocaine trafficking; political corruption; money laundering
Distinctive Feature
Preferred corruption to violence; demonstrated that criminal organizations can achieve political capture through economic means

Chapters 22, 24

Sinaloa Cartel

Origin
Mexico, Sinaloa state; rose to dominance in 1990s–2000s
Structure
Federated network led historically by Joaquín "El Chapo" Guzmán

Cocaine, heroin, methamphetamine, and fentanyl trafficking; corruption

Section 5

Major Figures

Brief reference entries for the most significant individuals discussed in the textbook. These are identification summaries only. For biographical depth, consult the MGU Encyclopedia.

Al Capone (1899–1947)

American Gangster / Prohibition-Era Criminal Leader

Rose to dominate Chicago organized crime during Prohibition. Built a criminal empire spanning bootlegging, gambling, and prostitution. His prosecution for tax evasion in 1931 illustrated the vulnerability of criminal leaders to financial investigation.

See Chapters 11, 12, 16

Lucky Luciano (1897–1962)

American Mafia / Reorganizer of the Commission

Born Salvatore Lucania in Sicily. Ended the Castellammarese War and created the Commission, transforming Italian American organized crime from warring factions into a stable, rationalized syndicate. Considered the architect of the modern American Mafia.

See Chapters 13, 14, 15

Meyer Lansky (1902–1983)

American Organized Crime / Financial Operations

Lansky developed the financial infrastructure of American organized crime, pioneering money laundering through offshore banking in the Bahamas and Switzerland. His career illustrated the essential role of financial expertise in sustaining criminal enterprise.

See Chapters 13, 25, 28

Pablo Escobar (1949–1993)

Colombian Cartel Leader / Medellín Cartel

Led the Medellín Cartel to unprecedented wealth and violence. Pioneered narco-terrorism against the Colombian state, assassinating judges, politicians, and police. His death in 1993 demonstrated that eliminating a leader does not destroy the criminal market he served.

See Chapters 22, 24, 34

Joaquín "El Chapo" Guzmán (b. 1957)

Sinaloa Cartel / Mexico

Led the Sinaloa Cartel to dominance in North American drug trafficking. Escaped Mexican maximum-security prisons twice before his extradition to the United States and conviction in 2019. His career illustrates both the reach of cartel corruption and the limits of individual prosecution.

See Chapters 23, 24, 34

John Gotti (1940–2002)

Gambino Family / American Mafia

Rose to lead the Gambino Family through the murder of Paul Castellano in 1985. Known as the "Teflon Don" for escaping early prosecutions. His 1992 conviction on RICO charges, achieved through the testimony of his underboss Sammy Gravano, marked a turning point in the Mafia's decline.

See Chapters 14, 16

J. Edgar Hoover (1895–1972)

FBI Director / Law Enforcement

Long refused to acknowledge organized crime as a federal concern, hampering early law enforcement responses. His eventual engagement with the Mafia in the 1960s, under political pressure from Robert Kennedy, came after decades of missed opportunity.

See Chapters 15, 16, 32

Robert Kennedy (1925–1968)

U.S. Attorney General / Anti-Organized Crime Campaign

As Attorney General, Kennedy pursued organized crime with unprecedented federal resources, launching investigations that would eventually produce the prosecutorial tools and political will that dismantled the Mafia in the 1980s.

See Chapters 15, 16, 33

Joseph Valachi (1903–1971)

American Mafia Informant

Former Genovese family soldier who became the first Mafia insider to testify publicly before Congress in 1963, confirming the existence and structure of Cosa Nostra. His testimony reshaped law enforcement and public understanding of the American Mafia.

See Chapters 14, 16

Rudolph Giuliani (b. 1944)

U.S. Attorney / RICO Prosecution

As U.S. Attorney for the Southern District of New York, Giuliani led the "Commission Trial" of 1985–86 that convicted the leaders of all five New York families under RICO. Represented the most decisive legal blow against the American Mafia's central leadership.

See Chapters 16, 33, 34

Giovanni Falcone (1939–1992)

Italian Anti-Mafia Magistrate

Led the Palermo "Maxi Trial" (1986–87) that resulted in 360 Mafia convictions. Assassinated by the Sicilian Mafia in 1992. His career embodied the cost and complexity of sustained anti-organized crime prosecution in societies where criminal organizations have deep social roots.

See Chapters 6, 16, 32, 34

Semion Mogilevich (b. 1946)

Russian Organized Crime / International Financial Crime

Often described as the most dangerous organized crime figure in the world by law enforcement agencies. Runs international criminal networks involved in fraud, money laundering, arms trafficking, and energy-sector corruption across multiple continents.

See Chapters 19, 28, 31
Section 6

Key Laws and Legal Concepts

The following are the most important statutes, legal doctrines, and international instruments discussed in the textbook. For full legal analysis, consult the MGU Reference collection on criminal law.

Volstead Act and the Eighteenth Amendment (U.S., 1919–1920)

The constitutional prohibition of alcohol and its implementing legislation created the largest criminal market in American history to that point. The Prohibition era transformed loosely organized bootleggers into the national criminal syndicates that would define American organized crime for the rest of the twentieth century.

Discussed in Chapters 11, 12, 37

Racketeer Influenced and Corrupt Organizations Act — RICO (U.S., 1970)

RICO allows federal prosecutors to charge members of an ongoing criminal enterprise for any two criminal acts committed on its behalf within ten years, and to seize assets connected to the enterprise. The statute transformed organized crime prosecution by targeting the organization rather than individual crimes, making it possible to dismantle criminal hierarchies rather than merely prosecute individual offenders.

Discussed in Chapters 16, 33, 34

Asset Forfeiture Laws

Statutes allowing the government to seize assets derived from or used in criminal activity. Civil forfeiture (no criminal conviction required) and criminal forfeiture (following conviction) have become central tools in attacking the financial infrastructure of criminal organizations, though they have also generated significant civil liberties controversy.

Discussed in Chapters 16, 28, 33

Witness Protection Program (U.S. Marshals Service, established 1970)

Formally established alongside RICO, the Witness Security Program (WITSEC) provided the essential complement to legal tools by giving potential informants a credible promise of safety. Without it, RICO prosecutions against major organized crime figures would have been far harder to sustain.

Discussed in Chapters 16, 33

Bank Secrecy Act (U.S., 1970) and Anti-Money Laundering Frameworks

Required banks to maintain records and report suspicious transactions, creating the foundation of modern anti-money laundering law. Subsequent expansions, including the USA PATRIOT Act provisions, extended these requirements globally. Criminal organizations responded by developing increasingly sophisticated layering techniques, including offshore structures and, later, cryptocurrency.

Discussed in Chapters 28, 33

UN Convention Against Transnational Organized Crime (Palermo Convention, 2000)

The principal international legal instrument addressing organized crime globally. Requires signatory states to criminalize participation in organized criminal groups, money laundering, corruption, and obstruction of justice, and to cooperate in law enforcement, extradition, and mutual legal assistance.

Discussed in Chapters 32, 33

Extradition Treaties

Bilateral and multilateral agreements requiring states to surrender fugitives to face prosecution in another country. Extradition was fiercely resisted by Colombian cartels in the 1980s and has remained a central tool and point of criminal resistance in anti-organized crime efforts globally.

Discussed in Chapters 22, 33

Financial Action Task Force (FATF)

An intergovernmental body that sets international standards for combating money laundering and terrorist financing. FATF's "blacklist" and "greylist" of non-compliant jurisdictions creates economic pressure on states that serve as offshore financial havens for criminal wealth.

Discussed in Chapters 28, 32, 33
Section 7

Criminal Markets

Organized crime is fundamentally a business. Each of the following markets represents a sector of the illegal economy that criminal organizations have exploited across history. The textbook traces each market's development and the organizations that have dominated it.

Drug Trafficking

The largest illegal commodity market in the world, encompassing the production, transportation, and distribution of cocaine, heroin, methamphetamine, fentanyl, cannabis, and synthetic drugs. The global drug trade generates estimated revenues of hundreds of billions of dollars annually. It has funded criminal organizations on every continent and directly shaped the politics of producer and transit countries.

See Chapters 21, 22, 23, 24, 31, 35

Illegal Gambling

One of the oldest and most consistent sources of criminal revenue. Illegal casinos, sports betting operations, and underground lotteries (numbers games) have funded organized crime from Prohibition-era America through the present. Where legal gambling expands, illegal operations typically contract, but rarely disappear entirely.

See Chapters 10, 13, 15, 17, 25

Labor Racketeering

The infiltration and exploitation of labor unions and legitimate industries, particularly construction, transportation, and longshoring. At its height, the American Mafia controlled significant portions of the U.S. construction industry and several major unions, extracting tribute from both employers and workers.

See Chapters 15, 16

Loan Sharking (Usury)

The provision of credit at illegally high interest rates, enforced through violence or its credible threat. Loan sharking provides both income and leverage over borrowers, creating long-term relationships of dependency and exploitation that have been a staple of criminal enterprise across history.

See Chapters 3, 10, 15

Human Trafficking and Smuggling

The movement of human beings across borders by force, fraud, or coercion for labor or sexual exploitation (trafficking) or for profit with the migrant's knowledge and consent (smuggling). Both have become major criminal revenue streams as globalization increased the demand for cheap labor and migration barriers multiplied.

See Chapters 20, 26, 31

Smuggling and Contraband

The movement of prohibited or taxable goods across borders to evade tariffs, prohibitions, or regulations. Contraband trade is as old as taxation itself and has involved tobacco, alcohol, fuel, weapons, wildlife, cultural artifacts, and luxury goods. It often coexists with and subsidizes more serious criminal activity.

See Chapters 3, 4, 5, 20, 27

Counterfeiting and Intellectual Property Theft

The manufacture and distribution of counterfeit currency, luxury goods, pharmaceuticals, electronics, and media. Criminal counterfeiting operations generate substantial revenue with relatively low enforcement risk compared to drug trafficking, and have increasingly been absorbed into transnational criminal networks.

See Chapters 18, 27, 31

Money Laundering

Not a criminal market in itself but the essential infrastructure of all others. Money laundering integrates criminal proceeds into the legitimate economy through placement, layering, and integration. Techniques have evolved from simple cash businesses through offshore shell corporations to, most recently, cryptocurrency mixing services.

See Chapters 13, 15, 19, 28, 30

Cybercrime

A rapidly growing sector encompassing ransomware, phishing, identity theft, card fraud, business email compromise, and the operation of criminal marketplaces. Cybercrime organizations have achieved revenues comparable to major drug trafficking networks while requiring minimal physical infrastructure and carrying lower personal risk for operators.

See Chapters 29, 30, 31, 36

Arms Trafficking

The illegal trade in weapons, from small arms to heavy military equipment. Arms trafficking has been a significant activity for Balkan criminal networks, Russian organized crime, and various cartel-adjacent organizations, often moving weapons from surplus military stocks into conflict zones or high-violence criminal markets.

See Chapters 20, 23, 31

The Dark Web (global) No physical location but a critical jurisdiction of criminal commerce; hosts drug markets, cybercrime services, and financial crime infrastructure Silk Road successors; ransomware groups; hacking forums 29, 30
Section 9

Key Terms

The following terms are essential to the textbook's analysis. Each definition is concise. For fuller discussion, consult the MGU Dictionary of Criminal Justice.

Black market
An illegal market for goods or services prohibited by law or made scarce by regulation. Black markets emerge wherever law creates demand without eliminating it.
Bootlegging
The illegal production, transportation, and sale of alcohol, particularly during U.S. Prohibition (1920–1933). The bootlegging economy created the organizational foundations of modern American organized crime.
Cartel
An organization of drug producers and traffickers that attempts to control supply and price in drug markets. Unlike legitimate cartels, criminal cartels enforce agreements through violence rather than law.
Commission, the
The governing body created by Lucky Luciano in 1931 to regulate relations among the major Italian American crime families in the United States, resolve disputes, and prevent destructive internal wars.
Corruption
The abuse of entrusted power for private gain. In the context of organized crime, corruption refers to the purchase or coercion of public officials to protect criminal operations from law enforcement.
Cosa Nostra
"Our thing" in Italian. The self-referential term used by members of the American Mafia and the Sicilian Mafia to describe their organizations.
Dark web
A portion of the internet accessible only through anonymizing software (such as Tor) that hosts criminal marketplaces, hacking forums, and other illicit services.
Extortion
The obtaining of money or compliance through threats of harm. Protection rackets, which charge businesses for "protection" against violence the extorter could itself inflict, are the most common organized crime form.
Failed state
A state that has lost the ability to enforce law and deliver basic governance. Failed or fragile states provide the most fertile environments for organized crime by eliminating effective law enforcement and creating demand for criminal governance.
Fencing
The purchase and resale of stolen goods. Fencing networks are an essential component of property crime ecosystems and often connect petty criminals to organized criminal enterprises.
Human trafficking
The recruitment, transportation, or receipt of persons by force, fraud, or coercion for exploitation, including forced labor and commercial sexual exploitation. Distinguished from smuggling by the element of exploitation.
Integration (money laundering)
The final stage of money laundering in which cleaned funds re-enter the legitimate economy as apparently lawful assets such as real estate, businesses, or investment portfolios.
Labor racketeering
The infiltration and corrupt control of labor unions or entire industries to extract payments from employers, divert union funds, and use labor power as a criminal commodity.
Layering (money laundering)
The second stage of money laundering, in which criminal funds are moved through a complex series of financial transactions to obscure their origin and make tracing difficult.
Loan sharking
The provision of illegal credit at usurious interest rates, enforced through the threat of violence. A traditional organized crime revenue stream and mechanism of control over debtors.
Money laundering
The process of making illegally obtained money appear legitimate by passing it through a sequence of banking or commercial transactions. Consists of placement, layering, and integration stages.
Narco-terrorism
The use of terrorist violence by drug trafficking organizations against state institutions, officials, or civilian populations to intimidate governments into non-enforcement of drug laws.
Omertà
The Mafia code of silence. A norm that prohibits members from cooperating with law enforcement or acknowledging the organization's existence. Its enforcement through violence is essential to Mafia institutional cohesion.
Organized crime
A sustained enterprise operated by a group for criminal profit, using violence or its threat as an enforcement mechanism. Distinguished from individual crime by its institutional character and from terrorism by its primarily economic rather than political motivation.
Piracy
The commission of robbery or criminal violence at sea. Ancient piracy represents one of the earliest documented forms of organized criminal enterprise, and contemporary maritime piracy remains a significant criminal activity in several regions.
Placement (money laundering)
The first stage of money laundering, in which criminal cash is introduced into the financial system, often through cash-intensive businesses or currency exchange.
Protection racket
A criminal enterprise in which businesses or individuals are charged for "protection" that the criminal organization itself provides against threats it could itself create. One of the oldest and most persistent forms of organized crime.
Ransomware
Malicious software that encrypts a victim's data and demands payment (usually in cryptocurrency) for the decryption key. Ransomware gangs have become major criminal enterprises in the twenty-first century.
RICO
The Racketeer Influenced and Corrupt Organizations Act (U.S., 1970). A federal statute that allows prosecution of criminal enterprise participants for any two predicate crimes committed within ten years, transforming organized crime prosecution.
Shell corporation
A company with no genuine business activity, used to conceal the true ownership of assets or to facilitate financial transactions for money laundering purposes.
Smuggling
The illegal movement of goods or persons across borders to evade tariffs, prohibitions, or regulations. One of the oldest forms of organized criminal activity, dating to the earliest formation of trade and taxation.
State capture
A condition in which criminal organizations achieve sufficient influence over political institutions that state policy is shaped to serve criminal interests rather than the public interest. Distinguished from ordinary corruption by its systemic, institutionalized character.
Trafficking
The movement and trade of prohibited goods or exploited persons. Drug trafficking and human trafficking represent the two most significant trafficking markets in terms of criminal revenue and human harm.
Triad
A Chinese criminal organization, historically derived from secret political societies and evolved into criminal enterprises active across Hong Kong, mainland China, Taiwan, and Chinese diaspora communities worldwide.
Witness protection
Government programs that provide new identities, relocation, and financial support to witnesses who testify against organized crime. Essential complement to RICO prosecution; without it, the threat of violence makes most insider testimony impossible to obtain.
Yakuza
Japanese organized crime syndicates with long historical roots, distinctive ritual practices, and a historically semi-open relationship with Japanese society and politics.

A Note on the Study of Organized Crime

The study of organized crime is, at its core, the study of what happens when human beings find ways to supply demand that law refuses to satisfy, to deliver order that states fail to provide, and to accumulate power in the gaps that every society leaves between its rules and its enforcement. The history told in this textbook spans three thousand years and every inhabited continent, yet it returns, again and again, to a small number of recurring conditions: prohibition, demand, corruption, violence, adaptation, and cycle.

That recurring cycle is perhaps the most important lesson the book offers. Every era has believed that it faced a uniquely dangerous form of organized crime, and every era has been partly right. The Prohibition-era gangster, the Cold War Mafia don, the Colombian narco-terrorist, and the twenty-first-century ransomware operator each represent genuinely new adaptations to genuinely new environments. But beneath each new form runs the same logic: find a market the law prohibits, supply it, protect the supply chain, corrupt whoever needs corrupting, and use violence only as much as profit requires.

Understanding that logic does not excuse the human suffering that organized crime produces. It does, however, provide the analytical tools necessary to think clearly about why organized crime persists, what responses have worked, what responses have failed, and what future challenges are likely to look like. The MGU Library's collections, referenced throughout this guide, are designed to support that thinking across many disciplines, from criminal justice and political economy to international law and digital security. The invitation to continue studying, questioning, and connecting ideas is the most important thing any textbook's back matter can extend.
