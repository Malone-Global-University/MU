Part VI — Sengoku Japan and Reunification
35

The Sengoku Daimyō

Japanese History: From Jōmon Japan to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

By the middle of the sixteenth century, a traveler moving across the Japanese archipelago would have passed through a shifting patchwork of zones of effective authority that no map issued from Kyoto could any longer describe with confidence. The edges of these zones were rarely sharp lines; they were contested, fluctuating margins where a ruler's tax collectors, road patrols, and law applied firmly near the center of his power, more loosely toward the frontier, and not at all beyond it — until the next campaign, marriage, or negotiated submission redrew the picture again. The shogun still existed. The emperor still reigned in Kyoto, presiding over a court whose ceremonies continued much as before. But the actual work of governing — assessing land, organizing armies, judging disputes, keeping roads passable, deciding who could build a mill or hold a market — had shifted decisively into the hands of regional rulers who owed the center only intermittent deference, if that.

Chapter 34 told the story of collapse: how the Ōnin War tore apart the fragile compromises that had allowed the Ashikaga shoguns to preside, however imperfectly, over a nationwide order of shugo governors, court nobles, and religious institutions. That war left Kyoto burned, the Ashikaga house diminished to a shadow of its former authority, and warrior houses across the provinces newly free to pursue their own ambitions without meaningful central restraint. It would be easy, and it would also be wrong, to treat the century that followed simply as a story of chaos — of endless, directionless violence in which government itself disappeared until Oda Nobunaga arrived to restore order. That version of events flatters the eventual unifiers by making everything before them look like mere noise. It is not what the evidence shows.

What the evidence shows instead is a remarkable, uneven, and often violent process of political reconstruction at the regional level. Across the provinces of Japan, warrior families — some descended from the old shugo governors, some risen from positions as their deputies or local agents, some climbing from comparatively modest origins through military success and shrewd alliance — set about building something that the old Muromachi order had never quite achieved: territorial governments capable of exercising real, continuous authority over defined stretches of land. These rulers, whom historians call the Sengoku daimyō, did not simply command armies. They surveyed and taxed land, issued law codes that bound their retainers and subjects alike, built castles that served as administrative headquarters as much as fortresses, negotiated continually with villages, temples, and independent local warriors, and competed with their neighbors in a system that rewarded organizational capacity as much as battlefield courage.

This chapter is about that process of regional state-building. It asks how it was possible for authority to fragment so completely at the national level while becoming, in many provinces, more coherent, more intrusive, and more effective at the regional level than anything the Ashikaga shoguns had managed to sustain even at the height of Muromachi power. It asks what actually made a warrior lord a "Sengoku daimyō" rather than simply a strong local warrior among many — a question that turns out to have less to do with pedigree than with the practical capacities of territorial rule: the ability to organize retainers, extract and manage revenue, adjudicate disputes, defend a domain, and, when circumstances allowed, expand it.

It is important, from the outset, to resist two temptations that popular accounts of this period often indulge. The first is to romanticize the era as a stage for individual heroism, in which battlefield brilliance alone explains a lord's success. Battlefield skill mattered, and Chapter 36 will examine in detail how warfare itself changed during this period. But sustained territorial power depended at least as much on unglamorous administrative work: keeping accurate land registers, settling inheritance disputes without alienating retainers, maintaining roads and markets, and persuading villages that submission to a particular lord's rule was more bearable than the alternative. The second temptation is to imagine the Sengoku daimyō as rulers of tidy, modern-looking states with fixed borders, uniform bureaucracies, and a monopoly on the legitimate use of force. No Sengoku domain achieved anything of the kind. Authority remained personal, negotiated, and frequently contested from within as well as from without. Villages retained real collective capacity to resist or bargain. Local warrior families jealously guarded bases of their own. Religious institutions could field armed followers and command loyalties that rivaled a daimyō's own.

The chapter therefore asks readers to hold two ideas together that might initially seem to contradict each other. Japan as a whole, during the century or more this chapter surveys, lacked anything resembling effective central government. And yet, within that fragmentation, many individual domains became more intensively governed — more capable of mobilizing resources, adjudicating disputes, and sustaining organized armies over long periods — than the provinces of Ashikaga Japan had generally been a century earlier. Political disintegration at the national scale and political intensification at the regional scale were not opposites. They were, in large part, the same historical process viewed from two different vantage points.

By the chapter's end, students should understand the Sengoku daimyō not as agents of pure destruction clearing the ground for reunification, nor as tidy proto-modern state-builders, but as regional rulers experimenting, often under severe and continuous military pressure, with the practical problems of territorial government: how to bind retainers, how to extract revenue reliably, how to keep the peace within a domain's borders, and how to survive — and, where possible, grow — in a competitive world of rival domains. That experimentation, more than any single battle or biography, is what made the reunification chronicled later in this Part conceivable at all.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how Sengoku daimyō emerged from several distinct pathways, including surviving shugo houses, shugodai deputies, local warrior families, and newly risen lords.
  2. Define the category "Sengoku daimyō" functionally, in terms of territorial authority, retainer organization, revenue administration, and lawmaking capacity, rather than as a fixed social rank or office.
  3. Describe the methods by which regional rulers attempted to convert scattered and overlapping rights into more coherent control over contiguous territory.
  4. Analyze the function of castles as centers of lordship, administration, and regional political geography, distinct from their purely military architecture.
  5. Trace the methods daimyō used to organize retainers into more dependable hierarchies of service and obligation.
  6. Explain how domains assessed land, collected revenue, and financed sustained military competition.
  7. Evaluate the purpose and limits of domain law codes (bunkokuhō) as instruments of territorial governance.
  8. Assess the continuing independent capacity of villages, local warriors, and religious institutions, and the negotiated character of daimyō rule.
  9. Compare different regional paths to territorial power, using case studies such as the Later Hōjō, Takeda, Uesugi, Mōri, and Shimazu.
  10. Explain the apparent paradox of simultaneous national fragmentation and regional political intensification.
  11. Distinguish the political organization of military power, covered in this chapter, from the mechanics of Sengoku warfare covered in Chapter 36.
  12. Avoid anachronistic descriptions of Sengoku domains as modern nation-states with fixed borders and uniform bureaucracy.

Historical Context

Chapter 34 traced the breakdown of central authority that culminated in the Ōnin War (1467–1477) and the decades of continuing conflict that followed. The Ashikaga shogunate, weakened by succession disputes, factional rivalry among great shugo houses, and its own structural dependence on the cooperation of powerful regional governors, lost the capacity to arbitrate disputes among its nominal subordinates. Kyoto itself was devastated by street-to-street fighting, and the authority the shoguns had once exercised over provincial appointments, taxation, and military mobilization dissolved into a patchwork of rival claims. By the early sixteenth century, the shogunate retained genuine prestige, legitimating authority, and intermittent political influence — it remained, for instance, a contested institution that competing claimants and their regional backers still fought to control during Ashikaga Yoshitane's return to office between 1508 and 1521 — but it no longer possessed consistent capacity to compel regional rulers to obey its rulings.

This chapter picks up the story at the point where that collapse becomes generative rather than merely destructive. The shugo system that Chapter 29 described — in which the Ashikaga shoguns appointed provincial governors (shugo) who, over generations, accumulated substantial local power while still deriving their formal authority from Kyoto — had already been under strain for decades before the Ōnin War. Many shugo governed their provinces from Kyoto itself, delegating actual administration to deputies known as shugodai, who in turn often built their own local power bases, their own networks of retainers, and their own ambitions. When central authority collapsed, these accumulated local capacities did not vanish; they became the raw material from which new, more territorially concentrated forms of lordship could be built.

Students should keep three points from earlier chapters firmly in view while reading this one. First, the imperial court and the office of shogun did not disappear during the Sengoku period; both persisted as sources of ceremonial legitimacy that ambitious warriors continued to find useful, even as their practical power became sharply constrained and increasingly contested. Second, the shōen estate system and the complex, overlapping web of proprietary rights described in earlier chapters on Heian and Kamakura Japan had already been eroding for centuries under pressure from provincial warriors and absentee proprietors alike; the Sengoku daimyō accelerated and substantially advanced, rather than completed, the long transition from estate-based to more directly territorial forms of control over land — a transition that would continue still further under Hideyoshi's nationwide surveys and the early Tokugawa settlement, examined in later chapters. Third, Buddhist institutions, from the great monastic complexes of Kyoto and Nara to popular religious movements such as the Ikkō sect examined further below, had long possessed independent landholdings, economic resources, and, in some cases, armed followers; they remained significant political and military actors throughout the period this chapter examines, not merely obstacles that daimyō eventually swept aside.

The term "Sengoku," meaning "Warring States" — borrowed from a much earlier period of Chinese history — describes the roughly century and a half between the Ōnin War and the definitive political settlements imposed by Oda Nobunaga, Toyotomi Hideyoshi, and Tokugawa Ieyasu at the close of the sixteenth and opening of the seventeenth centuries. Like most period labels, "Sengoku" is a retrospective analytical convenience rather than a term contemporaries used to describe a fixed span of years with agreed beginning and end dates; conditions varied considerably by region, and the pace of consolidation differed sharply from province to province. This chapter focuses on the middle decades of that long period, roughly the early sixteenth century through the 1560s and 1570s, when regional territorial rule reached its most developed and most widely documented forms, before the accelerating campaigns of unification examined in Chapters 39 through 41 began to absorb one domain after another into larger political structures.

Section 35.1

From Shugo Daimyō to Sengoku Daimyō

The collapse of Ashikaga arbitration described in Chapter 34 did not instantly sweep the older Muromachi warrior houses from the political stage. Many shugo daimyō — the great provincial governors whose authority had rested, at least formally, on appointment from Kyoto — survived the Ōnin War and continued to rule for decades afterward. Some, such as the Imagawa house of Suruga and Tōtōmi or the Takeda house of Kai, successfully adapted inherited shugo authority into the more intensively territorial forms of rule this chapter describes, becoming Sengoku daimyō in substance even as they retained titles and pedigrees rooted in the earlier Muromachi order. For these houses, the Sengoku transition was less a rupture than an intensification: the same family continued to rule the same province, but governed it in new ways, under new pressures, with new tools.

Other shugo houses did not survive the transition at all. Provincial governors who had grown comfortable relying on inherited prestige, absentee administration from Kyoto, or the cooperation of subordinates whose loyalty could no longer be taken for granted found themselves outmaneuvered, displaced, or absorbed by more energetic rivals. In case after case across the provinces, it was not the shugo but a subordinate — a shugodai deputy who had spent decades actually administering the province in the shugo's name, or a locally rooted warrior family that had built its own retainer network and its own base of agricultural revenue — who emerged as the effective ruler as central oversight became increasingly weak and inconsistently enforceable. Historians describe this pattern with the term gekokujō, "the low overcoming the high," and it captures something real and important about the period: formal rank and inherited office no longer guaranteed actual power in the way they once had, at least nominally, under a functioning Ashikaga order.

It would be a mistake, however, to treat gekokujō as a universal law explaining every Sengoku daimyō's rise, and this textbook resists that oversimplification. Many successful Sengoku rulers were not upstarts overthrowing their betters but members of established warrior lineages who simply proved more capable than their rivals — including rivals within their own extended families — at holding a domain together under pressure. Succession disputes within a single house, in which one branch or one claimant triumphed over others through a combination of military force, retainer support, and political calculation, produced at least as many Sengoku daimyō as did dramatic reversals of an established social hierarchy. The rise of the Later Hōjō, examined in detail in Section 35.9, combined elements of both patterns: a warrior connected to Muromachi shogunal service but lacking standing as an established Kantō shugo magnate, building new territorial authority in a region where older shugo structures had already grown weak and contested for reasons largely independent of his own actions.

Historical Vocabulary
Sengoku Daimyō

Sengoku daimyō ("Warring States great names") is the modern historical term for the regional warrior rulers who exercised substantial, largely independent territorial authority in Japan during the roughly century and a half following the Ōnin War. No contemporary law or office defined the category with precision; historians apply it functionally to rulers who organized retainers, administered land and revenue, issued domain law, and defended or expanded a recognized territory without meaningful oversight from the shogunate.

The term deliberately distinguishes these rulers from the shugo daimyō of the earlier Ashikaga order, whose authority derived formally from shogunal appointment, even though several Sengoku daimyō houses, such as the Takeda and Imagawa, descended directly from shugo lineages. Students should treat "Sengoku daimyō" as an analytical category describing a practical form of rule, not a fixed title, rank, or universally shared social origin.

What united these different pathways — surviving shugo houses, ambitious shugodai, locally rooted warrior families, and newcomers who built power through military success and calculated alliance — was not a shared origin but a shared problem: how to convert whatever advantages a family possessed, whether inherited title, administrative experience, local landholding, or battlefield reputation, into durable, continuous territorial authority in a world where no higher power could be relied upon to enforce anyone's claims. The remainder of this chapter examines how rulers who succeeded at that problem actually did so.

Section 35.2

What Made a Sengoku Daimyō?

No contemporary official document defined a fixed office or rank called "Sengoku daimyō." The term is a modern historical category, used by scholars to distinguish this generation of regional rulers analytically from the shugo daimyō who preceded them and from lesser local warriors who never achieved comparable territorial authority. Because it is an analytical category rather than a legal title, historians apply it functionally: a Sengoku daimyō is best understood as a warrior ruler who exercised substantial, largely independent territorial authority — who could organize and command a body of retainers, administer land and revenue within a recognized sphere of control, issue binding rules for that territory, and defend it, or expand it, against rival powers, all without depending on meaningful oversight from the shogunate or any other higher authority.

This functional definition matters because it allows the category to include rulers of very different backgrounds, scale, and formal status. A daimyō such as Takeda Shingen, descended from a shugo house with centuries of accumulated prestige, and a daimyō such as Hōjō Sōun, from an Ise lineage connected to Muromachi shogunal service but without comparable inherited territorial standing in the Kantō, both qualify as Sengoku daimyō under this definition because both built and sustained the same kind of practical territorial authority, even though their starting points could hardly have been more different. The definition also allows historians to exclude rulers who possessed grand titles but little practical power — a shugo who retained a court-recognized appointment but had lost all capacity to govern the province in question does not qualify, regardless of formal status, while a shugodai or kokujin leader who had assumed genuine territorial control does.

Several practical capacities recur across the domains this chapter examines, and together they give the category real content. A Sengoku daimyō typically commanded a hierarchy of retainers bound by land grants, service obligations, and, in many cases, more direct mechanisms of control such as hostage arrangements, discussed in Section 35.5. A Sengoku daimyō typically possessed some functioning system for assessing landholding and extracting revenue from it, whether through inherited estate rights, direct survey, or negotiated arrangements with village communities, discussed in Section 35.6. A Sengoku daimyō typically claimed, and to a meaningful degree exercised, the authority to issue binding rules — settling disputes, regulating markets, disciplining retainers — within a recognized territory, discussed in Section 35.7. And a Sengoku daimyō typically maintained a fortified headquarters, examined in Section 35.4, from which these functions of lordship, administration, and defense were coordinated.

None of these capacities, on its own, was unique to the Sengoku period; earlier shugo and even earlier estate holders had exercised versions of each. What distinguished the Sengoku daimyō was the degree to which these capacities became concentrated in a single ruler's hands, exercised continuously and directly rather than delegated loosely to subordinates or shared uneasily with a distant central authority, and sustained under conditions of near-constant military competition that punished weakness swiftly and rewarded organizational effectiveness. The Sengoku daimyō, in short, represents not a new kind of authority invented from nothing, but an intensification and concentration of powers that had existed in more diffuse form throughout the medieval period.

Section 35.3

Building Territorial Domains

Earlier chapters have described how landholding in Heian and Kamakura Japan involved a dense, overlapping web of proprietary rights: absentee court and religious proprietors holding nominal title to shōen estates, provincial stewards and land managers exercising practical control on the ground, and warrior families acquiring rights of taxation, jurisdiction, or military service that did not always correspond neatly to any single, continuous block of territory. A single village might, in principle, owe obligations to several different claimants at once, each possessing a distinct and partial right rather than any one party possessing full, exclusive control. This layered, fragmented pattern of landholding had already been eroding for centuries by the time the Sengoku period began, but it was the daimyō of the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries who pushed the process furthest, working to convert scattered and overlapping rights into more coherent, contiguous, and exclusively controlled territory.

Several methods recur across the domains examined in this chapter. Outright conquest was the most direct: a daimyō defeated a rival in battle, then absorbed the defeated house's landholdings, retainers, and administrative apparatus into his own domain, sometimes retaining defeated retainers in subordinate positions and sometimes replacing them with his own trusted followers. Strategic marriage served a related purpose with less bloodshed, binding neighboring houses into alliances that might later be converted, through inheritance, adoption, or negotiated absorption, into outright territorial consolidation; the marriage politics that bound houses such as the Takeda, Imagawa, and Hōjō together for a time, discussed further in Section 35.9, illustrate how alliance and rivalry could coexist within the same relationships across different periods.

The submission of local warriors — kokujin who held independent bases but recognized the practical advantages of aligning with a stronger neighbor rather than risking destruction by resisting — added territory and manpower without requiring a pitched battle at all, though it typically required the daimyō to grant meaningful concessions in return, a theme examined further in Section 35.8. Confiscation and regranting of land, particularly following a defeated rebellion or a purge of unreliable retainers, allowed a daimyō to redistribute holdings to more dependable followers, simultaneously punishing disloyalty and strengthening the loyalty of those who benefited from the redistribution. And the gradual absorption of smaller, weaker powers — through a combination of pressure, negotiation, and opportunism — allowed larger domains to grow incrementally rather than through any single decisive campaign.

Students should resist imagining that these methods produced neatly bounded, fully centralized territories comparable to a modern state's sovereign borders. Domain boundaries in the Sengoku period were frequently contested, fluctuating, and porous. A daimyō's authority might be firm at the administrative center and progressively weaker toward the frontier, where loyalties could shift with the fortunes of war. Enclaves of resistant local power, discussed further in Section 35.8, could persist for years within a domain's nominal territory. And the same family that expanded successfully for a generation might contract just as sharply in the next, as the fates of the Imagawa and, eventually, the Takeda houses would demonstrate. Territorial consolidation in this period was a continuous, effortful process rather than a condition that, once achieved, could simply be maintained without further exertion.

Section 35.4

Castles, Headquarters, and Political Geography

Every Sengoku daimyō of significance built or occupied a fortified headquarters, and it is tempting to think of these castles primarily in military terms: as fortifications designed to withstand siege. Chapter 36 will examine that military dimension in detail, tracing the evolution of castle architecture, defensive engineering, and siege warfare as part of the broader transformation of Sengoku-era combat. This chapter, by contrast, asks a different question: what did a castle do for a daimyō's government, considered apart from its purely defensive function?

The answer is that a Sengoku castle functioned above all as a center of lordship — a fixed point from which authority radiated outward across a domain. A daimyō's principal castle typically housed his own residence and that of his immediate family, storehouses for tax rice, weapons, and other essential supplies, and quarters or nearby residences for the senior retainers who administered the domain on his behalf. It served as the site where retainers gathered to receive instructions, where disputes among vassals might be brought for judgment, where hostages from allied or subordinate families, discussed in Section 35.5, could be housed under the daimyō's watchful eye, and where visiting envoys from neighboring powers conducted negotiations. In this sense, the castle functioned much as a capital functions for a larger state: not merely a fortified position but the administrative and symbolic center of an entire territorial government.

Around many principal castles, daimyō encouraged or compelled the growth of castle towns (jōkamachi), drawing merchants, artisans, and retainers into planned or semi-planned settlements clustered near the seat of lordship. This process, which accelerated further under the unifiers examined in Chapters 39 through 41, served several purposes at once: it concentrated skilled craftsmen capable of supplying arms and equipment, it generated commercial revenue that the daimyō could tax or otherwise benefit from, and it brought retainers physically closer to their lord, making it easier to summon them quickly and harder for them to maintain fully independent local power bases of their own. The relocation of retainers from scattered rural strongholds into castle towns, where it occurred, represents one of the more significant administrative innovations of the period, gradually transforming a lord's following from a loose network of locally rooted vassals into a more centralized retainer band housed near the seat of government.

A domain's political geography extended well beyond its principal castle. Secondary fortifications, garrisoned by trusted retainers or local warriors who had submitted to the daimyō's authority, secured frontiers, guarded strategic roads and river crossings, and provided fallback positions in the event of invasion. The distribution of these subsidiary strongholds across a domain reveals a great deal about where a daimyō's authority was firm and where it remained contested or thin. A dense network of secure fortifications toward a domain's interior, thinning toward exposed frontiers facing rival powers, was a common pattern, and students examining the Map Study feature below should look for exactly this kind of variation in the density and placement of fortified centers across different regional domains.

Section 35.5

Retainers, Vassalage, and the Organization of Power

A Sengoku daimyō's power rested, in the most immediate sense, on his ability to command the loyalty and military service of a body of retainers, and much of the practical work of Sengoku governance involved converting a heterogeneous collection of warrior families — some tied to a house by generations of hereditary service, some newly submitted local warriors with their own independent bases, some distant kin bound by marriage or adoption — into something resembling a dependable, hierarchical retainer band, often referred to collectively as a kashindan.

This was never a simple or fully achieved project. Hereditary retainers who had served a house for generations typically occupied the most senior and trusted positions, often holding substantial landed income and significant roles in domain administration; several of the domain law codes discussed in Section 35.7 devote considerable attention to regulating disputes among precisely these senior families, whose cooperation was indispensable but whose rivalries could threaten a domain's stability if left unmanaged. Newly submitted kokujin, by contrast, frequently retained a good deal of practical autonomy over their own local holdings even after formally entering a daimyō's service, and daimyō often had to tread carefully, confirming existing local privileges and rights (a practice termed ando, or confirmation) rather than attempting to strip such warriors of their bases outright, at least in the initial stages of incorporation.

Land grants in exchange for military and administrative service formed the basic currency of vassalage throughout the period, continuing patterns already familiar from earlier warrior government but administered with increasing precision as domains developed more systematic assessment methods, discussed in Section 35.6. A retainer's landed income, frequently expressed in terms of assessed monetary value (kandaka) rather than simple acreage, determined the scale of military service — the number of armed men, horses, and equipment — he was obligated to provide when summoned. This linkage between assessed income and military obligation gave daimyō a predictable basis for mobilizing armies of a known approximate size, a significant administrative achievement that Chapter 36 will show translating directly into battlefield capacity.

Several domains supplemented land grants and hereditary obligation with more direct mechanisms for securing loyalty. Hostage arrangements, in which family members of subordinate or allied warriors resided at a daimyō's headquarters, provided a tangible check against betrayal, particularly in relationships with recently submitted or only partially trusted vassals; such arrangements appear repeatedly in the alliance politics among houses such as the Takeda, Imagawa, and Hōjō discussed in Section 35.9. Written oaths, sometimes sworn before deities and accompanied by religious sanctions for violation, formalized obligations of loyalty in ways that carried real weight within the period's own belief systems, even though such oaths, like any other instrument of political trust, were broken when circumstances made betrayal sufficiently advantageous.

Students should resist imagining Sengoku retainer bands as bureaucratic employees of a modern-style state, bound by impersonal contract and interchangeable within an administrative hierarchy. Vassalage in this period remained fundamentally personal, rooted in kinship, marriage, hereditary obligation, and direct relationships of trust and reciprocal benefit between a lord and his followers. Even the most systematically organized domains, such as the Later Hōjō and Takeda examined in Section 35.9, continued to rely on these personal bonds as the ultimate foundation of loyalty, using administrative tools — surveys, law codes, hostage arrangements, and assessed obligations — to reinforce and make more predictable a relationship that remained, at its core, a matter of personal fealty rather than impersonal office.

Section 35.6

Land, Agriculture, Taxation, and Revenue

Sustained territorial rule, and the sustained warfare that so often accompanied it, required predictable resources, and no resource mattered more than agricultural land and the revenue it could generate. The economic foundation of nearly every Sengoku domain rested on rice cultivation and the dues, in kind or in cash equivalent, that villages and individual cultivators owed to whichever authority controlled the land in question. Converting the tangled inheritance of shōen-era proprietary rights, discussed in Section 35.3, into a reliable and legible system of assessment and collection was therefore one of the central administrative challenges facing any daimyō who wished to govern effectively rather than merely claim authority in name.

Some domains pursued this challenge through direct, systematic survey. The Later Hōjō, ruling the Kantō region from their headquarters at Odawara, conducted cadastral surveys (kenchō) of villages under their control, compiling detailed registers of landholding, productive capacity, and the specific dues owed by named households and communities. These surveys allowed the Hōjō administration to assess revenue with a precision that earlier estate-based arrangements had rarely achieved, and to identify quickly when local officials or intermediaries were skimming revenue that should have reached the daimyō's own treasury. It is worth noting explicitly, and this textbook does so deliberately, that these Hōjō surveys anticipate in important ways the far more extensive nationwide cadastral surveys that Toyotomi Hideyoshi would later conduct, examined fully in Chapter 40; the Hōjō achievement was real but regional, a foundation on which later, more sweeping projects would build rather than a fully realized precursor to them.

Other domains relied more heavily on negotiated arrangements with existing village organizations and local intermediaries, confirming customary dues and adjusting them incrementally rather than undertaking comprehensive resurvey. Assessed values were frequently expressed in kandaka, a monetary valuation of a landholding's productive capacity that could be used consistently to calculate both the dues owed to the daimyō and the military service obligations of retainers holding land in fief, discussed in Section 35.5. This system offered daimyō a workable, if imperfect, common currency for administering an otherwise bewildering variety of local landholding arrangements inherited from the estate system.

Revenue from sources beyond agricultural land also mattered considerably, particularly for domains with active commercial centers, ports, or roads. Tolls collected at barrier stations (sekisho) along major roads and river crossings generated income but also, when excessive or arbitrarily multiplied, provoked resentment from merchants and travelers; some daimyō, recognizing the commercial benefits of freer movement, abolished or reduced such tolls within their own territories, a policy that Nobunaga would later pursue much more aggressively as part of his commercial reforms, examined in Chapter 39. Market privileges, including policies that historians describe using the term rakuichi ("free market"), which reduced guild monopolies and tolls to encourage commercial activity in castle towns, appear in several domains during this period, again anticipating policies that later unifiers would apply more systematically and on a larger scale.

Financing sustained military campaigns placed heavy and continuous demands on domain finances, and daimyō who mismanaged this balance — overtaxing cultivators to the point of provoking flight or unrest, or failing to reward retainers adequately for the burdens of service — risked undermining the very foundations of their power. The relationship between revenue extraction and political stability recurs throughout this chapter's treatment of villages and negotiated rule in Section 35.8, and it underscores a point worth stating plainly: Sengoku territorial power was not simply seized and held by force. It had to be paid for, continuously, out of resources that ordinary cultivators produced and that daimyō administrations had to assess, collect, and manage with reasonable competence to remain viable at all.

Section 35.7

Daimyō Law and Domain Administration

One of the clearest markers of Sengoku territorial governance is the emergence of domain law codes, known collectively as bunkokuhō — literally, "laws of the divided provinces." A number of the period's most prominent daimyō houses, including the Date, Imagawa, Takeda, Chōsokabe, and several others, issued written codes of rules governing conduct within their territories, and the survival of several of these documents gives historians unusually direct access to how daimyō understood their own authority and the practical problems they considered most urgent to regulate.

The content of these codes varied by house and by period, but recurring themes appear across nearly all of them. Rules governing retainer conduct addressed matters such as private quarrels among vassals, unauthorized alliances or marriages with families outside the domain (which might threaten a daimyō's control over his own retainer band's loyalties), gambling, and breaches of military discipline. Rules governing land tenure and inheritance sought to prevent disputes over landholding from escalating into violence among retainers, establishing procedures for adjudicating competing claims and, in some codes, restricting a retainer's ability to alienate landed income without the daimyō's approval. Rules governing debt, including provisions for periodic debt cancellation edicts (tokusei) that had precedent in earlier Muromachi-era practice, addressed the recurring problem of indebted retainers or cultivators falling into arrangements that threatened either their capacity for military service or their basic economic survival. Rules governing markets, road use, and public order extended the daimyō's claimed authority into the everyday commercial and social life of castle towns and surrounding villages. And rules addressing the treatment of religious institutions attempted to regulate, without necessarily eliminating, the independent landholding and political influence of temples and shrines within a domain's territory.

It would be a serious historical error to describe the emergence of bunkokuhō as the arrival of a modern legal system in Japan, and this textbook explicitly avoids that framing. These codes were not comprehensive legislative programs enacted by a representative body and applied uniformly through an independent judiciary. They were, rather, expressions of a daimyō's claimed authority to settle disputes and regulate conduct within a territory he controlled, issued unilaterally, subject to revision at the daimyō's discretion, and enforced through the same personal and military mechanisms — retainer loyalty, garrisoned strongholds, the daimyō's own household administration — that sustained every other dimension of Sengoku rule. Several codes explicitly acknowledge their own limitations, addressing specific recurring problems rather than attempting comprehensive coverage of every conceivable dispute, and some codes were revised or supplemented by successive generations of the same ruling house as circumstances changed.

What matters historically is not that bunkokuhō achieved anything resembling a modern rule of law, but that regional rulers increasingly claimed, and in practice exercised, the authority to issue binding rules and adjudicate disputes within territories they controlled — authority that, under the earlier Muromachi order, had been at least formally reserved to higher powers, however imperfectly those higher powers had actually exercised it. Historical Vocabulary
Bunkokuhō

Bunkokuhō ("laws of the divided provinces") refers to the domain law codes issued by individual Sengoku daimyō houses during the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries. Surviving examples include the Imagawa house's Kana Mokuroku (1526, with articles added by Imagawa Yoshimoto in 1553), the Date house's Jinkaishū (1536), and the Takeda house's Kōshū Hatto no Shidai (1547), among others.

These codes typically addressed retainer discipline, land tenure and inheritance disputes, debt, market regulation, and, in some cases, religious institutions. They were issued unilaterally by a daimyō rather than through any representative or judicial process, and their immediate enforcement rested on the daimyō's own claimed and exercised territorial power rather than on any higher legal or constitutional source — even though their actual contents frequently drew upon older legal traditions, such as the Kamakura-period Goseibai Shikimoku, and upon the precedents and local custom already circulating among neighboring houses; the Takeda code, for instance, drew in part on earlier legal traditions and on Imagawa law from a neighboring domain. Students should understand bunkokuhō as evidence of increasingly assertive regional governance built upon inherited legal custom, not as evidence of a wholly new or a modern legal order.

Section 35.8

Villages, Local Warriors, Temples, and Negotiated Rule

It would badly distort the history of this period to describe Sengoku daimyō rule as something simply imposed from above upon a passive rural population. Villages in Sengoku Japan frequently possessed real collective organization and real capacity to act in their own interest, whether by petitioning a lord for relief from excessive dues, by relocating cultivators to escape an especially exploitative administration, by organizing collective resistance when pressed too far, or, more routinely, by negotiating the specific terms — the amount and form of dues, the scope of exemptions, the handling of disputes — under which they would accept a particular daimyō's authority. Daimyō who wished to extract reliable revenue and stable military manpower from the countryside had strong practical incentives to bargain with village communities rather than attempting to rule by naked coercion alone, and the confirmation-of-privilege practices discussed in Section 35.5 in the context of local warriors had close parallels in a daimyō's dealings with village institutions as well.

Local warrior families, the kokujin introduced in Section 35.1, presented a related but distinct challenge. Unlike ordinary cultivators, kokujin possessed their own armed retinues, their own fortified residences, and, frequently, generations of accumulated local authority and prestige within their home districts. A daimyō seeking to incorporate such a family into his domain typically could not simply dispossess them without provoking costly resistance; more commonly, he confirmed their existing local rights and privileges in exchange for their military service and formal submission, a negotiated arrangement rather than a conquest in the fullest sense. Some kokujin families integrated so successfully into a daimyō's retainer band that, over succeeding generations, they became indistinguishable from hereditary vassals; others retained enough independent standing to switch allegiance when a rival power seemed more promising, a persistent source of instability that daimyō governments had to manage continuously rather than resolve once and for all.

Religious institutions posed a still more formidable challenge to daimyō authority in some regions, and nowhere more dramatically than in the case of the Ikkō-ikki, religious leagues organized around Jōdo Shinshū congregations that combined intense devotional commitment with substantial organizational and military capacity. In Kaga Province, Ikkō-ikki forces overthrew the province's shugo governor in the late fifteenth century and governed the province independently, without daimyō rule in the conventional sense, for approximately a century, an arrangement sometimes described, with some exaggeration but real underlying substance, as "a province run by peasants." Elsewhere, fortified temple complexes and their affiliated congregations could field substantial armed followings, control significant landholdings, and negotiate with neighboring daimyō from a position of genuine independent strength, resisting incorporation for extended periods before eventual defeat, negotiated settlement, or, in some notable later cases examined in Chapter 39, violent suppression at Nobunaga's hands.

The larger point this section establishes is that Sengoku state-building was not a one-directional process imposed by daimyō upon an inert countryside. It was, rather, a continuous negotiation among multiple centers of social and military power — daimyō households, hereditary retainers, kokujin local warriors, village communities, and religious institutions — in which daimyō generally possessed significant advantages in scale and organization but rarely possessed anything approaching unchecked authority. Understanding Sengoku territorial rule as negotiated rather than simply imposed is essential to avoiding the anachronistic image of a modern, fully sovereign state that this textbook has repeatedly cautioned against.

Section 35.9

Regional Competition and Different Paths to Power

No single template captures how Sengoku territorial states actually formed, because geography, inherited institutions, economic resources, and the particular configuration of local rivals varied enormously from region to region. This section examines several well-documented regional cases, chosen not because they exhaust the period's diversity but because, taken together, they illustrate several distinct paths to territorial power.

The Later Hōjō of the Kantō region offer perhaps the clearest example of a domain built substantially from the ground up. Hōjō Sōun, born Ise Shinkurō (also known as Ise Moritoki), belonged to a branch of the Ise lineage with ties to Muromachi shogunal service; older popular accounts cast him as a socially obscure adventurer or even a masterless wanderer, but more recent scholarship has revised this picture considerably. What remains true, and what makes him a valuable example for this chapter, is that he was not an established Kantō shugo magnate: he built new territorial authority largely from outside the region's existing power structure rather than inheriting it. He established control over Izu Province around 1493 and expanded steadily into Sagami and, eventually, much of the surrounding Kantō region over the following decades under his successors, who adopted the name "Hōjō" — unrelated to the medieval Hōjō regents examined in earlier chapters — to claim a prestigious historical association with the region. The Later Hōjō became particularly well known for administrative sophistication: the systematic cadastral surveys discussed in Section 35.6, careful management of castle town commerce at their headquarters in Odawara, and a reputation, reflected in surviving records, for comparatively moderate taxation that helped secure the loyalty of villages within their expanding domain. Their example demonstrates that a warrior family without standing as an established regional shugo house could nonetheless construct one of the period's more durable and effectively administered territorial states.

The Takeda of Kai Province, by contrast, descended from an established warrior lineage with roots extending back to the Kamakura period, and their most famous ruler, Takeda Shingen, inherited a domain already possessing meaningful institutional foundations. Kai's mountainous, relatively poor agricultural terrain posed distinct challenges that shaped Takeda administration, including significant investment in flood control and land reclamation projects alongside the more familiar apparatus of retainer organization and domain law reflected in the Kōshū Hatto discussed in Section 35.7. The Takeda domain's prolonged rivalry with the Uesugi house of Echigo Province under Uesugi Kenshin, marked by repeated campaigns and a famous series of confrontations at Kawanakajima, illustrates how sustained competition between comparably matched neighboring powers could shape both houses' military and administrative development over decades without either achieving decisive victory — a pattern Chapter 36 will examine further from the standpoint of military technique.

The Mōri house of western Honshu illustrates a third pattern: expansion achieved primarily through calculated alliance, marriage politics, and opportunistic warfare rather than either inherited shugo prestige or a single founder's rise from outside an established regional power structure in the manner of Hōjō Sōun. Mōri Motonari, ruling initially over a locally rooted domain in Aki Province, expanded his family's territory in stages over the mid-sixteenth century. He first defeated Sue Harukata, who had already overthrown the Ōuchi house's own ruler, at the naval battle of Itsukushima in 1555, and went on to dismantle the remnants of the Ōuchi regime by 1557; only afterward, over the following decade, did he turn to the piecemeal conquest of the rival Amago house, achieving its decisive defeat in the 1560s. Throughout this extended campaign, Motonari skillfully managed alliances and rivalries among neighboring warrior families, eventually establishing Mōri dominance over much of the Chūgoku region. His success demonstrates that careful diplomacy, strategic patience, and a willingness to defeat rivals in sequence rather than all at once could, over a sufficiently long career, achieve territorial consolidation on a scale that matched or exceeded domains built on more sudden military conquest alone.

Finally, the Shimazu house of Satsuma and southern Kyushu illustrates how regional geography shaped the specific character of territorial consolidation. An established warrior house with roots extending back to the Kamakura period, the Shimazu consolidated authority over Satsuma, Ōsumi, and eventually much of southern Kyushu during the sixteenth century, in a region whose maritime connections to the Ryukyu Kingdom and broader East Asian trading networks, examined in earlier chapters on Ryukyu and Muromachi commerce, gave Kyushu domains economic opportunities and external relationships that inland Honshu domains such as the Takeda simply did not share. Kyushu's relative geographic distance from Kyoto and the central battlegrounds of the Sengoku period also meant that regional powers there developed along a somewhat different timeline and with different external pressures than domains closer to the political center.

Taken together, these cases demonstrate that "Sengoku daimyō" describes a functional category achieved by remarkably different routes: inherited prestige intensified through effective administration, authority built from outside an established regional power structure through systematic territorial development, patient alliance-building punctuated by decisive warfare fought in careful sequence, and consolidation shaped by distinct regional geography and external connections. No single narrative of rise, whether gekokujō or dynastic continuity, adequately captures this diversity, and students should resist the temptation to flatten these varied trajectories into a single Sengoku "type."

Section 35.10

Fragmented Japan, Stronger Domains

This chapter opened with an apparent paradox: that Japan as a whole, during the Sengoku period, lacked anything resembling effective central government, while many individual domains developed forms of territorial administration more intensive and more capable than those the provinces of Ashikaga Japan had generally sustained a century earlier. Having examined how daimyō built territorial domains, organized castles and retainers, assessed land and revenue, issued binding law, and negotiated continuously with villages, local warriors, and religious institutions, students are now in a position to understand why this apparent contradiction is not really a contradiction at all.

Political fragmentation at the national scale and political intensification at the regional scale were, in large part, two faces of the same underlying process. The collapse of Ashikaga central authority removed the mechanisms — however imperfect they had been — that had once allowed provincial governors to rule with comparative laxity, delegating administration to deputies and relying on inherited prestige rather than continuous, exacting oversight of their own territories. Once that central check disappeared, survival itself came to depend on precisely the administrative capacities this chapter has traced: reliable revenue, dependable retainers, defensible strongholds, and functioning mechanisms for resolving disputes before they escalated into costly internal conflict. Domains that failed to develop these capacities were absorbed by rivals that succeeded in developing them. Sustained military and political competition, in other words, selected relentlessly for administrative effectiveness, producing over several generations a landscape of increasingly well-organized regional states even as the country as a whole remained divided among dozens of competing powers.

This dynamic also explains why the eventual reunification examined in Chapters 39 through 41 became achievable in the way that it did. Oda Nobunaga, Toyotomi Hideyoshi, and Tokugawa Ieyasu did not invent territorial administration, domain law, systematic land assessment, or organized retainer bands; they inherited these tools, already developed and tested across dozens of competing domains over the preceding century, and applied them at an unprecedented scale as they absorbed one territorial state after another into an increasingly unified political order. The administrative techniques the Later Hōjō pioneered in the Kantō, the retainer discipline the Takeda codified in their domain law, and the market and toll policies that several daimyō experimented with on a regional scale all found their way, in modified and expanded form, into the nationwide projects of the unification period. Reunification, in this sense, was less a rejection of Sengoku fragmentation than its logical culmination: the eventual absorption of many well-organized regional states into a single, still larger one.

None of this was inevitable, and none of it happened without immense human cost, contingency, and continued resistance from villages, religious institutions, and rival houses at every stage. The chapters that follow will trace how sustained military competition transformed the actual conduct of war (Chapter 36), how new religious and commercial forces from beyond Japan's shores entered this competitive landscape (Chapter 37), how elite cultural practices such as the tea ceremony became entangled with Sengoku politics (Chapter 38), and how Nobunaga, Hideyoshi, and Ieyasu, in turn, built upon the foundations this chapter has described to bring an end, at last, to more than a century of political fragmentation. The governing question for Chapter 36 follows directly from everything examined here: how did sustained military competition among these increasingly organized territorial states reshape armies, fortifications, weapons, logistics, and the very conduct of battle itself?

Creator: Issued under the authority of Takeda Shingen and the Takeda house administration · Date: 1547, with later additions · Type: Domain law code (bunkokuhō) for the Takeda domain in Kai Province

Context: The Kōshū Hatto no Shidai, often referred to simply as the Kōshū Hatto, is among the most extensive surviving Sengoku domain law codes, comprising dozens of articles addressing retainer conduct, land tenure and inheritance disputes, taxation, criminal matters, and the daimyō's own claimed authority to serve as final arbiter of disputes among his vassals. Like other bunkokuhō discussed in Section 35.7, it was issued unilaterally by the ruling house rather than through any representative process, and it was revised over time as circumstances required.

Paraphrase of representative provisions (not a verbatim translation): The code addresses matters such as the settlement of boundary and inheritance disputes among retainers without resort to private violence, restrictions on retainers forming marriage alliances or other ties with outside houses without the daimyō's knowledge, penalties for retainers who abandon their assigned posts during military service, and the daimyō's reserved authority to review and overturn judgments made by his own subordinate officials when those judgments proved unjust.

Analysis: The code's recurring emphasis on preventing private warfare among retainers, regulating outside marriage alliances, and reserving final judicial authority to the daimyō himself reveals a ruler acutely concerned with two linked problems: the danger that internal retainer disputes might fracture the domain's cohesion, and the danger that individual retainers might develop external loyalties that could threaten the daimyō's own control. The code's detailed, almost granular attention to specific disputes suggests that these were not hypothetical concerns but recurring, practical problems the Takeda administration had actually confronted. At the same time, the code's dependence on the daimyō's personal authority for final enforcement, rather than any independent judicial institution, confirms this textbook's broader point that bunkokuhō represented an assertion of territorial lordship rather than the emergence of an impersonal legal system.

Historical Debate

Were Sengoku Domains "States"?

The question: Historians disagree over how far the vocabulary of "the state" should be applied to Sengoku daimyō domains. Some scholars find the comparison illuminating; others consider it potentially misleading. How should students evaluate this disagreement?

Interpretation A — Domains functioned as genuine territorial states: Some historians argue that Sengoku daimyō domains exhibited the essential features of territorial states in a meaningful comparative sense: defined, if fluctuating, territory; a recognized ruling authority; systematic taxation; codified law; and organized military forces capable of both defense and expansion. Under this view, using the vocabulary of statehood helps students appreciate the genuine administrative sophistication daimyō achieved and situates Japanese political development within broader global patterns of early modern state formation.
Interpretation B — "State" risks importing anachronistic assumptions: Other historians caution that the term "state," especially as commonly understood today, implies a degree of fixed territorial sovereignty, impersonal bureaucratic administration, and monopoly over legitimate violence that no Sengoku domain actually achieved. Authority remained personal rather than institutional, borders remained contested and shifting, and independent centers of power — kokujin, village communities, religious leagues — retained real autonomy that a fully sovereign state would not tolerate. Under this view, terms such as "territorial lordship" or "regional domain" more accurately capture the period's political reality without smuggling in assumptions drawn from later European or modern political theory.

Evidence: Both positions draw on the same body of evidence examined in this chapter — domain law codes, cadastral surveys, castle administration, retainer organization — but weigh differently how closely these institutions resembled the defining features of statehood as usually defined by political theorists.

Why it matters: How historians resolve this question shapes how they describe the relationship between Sengoku daimyō rule and the more centralized state structures that Hideyoshi and the Tokugawa shogunate would later build, examined in Chapters 40 through 42, and shapes broader comparative arguments about Japan's place within global patterns of early modern political development.

Student task: Using evidence from this chapter — including the Takeda law code excerpted above, the Later Hōjō's cadastral surveys, and the continued independent power of the Ikkō-ikki — argue for or against describing Sengoku daimyō domains as "states," and explain what is gained and what is risked by that choice of vocabulary.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From Muromachi Shugo Rule to Sengoku Territorial Lordship

What Changed

  • Provincial authority shifted from formal appointment by a functioning shogunate toward authority claimed and enforced independently by regional rulers
  • Land administration moved from the tangled, overlapping proprietary rights of the shōen system toward more direct, systematically surveyed and assessed territorial control in the more developed domains
  • Retainer relationships became more intensively documented and regulated, through land assessment (kandaka), domain law (bunkokuhō), and, in some cases, hostage arrangements
  • Castles evolved from one element among several forms of warrior residence into concentrated centers of administration, retainer organization, and, increasingly, urban commercial life through associated castle towns

What Stayed the Same

  • Authority remained fundamentally personal, rooted in kinship, marriage, and direct lord-vassal relationships rather than impersonal bureaucratic office
  • Villages and local warriors retained meaningful, if unequal, bargaining power and continued to shape the practical terms of the authority exercised over them
  • Religious institutions continued to hold significant landed wealth and, in some cases, independent military capacity
  • The imperial court and the office of shogun persisted as sources of ceremonial legitimacy, even as their practical governing power continued to decline

Why the pattern matters: Treating Sengoku territorial lordship as an intensification and reorganization of tools already present under Muromachi rule, rather than as an entirely new invention, helps students see the reunification of the later sixteenth century as building upon a century of regional institutional development rather than emerging from nothing.

Cause and Effect

From Fragmentation to Territorial States

Long-term causes

The gradual, centuries-long erosion of the shōen estate system and the corresponding growth of provincial warrior power had already been transferring practical authority over land from distant proprietors to local warrior families well before the Ōnin War.

Short-term causes

The Ōnin War (1467–1477) and the decades of continuing conflict that followed destroyed the Ashikaga shogunate's remaining capacity to arbitrate disputes among shugo houses, removing the central check that had previously constrained, however imperfectly, the pace and scale of provincial consolidation.

Effect

Regional warrior rulers, competing continuously for survival and advantage, built increasingly organized territorial domains, featuring systematic land assessment, codified domain law, disciplined retainer hierarchies, and administratively significant castle headquarters.

Long-term consequence

The administrative tools and organizational capacities developed across dozens of competing Sengoku domains provided the practical foundation on which Nobunaga, Hideyoshi, and Ieyasu would later build a unified national political order, examined in Chapters 39 through 42.

Historical complexity: The relationship between fragmentation and consolidation was not a simple, linear progression toward an inevitable reunification. Individual domains rose and fell throughout the period, alliances shifted repeatedly, and no single outcome was guaranteed at any given point; the eventual reunification represents one possible outcome among several that contemporaries could reasonably have imagined.

Everyday Life and Environment

Living under a Sengoku Lord

For ordinary cultivators, artisans, and merchants, the practical experience of Sengoku political consolidation was mixed and highly dependent on which domain, and which period within that domain's history, a person happened to live under. Taxation could be burdensome, particularly during periods of active military campaigning, when a daimyō's demand for rice, labor, and manpower for military service intensified sharply; villages sometimes petitioned for relief, relocated cultivators to escape especially harsh administrations, or, in more extreme cases, resisted collectively, as the recurring negotiations discussed in Section 35.8 illustrate. Military requisitions, including the corvée labor frequently demanded for castle construction, road maintenance, and the movement of military supplies, fell heavily on rural communities located near a domain's centers of military activity.

At the same time, a well-administered domain could offer real, if partial, benefits compared to the alternative of unchecked local violence: more predictable taxation than the shifting and sometimes conflicting demands of the overlapping shōen-era proprietors described in Section 35.3, more secure roads for merchants once a daimyō suppressed banditry and reduced excessive tolls, growing castle-town markets that created new opportunities for artisans and traders, and, in domains such as the Later Hōjō that pursued a deliberate policy of comparatively moderate taxation, a genuine degree of protection from the arbitrary predation of unchecked local warlords. Villages generally preferred, when they had any meaningful choice in the matter, a stable and moderately governed daimyō domain to a contested frontier where no single authority could guarantee security.

Students should therefore avoid two equally misleading extremes: imagining Sengoku consolidation as a story of uniform popular suffering under warlord oppression, or imagining it as a benevolent process that ordinary people welcomed without reservation. The evidence supports neither extreme. Daimyō rule brought real burdens and real, if unevenly distributed, benefits, and how any particular community experienced the Sengoku transition depended heavily on the specific domain, the specific period, and the specific balance a given daimyō administration struck between the competing pressures examined throughout this chapter.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how the political fragmentation that followed the Ōnin War, traced in Chapter 34, generated a remarkable and uneven process of regional state-building across the Japanese archipelago. Rather than treating the Sengoku period as an era of pure chaos awaiting rescue by later unifiers, this chapter has shown that many regional warrior rulers converted the collapse of central Ashikaga authority into an opportunity to build more intensively governed territorial domains than the Muromachi order had generally sustained at the provincial level.

Sengoku daimyō emerged through several distinct pathways rather than a single pattern. Some, such as the Takeda and Imagawa houses, descended from established shugo lineages that successfully adapted inherited authority to new circumstances. Others, exemplified most clearly by Hōjō Sōun and the Later Hōjō, rose from outside the established regional shugo power structure through the pattern historians call gekokujō, building new territorial authority where older structures had weakened. Still others, such as the Mōri, expanded through patient alliance-building and calculated warfare over an extended career, while regional cases such as the Shimazu in Kyushu demonstrate how distinct geography and external connections shaped the specific character of territorial consolidation.

Across these varied pathways, several common administrative capacities defined what it meant to be a Sengoku daimyō: the ability to organize retainers into more dependable hierarchies through land grants, assessed obligations, hostage arrangements, and, in some domains, systematic cadastral surveys; the ability to extract and manage revenue reliably enough to sustain continuous military competition; the authority to issue binding domain law, or bunkokuhō, addressing retainer conduct, land disputes, and public order; and the maintenance of castle headquarters that functioned as centers of administration and lordship as much as fortifications, a distinction this chapter has drawn deliberately in order to leave the military and architectural dimensions of castle-building to Chapter 36.

The chapter has been equally insistent that this process of territorial consolidation was negotiated rather than simply imposed. Villages retained real collective capacity to bargain over the terms of their obligations. Local warrior families, the kokujin, frequently entered daimyō service through negotiated confirmation of existing privileges rather than outright conquest. And religious institutions, most dramatically the Ikkō-ikki leagues that governed Kaga Province independently for roughly a century, could rival daimyō authority outright in certain regions and periods. Sengoku state-building, in short, involved continuous negotiation among multiple centers of social and military power, not a one-directional imposition of authority upon a passive countryside.

Regional case studies of the Later Hōjō, Takeda, Uesugi, Mōri, and Shimazu houses have illustrated the genuine diversity of paths to territorial power, shaped by differing inherited institutions, geography, economic resources, and patterns of local rivalry. No single template — neither gekokujō nor dynastic continuity — adequately explains this diversity, and the chapter has consistently resisted flattening these varied trajectories into a single Sengoku type.

The chapter's central argument resolves an apparent paradox that framed its opening: Japan as a whole grew more politically fragmented during this period even as many individual domains became more intensively and effectively governed. These two developments were not opposites but two faces of a single underlying process, in which the weakening of effective central Ashikaga oversight subjected regional rulers to a competitive pressure that selected relentlessly for administrative effectiveness. Domains that failed to develop reliable revenue, dependable retainer organization, and functioning legal authority were absorbed by domains that succeeded in developing these capacities.

This dynamic, in turn, explains why the reunification examined in the chapters that follow became achievable in the specific way that it did. Nobunaga, Hideyoshi, and Ieyasu did not invent territorial administration from nothing; they inherited and then applied, at an unprecedented national scale, administrative techniques and organizational capacities that dozens of competing Sengoku domains had already developed and tested over the preceding century. The Later Hōjō's cadastral surveys, the Takeda's codified domain law, and the market and toll policies several daimyō experimented with on a regional scale all anticipate, in more limited form, the nationwide projects examined in Chapters 39 through 42.

Finally, this chapter has deliberately preserved a strict boundary with Chapter 36. It has examined the political organization of military power — who commanded warriors, how retainers were bound to domains, how taxation sustained armies, and why castles functioned as centers of lordship — while leaving the mechanics of Sengoku warfare itself, including ashigaru infantry, fortification engineering, firearms, and battlefield organization, for the next chapter's direct treatment.

Students who understand the Sengoku daimyō as builders of increasingly sophisticated, if never fully centralized or uncontested, regional territorial states are well prepared to examine, in the chapters that follow, how sustained competition among these states transformed warfare itself, brought new religious and commercial forces into Japan's political landscape, and ultimately produced the reunification that closes this Part of the textbook.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Sengoku daimyō
A regional warrior ruler who exercised substantial, largely independent territorial authority, defined functionally by retainer organization, revenue administration, lawmaking capacity, and military defense rather than by fixed rank or office.
Shugo daimyō
A provincial governor of the earlier Ashikaga order whose formal authority derived from shogunal appointment; some evolved into Sengoku daimyō, while others were displaced by subordinates or rivals.
Shugodai
A deputy who administered a province on behalf of an often-absentee shugo governor; many shugodai built independent local power that outlasted their nominal superiors' authority.
Gekokujō
"The low overcoming the high"; a recurring pattern in which subordinates displaced the lords they had formerly served, though not a universal explanation for every Sengoku daimyō's rise.
Kokujin
Locally rooted warrior families with independent landholdings and followings, whose submission, resistance, or negotiated incorporation shaped the actual extent of a daimyō's authority.
Kashindan
A daimyō's organized retainer band, combining hereditary vassals, newly submitted local warriors, and kin bound by marriage or adoption.
Bunkokuhō
Domain law codes issued unilaterally by individual Sengoku daimyō houses, addressing retainer conduct, land tenure, debt, and public order.
Kenchō
Cadastral land surveys, most notably conducted by the Later Hōjō, establishing detailed registers of landholding and revenue capacity.
Kandaka
A monetary assessment of a landholding's productive capacity, used to calculate both dues owed to a daimyō and the military service obligations of retainers holding land in fief.
Ikkō-ikki
Religious leagues organized around Jōdo Shinshū congregations, capable of independent territorial governance, most notably in Kaga Province for approximately a century.
Ando (confirmation of privilege)
A daimyō's formal confirmation of a local warrior's or village's existing rights and privileges in exchange for submission and service.
Jōkamachi (castle town)
An urban settlement developed around a daimyō's principal castle, concentrating retainers, merchants, and artisans near the seat of lordship.
Honjō (castle headquarters)
A daimyō's principal fortified residence and administrative center, from which territorial governance was coordinated.
Sekisho (toll barrier)
A checkpoint along a road or river crossing where tolls were collected; some daimyō reduced or abolished such tolls to encourage commerce.
Rakuichi (free market policy)
Policies reducing guild monopolies and commercial tolls to stimulate market activity, especially in castle towns.
Tokusei (debt cancellation edict)
A decree canceling or reducing debts, addressing recurring economic distress among retainers or cultivators; a practice with precedent in earlier Muromachi governance.
Hostage politics
The practice of housing family members of subordinate or allied warriors at a daimyō's headquarters to secure loyalty and deter betrayal.
Territorial domain
The recognized, though often fluctuating and contested, sphere of a daimyō's practical territorial authority.
Shōen (estate)
The medieval landed estate system, characterized by overlapping proprietary rights, whose gradual erosion Sengoku daimyō completed by converting scattered rights into more direct territorial control.
Negotiated authority
The historically accurate understanding of daimyō rule as continuously bargained with villages, local warriors, and religious institutions, rather than simply imposed upon them.
Later Hōjō (Go-Hōjō)
The house founded by Hōjō Sōun, ruling the Kantō region from Odawara and noted for systematic cadastral surveys and administrative sophistication.
Takeda house
The ruling house of Kai Province, noted for the detailed Kōshū Hatto domain law code and its prolonged rivalry with the Uesugi.
Uesugi house
The ruling house of Echigo Province under Uesugi Kenshin, chief rival of the Takeda in central and northern Honshu.
Mōri house
A western Honshu house that expanded dramatically under Mōri Motonari through alliance, marriage politics, and conquest.
Shimazu house
An established warrior house that consolidated authority over Satsuma, Ōsumi, and much of southern Kyushu.

Major People, Houses, and Places

Identification & Significance

Hōjō Sōun (Ise Shinkurō)
Founder of the Later Hōjō house; established control over Izu Province around 1493, beginning one of the Sengoku period's most systematically administered domains.

Ruler of the Takeda domain in Kai Province; associated with the detailed Kōshū Hatto domain law code and the prolonged rivalry with Uesugi Kenshin.

Issuer of the Jinkaishū (1536), an early and influential bunkokuhō domain law code in northeastern Honshu.

Ruling house of Suruga and Tōtōmi; Imagawa Ujichika issued the Imagawa Kana Mokuroku domain law code in 1526, later expanded by Imagawa Yoshimoto in 1553; a significant regional power along the Tōkaidō route.
Odawara
Principal castle headquarters and castle town of the Later Hōjō domain in the Kantō region.
Kai Province
Mountainous home province of the Takeda domain, whose challenging agricultural terrain shaped distinctive administrative priorities.
Kawanakajima
Site of a famous series of inconclusive battles between the Takeda and Uesugi domains during their prolonged mid-sixteenth-century rivalry.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has carried forward the story of political fragmentation introduced in Chapter 34 and shown it to be, in important respects, also a story of political construction. The Sengoku daimyō who emerged from the wreckage of Ashikaga central authority were not merely warlords presiding over chaos; they were, in their most successful and best-documented forms, builders of increasingly sophisticated regional governments, grappling with the same fundamental problems of taxation, retainer loyalty, legal authority, and territorial control that governments have confronted in many times and places, adapted to the specific pressures and possibilities of sixteenth-century Japan.

The chapter's insistence on negotiated rather than simply imposed authority matters beyond its immediate subject. It demonstrates that even in a period defined by warfare and competition, ordinary villages, local warriors, and religious communities retained real historical agency, shaping the terms on which daimyō authority actually operated rather than simply receiving it. Students should carry this lesson into later chapters of this textbook, particularly those addressing the more centralized authority of the unification period and the Tokugawa shogunate, where similar questions about the negotiated, incomplete, and contested character of even seemingly powerful political authority will recur in new forms.

The chapter has also planted an important seed for the remainder of Part VI: the recognition that reunification, examined in Chapters 39 through 41, did not spring from nothing but built directly upon administrative tools and organizational capacities that dozens of competing Sengoku domains had already developed and tested over the preceding century. This complexity anticipates the textbook's continuing insistence that major historical transformations rarely emerge from a single individual's genius alone, but from long processes of institutional development, contested and uneven, upon which particular individuals are eventually able to build.

Students who understand the Sengoku daimyō as builders of genuine, if incomplete and contested, territorial governments are well prepared to move forward into Chapter 36, where the military transformation that sustained and was sustained by these territorial states — ashigaru infantry, fortification engineering, firearms, and changing battlefield organization — receives the detailed treatment this chapter has deliberately reserved for it.
