The Black Ships and the Crisis of the Shogunate

Japanese History: From Jōmon Japan to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

On the eighth day of July in 1853, four warships under the flag of the United States entered the waters off Uraga, at the mouth of Edo Bay. Two of them moved against the wind under steam power, trailing dark smoke; all four had hulls painted black. For the people who watched from the shore, and for the officials who hurried to meet the vessels in small guard boats, the ships were an unmistakable fact long before anyone could say what they meant.

The commander of the squadron, Commodore Matthew C. Perry of the United States Navy, carried a letter from President Millard Fillmore addressed to the ruler of Japan. He had been instructed to seek protection for shipwrecked American sailors, access to supplies and coal for American vessels crossing the Pacific, and a framework for continuing relations. He also carried, as his own preference, a conviction that a display of force would accomplish what quiet requests had failed to accomplish in the past. Local officials told him that foreign business was to be handled at Nagasaki. Perry declined to go there. He insisted on delivering the President's letter to a representative of the highest authority he could reach, and he let it be understood that if he were refused he would take his ships farther up the bay toward the capital.

What happened next is one of the most familiar episodes in modern Japanese history, and also one of the most easily misunderstood. In popular memory the Black Ships (kurofune) sometimes appear as a single dramatic stroke by which a sleeping, isolated country was awakened by a foreign power and propelled, almost at once, into the modern world. Historians have long been wary of that picture. Tokugawa Japan was not sealed off from the world, as Chapter 48 explained. Its officials had followed Western maritime activity for decades, had received repeated warnings from Dutch traders at Nagasaki that an American expedition was coming, and had already revised their policy toward foreign ships more than once. Perry's arrival did not introduce Japan to the problem of foreign pressure. It forced the shogunate to answer a problem it had been trying to postpone.

This chapter therefore asks a question that is broader than diplomacy. How did the Tokugawa shogunate's efforts to manage foreign pressure transform an international diplomatic problem into a crisis of domestic political authority? The answer unfolds across the years 1853 to 1857, though its roots reach back to the Opium War and earlier encounters. The shogunate negotiated, delayed, conceded limited access, and invested in defense, and in doing so it also consulted the daimyō more widely than it ever had, informed the imperial court, and discovered that decisions about foreign policy could no longer be treated as the private business of a few senior councilors in Edo. Every one of these steps was defensible at the moment it was taken. Together they loosened the political settlement on which Tokugawa rule depended.

The chapter also insists on the plural nature of the story. Perry's squadron visited the Ryukyu Kingdom and the Ogasawara (Bonin) Islands before it reached Uraga, and it concluded a separate agreement with Ryukyuan authorities in 1854. Russian, British, and Dutch representatives pursued their own negotiations, and the Russian treaty of 1855 drew a boundary through the northern islands of the Kuril chain where Ainu communities had lived and traded for centuries. Coastal villages and port towns such as Uraga, Shimoda, and Hakodate absorbed the practical consequences of the treaties. Daimyō with long coastlines paid for fortifications. The Japanese people who encountered the foreigners were not a single audience, and the chapter attempts to show how differently shogunal officials, domain lords, coastal residents, and Ryukyuan authorities experienced the same events.

The evidence for these events is unusually rich and unusually partisan. American accounts include the official narrative of the expedition compiled from Perry's journals and the lithographs and paintings of artists attached to the squadron. Japanese evidence includes the correspondence and reports of shogunal officials, the written opinions the shogunate solicited from the daimyō, domain and local records, diaries, printed broadsheets (kawaraban), and woodblock images of the ships and their crews. Dutch records from Dejima, Russian reports, British correspondence, and Ryukyuan materials add further angles of view. Historians use all of them with caution, because each was written to explain, persuade, or justify, and the later stories told about the Black Ships often say more about what later generations wanted to remember than about what happened in 1853.

Students should keep several boundaries in mind. Chapter 50 examined the intellectual, social, and economic conditions that preceded the crisis, including Dutch learning and the fiscal and political strains of the late Tokugawa period. The present chapter follows foreign pressure, the first agreements, military vulnerability, and the earliest domestic political responses. Chapter 52 will take up the 1858 commercial treaties, the treaty ports, the politics of imperial loyalism and anti-foreign activism, and the violence that followed. Chapter 53 will narrate the eventual overthrow of the shogunate. Perry did not single-handedly cause the Meiji Restoration, and this chapter does not claim that he did. It ends with a government that is still functioning, still adapting, and still capable of acting, but confronted by diplomatic and institutional pressures that its existing arrangements were poorly designed to resolve.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Describe the late Tokugawa governing structure and explain why foreign policy rested formally with the shogunate while the legitimacy of the system rested partly on imperial and domain relationships.
  2. Explain why describing Tokugawa Japan before 1853 as "isolated" is inadequate, using evidence from Nagasaki, Tsushima, Ryukyu, Matsumae, and the Ainu trade.
  3. Trace the sequence of foreign approaches from the Russian and British encounters of the early nineteenth century through the Biddle mission of 1846, and assess how shogunal maritime policy changed.
  4. Analyze how the Opium War, American Pacific expansion, steam navigation, and the China trade shaped both Japanese threat assessments and American objectives.
  5. Reconstruct the events at Uraga in July 1853 and evaluate the roles of Perry, the Uraga magistrates, and the interpreters.
  6. Evaluate the strategic options available to Abe Masahiro and the shogunal council, and explain why the choice was not a simple one between "opening" and "isolation."
  7. Interpret the provisions of the Treaty of Kanagawa and distinguish it from the commercial treaties examined in Chapter 52.
  8. Compare the American, British, Russian, Dutch, and Ryukyuan agreements of 1854–1857, noting what each reveals about the geography of foreign pressure.
  9. Assess the military, fiscal, and institutional consequences of the crisis, including coastal fortification, naval training, and the broader consultation of the daimyō.
  10. Distinguish documented Japanese reactions to the Black Ships from later legends of national panic, and explain how Perry's agency, Japanese diplomatic agency, and coercion all belong in an accurate account.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How did the shogunate's established framework for foreign relations both allow and limit its response to the American demands of 1853?
  2. Why did a diplomatic encounter at Uraga produce consequences for the relationship among the shogun, the daimyō, and the imperial court?
  3. To what extent did the shogunate's choices in 1853–1854 reflect weakness, and to what extent did they reflect calculated strategy?
  4. What did the treaties of 1854–1855 actually grant to foreign powers, and what did they not grant?
  5. How should historians evaluate the phrase "opening of Japan," given Japanese agency, American coercion, and the continuities with earlier regulated foreign relations?
  6. How did the Ryukyu Kingdom, the Ainu communities of the northern islands, and the port towns of Shimoda and Hakodate experience the new treaty environment differently from officials in Edo?
  7. What kinds of evidence allow historians to reconstruct the events of 1853–1857, and whose perspectives does each kind of evidence represent or omit?
  8. Why is it misleading to treat Perry's arrival as the single cause of the collapse of the Tokugawa order?

Historical Context

The Tokugawa shogunate, founded after the battle of Sekigahara in 1600 and the Tokugawa settlement described in Chapter 41, governed through a system historians call bakuhan, the combination of the shogunal government (bakufu) and some two hundred and sixty semi-autonomous domains (han). Chapter 42 examined this system in detail. For present purposes the essential point is that the shogun commanded the largest territorial base and the highest military authority, controlled the major cities and mines, and held responsibility for relations with foreign countries, but did not rule the domains directly. Daimyō governed their own territories, maintained their own retainers, and owed the shogun loyalty, service, and periodic residence in Edo. Within the shogunate, policy was managed by a council of senior councilors (rōjū) drawn from hereditary (fudai) daimyō houses. The outside (tozama) daimyō, descended from lords who had accepted Tokugawa leadership only after Sekigahara, such as those of Satsuma and Chōshū, were traditionally excluded from the shogunate's central offices.

The imperial court in Kyoto occupied a distinctive and often misunderstood place. The emperor had no administrative or military role in day-to-day government, but the court conferred the titles, including that of shōgun itself, through which Tokugawa rule was formally legitimized. For most of the Tokugawa period this relationship was stable and rarely tested. Shogunal officials in Kyoto monitored the court, and the court was not normally consulted on diplomatic matters. That convention would come under strain in 1853. Chapter 50 traced the intellectual developments that were slowly changing how educated samurai thought about the court, the domains, and Japan's place in the world, including Mito scholarship and the study of Dutch learning (rangaku).

The shogunate entered the 1850s with real institutional strengths and real fiscal and political difficulties. Its reform programs of the early nineteenth century, including the Tenpō reforms of the early 1840s, had mixed results. Agricultural and urban crises, the strain on samurai finances, and large-scale rural unrest recorded in earlier chapters weakened confidence without breaking it. Domains were not uniform. Some, such as Satsuma, Chōshū, Saga, Mito, and Echizen, had undertaken reforms that strengthened their administration and finances. Others were heavily indebted. These differences shaped how individual daimyō responded when asked for their opinions in 1853.

Foreign relations, meanwhile, operated through a set of regulated channels rather than a policy of total closure. The Dutch trading post at Dejima in Nagasaki and Chinese merchants at Nagasaki maintained commerce under supervision. Korea sent periodic embassies and traded through the Sō domain on Tsushima. The Ryukyu Kingdom, which was both subordinate to Satsuma and a tributary of Qing China, maintained its own diplomatic and commercial position. The Matsumae domain managed trade with Ainu communities and with the northern world beyond Ezo. This chapter begins where those arrangements encountered a new kind of pressure: steam-powered Western warships operated by a state with global commercial ambitions and a Pacific coastline.

I. The Tokugawa Order Confronts an Expanding Maritime World

The first four sections establish what the Tokugawa government was, what it believed about foreign relations, and what it had already learned from earlier encounters before 1853.

Section 51.1

The Tokugawa Political Order on the Eve of the Crisis

By the middle of the nineteenth century the shogunate had governed for more than two and a half centuries, an extraordinary span that shaped how its officials understood their own authority. In the 1840s and early 1850s the senior councilors in Edo were men who had inherited both a body of precedent and a set of institutional habits. Decisions were prepared by subordinate officials, deliberated in council, and issued in the name of the shogun. The system placed a premium on consensus within a narrow circle, on adherence to precedent, and on avoiding open disagreement. These were strengths in a stable environment. They were liabilities when a problem required rapid, visible, and unprecedented decisions.

Foreign affairs had long been treated as a prerogative of the shogunate, but the shogunate's capacity to execute policy depended on the domains. In the event of a coastal emergency, the shogunate relied on daimyō to provide troops, garrison coastal batteries, and bear the cost. Since the late 1840s, the shogunate had assigned responsibility for the defense of Edo Bay to a small group of major domains, including Aizu and Hikone on the Sagami coast and Kawagoe and Oshi on the Bōsō side, which meant that the practical defense of the capital's waterways rested on forces that answered to their own lords. This was an administrative arrangement that functioned adequately for ordinary patrols but had never been tested by an adversary armed with steam vessels and shell guns.

Imperial legitimacy formed a quieter but important background. The shogunate's authority was formally derived from imperial appointment, and the shogun's claim to govern "all under heaven" on the emperor's behalf was an assumption of political life. The court itself had grown somewhat more assertive in the early nineteenth century, as the court nobility and a number of scholars cultivated renewed interest in its history and role, a trend traced in Chapter 50. Yet before 1853, the court did not ordinarily intervene in foreign policy, and the shogunate did not ordinarily inform it of diplomatic business. The decision to depart from that pattern would be one of the most consequential of the crisis.

Finally, political tensions already existed within the elite. The three collateral houses of Owari, Kii, and Mito (the gosanke) held a special status as branches of the Tokugawa family. Within that group, the domain of Mito, led by Tokugawa Nariaki, had developed a tradition of scholarship and political commentary that combined reverence for the court with urgency about maritime defense. Tozama domains such as Satsuma and Chōshū, with substantial resources and growing administrative competence, were increasingly interested in questions of national policy. None of this amounted to open opposition to the Tokugawa in 1853. It did mean that when the shogunate asked for opinions, there were people with strong views ready to give them.

Section 51.2

Regulated Foreign Relations and the Limits of Sakoku

The shogunate's foreign policy in 1853 is often summarized in a single word, sakoku, usually translated as "closed country." The word is a later coinage and conveys more rigidity than the historical arrangements possessed. Seventeenth-century Tokugawa policy had restricted Japanese travel abroad, prohibited Christianity, and limited the Europeans permitted to trade, but it had never abolished foreign relations. What it created was a system of regulated channels in which the shogunate or a designated domain controlled each point of contact.

At Nagasaki, Dutch traders at Dejima and Chinese merchants in a separate quarter conducted commerce under magistrate supervision. The Dutch were required to deliver annual reports, known as oranda fūsetsugaki, summarizing world events for the shogunate. Through these reports, and through the community of interpreters and scholars who worked with Dutch texts, the shogunate maintained a steady stream of information about European politics, science, and military developments. This was one of the principal channels through which Dutch learning (rangaku) entered Japanese intellectual life.

Korea maintained diplomatic relations with Japan through the Sō domain of Tsushima, which managed trade and communications at Pusan, and sent missions to the shogunate on a periodic basis. The Ryukyu Kingdom, brought under Satsuma's domination after 1609 while retaining its tributary relationship with the Chinese court, served as an intermediary in trade and information between Japan and China. In the north, the Matsumae domain regulated commerce with Ainu communities and, through them, with Sakhalin and the Asian continent. Each of these relationships had its own rules, its own officials, and its own interests, and none depended on Edo's willingness to admit the world broadly. The "closed country" was therefore a country with a small number of carefully controlled doors.

The distinction matters because it clarifies what Perry actually challenged. He did not confront a society with no foreign relations. He confronted a policy by which diplomatic and commercial access was limited to particular partners, at particular places, on terms set by the shogunate. The American demand was not that Japan acknowledge the existence of the outside world, which it already did. It was that Japan treat a new category of visitor, with a different understanding of treaty relations, on terms that the old categories did not accommodate.

Historical Vocabulary
Sakoku

Sakoku ("closed country" or "chained country") is a term coined in 1801 by the Nagasaki interpreter and scholar Shizuki Tadao, who used it as the title for his translation of a section of Engelbert Kaempfer's description of Japan. It did not appear in official Tokugawa documents as a name for national policy. In the seventeenth century the shogunate issued a series of regulations restricting overseas travel, banning Christianity, and limiting trade, but these were administered through specific channels at Nagasaki, Tsushima, Satsuma/Ryukyu, and Matsumae.

Historians now generally prefer to describe the system as regulated foreign relations, or in a phrase sometimes used in recent scholarship, a system of "four gateways." The term sakoku remains in wide use and students will encounter it, but it should be handled as a later label that reflects the shogunate's limits on access rather than the absence of contact.

Section 51.3

Foreign Ships, Diplomatic Encounters, and Coastal Anxiety

The arrival of Perry's squadron was not the first time a foreign vessel had challenged the shogunate's management of the coast. In 1792 the Russian officer Adam Laxman visited Nemuro in eastern Ezo seeking trade. In 1804 Nikolai Rezanov spent months at Nagasaki and was ultimately refused. In 1808 the British frigate Phaeton entered Nagasaki harbor during the Napoleonic Wars, seeking Dutch ships, and left after obtaining supplies; the Nagasaki magistrate, humiliated by the breach of security, took his own life. The incident produced a lasting concern in Edo about the vulnerability of Nagasaki and the coast more broadly.

The shogunate's response in the following decades oscillated. In 1825, amid growing numbers of whaling vessels and incidents of foreign sailors landing in search of provisions, it issued an order directing coastal authorities to drive off foreign ships without hesitation (ikokusen uchiharai-rei). The policy was clear but difficult to enforce. In 1837 an American merchant vessel, the Morrison, which carried Japanese castaways along with American merchants and missionaries, was fired upon at Uraga and again at Kagoshima. The incident was widely discussed in Japan, including among rangaku scholars who criticized the policy as reckless, and it contributed to the debate over how to treat vessels that were not hostile.

American approaches in the 1840s illustrated the limits of earlier methods. In 1846 Commodore James Biddle arrived at Uraga with two warships and asked about opening trade. Biddle's instructions were to seek relations without using force, and when the shogunate declined, he left. During the visit a Japanese guard reportedly shoved Biddle, and he did not respond. Such an exchange was read by some Americans as evidence that quiet diplomacy was futile and by some Japanese officials as evidence that firmness could succeed. The American naval commander James Glynn's mission to Nagasaki in 1849 to recover American sailors held there reinforced a different conclusion, that firmness backed by naval presence could produce results. These interpretations shaped Perry's thinking.

The shogunate itself had adjusted. In 1842, informed of the Qing defeat in the Opium War, the council replaced the 1825 order with a policy allowing foreign ships in distress to receive fuel and water. In 1844 King William II of the Netherlands wrote to the shogun urging that Japan open itself to foreign relations to avoid conflict. The shogunate declined but did not ignore the message. By the early 1850s, the shogunate's maritime defense policy was therefore a mixture of caution, accommodation in emergencies, and unwillingness to commit to a general change. It was not blind to the danger, and it was not ready to meet it.

Section 51.4

The Opium War, American Expansion, and the Pacific Strategic Environment

The Opium War of 1839–1842 gave Japanese officials a vivid example of what Western naval power could do. Britain's defeat of Qing forces and the Treaty of Nanjing of 1842, which ceded Hong Kong and opened several Chinese ports to British commerce, were reported to Edo through Dutch and Chinese sources at Nagasaki. For many in the shogunate the lesson was that the greatest regional power could be defeated by a Western naval force and that Japan, with weaker coastal defenses than China, was exposed. Chinese writings on the subject, notably Wei Yuan's Illustrated Treatise on the Maritime Kingdoms, circulated in Japan in the following years and influenced a generation of officials and scholars who studied coastal defense. Artillery experts such as Takashima Shūhan demonstrated Western-style gunnery to the shogunate in 1841, and domains with the means began to acquire and cast Western-style cannon.

Meanwhile, the United States was expanding across the continent and into the Pacific. By 1848 it had acquired California, and the Gold Rush that followed intensified travel and trade across the Pacific. American whalers hunted in the waters off Japan in growing numbers, and crews that were wrecked on Japanese shores sometimes reported harsh treatment while held in custody. In 1844 the Treaty of Wanghia gave the United States commercial access to China similar to that obtained by Britain. American merchants and politicians who looked toward the China trade saw Japan as a natural stopping point for steam vessels, which required frequent coaling, on routes between San Francisco and Shanghai.

These developments created a convergence of interests that explains the timing of the Perry expedition. Steam navigation made coal depots essential. Whaling made refuge for shipwrecked crews a humanitarian and commercial matter. The China trade made a Japanese entrepôt attractive, and a naval power with new Pacific coastlines wanted a diplomatic position in East Asia. Perry himself, a strong advocate of steam naval power, also saw the expedition as a means of strengthening American influence against Britain and Russia. The expedition's goals were thus not primarily territorial conquest. They were the creation of access, protection, and an enduring diplomatic framework, achieved if possible without war, but backed by a visible willingness to use force.

II. Commodore Perry and the First Black Ships Encounter

The next five sections follow Perry's expedition from its planning and its first landfalls in the Ryukyus and the Bonins to the delivery of the President's letter and the reactions that followed.

Section 51.5

Commodore Matthew Perry and the American Expedition

Matthew Calbraith Perry was a veteran naval officer, the brother of the hero of the Battle of Lake Erie, and a prominent advocate for steam-powered warships in the United States Navy. When the administration of President Fillmore decided in 1852 to send an expedition to Japan, Perry was chosen to command the East India Squadron. He negotiated for substantial authority over the mission's planning, including the size of the squadron and the form of his instructions. He sailed from Norfolk, Virginia, in November 1852 aboard the steam frigate Mississippi, traveling via the Atlantic and Indian Oceans, with stops that included Cape Town, Singapore, Hong Kong, and Shanghai, before assembling his force in East Asian waters.

His instructions, issued under the authority of the Navy and State Departments, directed him to seek protection for shipwrecked American seamen, permission for American ships to obtain supplies and coal, and the opening of one or more ports for commercial intercourse. He was authorized to use force only for self-defense and in response to violence, a limit that Perry interpreted in practice with considerable latitude. He studied earlier accounts of Japan, including Dutch and Russian materials, and concluded that the Japanese respected displays of dignity and strength. He planned to remain aloof, to deal only with officials of the highest rank, and to use the visible presence of his ships, including his steam vessels, to underline American seriousness.

Students should keep in mind the distinction between Perry's explicit objectives and the larger ambitions that motivated his government and its supporters. The practical goals were access to provisions, protection of sailors, and diplomatic recognition. The larger ambition, shared by many American commercial and political figures, was commercial expansion and a Pacific presence. The first could be pursued through a limited treaty. The second would require continued pressure and further agreements, as the events of 1856–1858 would show. Perry's own words and writings reveal a man conscious of acting for national and, as he saw it, civilizational ends, a self-understanding that later Japanese and non-Japanese historians have interpreted with varying degrees of sympathy.

Section 51.6

The Expedition's Encounters with Ryukyu and the Bonin Islands

Perry did not go directly to Edo Bay. In May 1853 he reached Naha, the principal port of the Ryukyu Kingdom, where he intended to establish a base for the expedition. The kingdom's authorities, who maintained relations with Satsuma and the Qing and were wary of any new arrangements that might disturb them, attempted to dissuade him from visiting the royal capital at Shuri. Perry went anyway. On June 6, 1853, he led a procession of marines and officers to Shuri Castle, an assertive display of the squadron's capacity and determination. Ryukyuan officials received him, offered hospitality, and sought to limit the consequences of the visit. Perry then departed for the Bonin Islands, and on his return left some ships at Naha.

The Ogasawara (Bonin) Islands, a group of small volcanic islands in the Pacific south of Edo, were a strategic location for steam vessels. Perry visited Peel Island (Chichijima) in June 1853, where he found a small multinational settlement, and purchased land for a possible coaling station at Port Lloyd. The Japanese shogunate had earlier asserted claims to the islands and in the following years would send surveying and settlement parties. American interest in the islands foreshadowed the extension of treaty-era diplomacy to the Pacific frontier and the later question of sovereignty over them, which was settled in Japan's favor in the following decades.

For the Ryukyu Kingdom, the expedition's arrival was a complicated and unwelcome event. The kingdom was both subordinate to Satsuma and tributary to the Qing, a dual allegiance that Ryukyuan officials had managed with great skill for two centuries. The sudden appearance of an American squadron, demanding supplies and diplomatic recognition, threatened to expose that balance. Ryukyuan officials had been dealing with earlier European visits, including British and French naval ships and missionaries, and had learned to defer, delay, and deflect. Their approach in 1853 was consistent with that experience. The kingdom had its own interests and its own diplomatic strategy. It was not merely a stage on which Perry's expedition performed. Section 51.24 returns to the compact that resulted.

Section 51.7

July 1853: The Black Ships Arrive at Uraga

On July 8, 1853, Perry's four vessels, the steam frigates Susquehanna (his flagship) and Mississippi and the sailing sloops of war Plymouth and Saratoga, entered the channel at Uraga. Uraga was the checkpoint where the shogunate inspected shipping bound for Edo, and its two magistrates (Uraga bugyō) represented shogunal authority on the water. Guard boats from the coast approached the squadron. The Americans refused to allow Japanese officials aboard the flagship and, through interpreters, indicated that Perry would deal only with a high-ranking representative. Japanese officials, in turn, informed the Americans that foreign affairs were conducted at Nagasaki and that no letter could be received at Uraga. The Americans did not accept that answer.

The communications were conducted largely through the Dutch language. Japanese interpreters, including Hori Tatsunosuke, who had trained in Dutch, translated between Dutch and Japanese, and the Americans used their own Dutch and Chinese interpreters, including Anton Portman and the missionary-linguist Samuel Wells Williams. The first Japanese to deal with the squadron were lower-ranking local officers, including Nakajima Saburōsuke and Kayama Eizaemon, who were presented as deputies of the Uraga magistrate. The Americans, who understood that rank in such matters was part of the negotiation, held out for someone of higher standing. The Japanese were, by training and policy, accustomed to controlling the procedure through which foreigners were addressed.

The squadron also made clear that it would act if refused. American survey boats entered the bay to take soundings, in disregard of Japanese instructions to withdraw. Ships' guns were used in demonstrations and salutes, and the steamers moved against the tide in a manner that shogunal observers noted. Perry reportedly warned that if the letter were not received he would proceed to Edo with his squadron and deliver it himself. The shogunate, unwilling to risk a confrontation with a squadron it could not match and unable to reach Nagasaki-based procedures in time, chose to receive the letter. This was not a surrender of principle in the eyes of officials, who treated the receipt as a one-time exception to avoid conflict. It was nonetheless a departure from the established practice that foreign communication be conducted at Nagasaki.

For Japanese coastal defense, the confrontation was revealing. The batteries at Uraga and along the bay were designed for a different kind of adversary, and the domains assigned to the defense of the bay had not yet had the experience of facing steam warships. The shogunate knew in advance that Perry was coming, since Dutch officials had passed along advance notice, but its measures had been limited. In the narrow sense of the term, Perry's squadron achieved its immediate objective not through battle but through the credible possibility of it.

Historical Vocabulary
Kurofune (Black Ships)

Kurofune, literally "black ships," is the Japanese term for the Western vessels that arrived in the mid-nineteenth century, and especially for Perry's squadron. The name reflects the tar-blackened hulls of the ships and, in the case of the steamers, the dark smoke from their funnels. The term had already been applied to Portuguese ships in the sixteenth century, but in modern usage it refers principally to the 1853–1854 expeditions and has become a shorthand for the beginning of Japan's mid-century crisis.

Students should be cautious about the term's connotations. "Black Ships" has become part of a national narrative that sometimes implies a sudden shock. It is more accurate to see the kurofune as the dramatic arrival of a challenge that officials had been anticipating, and whose significance lay in the political choices that followed rather than in the ships alone.

Section 51.8

The President's Letter and Diplomacy Under Naval Pressure

On July 14, 1853, at Kurihama, a short distance from Uraga, a ceremony was held for the delivery of the President's letter. Perry came ashore with a large escort of sailors and marines, accompanied by a band, and the Japanese had constructed a reception hall for the occasion. The Uraga magistrates, Toda Ujiyoshi and Ido Hiromichi, received the letter and a credential from Perry, together with a translation. They issued a receipt and explained that, because the shogun himself could not respond to foreign communication quickly, a reply would require time. Perry declared that he would depart and return the next year with a larger force to receive the answer.

The letter itself, which Perry carried together with additional documents and gifts, expressed friendship from the United States, described the American continental reach and the growth of steam communication across the Pacific, and requested that Japan permit trade and provide assistance to shipwrecked sailors and ships in need of supplies. It was addressed to the "Emperor of Japan," a Western convention that reflected uncertainty about the actual locus of authority, and it suggested that Japan could, if it wished, suspend its restrictions on foreign trade for a limited trial period of several years. Perry added to the formal communication a pair of white flags, with the explanation that they might be raised if the Japanese preferred to avoid war. The gesture, which is recorded in American accounts, was intended as pressure and was understood as such.

Japanese officials were attentive to protocol throughout. They handled the sequence of ceremonies with care, preserved distinctions of rank, and conveyed to the Americans that this was an exceptional courtesy rather than a precedent. Interpreters negotiated minor details. The shogunate recorded the whole event, and its documents show a concern both to avoid any commitment and to keep the Americans from escalating. The decision to defer a substantive reply was as much a tactical choice as a diplomatic one. It bought time to translate and circulate the letter, to consult within the government, and to prepare.

Perry then sailed deeper into Edo Bay, to a point within sight of the capital's waterfront, a demonstration that was neither accidental nor inadvertent. When the squadron left on July 17, it left behind a shogunate that had, for the first time in generations, received a formal diplomatic communication from a Western state outside the channels the shogunate had defined, and that had agreed to answer it.

Section 51.9

Fear, Curiosity, Rumor, and Japanese Representations of the Black Ships

How did ordinary people respond? A widespread story holds that Edo was thrown into a general panic, with residents fleeing the city in terror. The evidence supports a more complicated picture. Contemporary diaries and reports record alarm, particularly among officials and households near the coast, and some residents moved belongings or relatives to safer locations. But they also record a great deal of curiosity. Crowds gathered at vantage points along the shore. Samurai, merchants, and artisans came to see the ships, and some sought to board them. Artists and printmakers, including those who produced single-sheet kawaraban broadsheets and woodblock prints, circulated images of the vessels and their crews within weeks.

These images are valuable but must be read carefully. Some depicted Perry and his officers in caricature, with exaggerated features that reflected both fear and fascination. Others presented the ships with reasonably accurate detail, often copying from earlier prints or from the observations of those who had seen them. A well-known pun, often cited in later histories, played on the name of a brand of tea, jōkisen, which also sounds like the word for steamship, and on the number four, to suggest that four cups of tea could keep one awake all night just as four ships had. Its exact origin is uncertain, but the genre of satirical verse and print was real, and it reflects the vigor of urban popular commentary.

Official reports and domain correspondence provide a different kind of evidence. Local officials recorded movements of the squadron and discussed defenses and procedures. Letters from daimyō retainers in Edo to their home domains conveyed news and opinion. These sources suggest that the shock was strongest among people who understood the military implications, and that popular reaction varied by location, class, and access to information. A retrospective narrative of universal terror, which served later nationalist and modernizing histories, tends to flatten this variation. The more reliable conclusion is that the arrival made foreign policy a subject of public conversation on a scale not seen before, in the capital and in the provinces.

III. The Shogunate's Political and Military Crisis

The months between Perry's first departure and his return were months of decision-making under pressure. The next five sections examine how the shogunate organized its response and what its choices revealed about its political and military position.

Section 51.10

Abe Masahiro and the Problem of National Decision-Making

Abe Masahiro, lord of Fukuyama domain in Bingo Province, had become a senior councilor in 1843 and was, by 1853, the leading figure in the council. He was in his mid-thirties, capable, conciliatory in manner, and experienced in the administration of the shogunate. He faced an unusual problem. The shogunate's foreign policy had been defined by precedent, and precedent offered no clear guide for a demand backed by a naval squadron the shogunate could not defeat. Yet the council could not simply accede, because to do so would appear to abandon the principles that had justified Tokugawa foreign policy for two centuries, and might provoke criticism from within the elite.

Abe's dilemma was therefore as much political as diplomatic. A response that accepted the American requests risked alienating domains and the court. A response that refused risked war with an adversary whose ships could reach the capital. The shogun, Ieyoshi, was in poor health and died on July 27, 1853, weeks after Perry's departure, which removed any possibility of decisive leadership from the top. Abe, in effect, bore the principal burden of the crisis, and he sought a way to make a decision that would be defensible whichever way events turned.

The approach he adopted combined practical preparation with broad consultation. He ordered the translation of Perry's letter and circulated it, together with a request for opinions, to the daimyō and to shogunal officials. He restored Tokugawa Nariaki of Mito, a prominent critic of the shogunate's coastal defense, to an advisory role on maritime defense. He directed work on fortifications and ordered a review of military organization. The measures reflected both a genuine search for advice and a political calculation: by involving those who might later object, Abe hoped to build support for whatever the shogunate eventually decided. The consequence, discussed in the next section, was that a decision-making tradition built on exclusivity began to give way to something broader.

Section 51.11

Consultation with the Daimyō and Competing Political Strategies

In the summer of 1853 the shogunate did something it had rarely done on a matter of foreign policy: it asked for the views of daimyō and officials outside the council. The circulation of the President's letter, with a request for opinions on how to respond, reached lords of all ranks, from the Tokugawa collateral houses to tozama domains, as well as shogunal retainers. The replies, compiled in later collections of documents from the period, ran to many hundreds. They did not form a consensus.

Broadly, several positions can be distinguished. Some respondents advocated firm refusal of any concession, relying on tradition and, in some cases, on a belief that Japanese warriors could defeat the foreigners in close combat. Others argued that refusal could not be enforced without modern ships and guns and recommended delaying while preparing. A smaller group recommended accepting limited relations, including trade, as a means of obtaining the resources and technology needed for defense. Daimyō such as Ii Naosuke of Hikone are generally associated with an accommodationist view, while Tokugawa Nariaki's position combined a refusal in principle with practical preparation. Shimazu Nariakira of Satsuma and Matsudaira Yoshinaga of Echizen were interested in reform and military strengthening and were more open to accommodation as a pragmatic course.

The significance of the exercise lay as much in the process as in the content. By soliciting opinions, the shogunate implicitly acknowledged that foreign policy affected all domains and that its decisions required wider legitimacy. The shogunate had not abandoned its claim to decide, but it had admitted that the decision was one in which others had a stake. Participants came away with a sense that they had a right, even a duty, to express views on national questions. Historians have often seen this as the beginning of a new political culture, in which questions previously regarded as the shogunate's business became subjects of public deliberation, a development that the next chapters will follow.

Section 51.12

Shogunal Succession and Institutional Uncertainty

The timing of the crisis compounded its difficulty. Tokugawa Ieyoshi, the twelfth shogun, died on July 27, 1853, only days after Perry's squadron left Edo Bay. By the conventions of the shogunate, his death was not announced publicly at once, and the formal installation of his successor, Tokugawa Iesada, took place in the autumn. Iesada, a son of Ieyoshi, was a frail man in his late twenties who played little active role in government. For the rest of his reign, which lasted until 1858, the shogunate's leadership would rest with the council and with those who could influence it.

This is not to say that government stopped. The administrative machinery of the shogunate, from the offices of the council to the magistrates in Nagasaki, Uraga, and other posts, continued to function. But the combination of a weak shogun, a crisis, and the broadening of consultation created an environment in which authority was diffuse. Questions that would have been resolved by a decisive shogun fell to senior councilors, and as the succession itself soon became a topic of contention, the issue of who would lead in the future began to merge with the question of how to respond to the foreigners. Iesada had no heir, and as the 1850s advanced the question of succession would become a flashpoint, a story that belongs to Chapter 52.

For this chapter, the important point is that institutional uncertainty shaped decisions. Abe's cautious, consultative style may have been the best available strategy in the circumstances, but it was also a product of the absence of a strong head. The shogunate in 1853–1854 was neither collapsing nor fully in command. It was a government attempting to manage a crisis with a leadership structure that lacked the capacity for a decisive, unified response.

Section 51.13

Coastal Fortifications, Artillery, and Military Vulnerability

The shogunate's military position in 1853 was weak in precisely the area where the foreigners were strong. Japanese forces in the early 1850s consisted of samurai retainers organized under domain and shogunal commands, with matchlock muskets and traditional bronze cannon of varied quality. They had little experience of modern naval warfare and no steam-powered warships. Coastal batteries around Edo Bay were designed to repel earlier types of vessels and could not be expected to resist a squadron of steam frigates with heavy shell guns. The practical difficulty was compounded by the fact that the defenders belonged to different commands, with differing equipment, training, and budgets.

Within weeks of Perry's departure, the shogunate began to address the problem. It engaged the shogunal official Egawa Hidetatsu, who had studied Western gunnery, to design and construct a series of island batteries in Edo Bay, the works known as Odaiba. The plan envisaged about a dozen artificial islands arranged to cover the sea approach to Edo, though only a portion of them were completed. The shogunate also lifted the long-standing prohibition on the construction of large oceangoing ships in September 1853, allowing both the shogunate and domains to build Western-style vessels. Domains such as Saga, Mito, and Satsuma, which had already begun casting Western-type cannon in reverberatory furnaces, expanded their efforts, and the shogunate drew on their expertise.

These efforts were serious, but their limits are equally clear. Constructing fortifications and casting cannon required money and skilled labor that the shogunate and many domains lacked. The batteries were under construction when Perry returned in 1854 and could not be said to change the balance. The effort also placed new burdens on domains and local communities, an issue taken up in Section 51.26. Historians generally agree that the shogunate's military measures were meaningful in laying foundations but insufficient to alter, in the short run, the fundamental asymmetry between a modern navy and a coastal defense system designed for earlier conditions.

Section 51.14

Resistance, Negotiation, and the Search for a Viable Strategy

By the winter of 1853–1854 the shogunate faced a limited number of strategic options, none free of cost. It could refuse the American request and prepare for war. It could accept the American proposals more or less as presented. It could attempt to reach a narrow agreement that provided for the humanitarian and supply requests while denying trade. Or it could try to delay once more and hope the Americans would be discouraged. Each option had defenders, and each carried risks.

Refusal risked a naval conflict that Japan was not equipped to win. The Opium War had shown what could follow, and shogunal officials could not know whether the Americans would behave differently from the British. Acceptance risked political repercussions at home and a precedent that might invite additional demands from other powers, including Britain and Russia, whose ships were also in the region. Delay had already been used and could not be repeated indefinitely, since Perry had announced his return. This left the third option, a limited treaty, as the course that offered the best chance of balancing the competing risks.

The choice was therefore not between "opening" and "isolation," as popular accounts sometimes suggest. It was a choice among degrees of concession, timing, and the specific forms of access, with each increment affecting how others would respond. The shogunate's eventual strategy was to grant what it considered least damaging, namely provisioning, assistance to shipwrecked sailors, and access to ports far from Edo, while withholding trade and residence. The question of whether this was a wise course, or a prudent delay that merely postponed greater concessions, is addressed in the Historical Debate later in the chapter.

IV. Perry's Return and the Treaty of Kanagawa

The next five sections follow the negotiation of the first treaty, analyze its provisions, and ask what its implementation, and the phrase "opening of Japan" itself, actually meant.

Section 51.15

The Return of the American Squadron in 1854

Perry returned to Edo Bay on February 11, 1854, earlier than the shogunate had expected, with a larger force than before. His squadron began with seven vessels and was reinforced to nine, including the steam frigate Powhatan, which became his flagship. He had spent the winter in Hong Kong and Naha, and he had informed the Japanese that he would return in the spring; by coming in February, he asserted the initiative and increased the pressure.

The shogunate was prepared in the sense that it had settled on a general approach, but it had not finished its fortifications or its internal discussions. Officials had to decide where negotiations should take place, who would conduct them, and how far they could go. The Americans pressed for talks at Kanagawa or even Edo; the shogunate sought to keep them as far from the capital as possible. After some maneuvering the two sides agreed on a site at Yokohama, then a small fishing village on the bay, where the Japanese built a temporary treaty house. The choice of Yokohama, a place of no previous importance, would give the village a historical future it had not anticipated, as Chapter 52 will show.

Behind the negotiations lay a shift in leverage. In 1853, the Americans had demanded a hearing and the shogunate had deferred. In 1854, both sides knew that a deferral would not suffice. The shogunate had to decide what it would concede, and Perry had to decide what he would accept. Perry, aware that his instructions did not authorize conquest and that his government wanted a treaty, was prepared to settle for limited gains. The shogunate, aware that Perry would not leave empty-handed, was prepared to concede them.

Section 51.16

The Yokohama Negotiations and Japanese Diplomatic Leadership

The negotiations opened on March 8, 1854. Japan's chief negotiator was Hayashi Fukusai, the head of the Hayashi family of Confucian scholars who served the shogunate in an official capacity and who had experience in correspondence and protocol. He was assisted by other officials, including Ido Hiromichi and Izawa Masayoshi, and by interpreters. The choice of a Confucian scholar as chief negotiator may seem unexpected, but the Hayashi house held a high rank and a role in diplomatic documents that made it a suitable representative. Perry led the American side, assisted by Williams and other interpreters.

The talks were conducted largely in Dutch, with Chinese used for certain documents, and each side relied on interpreters whose skills and interpretive choices shaped the outcome. The Japanese negotiators were not merely reactive. They pursued a clear strategy: to offer limited concessions in a form that would not set a precedent for general trade, to locate any opened ports away from Edo, and to retain as much control as possible over the conditions of foreign access. They argued, delayed, and drew on knowledge of Western diplomatic practice gained through Dutch sources. The Americans, for their part, pressed for the opening of several ports, including Nagasaki, and for provisions that would allow a consul and trade.

The negotiations were also accompanied by ceremony. Both sides exchanged gifts. The Americans presented items that included a quarter-scale steam locomotive with track, a telegraph, clocks, firearms, books, and agricultural implements, and the demonstration of the telegraph and the small locomotive drew the interest of Japanese officials. The Japanese presented lacquerware, silks, and ceramics, and arranged for sumo wrestlers to carry rice bales to the American boats, which the Americans noted with interest. Banquets were held, and American sailors staged a minstrel performance for their hosts, an episode recorded in American accounts and one that illustrates the cultural distance between the parties. These exchanges were not mere entertainment. For the Japanese they were a means of displaying competence and dignity, and for the Americans a means of establishing the conditions of friendship.

Section 51.17

The Treaty of Kanagawa: Provisions and Diplomatic Significance

The agreement signed on March 31, 1854, the Treaty of Kanagawa (the treaty bears the name of the nearby port, although it was concluded at Yokohama), consisted of twelve articles. It established "peace and amity" between the United States and Japan, a formulation that carried the language of Western diplomacy into the shogunate's relations. It opened two ports, Shimoda on the Izu Peninsula and Hakodate in Ezo, to American ships, where they could obtain wood, water, provisions, and coal, with Shimoda opening immediately and Hakodate the following year. It provided that shipwrecked American sailors would be treated kindly and could be sent to those ports. It allowed the exchange of goods under the supervision of Japanese officials and the payment of purchases in gold or silver coin.

Two provisions had effects beyond their immediate subject. The first was the most-favored-nation clause, which stipulated that any privilege granted by Japan to another nation would be granted to the United States as well. The effect was to ensure that the concessions Japan made to later treaty partners would automatically extend to the Americans, and vice versa, tying all subsequent arrangements together. The second was a provision allowing the United States to station a consul at Shimoda, provided that both governments agreed that it was necessary, after a period of eighteen months. The wording of this clause differed in the English and Japanese texts, and the difference would lead to controversy when Townsend Harris arrived to take up his post, as explained in Section 51.27. The treaty also left open important matters, including the status of Americans on Japanese soil and the terms of trade.

What the treaty did not provide is as important as what it did. It did not authorize general commerce. It did not open Edo, Osaka, Nagasaki, or other major cities. It did not grant extraterritorial rights, which would appear in later agreements. It did not impose a tariff or exchange regime. It was, in essence, a treaty of access for provisioning and humanitarian protection, with a framework for further negotiation. The distinction is essential for separating this chapter from the next. The commercial and legal regime that would so alter Japan's position belongs to the treaties of 1858, not to Kanagawa.

Diplomatically, however, the treaty marked a rupture with the shogunate's earlier practice. For the first time, Japan agreed to a treaty with a Western power outside the Dutch and Chinese systems, accepted the principle of diplomatic reciprocity in a Western form, and acknowledged American privileges at its ports. It did so under pressure, but not without bargaining. Japanese negotiators secured the exclusion of trade, the selection of ports distant from the capital, and a delay in the opening of Hakodate, outcomes that reflected the shogunate's priorities. To describe the treaty as simply imposed is to ignore these achievements. To describe it as a free exchange is to ignore the squadron anchored in the bay.

Historical Vocabulary
Most-favored-nation clause

A most-favored-nation (MFN) clause is a treaty provision under which one party agrees that any privilege it grants to a third country will be extended automatically to its treaty partner. In the Treaty of Kanagawa, the clause meant that Japan could not grant concessions to Britain, Russia, or any other state without extending them to the United States.

The clause seems modest, but its effect was to bind Japan's diplomatic arrangements together. Once one power obtained a privilege, the others could claim it, and the shogunate lost the ability to bargain with each separately. Because later treaties added further privileges, including commercial and legal rights, the clause became one mechanism by which the unequal-treaty system, examined in Chapter 52, expanded.

  4. What can historians not establish from the treaty text alone about how it was understood or implemented?
Section 51.18

Shimoda and Hakodate: Implementing the New Treaty Order

Treaties require implementation, and the practical consequences of Kanagawa fell on the two ports. Shimoda, a harbor town on the southern tip of the Izu Peninsula, was a small port of modest size. Hakodate, on the southern coast of Ezo, was the principal port of the Matsumae domain's territory and a trading and fishing center. Neither had been prepared for foreign visitors. The shogunate dispatched officials to supervise arrangements and, in 1854, established a Hakodate magistracy as well, extending shogunal oversight to a region that had been largely under Matsumae's management.

Perry visited both ports in April and May 1854, inspecting harbors and negotiating details. At Shimoda, supplementary regulations signed in June 1854 set rules for the movement of Americans, including limits on how far they could travel from the port, and for the conduct of trade. Local officials had to build and maintain facilities, supply provisions, and keep order, often working through temples that were used as lodgings. Residents encountered foreign sailors, bartered, observed the customs of visitors, and in some cases found the encounters difficult, as disputes arose over conduct and over the burial of sailors who died while in port. In April 1854 the scholar and activist Yoshida Shōin attempted to board one of Perry's ships at Shimoda to travel abroad, an act of defiance against shogunal restrictions that led to his imprisonment and made him a figure in later political memory.

The experience in the ports was not uniform. Shimoda's small population dealt with a limited number of visitors, and the town gained a short-lived prominence. Hakodate, a larger port with an established commercial role and a mixed population that included Wajin settlers and Ainu communities in the surrounding region, felt the arrival of American and, soon after, Russian and other vessels as an extension of its role in a wider maritime world. In both cases, officials used the treaty to maintain control over contact, and in both cases the contact itself generated new questions about authority, jurisdiction, and the limits of regulation.

Everyday Life and Environment

Port Towns and the Daily Texture of the Treaty Order

For the residents of Uraga, Shimoda, and Hakodate, the treaty era meant extra labor, new regulations, and unfamiliar visitors. Boatmen, porters, and suppliers provided services. Officials and interpreters spent long hours in negotiations over provisions, prices, and boundaries. Temples lodged visitors and, in some cases, hosted official meetings. Townspeople watched sailors walk the streets, trade goods, and attend funerals for shipmates who died abroad.

The evidence for these experiences is uneven, since most surviving records were written by officials or visitors rather than by residents themselves. What they suggest is a mixture of wariness, curiosity, opportunity, and strain. Local authorities were responsible for maintaining the boundary between foreign sailors and the community. Merchants and suppliers sought to profit from the new demand. Households near the harbor lived with the knowledge that disputes could escalate. These local experiences were the ground on which the larger political questions were enacted.

Section 51.19

What Did the Opening of Japan Actually Mean?

The phrase "the opening of Japan" (kaikoku) is a standard label for the events of 1853–1854, but it needs scrutiny. The Treaty of Kanagawa opened two ports for provisioning and humanitarian assistance. It did not establish free trade, permit foreigners to travel freely, or alter the shogunate's control over most aspects of foreign contact. In that sense, Japan in 1854 was not yet "open" in the sense the word later acquired. What had changed was the principle: the shogunate had agreed to receive foreign diplomatic representation, to enter into a treaty framework, and to concede access to its territory, and it had done so under the pressure of foreign arms.

Several points deserve emphasis. First, the shogunate retained significant agency. It negotiated, delayed, selected ports, excluded trade, and framed the agreement in ways that served its interests. Second, the circumstances were coercive. The American squadron's presence, its surveying of the bay, and its explicit threats shaped what was possible. Third, the treaty was not the end of the process. It created a framework that could be, and would be, extended. The next chapter's commercial treaties built on it. The accurate description is that Kanagawa began a transition from the Tokugawa system of controlled channels to a treaty-based system, a transition whose speed and consequences were not determined in advance.

Students should also avoid reading the story as one in which Japan was simply acted upon. Japanese officials, scholars, and domains were making their own assessments, building on decades of information about the world and a developed tradition of diplomacy. The shogunate's decisions reflected strategy and choice, even when the available options were narrow. At the same time, it would be wrong to understate the asymmetry. The agreement was reached because one party commanded a naval force the other could not match.

Continuity and Change Over Time

The Transformation of Tokugawa Foreign Relations

What Changed

  • The shogunate accepted a treaty with a Western state outside the Nagasaki framework, with a most-favored-nation clause tying later concessions together
  • Ports at Shimoda and Hakodate, away from Nagasaki, were opened to foreign ships
  • Foreign policy was discussed beyond the council, through consultation with daimyō and notification of the court
  • Military and naval institutions began to be reorganized along Western lines

What Stayed the Same

  • The shogunate remained the formal conductor of foreign relations, and the court did not take over diplomacy
  • Trade remained limited, and Nagasaki, Tsushima, Satsuma/Ryukyu, and Matsumae retained their earlier roles
  • The domain structure, status hierarchy, and daily regulation of society were unaltered
  • Officials continued to rely on Dutch-language interpreters and on precedent in handling diplomacy

Who experienced change most directly? Shogunal officials, interpreters, and port-town residents experienced the change immediately. Who experienced greater continuity? Most villagers and inland communities saw little change in daily life in these years. Why the pattern matters: The shogunate changed its principles faster than it changed its institutions, a mismatch that would generate strain in the following years.

Historical Debate

Was Tokugawa Accommodation Weakness or Calculated Strategy?

The question: When the shogunate agreed to a treaty with the United States in 1854, was it demonstrating that it lacked the strength to resist, or was it adopting a calculated strategy to preserve its governing capacity?

Interpretation A — Accommodation as weakness: Some historians emphasize that the shogunate could not match American naval power, had delayed preparation despite Dutch warnings, and conceded under pressure. On this view, its concessions revealed the limits of Tokugawa authority, encouraged further demands, and undermined its claim to protect the realm, making subsequent crisis nearly inevitable.
Interpretation B — Accommodation as strategy: Other historians emphasize that the shogunate chose a limited treaty after weighing options, secured exclusion of trade and distant ports, bought time for military reform, and avoided a war it could not win. On this view, the shogunate acted rationally, and what followed was not determined by the decision to negotiate.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on the same sources: the shogunate's own records of deliberation, the daimyō memorials, the treaty texts, and the record of military preparation. They differ in emphasis, and many scholars combine elements of both, noting that a strategy can be rational and still have destabilizing consequences.

Why it matters: How historians answer this question shapes how they understand the shogunate's final years. If it was simply weak, its fall seems almost predetermined. If it was strategic, then its later difficulties arose from the interaction of choices and circumstances, including domestic politics, rather than from a single failure in 1854.

Student task: Identify one piece of evidence from this chapter that supports each interpretation, and explain what additional evidence would help determine whether the shogunate's approach was a strategy or a reaction. Consider whether the two interpretations are mutually exclusive.

V. Russia, Britain, Ryukyu, and the Expansion of Treaty Diplomacy

Perry's treaty was the first of several. The next five sections show how British, Russian, Dutch, and Ryukyuan negotiations extended treaty diplomacy across Japan's maritime frontiers, from Nagasaki to the Kuril Islands to Naha.

Section 51.20

Britain and the Anglo-Japanese Agreement of 1854

Britain approached Japan with different priorities from those of the United States. It already held Hong Kong, dominated the China trade, and was deeply engaged in the wars and diplomacy of the Qing empire. Its interest in Japan was real but secondary, and in 1854 it was overshadowed by the Crimean War, in which Britain and France fought Russia. British naval commanders in East Asia were charged with locating and engaging Russian ships, and Japanese ports were potentially relevant as places where Russian vessels might shelter or British vessels might obtain supplies.

Rear Admiral James Stirling, commanding the British China station, arrived at Nagasaki in September 1854 with a small squadron, seeking information about Russian ships and permission for British vessels to use Japanese ports. Nagasaki officials, who had no authority to conclude a treaty but understood the importance of the matter, referred the question to Edo while negotiating a provisional arrangement. The resulting agreement, signed on October 14, 1854, opened Nagasaki and Hakodate to British ships for supplies and repairs, and it contained provisions on the treatment of British subjects and on Japan's neutrality that reflected Stirling's wartime concerns. It was ratified in 1855 after review in London and by the shogunate.

The Anglo-Japanese agreement was modest, and it did not confer general trade rights. Its significance lies in what it showed about the dynamics of treaty diplomacy. Once the United States had received access, other powers moved to secure comparable terms, and the shogunate, aware of the most-favored-nation principle and of the risk of antagonizing a power with Britain's naval strength, accepted. The shogunate was also learning to treat treaty-making as a routine matter. It negotiated efficiently, limited the substance, and sought to avoid the dramatic confrontations of the summer of 1853.

Section 51.21

Admiral Putyatin and Russian Diplomacy in Japan

Russia was Japan's northern neighbor, and its relationship with Japan had a longer history than that of the United States. Russian contacts in the north, extending back to the late eighteenth century, had involved fur traders, explorers, and diplomats, and they had produced both agreements and clashes. By the 1850s, Russia was expanding its presence in the Amur region and on the Pacific coast and wished to establish a diplomatic arrangement with Japan, in particular to fix the boundary in the Kuril Islands and Sakhalin and to obtain access to Japanese ports.

Vice Admiral Yevfimiy Putyatin arrived at Nagasaki in August 1853, a few weeks after Perry's departure from Uraga, aboard the frigate Pallada. Among those on board was the writer Ivan Goncharov, whose later travel narrative provides a valuable Russian account. Unlike Perry, Putyatin went to Nagasaki, the port designated for foreign business, and sought to negotiate through the established procedures. He did not threaten force. The shogunate sent senior officials, including Kawaji Toshiakira and Tsutsui Masanori, to negotiate with him, and talks were held at Nagasaki in early 1854. Putyatin left without an agreement, partly because of the shogunate's slowness and partly because the outbreak of war with Britain and France in the Crimea made his position in the Pacific precarious.

The contrast with Perry is instructive. Russian diplomacy relied on persistence, protocol, and the credibility of a long-standing northern relationship rather than on overt coercion, and Putyatin's willingness to follow Nagasaki procedure initially made it easier for the shogunate to engage without conceding a general change of principle. Yet the underlying pressure was the same. A power with naval reach and territorial interests was asking to establish formal relations, and the shogunate, having signed the Treaty of Kanagawa, found it hard to refuse a similar arrangement with another power. Putyatin returned in late 1854 and negotiated at Shimoda, where events took an unexpected turn.

Section 51.22

The Treaty of Shimoda and the Northern Frontier

Putyatin reached Shimoda in the autumn of 1854 aboard the frigate Diana, and negotiations began in December. On December 23, 1854, a major earthquake struck the Tōkai coast, and the resulting tsunami devastated Shimoda and badly damaged the Diana, which later sank while being towed for repairs. Japanese officials and Russian sailors worked together amid the destruction, and with Japanese carpenters' assistance the Russians built a replacement schooner at nearby Heda, a project that became an episode in the history of Western-style shipbuilding in Japan. The negotiations resumed after the disaster, and the treaty was signed on February 7, 1855.

The Treaty of Shimoda opened Shimoda, Hakodate, and Nagasaki to Russian ships and provided for consular representation. Most significantly, it drew a boundary in the Kuril Islands between Etorofu, which was to belong to Japan, and Urup, which was to belong to Russia, and it left Sakhalin undivided, with Japanese and Russians to continue to share it. This settlement fixed a line in waters that neither government had previously defined by treaty. The Kuril question has remained a subject of dispute between Japan and Russia into the present, as later treaties and the Soviet occupation of 1945 reshaped the northern boundary, but the 1855 agreement is the first formal foundation of the Japanese claim.

The northern waters were not empty. The Kuril Islands and Sakhalin were home to Ainu communities, with their own trade networks, kinship ties, and relations with both Wajin (ethnic Japanese) and Russian traders. Many Ainu worked in the fisheries and trading posts run by Wajin merchants under the Matsumae domain's system, often under harsh conditions. The treaty was negotiated between the shogunate and Russia, and Ainu people were not consulted. The line drawn between Etorofu and Urup divided communities and defined sovereignty above the heads of those who lived there. Soon afterward, in 1855, the shogunate also took direct control of much of Ezo from Matsumae, in part to strengthen defense and administration, further altering the position of the Ainu. Their history in this period belongs not only to the story of Japanese and Russian diplomacy but to a longer history of Ainu communities facing external pressure from two expanding states.

Section 51.23

Dutch Relations and the Transformation of Established Diplomatic Networks

The Dutch at Dejima occupied an unusual position in these years. For two centuries they had been the only Europeans permitted regular trade, and in the early 1850s they remained the shogunate's most important source of information about Europe. In 1852 the head of the Dejima post, Jan Donker Curtius, conveyed a warning that an American expedition was being organized. The shogunate received it with attention but did not make decisive preparations, a fact that later critics and historians have noted. Dutch, as the language of diplomacy between Japan and Western powers, shaped the negotiations of 1853–1855, and the interpreters who spoke it became indispensable intermediaries.

When the other treaties were concluded, the Dutch moved to protect their position. In November 1855 Donker Curtius concluded a provisional agreement at Nagasaki that relaxed some of the old restrictions on the Dutch and provided for a fuller framework of rights, followed by further arrangements in 1856 and 1857. The Netherlands also contributed to the shogunate's effort to build a navy. In 1855 the Dutch government presented a steam warship, which the shogunate named Kankō Maru, and Dutch officers were soon engaged to train Japanese students in navigation, gunnery, and related fields, as discussed in Section 51.25.

The Dutch connection illustrates how the new treaty environment grew from earlier arrangements rather than simply replacing them. The scholars, interpreters, and officials trained in rangaku, who had worked for decades with Dutch texts, were the ones best equipped to read, translate, and evaluate the new international documents. The Dutch relationship thus supplied the shogunate with both information and personnel at the moment it needed them most. At the same time, the arrival of other powers meant that the Dutch monopoly was ending, and Dejima's special role, long a distinctive feature of Tokugawa foreign relations, began to fade.

Section 51.24

Perry's Ryukyu Agreement and the Wider Geography of Foreign Pressure

After concluding the treaty at Yokohama and visiting Shimoda and Hakodate, Perry returned to Naha. There, on July 11, 1854, he concluded a compact with the Ryukyuan government. The agreement provided that American ships and citizens would be treated with friendship, that vessels could obtain supplies and fuel at Naha, that shipwrecked sailors would be assisted, and that Americans would have access to the port with certain regulations and a burial place at Tomari. It was prepared with attention to language, with Chinese playing a role in Ryukyuan diplomatic communication, and it was signed by a representative of the Ryukyuan government.

The circumstances were not those of an agreement between equals. Perry's squadron had been a presence in Naha for more than a year, and Ryukyuan authorities had been pressed repeatedly to supply provisions and to permit visits. In the weeks before the compact, an American sailor, William Board, was accused of assaulting a Ryukyuan woman and died after being pursued by local residents. Perry used the incident to press for formal arrangements. The kingdom's authorities were in a constrained position. They were subordinate to Satsuma, which closely watched events, tributary to the Qing, to whom they owed obligations of loyalty and communication, and exposed to the guns of an American squadron. Their approach was to concede what was necessary while preserving their basic position, and in the following years they concluded similar agreements with France (1855) and the Netherlands (1859).

The compact demonstrates that the new treaty environment extended beyond the main islands. The Ryukyu Kingdom, whose subtle dual relationships had been a feature of East Asian diplomacy for centuries, now faced a Western demand for treaty relations that did not fit the existing categories of tributary or domain. The kingdom's autonomy and its position between Japan and China were placed under strain. This pattern, in which pressure on Japan from the West affected the kingdom's own status, would have longer-term consequences for Ryukyu/Okinawa, and the story will be taken up in later chapters on the Meiji annexation and the creation of Okinawa Prefecture.

VI. Domestic Consequences and the Threshold of Political Upheaval

The final four sections examine how the treaty environment reshaped military institutions, domains, and local communities, and how American commercial ambitions led toward the negotiations of 1858.

Section 51.25

Naval Training, Western Technology, and the Reorganization of Defense

The shogunate's response to its military weakness accelerated after the treaties. In October 1855, with Dutch assistance, it opened the Nagasaki Naval Training Center, where Dutch naval officers instructed Japanese students in navigation, gunnery, steam engineering, and related subjects using the steamship donated by the Netherlands as a training vessel. Students came from the shogunate's retainers and from domains, and included Katsu Kaishū, who would become one of the most prominent shogunal naval figures. In Edo, the shogunate established a military training school, the Kōbusho, in 1856, to teach Western-style gunnery and tactics, and the Institute for the Investigation of Barbarian Books (Bansho Shirabesho), an office for translation and the study of Western texts, was founded in the same period.

Shipbuilding also advanced. The shogunate built its first Western-style warship, the Hōō Maru, at Uraga, and domains such as Satsuma and Saga undertook their own projects. In 1857 the shogunate acquired another steam warship from the Netherlands, later named Kanrin Maru. These efforts depended on Dutch advisers, on translated manuals, and on craftsmen who adapted Western designs to Japanese materials. They were slow and expensive, and they produced vessels that were modest by Western standards.

It is important to distinguish these defensive measures from the larger transformations that came later. The institutions of the mid-1850s were responses to a specific, immediate threat. They did not constitute the comprehensive military modernization of the 1860s, when the shogunate and domains acquired modern firearms in large numbers and reorganized their forces, nor the radical restructuring after 1868, which belongs to later chapters. What they did show was a willingness to adopt Western technology and learning at the shogunate's own initiative, a pattern of selective borrowing that long preceded the Meiji period and that cannot be reduced to passive imitation.

Section 51.26

Domains, Coastal Communities, and the Social Consequences of Diplomatic Change

The crisis imposed material costs. Coastal fortifications required labor, timber, stone, and money, and the shogunate asked domains to share the burden. Daimyō responsible for Edo Bay, including Aizu, Hikone, Kawagoe, and Oshi, took on garrison duties and construction projects at a time when many were already heavily indebted. Casting cannon required metal, and some domains, notably Mito, melted down temple bells for the purpose, an act with both practical and symbolic significance. For villagers and townspeople, these demands meant additional levies, labor service, and, at times, disruption of ordinary life.

Natural disasters compounded the strain. The great Tōkai and Nankai earthquakes of December 1854 devastated coastal communities, including Shimoda, and in November 1855 a severe earthquake struck Edo, killing thousands and destroying much of the city, and claiming the life of the Mito scholar Fujita Tōko, a prominent thinker of the movement for maritime defense. These disasters did not cause the political crisis, but they overlapped with it, straining resources and reinforcing a sense of instability, and they remind students that the political story unfolded in a physical and social environment that was itself unsettled.

At the same time, foreign policy became a subject of wider discussion. Domain retainers in Edo exchanged letters with their home domains. Written opinions, copies of the treaty, and reports circulated through personal networks. Scholars who studied the West, such as Sakuma Shōzan and his students, gave lectures and advice. Printed broadsheets disseminated news. Regional responses varied. Domains with a tradition of reform and military preparation, like Satsuma and Mito, engaged actively, while others were slower to respond or focused on local concerns. The upshot was that foreign policy ceased to be exclusively the business of the shogunate's administrators and became a matter of concern for domain officials, scholars, and in some measure ordinary townspeople, a change with lasting political consequences.

Multiple Perspectives

The Treaty Order of 1853–1855: Four Experiences

Note: these perspectives describe categories of experience reconstructed from the evidence; the groups held unequal power, and the purpose is historical complexity rather than equivalence.

Shogunal officials: Officials such as Abe Masahiro and the Uraga magistrates sought to keep the shogunate in control of foreign relations, avoid war, and gain time. They saw the treaty as a managed concession, and their concern was maintaining authority and precedent while preparing defenses. They worked within a system that rewarded caution and precedent, and they were criticized from both directions, for conceding too much and for preparing too little.
Daimyō: Daimyō responded according to their positions. Collateral and fudai lords close to the shogunate, such as Ii Naosuke, tended to emphasize practical accommodation. Reform-minded lords like Shimazu Nariakira and Matsudaira Yoshinaga looked to military and administrative reform, and Tokugawa Nariaki called for resistance together with preparation. Coastal daimyō also bore the costs of defense. For many, the consultation of 1853 expanded their sense of a stake in national policy.
Coastal residents: In Uraga, Shimoda, and Hakodate, townspeople faced extra labor, foreign visitors, and regulations. Some saw opportunities for trade and service, others feared disorder, and many were simply curious. They did not shape policy, but they lived with its consequences, and their experiences varied by place, occupation, and status. Ainu communities in the north were affected by a boundary they had no part in drawing.
Ryukyuan authorities: Officials of the Ryukyu Kingdom, answerable to both Satsuma and the Qing, sought to limit the consequences of the American presence while preserving their autonomy. They supplied provisions, negotiated the compact of 1854, and managed relations with other powers. Their interests did not coincide with those of the shogunate, and their position illustrates how the crisis in the main islands had distinct and consequential effects in the south.
Section 51.27

Townsend Harris and the Movement Toward Commercial Negotiations

The limited arrangements of 1854 did not satisfy American commercial ambitions. Merchants and officials in the United States wanted a treaty of trade, not merely access to provisions, and they saw the Treaty of Kanagawa as a first step. The administration of President Franklin Pierce appointed Townsend Harris, a New York merchant with experience in Asia, as the first American consul general to Japan. Harris arrived at Shimoda in August 1856 aboard the USS San Jacinto, accompanied by the Dutch-speaking interpreter Henry Heusken, and established his consulate at a temple in the vicinity.

His reception was cool. Shimoda officials, citing the Japanese text of the treaty, argued that the appointment of a consul required mutual agreement that the need had arisen and that it had not. Harris relied on the English text, which he read as permitting the appointment. The disagreement over the clause illustrates the problem of treaties negotiated through several languages. Harris waited, pressed his case, and gradually gained the cooperation of Japanese officials, with whom he developed a patient, if sometimes strained, working relationship.

In June 1857, Harris and the shogunate's representatives signed the Shimoda Convention, an additional agreement that allowed Americans to reside at Shimoda and Hakodate, opened Nagasaki to American ships, and provided for American consular jurisdiction over American citizens in the ports, an early form of extraterritoriality. It also fixed an arrangement for the exchange of currency that would later become controversial. By then, a change in the shogunate's leadership had occurred. Abe Masahiro had stepped down as chief of the council in 1855, passing the position to Hotta Masayoshi, a senior councilor more sympathetic to the case for foreign trade. Abe died in July 1857. In December 1857, Harris was received in audience by the shogun Iesada at Edo Castle and presented the case for a commercial treaty to Hotta, warning of Britain's growing power in China. The full treaty of 1858 and the political consequences that followed belong to Chapter 52.

Section 51.28

From Diplomatic Accommodation to a Crisis of Political Legitimacy

By the end of 1857 the shogunate had concluded or was preparing agreements with the United States, Britain, Russia, and the Netherlands, was building a modest navy and training school, and was in negotiations that would lead to a commercial treaty. It had survived the death of a shogun, major disasters, and the challenge of foreign pressure. In many respects it was functioning effectively as a government. Yet beneath this surface, the political basis of its authority had changed.

Several developments contributed to the change. Concessions to foreign powers, however carefully limited, exposed the gap between the shogunate's claim to protect the realm and its ability to do so. Military weakness, evident in the first confrontation and only partly remedied since, remained a source of vulnerability. The consultation of the daimyō, intended to build support, had expanded the circle of those who felt entitled to participate in national policy and revealed deep disagreement. The notification of the imperial court, a departure from precedent, drew the court into foreign policy and gave it a role that it would use more actively in the years ahead. And the question of succession, with a childless and frail shogun, added a further source of uncertainty.

No single event produced a crisis of legitimacy. It emerged from the accumulation of responses, each reasonable in isolation, that together altered the political landscape. The shogunate had traded precedent for flexibility and had, in doing so, invited questions that its institutions were not designed to answer: who had the right to decide on matters of foreign policy, how should the court be involved, and what was the proper relationship between shogunate and domains? These questions would intensify with the commercial treaties of 1858, imperial loyalism, and the political violence examined in Chapter 52. The Black Ships did not cause the fall of the Tokugawa, but they set in motion a series of choices that made the government's position progressively harder to sustain.

Cause and Effect

How Naval Pressure Became a Domestic Political Crisis

Long-term causes

Western naval and commercial expansion in Asia, the lesson of the Opium War, American Pacific ambitions, the shogunate's reliance on precedent and limited institutions, fiscal strain on the shogunate and domains, and the growth of political discussion among samurai and scholars.

Short-term causes

Perry's arrival in 1853 and return in 1854, the shogun's death, the lack of a strong central leader, military weakness exposed at Uraga, and the pressure of simultaneous Russian, British, and Dutch approaches.

Event or transformation

The shogunate negotiated limited treaties, consulted the daimyō, informed the court, and began military reform, thereby altering the way foreign policy decisions were made and legitimated.

Immediate effects

Opening of Shimoda and Hakodate, extension of treaty practice to Britain, Russia, and the Netherlands, expansion of naval and technical education, and new fiscal burdens on domains and communities.

Long-term consequences

Growing political participation by domains and the court, deepening disagreement over foreign policy, the commercial treaties of 1858, the rise of imperial loyalism, and the eventual overthrow of the shogunate examined in Chapters 52 and 53.

Historical complexity

The outcome was not inevitable. Domestic factors, individual choices, international developments such as the Crimean War and the second Opium War, and natural disasters all shaped events. The crisis arose from the interaction of external pressure and internal political structure.

Why one cause is not enough: Attributing the shogunate's difficulties to Perry alone ignores earlier warnings, the shogunate's own choices, and the domestic politics of consultation and succession. Attributing them solely to internal weakness ignores the coercive international context.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that the Black Ships transformed an international diplomatic problem into a crisis of domestic political authority, and that they did so through the interaction of foreign pressure with the structure of the Tokugawa political system rather than through a single dramatic shock. The shogunate in 1853 was an experienced and capable government, with regulated channels of foreign contact, access to Dutch and Chinese information, and a record of adjusting its coastal policy. It was not an isolated or sleeping regime.

The pressure that confronted it came from converging developments: the Opium War, which demonstrated Western naval power; American continental and Pacific expansion, which created demand for coaling stations, protection for whalers, and access to the China trade; and the technology of steam navigation. Earlier approaches by Russian, British, and American ships had been refused or deflected. Commodore Perry's expedition combined a limited set of stated objectives with an explicit willingness to use force and with a determination to bypass Nagasaki and deal with the highest authority he could reach.

At Uraga in July 1853, the shogunate chose to receive President Fillmore's letter rather than risk confrontation, and then deferred a reply. The decision allowed it to preserve the formal principle that foreign business was handled by established procedures, while acknowledging in practice that the procedures had been overridden. The encounter exposed military vulnerability, and the death of the shogun Ieyoshi weeks later deprived the government of strong leadership at the moment it was needed.

Abe Masahiro responded by combining preparation with consultation. The circulation of the letter and the request for opinions from daimyō and officials produced a wide range of views, from resistance to limited accommodation, and implicitly acknowledged that foreign policy was a matter of concern for the domains. The court was informed, departing from precedent. These steps were reasonable ways to build support, but they widened the circle of those who felt entitled to participate in national decisions.

When Perry returned in 1854, the shogunate negotiated the Treaty of Kanagawa. Japanese negotiators, led by Hayashi Fukusai, secured the exclusion of general trade and the choice of Shimoda and Hakodate, ports distant from the capital, while conceding access for supplies, protection for shipwrecked sailors, a most-favored-nation clause, and a provision for a consul. The treaty was neither a free exchange nor a simple imposition. It established the principle of treaty relations with a Western power outside the Nagasaki system and set a precedent for others.

The precedent was quickly followed. Britain concluded an agreement at Nagasaki in 1854, shaped by the Crimean War. Russia, after the earthquake and tsunami that destroyed the Diana, signed the Treaty of Shimoda in 1855, drawing a boundary between Etorofu and Urup through waters inhabited by Ainu communities and leaving Sakhalin undivided. The Dutch secured a revised framework and helped establish naval training. Perry concluded a compact with the Ryukyu Kingdom, whose position between Satsuma and the Qing was strained by the American demand. The geography of foreign pressure thus extended from Nagasaki to the Kurils to Naha.

Domestically, the shogunate began reforming its defenses. It built the Odaiba batteries, lifted the ban on large ships, opened the Nagasaki Naval Training Center, and established the Kōbusho and the Bansho Shirabesho. These efforts reflected selective borrowing and initiative, not passive imitation, but they were limited and costly. The burdens fell on domains, coastal communities, and laborers, at a time when major earthquakes also devastated Shimoda and Edo. Foreign policy became a subject of discussion among domain officials, scholars, and townspeople.

By 1857 Townsend Harris was pressing for a commercial treaty, and the shogunate's leadership had shifted toward Hotta Masayoshi. The shogunate was still functioning and adapting, but concessions, military weakness, disagreement among daimyō, the involvement of the court, and uncertainty about succession had unsettled the foundations of its authority. The questions of who could decide foreign policy and how the court and domains should participate remained unresolved, and they would dominate the following years.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a political system can be destabilized by decisions that are individually sensible. The shogunate's officials were not fools or fatalists. They gathered information, weighed options, bargained, and prepared. Yet each step they took to manage the foreign threat altered the distribution of political authority at home. The lesson is not that the Tokugawa order was doomed, but that its institutions were poorly suited to a problem that required both rapid decisions and broad legitimacy.

The chapter also reveals the plurality of Japan's frontiers. Perry's expedition touched the Ryukyu Kingdom, the Bonins, Shimoda, and Hakodate; the Russian treaty cut through Ainu lands; and the Dutch connection at Nagasaki shaped how the crisis was understood. A history centered only on Edo would miss these dimensions. The treaties also created precedents, notably the most-favored-nation principle and consular jurisdiction, that would structure Japan's international position for decades.

What the crisis shaped is visible in the chapters that follow. The widening of political participation, the involvement of the court, and the disagreement among daimyō prepared the ground for the politics of Chapter 52, with its commercial treaties, imperial loyalism, and violence. The naval and educational institutions of the mid-1850s were early expressions of a selective borrowing that continued, in different forms, through the Meiji period. And the Ryukyuan and Ainu experiences foreshadow the later incorporation of Okinawa and Hokkaido into the modern state.

Students should also carry forward a methodological caution. The Black Ships are among the most heavily mythologized episodes in Japanese history, and the legends of sudden awakening and universal panic persist because they offer a simple story. The evidence supports a more complicated and more interesting one, in which Japanese officials, domains, scholars, and ordinary residents, along with foreign diplomats and sailors, made choices within constraints, and in which the outcome was not predetermined.
