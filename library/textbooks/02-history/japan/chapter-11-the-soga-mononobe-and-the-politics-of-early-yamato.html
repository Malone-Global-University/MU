Part II — Yamato and the Formation of Ancient Japan
11

The Soga, Mononobe, and the Politics of Early Yamato

Japanese History: From Jōmon Japan to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

In the summer of 587, an alliance of Yamato princes and lineage leaders marched against the stronghold of a single powerful minister in the marshy lowlands of Kawachi. When the fighting ended, one of the two great houses that had dominated the Yamato court for a generation lay broken, its leader dead and its central influence shattered, while its rival emerged as the court's dominant power. To later Buddhist chroniclers, the episode would be remembered as a victory of the new faith over its enemies. The truth, as this chapter will show, was more tangled and more historically interesting than that memory allows.

Chapter 10 left Buddhism newly arrived at the Yamato court: a foreign cult, a set of images and texts, a controversial gift from the kingdom of Baekje, and, above all, an opportunity. It also left a court still organized as it had been for generations — as a shifting coalition of powerful uji, or lineage groups, gathered loosely around a paramount ruler whose authority depended on continuing consent from just such lineages. Two of those lineages, the Soga and the Mononobe, had for decades occupied the highest non-royal offices in the Yamato polity. This chapter follows what happened when a new religious practice, an old rivalry between two great houses, and an unusually unstable run of royal succession all became entangled with one another across the final decades of the sixth century.

The central episode of the chapter is well known in outline: in 587, forces loyal to Soga no Umako defeated and killed Mononobe no Moriya, ending Mononobe dominance at court and clearing the way for a dramatic expansion of Soga influence and of institutional Buddhist patronage. That much is not seriously disputed. What is far more easily misunderstood is why the conflict happened, who actually fought in it, and what it did and did not settle. Popular summaries often compress the episode into a tidy morality tale: a pious, progressive Soga clan champions Buddhism against a reactionary, xenophobic Mononobe clan defending the old gods, and the story ends with Buddhism triumphant and the Soga in unchallenged command of Japan. Nearly every clause of that sentence requires correction.

The Soga and Mononobe were not simply "for" or "against" a religion in the way a modern reader might imagine two political parties debating an issue. They were rival aristocratic houses whose power rested on marriage alliances with the royal line, control of dependents and estates, command of ritual and military functions at court, and connections — in the Soga case, unusually deep ones — to continental migrant communities and their skills. Buddhist patronage mattered to this rivalry because it offered new resources: new ritual prestige, new diplomatic connections to Baekje and the wider peninsular and continental world, new specialists whose skills a patron could claim as his own achievement. But the crisis that destroyed Mononobe power in 587 was set in motion above all by the death of a ruler and the resulting scramble among rival princes for the throne — a scramble in which Buddhist patronage was one resource among several, not the whole of the story.

Nor did 587 install Soga no Umako as an unchallenged ruler of Japan. The ruler who took the throne after the fighting, a prince remembered as Sushun, would himself be assassinated five years later, reportedly on Umako's own orders — a stark demonstration that the relationship between a great minister and a reigning sovereign remained genuinely unsettled even after Mononobe power had been broken. Only with the accession in 592 of the royal woman later known as Suiko did the Yamato court arrive at a new and comparatively durable political configuration, one that would make possible the more famous statecraft associated with the following reign and examined in Chapter 12.

This chapter, then, is deliberately a chapter about instability, coalition, and contingency rather than a chapter about the triumphant arrival of a new religion or the rise of a single dominant clan. It asks students to hold several kinds of causation in mind simultaneously: religious change, dynastic crisis, lineage rivalry, and the personal ambitions of specific historical actors, none of which can be fully separated from the others in this period. It also asks students to read the chronicle tradition that preserves this history — above all the Nihon Shoki, compiled more than a century after these events — with the same historical care this textbook has applied to court sources throughout Part II.

By the chapter's end, students should be able to explain how a court epidemic, a disputed succession, an armed coalition, and the death of Yōmei followed five years later by the assassination of Sushun reshaped the balance of power at Yamato — and why none of this yet amounted to the centralized, Buddhist-sponsoring, Soga-dominated state that later tradition sometimes implies. That state, if it existed at all, still lay ahead, in the reign explored in the chapter that follows.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why the Soga and Mononobe lineages held major political importance at the Yamato court independent of the Buddhist controversy.
  2. Describe the Nihon Shoki tradition connecting the 585 epidemic to disputes over Buddhist worship, and assess it as a historical source.
  3. Trace the succession crisis that followed the death of Yōmei in 587 and its connection to the outbreak of armed conflict.
  4. Distinguish a purely religious explanation of the Soga–Mononobe conflict from a multicausal explanation involving succession politics, lineage rivalry, and coalition-building.
  5. Analyze the 587 campaign against Mononobe no Moriya as a coalition action rather than a private contest between two families.
  6. Assess the consequences of Moriya's defeat for Buddhist institutional patronage, including the founding of Asukadera.
  7. Explain the killing of Sushun in 592 and what it reveals about the limits of Soga power after 587.
  8. Describe the accession of Suiko in 592 and evaluate competing characterizations of her political role.
  9. Identify continuities and changes in Yamato court politics between the early 570s and the early 590s.
  10. Distinguish events narrated in the Nihon Shoki from securely established historical fact, and identify likely retrospective shaping in the chronicle's account.
  11. Avoid anachronistic descriptions of sixth-century kami practice as an organized "Shinto" religion opposed to Buddhism as a rival faith.
  12. Contextualize the Soga–Mononobe conflict as a chapter in ongoing Yamato court politics rather than an isolated religious episode.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How did disputes over Buddhist patronage become entangled with lineage rivalry and succession politics at the late sixth-century Yamato court?
  2. Why did the Soga and Mononobe already matter to Yamato politics before Buddhism became a point of contention between them?
  3. What does the Nihon Shoki's account of the 585 epidemic reveal about how ritual causation, disease, and politics were understood at the Yamato court?
  4. Why did the succession crisis following Yōmei's death in 587 escalate into open warfare?
  5. Was the defeat of Mononobe no Moriya the outcome of a two-family rivalry, or of a broader coalition realignment among Yamato elites?
  6. How did Moriya's defeat change the conditions for Buddhist institutional patronage, and how did it not change them?
  7. What does the killing of Sushun in 592 suggest about the limits of Soga no Umako's power even after 587?
  8. How should historians characterize Suiko's accession in 592 — as a puppet arrangement, a compromise, or a meaningful political reconstruction?

Historical Context

Chapter 10 traced the arrival of Buddhism at the Yamato court, conventionally dated to the mid-sixth century, as a diplomatic gift from the Korean kingdom of Baekje that arrived bundled with images, texts, and ritual specialists. That chapter also introduced the court's initial, divided reaction: some lineages saw opportunity in the new religion's ritual and diplomatic resources, while others worried that welcoming an unfamiliar cult risked offending the kami whose goodwill was understood to protect the realm's health and harvests. Chapter 10 deliberately left the political consequences of that division unresolved, since the crisis it produced belongs to the reigns examined in this chapter.

Students should recall from Chapters 8 and 9 that the Yamato polity of this period was not a centralized state in any modern sense. It was better understood as a paramount lineage — the royal line — surrounded by a shifting constellation of powerful uji, each controlling its own dependents, estates, and specialized functions, and each capable of making or breaking a ruler's authority through its cooperation or its withdrawal of support. Offices such as ōomi ("great minister," associated with the Soga in this period) and ōmuraji ("great chieftain," associated with the Mononobe and the Ōtomo) marked leadership among these lineages rather than bureaucratic positions in a centralized administrative hierarchy of the kind that would only emerge after the Taika reforms described in Chapter 13.

Royal succession in this period did not follow a fixed rule of primogeniture. Succession moved among various branches of the royal line according to negotiation, precedent, and the relative strength of competing candidates' maternal kin and allied lineages — a pattern that made every royal death a potential occasion for crisis. This chapter's narrative, concentrated within roughly two decades, should be read against that structural instability: three rulers occupy the throne across the chapter's span. Yōmei died after a short reign marked by illness, and his death opened a succession crisis that culminated in armed conflict; Sushun, the ruler who followed, was assassinated in the later tradition on the orders of the court's leading minister. Only with the fourth ruler, Suiko, does the chapter arrive at a reign of unusual length and stability.

Finally, students should bear in mind a methodological point carried forward from Chapter 10: the principal narrative source for this period, the Nihon Shoki, was compiled in 720, more than a century after the events this chapter describes. Its account of the Soga–Mononobe conflict reflects the interests, religious commitments, and political circumstances of the early eighth-century court that produced it, including that court's investment in Buddhism as an established and legitimate element of royal rule. This does not mean the chronicle's narrative should be dismissed, but it does mean that its framing — particularly its tendency to present Moriya's defeat as a victory secured through Buddhist merit and divine favor — requires the same source-critical treatment applied to other retrospective court narratives throughout this textbook.

Section 11.1

A Court Divided

By the final third of the sixth century, Buddhism had gained a foothold at the Yamato court, but a foothold is not the same thing as an institution. Images and texts had arrived; a handful of specialists, some of continental migrant descent, maintained rudimentary worship; and at least one prominent lineage had begun to sponsor Buddhist practice as part of its own display of prestige. None of this yet amounted to a settled place for Buddhism within Yamato political life. Turning a foreign cult into a durable institution required sustained elite sponsorship — temples, ritual specialists supported over the long term, land and labor devoted to religious construction and maintenance — and sustained sponsorship required political power secure enough to commit such resources without being immediately challenged or reversed by rivals.

The Yamato court of this period was not a single unified government issuing binding decisions from a fixed center in the modern sense. It was, as earlier chapters have emphasized, a coalition of powerful lineages gathered around a paramount ruling house whose own authority depended on the continuing cooperation of those lineages. The principal actors relevant to this chapter therefore include not only the ruling house itself — the successive occupants of the throne whose reigns structure the narrative — but also the great uji whose support or opposition could make or unmake a ruler's position: the Soga, the Mononobe, and a wider circle of allied lineages, migrant-descended households, and ritual specialists whose interests intersected with the unfolding crisis.

It is important to resist, from the outset, a tempting but misleading shorthand: describing "the Soga" and "the Mononobe" as though they were political parties or ideological movements united behind a single, coherent position on Buddhism. Both were extended aristocratic households with their own internal hierarchies, marriage networks, and dependents, whose leaders — in this period, Soga no Umako and Mononobe no Moriya — made decisions shaped by the specific political circumstances they faced, not by fixed doctrinal commitments. As the next section shows, these two men and their houses had accumulated power and rivalry for reasons that had little to do with Buddhism before the religious controversy became entangled with their competition at all.

Section 11.2

Soga and Mononobe: Power Before the Buddhist Conflict

To understand why the crisis of 587 unfolded as it did, students must first understand why the Soga and Mononobe mattered at the Yamato court independent of any dispute over Buddhism. Both lineages had, by the mid-sixth century, become central to the functioning of the Yamato polity, but they drew their strength from different — and in some respects complementary — sources.

The Soga lineage's power rested substantially on marriage alliances with the royal house. Soga women had married into the ruling line across multiple generations, producing royal children whose maternal kin were therefore Soga, and giving the lineage a direct stake in the outcome of succession disputes whenever a Soga-descended candidate stood among the contenders. Beyond marriage politics, the Soga had also built influence through administrative and fiscal roles connected to the management of royal granaries, treasuries, and the labor and resources drawn from various dependent groups — functions that gave the lineage practical control over resources the court depended upon. Crucially, the Soga also maintained unusually close ties to continental migrant communities settled in the Yamato region, communities that brought skills in writing, accounting, craft production, and — increasingly, after the events described in Chapter 10 — Buddhist ritual practice. These connections positioned the Soga to sponsor and claim credit for exactly the kind of new continental learning, including Buddhism, that could enhance a lineage's prestige at court.

The Mononobe lineage, by contrast, drew its strength from a long-standing association with military functions and court ritual duties connected to weapons and, in some traditions, to purification rites, together with the ōmuraji title itself, which placed the lineage among the highest-ranking non-royal households at court. The Mononobe also commanded an extensive network of dependents and allied households built up over generations of service to the royal line, giving Moriya, as the lineage's head in this period, substantial independent capacity to mobilize armed support when a political crisis demanded it — a capacity that would prove decisive, in both directions, in 587.

Students should resist imagining these two houses as isolated blocs facing off across a clean political or ethnic divide. Elite kinship at the Yamato court cut across such boundaries in ways that complicate any simple two-camp narrative: later genealogical tradition records that Umako's wife was reportedly Moriya's sister, meaning that the two rival ministers were, by marriage, brothers-in-law. Whatever the precise accuracy of this specific genealogical claim, it illustrates a broader and well-supported point: the great lineages of Yamato were bound together by marriage even as they competed for position, and a political rivalry between two houses did not necessarily mean an absence of personal or family connection between their leaders.

Section 11.3

Bidatsu's Court and the Buddhist Crisis of 585

It is at this point, during the reign traditionally dated from 572 and associated with the ruler remembered as Bidatsu, that Chapter 10's religious background becomes directly entangled with court politics. The Nihon Shoki tradition preserves an account in which an epidemic afflicting the Yamato region became bound up with an argument over Buddhist worship: Mononobe no Moriya, joined by the ritual specialist Nakatomi no Katsumi, argued that the spread of disease reflected the anger of the kami at the toleration — and, in the Soga case, active sponsorship — of an unfamiliar foreign cult. According to this account, Buddhist images and structures associated with Soga patronage were attacked or destroyed in the aftermath of this argument, an act framed in the chronicle as an attempt to appease offended deities and restore the ritual order believed necessary for the community's health and security.

This episode deserves careful handling as a historical source rather than as a transparent factual report. It survives only through the Nihon Shoki's retrospective narrative, compiled in 720 by a court deeply invested in Buddhism's legitimacy, and its framing — in which resistance to Buddhism is directly punished by continuing misfortune, while Buddhist patronage is eventually vindicated — bears the marks of a narrative shaped for religious and political purposes rather than a neutral chronicle entry. Students should therefore learn to state the episode carefully: the Nihon Shoki connects the 585 epidemic to a court dispute over Buddhist worship and describes an attack on Buddhist images and structures in its aftermath. This is a meaningfully different — and more historically honest — claim than asserting flatly that "Buddhism caused an epidemic, so the court banned it," which mistakes the chronicle's own interpretive framework for an established causal fact.

Read this way, the episode becomes a valuable case study in its own right: it shows students how disease, ritual causation, and court politics could be understood as inseparable from one another in this period, and how a religious dispute could simultaneously function as a political maneuver — an opportunity for Moriya to publicly challenge Soga prestige and for the chronicle's later compilers to frame Mononobe resistance as both religiously mistaken and, in hindsight, politically doomed. Bidatsu's reign ended, according to the traditional chronology, not long after these events, with his death opening the succession question that would dominate the following years.

Section 11.4

Yōmei and the Escalation of Court Rivalry

The ruler remembered as Yōmei occupied the throne only briefly, traditionally from 585 to 587. Later sources associate Yōmei with personal interest in Buddhist devotion, particularly during an illness late in his reign, and portray this interest as sharpening the existing disagreement between Umako and Moriya over the proper place of Buddhist worship at court. As with the 585 epidemic narrative, students should treat this characterization of Yōmei's personal piety as chronicle tradition rather than as independently documented biography; what can be stated with more confidence is that the short reign coincided with, and likely intensified, the ongoing rivalry between the two great ministers.

It would be a mistake, however, to let the religious dimension of Yōmei's reign obscure the graver structural problem developing alongside it: the Yamato court was once again approaching a succession crisis, and this time the stakes were unusually high. Multiple candidates with plausible claims to the throne were available, each backed by different combinations of maternal kin and allied lineages. Among the figures who become important at this point are Yōmei himself; his rival or successor candidates, including Prince Anahobe and Prince Hatsusebe; Prince Takeda, another royal prince named in the tradition among those who aligned against Anahobe's succession; and, more distant from the immediate crisis but historically significant for later chapters, the royal woman Nukatabe, already an important figure at court as Bidatsu's former consort and the future ruler Suiko. Students should also note, without dwelling on him here, that Prince Umayado — later known by the more famous name Shōtoku — begins to appear in genealogical and biographical tradition around this period as well, though his major political role belongs to Chapter 12 rather than to this one.

The convergence of these two pressures — an intensifying religious dispute between the court's two leading ministers, and an approaching succession crisis with several plausible royal candidates — set the stage for the events of 587. Neither pressure alone would necessarily have produced armed conflict; together, they created conditions in which a disputed succession could easily become a proxy contest for the religious and lineage rivalries already dividing the court.

Section 11.5

Succession, Anahobe, and the Crisis of 587

Yōmei's death in 587 brought the succession question to a head. According to the traditional narrative, Prince Anahobe emerged as a leading claimant to the throne, and Mononobe no Moriya threw his considerable political and military weight behind Anahobe's candidacy. This alliance made sense in terms of the rivalries already discussed: an Anahobe succession, secured with Mononobe backing, would have preserved and likely extended Mononobe influence at court, at Soga expense.

Soga no Umako, for his part, did not stand alone in opposition. He assembled a broader coalition of princes and allied lineages opposed to Anahobe's succession — a coalition considerably wider than the Soga house acting on its own. This point deserves emphasis, because it is essential to understanding everything that follows: the crisis of 587 was not, at its core, a contest between two isolated family armies. It was a realignment of a substantial portion of the Yamato elite around competing succession outcomes, with the Soga–Mononobe rivalry serving as one significant axis of division rather than the totality of the conflict.

The sequence of events, as preserved in the traditional narrative, should be understood clearly:

  • The reigning ruler, Yōmei, dies, leaving the succession open.
  • Multiple candidates and their lineage backers begin contending for the throne.
  • Rival coalitions form around these candidates, drawing in princes and allied uji beyond the Soga and Mononobe houses themselves.
  • Prince Anahobe, Moriya's preferred candidate, is killed by the opposing coalition in early summer 587.
  • With his preferred royal candidate eliminated, Moriya becomes politically isolated, his position weakened even before any fighting directly involving his own house begins.
  • Armed confrontation between Moriya's forces and the broader anti-Anahobe coalition, now led decisively by Umako, follows shortly afterward.

This sequence should immediately complicate any simplistic "Buddhists fought anti-Buddhists" model of the conflict. Anahobe's death was a succession killing, not a religious one in any direct sense; Moriya's subsequent isolation was a consequence of backing the losing royal candidate as much as it was a consequence of his stance on Buddhist worship. The religious dispute traced in Sections 11.3 and 11.4 had certainly sharpened hostility between Umako and Moriya and had likely shaped which lineages aligned with which succession coalition, but the proximate cause of the fighting that followed was a contest over the throne itself.

Section 11.6

The Defeat of Mononobe no Moriya

With Anahobe dead and his own political position badly weakened, Mononobe no Moriya withdrew to his family's base of power in the Kawachi region, west of the Yamato basin, where the Mononobe held substantial estates and could draw on a large body of dependents and armed retainers. The coalition arrayed against him, led by Soga no Umako but including, according to the traditional narrative, Prince Hatsusebe, Prince Takeda, the young Prince Umayado, and other major lineages opposed to continued Mononobe dominance, marched against this stronghold in the middle of 587.

The fighting itself should be narrated with care regarding what the sources can and cannot establish. The Nihon Shoki tradition describes a hard-fought engagement in which Moriya's forces initially resisted successfully before the coalition regrouped and ultimately overwhelmed his position; Moriya was killed in the fighting, and Mononobe power at court collapsed sharply in its aftermath. The tradition states that half of Moriya's dependents and his house-site were assigned to the temple foundation later known as Shitennōji, a disposition of his household's resources rather than evidence of a wholesale transfer of Mononobe property into a Soga following. What can be said with confidence is that Mononobe central-court influence collapsed, while Umako emerged from the crisis as by far the most powerful figure at court.

The single most important accuracy guardrail for this section is this: Soga victory in 587 did not mean the Soga house's private army defeating the Mononobe house's private army in an isolated two-family duel. The coalition that destroyed Moriya's position was considerably broader than the Soga lineage alone, incorporating multiple princes and allied uji with their own reasons for opposing continued Anahobe-Mononobe influence. What occurred in 587 is better understood as a broader realignment of Yamato elite power, in which the Soga emerged as the chief beneficiary and organizing force, rather than as a simple bilateral clan war that the Soga happened to win outright on their own.

Later Buddhist tradition would add a further layer to this narrative: accounts describing Prince Umayado, present with the coalition forces, making vows to the Four Heavenly Kings (the Buddhist guardian deities of the four directions) in exchange for victory, and subsequently sponsoring temple construction in fulfillment of that vow. Such accounts should be classified clearly as later Buddhist hagiographic material — pious narrative shaped to associate the young prince with Buddhist merit and divine favor — rather than as straightforward contemporary battlefield reportage. They belong properly to the traditions surrounding Umayado developed more fully in Chapter 12, and they illustrate, in miniature, exactly the kind of retrospective religious framing this chapter has cautioned students to read critically throughout.

Section 11.7

Buddhism After 587

With Mononobe power broken, the most consistent and organized institutional opposition to expanded Buddhist patronage at the Yamato court had been removed. This created conditions considerably more favorable to large-scale Buddhist construction and sponsorship than had existed at any point since Buddhism's arrival, described in Chapter 10. The clearest evidence of this shift is the temple project traditionally dated to 588, when construction began on Asukadera, also known as Hōkōji, a major temple complex sponsored under Soga patronage in the Asuka region that would become the court's political and cultural center in the following decades.

It would nonetheless be a serious overstatement to conclude that "Buddhism won" in any total sense in 587. Kami rites continued alongside expanding Buddhist patronage; opposition to specific instances of Buddhist patronage, or unease about its proper place relative to established ritual practice, did not vanish simply because its most prominent institutional opponent had been defeated. What changed was narrower and more precise than a wholesale religious transformation: large-scale elite Buddhist patronage — the construction of major temples, the sustained support of ritual specialists, the public association of powerful lineages with Buddhist merit — became politically far easier to pursue once the Mononobe could no longer mobilize court-level opposition to it. Buddhism's institutional foothold, established in Chapter 10, now had the political space to grow into something considerably more substantial, a process this chapter's final sections and Chapter 12 will continue to trace.

Section 11.8

Sushun and the Limits of Soga Power

The prince who ascended the throne in the aftermath of the 587 crisis, Hatsusebe, is conventionally known to history by his posthumous name (shigō), Sushun, and reigned from 587 to 592. His accession placed a new ruler on the throne under conditions of enormously increased influence for Soga no Umako, whose coalition had just secured a decisive victory and whose lineage now stood without serious institutional rival among the great court houses.

What followed, however, should prevent students from concluding that 587 simply installed Umako as Japan's uncontested ruler in all but name. According to the traditional narrative, tensions developed over the following years between Sushun and Umako — tensions whose precise causes the sources do not spell out in reliable detail, but which evidently concerned the practical distribution of authority between a reigning sovereign and an extraordinarily powerful minister. In 592, according to the Nihon Shoki tradition, Umako ordered Sushun's assassination, and the killing was carried out by a figure named Yamato no Aya no Koma, a member of a lineage descended from continental migrants — itself a reminder of how thoroughly such migrant-descended households had become integrated into the highest-stakes affairs of the Yamato court by this period.

This episode carries a lesson of central importance to the chapter's overall argument: Umako's victory over Moriya in 587 did not settle the underlying constitutional relationship between a great lineage leader and Yamato kingship. Whatever authority a reigning sovereign was understood to hold, it evidently remained vulnerable to challenge by a minister whose coalition-building capacity had just been dramatically demonstrated on the battlefield. Students should also apply the same source-critical caution here that has structured the chapter throughout: the killing of Sushun is known to historians primarily through the later chronicle tradition, and its account of motive and orders should not be treated as independently, contemporaneously documented fact. What can be stated with confidence is that a reigning ruler died amid strong retrospective attribution of responsibility to the most powerful minister at court — itself sufficient evidence that the political order established in 587 remained genuinely unstable.

Section 11.9

Suiko's Accession and a New Political Balance

Following Sushun's death in 592, the throne passed to Nukatabe, also known by the honorific Toyomike Kashikiya-hime, the royal woman conventionally remembered to history as Suiko. She had already been an important figure at the Yamato court well before her accession, having served as the principal consort of the ruler Bidatsu, whose reign opened this chapter, and having thereby accumulated standing, connections, and political experience across the very crises this chapter has traced. Her accession is conventionally counted as the beginning of Japan's first securely attested reign by a female sovereign, though students should recognize that the precise institutional meaning of female rulership in this period differed considerably from later, more formalized conceptions of imperial succession developed after the ritsuryō reforms examined in Chapter 13.

Students should resist three tempting but misleading characterizations of Suiko's accession. She should not be understood simply as a powerless figurehead installed by Soga no Umako to legitimize his own continued dominance, since such a reading flattens a complex political actor into a passive instrument. She should not be understood merely as an incidental relative of Prince Umayado, whose own prominence would develop from 593 onward, alongside and partly through her reign, rather than in place of it. And she should not be understood as a mere compromise placeholder selected only because no male candidate commanded sufficient support, though the exhaustion of plausible alternatives after Yōmei's death and Sushun's assassination within five years may well have been part of the calculation behind her accession. Her long reign — which Chapter 12 will examine in full — is better understood as part of a genuine political reconstruction following more than a decade of succession instability, one in which Suiko herself, alongside Umako and the soon-to-be-prominent Prince Umayado, played an active and continuing political role.

This chapter closes at the threshold of that reconstruction rather than inside it. Umako remained extraordinarily powerful; Umayado, who would become politically prominent from 593 in the traditional chronology, stood at the edge of that development; Buddhist institutional patronage continued to expand; and new experiments in court ritual, administrative practice, and diplomacy with the continental powers were beginning to take shape. All of this belongs properly to Chapter 12, which examines Suiko's reign, Umayado's statecraft, the Twelve Cap Ranks, the Seventeen-Article Constitution, and renewed diplomatic missions to the Sui court in the depth those subjects deserve.

Section 11.10

What Changed in 587–592?

Before moving forward, it is worth pausing to assess analytically what the crises of 587 and 592 actually changed at the Yamato court, and what they left fundamentally unchanged. This assessment matters because it sets the terms for understanding Chapter 12's account of the Suiko-Umayado-Umako period as a genuine new development rather than a simple continuation of Soga dominance already secured in 587.

What changed: Mononobe dominance at the central court was sharply reduced, and Soga no Umako emerged as by far the most powerful figure at court, though the tradition does not support a picture of Mononobe estates and dependents being wholesale absorbed into a Soga following — only that half of Moriya's dependents and his house-site passed to the Shitennōji foundation. Large-scale institutional Buddhist patronage, exemplified by the Asukadera project begun in 588, became politically far easier to pursue than it had been while Mononobe opposition remained a serious court-level obstacle. Royal succession continued to depend on coalition-building among powerful lineages, but Umako's exceptional personal influence within that continuing coalition politics grew dramatically, as both the elevation and the subsequent killing of Sushun demonstrated. Buddhist institutions became more thoroughly embedded in the highest levels of Yamato politics than at any point since their arrival. And by 592, a new configuration of power had emerged around Suiko, Umako, and the soon-to-be-prominent Umayado, setting the stage for the statecraft examined in the next chapter.

What did not change: Yamato remained a coalition polity organized around powerful lineages rather than a centralized administrative state; nothing in this chapter's events created bureaucratic institutions comparable to those introduced after the Taika reforms in Chapter 13. The Soga did not simply "rule Japan" in any unqualified sense; Sushun's killing demonstrates that even Umako's dramatically increased power remained contested and constitutionally unsettled rather than absolute. Kami rites continued alongside expanding Buddhist patronage, and Buddhism had not become anything resembling a uniform state religion binding on the whole population. Lineage politics, marriage alliances, and coalition-building remained the fundamental mechanisms of Yamato political life, exactly as they had been before 585. And the reigning sovereign, whoever occupied the position, did not yet exercise anything approaching the administrative authority later associated with ritsuryō monarchs — a development that still lay decades in the future.

Holding both lists in mind together — what changed and what endured — gives Chapter 12 a coherent place to begin: a court reorganized around new figures and new possibilities, but still operating according to the older logic of lineage, coalition, and negotiated authority that this chapter has traced from Bidatsu's reign through Suiko's accession.

Historical Vocabulary
Ōomi and Ōmuraji

Ōomi ("great minister") and ōmuraji ("great chieftain") were the two highest non-royal titles at the Yamato court in this period, held in this chapter's events by Soga no Umako and Mononobe no Moriya respectively. These were not bureaucratic administrative posts of the kind later created under the ritsuryō system described in Chapter 13; rather, they marked recognized leadership among the great uji whose cooperation the royal house required to govern effectively.

Holding one of these titles conferred substantial prestige and practical authority — access to the ruler, influence over court decisions, and the ability to mobilize one's own lineage's dependents and resources in support of or opposition to royal policy — but it did not confer command over a standing state apparatus. The holder's real power depended, as this chapter has shown, on marriage connections, economic resources, dependent networks, and coalition-building skill rather than on the title alone.

Historical Vocabulary
Uji

An uji was an extended aristocratic lineage group, often linked to a claimed common ancestor, a hereditary function or occupation at court, and a body of dependents and subordinate households. The Soga and Mononobe were both uji of this kind, as were the many allied and rival lineages mentioned throughout this chapter.

Students should avoid treating an uji as equivalent to a modern political party or a simple extended family. An uji's membership, internal hierarchy, and relationship to the royal house were shaped by genealogy, marriage, economic function, and ritual role simultaneously, and its political behavior in a crisis such as that of 587 reflected the calculations of its leading members rather than any fixed ideological position.

Primary Source Window

Nihon Shoki: The Crisis of 587

Creator: Compilers of the Nihon Shoki, an official court chronicle · Date: events narrated, 587; text compiled, 720 · Type: Official court chronicle, combining annalistic record with retrospective narrative and religious framing

Context: The Nihon Shoki is the principal narrative source for the events of this chapter. Compiled more than a century after 587 by a court that had long since embraced Buddhism as an established element of royal legitimacy, its account of the crisis combines what may be genuine historical memory — the sequence of Yōmei's death, Anahobe's killing, and Moriya's defeat — with clearly retrospective religious interpretation that frames the outcome as vindication for Buddhist patronage.

Paraphrase, part one — Yōmei's illness and the Buddhist question (not a verbatim quotation): Late in his reign, the chronicle describes Yōmei as gravely ill and wishing to venerate the Buddha; when he sought the court's views on this, Moriya and Nakatomi no Katsumi are described opposing such devotion while Umako urged that the ruler's wish be honored, restating at the sickbed the same disagreement already visible in the 585 controversy.
Paraphrase, part two — succession, coalition, and defeat (not a verbatim quotation): Following Yōmei's death, the chronicle describes rival princes and their lineage supporters contending for the throne; Anahobe, backed by Moriya, is described as killed by the opposing coalition; Moriya is then described withdrawing to his Kawachi stronghold, where a coalition led by Umako, after an initially difficult engagement, ultimately defeats and kills him, an outcome the chronicle associates with the eventual flourishing of Buddhist institutions at court.

Analysis: Read carefully, this account layers together at least four distinguishable elements: a political sequence of succession and coalition conflict that may preserve real historical memory; a religious framing that interprets the outcome as evidence of Buddhist merit and kami displeasure at resistance to it; details — such as the precise course of the fighting or the content of vows made before battle — that may reflect later elaboration rather than contemporary record; and an overall narrative shape that reflects the religious and political context of an eighth-century court in which Buddhism was already institutionalized and in which lineages descended from the crisis's victors held continuing standing.

Questions for students:

  1. Does this source describe a religious war, a succession struggle, or both? What evidence within the account supports your answer?
  2. Which elements of the Nihon Shoki's account are more likely to preserve genuine historical memory, and which are more likely to reflect later religious or political shaping?
  3. Why might a chronicle compiled in 720 have had reasons to frame the events of 587 as a triumph secured through Buddhist merit?
  4. How does the more than century-long gap between the events described and the text's compilation affect how historians should use this source?
Historical Debate

Was the Soga–Mononobe Conflict Primarily About Buddhism?

The question: Historians and students alike must weigh how much explanatory weight to give religious controversy, as opposed to succession politics and lineage rivalry, in explaining the crisis of 587. How should the available evidence be balanced?

Interpretation A — The religious interpretation: Buddhist patronage genuinely mattered as a source of conflict in its own right. Moriya and allied ritual specialists opposed Buddhist worship on grounds connected to kami veneration and the perceived risk of offending established deities, while Umako sponsored Buddhism as a matter of genuine religious commitment as well as political advantage. On this view, the 585 epidemic dispute reflects a real and consequential disagreement about proper ritual practice, not merely a pretext for unrelated political maneuvering.
Interpretation B — The political and succession interpretation: The decisive crisis of 587 followed directly from Yōmei's death and revolved around rival royal candidates and the lineage coalitions backing them. On this view, the religious dispute of 585 supplied rhetoric and reinforced existing hostility between Umako and Moriya, but greater causal weight belongs to Yōmei's death, the rival royal candidates who emerged in its wake, and the lineage coalitions that formed around them, with the Buddhist controversy treated as an important contributing factor rather than the primary cause.
Interpretation C — The integrated interpretation: Religion was itself political in this period, and the two supposedly separate causes cannot be cleanly disentangled. Buddhist patronage affected prestige, alliance-building, ritual authority, and continental diplomatic connections, all of which fed directly into the succession contest; the succession contest, in turn, determined which lineage's religious commitments would shape court policy going forward. On this view, asking whether the conflict was "really" about religion or "really" about succession poses a false choice.

Evidence: All three interpretations draw on the same Nihon Shoki narrative, which explicitly links the 585 epidemic to Buddhist controversy while also clearly describing a succession contest following Yōmei's death in 587. The interpretations differ chiefly in how much independent causal weight they assign to each strand of the narrative.

Why it matters: How students resolve this question shapes how they understand the broader relationship between religious change and political power throughout Japanese history — a relationship this textbook will revisit in later chapters concerning State Shinto, religious persecution under the Tokugawa, and postwar new religious movements.

Student task: Using evidence from Sections 11.3 through 11.6, identify one piece of evidence that most strongly supports the religious interpretation and one that most strongly supports the political interpretation, and explain which combination of factors you find most historically persuasive.

Historical Debate

Did 587 Create "Soga Rule"?

The question: Some general accounts describe the period after 587 as one of Soga dominance over the Yamato court, verging on a claim that the Soga effectively ruled Japan. Is this characterization accurate?

Interpretation A — Soga dominance: Umako's power increased enormously after 587: the Mononobe, his chief institutional rival, had been destroyed; his 587 coalition had proven capable of eliminating a royal candidate, and Umako himself was later credited with ordering the assassination of a reigning sovereign in 592; and Buddhist institutions expanding under his patronage further consolidated his prestige. On this view, "Soga dominance" is a fair, if imprecise, shorthand for the following decades.
Interpretation B — Continuing coalition politics: Kings continued to matter as independent political actors; the coalition that defeated Moriya included many lineages beyond the Soga; Sushun's killing shows that even Umako's dramatically increased power remained contested rather than absolute; and Suiko became an active, independent political figure rather than a passive Soga instrument. On this view, Yamato politics after 587 remained fundamentally coalitional, with the Soga as the leading but not the sole or unchallenged power.

Evidence: The killing of Sushun in 592, on Umako's own reported orders, is the strongest single piece of evidence against a simple "Soga rule" narrative: if Umako already ruled unchallenged, the killing of a sitting sovereign would scarcely have been necessary. Conversely, the sheer scale of the Soga lineage's post-587 influence, evident in projects such as Asukadera, supports describing the Soga as the dominant force at court in this period.

Why it matters: This question prepares students directly for Chapter 12's treatment of Suiko, Umako, and Umayado together, since an oversimplified "Soga rule" narrative would obscure the genuine political agency Chapter 12 attributes to Suiko and to the developing statecraft of her reign.

Student task: Write a short paragraph evaluating whether "Soga dominance" or "continuing coalition politics" better describes Yamato court life between 587 and 592, using specific evidence from Sections 11.8 and 11.9.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Before and After 587

What Changed

  • Mononobe political dominance at court collapsed, and Soga no Umako emerged as by far the most powerful figure at court
  • Institutional Buddhist patronage expanded dramatically, exemplified by the Asukadera project begun in 588
  • The balance of power between reigning sovereign and leading minister became visibly more unstable, as demonstrated by Sushun's killing in 592
  • A new political configuration emerged by 592 around Suiko, Umako, and the rising Umayado

What Stayed the Same

  • The uji remained the fundamental unit of Yamato political organization
  • Royal succession continued to depend on coalitions of lineage support rather than fixed inheritance rules
  • Marriage remained a primary tool of political alliance-building
  • Kami rites continued alongside expanding Buddhist patronage
  • Continental migrant-descended lineages continued to play significant roles in court affairs
  • Great lineage leaders retained substantial independent authority over their own dependents and resources

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both the genuine changes of 587–592 and the deeper continuities beneath them prevents students from imagining Yamato court history as a series of disconnected dramatic episodes rather than a continuous, evolving political system that Chapter 12's statecraft will build upon rather than replace.

Cause and Effect

From Epidemic to Succession

Long-term causes

The arrival of Buddhism (Chapter 10) and its adoption as a Soga-sponsored source of prestige; the pre-existing rivalry between Soga and Mononobe rooted in marriage politics, administrative resources, and military and ritual functions; and a succession system dependent on shifting lineage coalitions rather than fixed inheritance.

Short-term causes

The 585 epidemic and its interpretation as evidence of kami displeasure at Buddhist worship; Yōmei's short reign and reported interest in Buddhist devotion; and, decisively, Yōmei's death in 587, which opened an immediate contest among rival royal candidates.

Effect

Coalition polarization around rival succession candidates; the killing of Prince Anahobe; the defeat and killing of Mononobe no Moriya by a broad coalition led by Umako; and the accession of Hatsusebe as Sushun under greatly increased Soga influence.

Long-term consequences

Expanded institutional Buddhist patronage, exemplified by Asukadera; continuing instability between ruler and minister, culminating in Sushun's killing in 592; and the accession of Suiko, establishing the political configuration explored in Chapter 12.

Historical complexity: No single cause — not the epidemic, not the succession dispute, not the Soga–Mononobe rivalry alone — is sufficient by itself to explain the events of 587–592. Each factor amplified and was amplified by the others, and historians who insist on isolating one master cause risk distorting a genuinely multicausal episode.

Everyday Life and Environment

Court Life Amid Crisis

While this chapter has necessarily focused on the highest levels of Yamato political conflict, students should remember that the great lineages whose rivalry structures the narrative depended on the labor, service, and material support of far larger dependent populations. The dependents and retainers who could be mobilized for the Kawachi campaign of 587, the craft specialists and laborers who began constructing Asukadera in 588, and the ritual practitioners who maintained both kami rites and the new Buddhist images described throughout this chapter, together represented a much wider circle of ordinary participants in this history than the named princes and ministers alone.

The epidemic that opened the crisis of 585 is also a reminder that court politics did not unfold in isolation from the everyday realities of disease, harvest, and physical survival that shaped life for the broader population of the Yamato region. Whatever the precise relationship between the epidemic and the religious dispute it triggered at court, the underlying disease itself would have caused genuine suffering among ordinary households — a dimension of the episode the chronicle tradition, focused as it is on elite political actors, records only indirectly, if at all.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced the political crisis that reshaped the Yamato court between the mid-570s and 592, centered on the destruction of Mononobe power in 587 and the eventual accession of Suiko five years later. It has argued throughout that this crisis cannot be reduced to a simple contest between a pro-Buddhist Soga lineage and an anti-Buddhist Mononobe lineage, however persistent that simplified narrative remains in popular summaries of the period.

The Soga and Mononobe mattered at the Yamato court for reasons that had little to do with Buddhism before the religious controversy became entangled with their rivalry at all: Soga strength rested on marriage ties to the royal house, administrative and fiscal roles, and connections to continental migrant communities, while Mononobe strength rested on long-standing military and ritual functions, the ōmuraji title, and an extensive network of dependents. The two houses were bound together by marriage even as they competed for position, a reminder that Yamato elite politics did not divide along clean, isolated factional lines.

The Nihon Shoki tradition connects the 585 epidemic to a dispute over Buddhist worship, an account this chapter has treated as historically significant chronicle tradition rather than as transparent contemporary reportage. The short reign of Yōmei intensified the rivalry between Umako and Moriya while a genuine succession crisis approached. Yōmei's death in 587 converted that rivalry into an open contest for the throne: Prince Anahobe, backed by Moriya, was killed by a broad coalition led by Umako, and Moriya himself was subsequently defeated and killed at his Kawachi stronghold by that same coalition — a coalition considerably wider than the Soga lineage acting alone.

Moriya's defeat removed the most significant institutional obstacle to large-scale Buddhist patronage at court, evident in the Asukadera project begun in 588, but it did not eliminate kami worship, resolve every tension surrounding Buddhism's place at court, or install Umako as an unchallenged ruler. The killing of Sushun in 592, reportedly on Umako's own orders, demonstrates directly that the relationship between a powerful minister and a reigning sovereign remained genuinely unsettled even after Mononobe power had been broken.

Suiko's accession later in 592 established a new and more durable political configuration, one in which Suiko herself, alongside Umako and the soon-to-be-prominent Prince Umayado, would shape the statecraft examined in the chapter that follows. This chapter has been careful not to characterize Suiko as merely a powerless instrument of Soga interests, instead treating her accession as part of a genuine political reconstruction following more than a decade of succession instability.

Throughout, this chapter has modeled a central historical-methods lesson carried forward from earlier chapters in Part II: the Nihon Shoki, compiled well over a century after the events it narrates, requires careful source criticism. Its account of 587 layers a plausible political sequence together with retrospective religious framing that reflects the religious and political context of the Buddhist-sponsoring court that produced the text in 720. Distinguishing these layers is essential to understanding the period accurately.

The chapter has also modeled a broader analytical lesson applicable well beyond this specific episode: major historical transformations rarely have a single cause. Religious controversy, succession politics, lineage rivalry, and individual ambition were inseparable from one another in the crisis of 587–592, and historians who insist on isolating any one of these as the "real" cause risk distorting a genuinely multicausal history.

Finally, the chapter has drawn a clean boundary with the chapters immediately surrounding it. Chapter 10 established that Buddhism had become institutionally possible at the Yamato court; this chapter has shown how that possibility became entangled with succession and lineage power, ending with Moriya's fall, Soga ascendancy, Sushun's killing, and Suiko's succession. Chapter 12 now takes up Suiko's reign, Umayado's statecraft, expanded diplomacy with the Sui court, and the political and institutional experiments that followed — developments made possible by, but analytically distinct from, the crisis this chapter has traced.

Students who understand 587 as neither simply "Buddhism defeats Shinto" nor simply a secular succession war, but as an episode in which religion, kinship, succession, military force, and lineage power had become genuinely inseparable, are well prepared to approach the richer statecraft of the Suiko-Umayado era examined next.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has carried the religious history introduced in Chapter 10 directly into the highest-stakes political conflict of the late sixth-century Yamato court. It has shown that a foreign religion's arrival at court could not remain a purely religious matter for long: Buddhist patronage became entangled almost immediately with the older, deeper currents of lineage rivalry and succession politics that had long structured Yamato political life, producing a crisis whose true complexity resists the simplified narratives that later tradition, and later popular retelling, sometimes prefer.

The chapter's insistence on multicausal explanation matters well beyond this specific episode. Students who learn to resist collapsing the events of 587–592 into a single tidy cause — religion alone, or succession politics alone — acquire an analytical habit this textbook will ask them to apply repeatedly: to the Ōnin War's tangle of succession dispute and shogunal weakness in Chapter 34, to the religious and political dimensions of the Shimabara Rebellion in Chapter 47, and to many later moments where politics and belief, personal ambition and structural condition, prove genuinely inseparable.

The chapter has also reinforced a source-critical lesson carried forward from Chapters 9 and 10: the Nihon Shoki, however indispensable as a narrative source, was compiled by a court with its own stakes in how these events should be remembered. Learning to separate plausible historical sequence from retrospective religious framing — recognizing, for instance, that Umayado's reported battlefield vow belongs to later hagiography rather than contemporary record — prepares students for the more extensive chronicle material Chapter 12 and subsequent chapters will continue to draw upon.

Finally, this chapter has deliberately left Suiko's reign unfinished, closing at the threshold of a political reconstruction rather than narrating it. That restraint matters: it preserves for Chapter 12 the full weight of Suiko, Umako, and Umayado's statecraft, the Twelve Cap Ranks, the Seventeen-Article Constitution, and renewed diplomacy with the Sui court, developments that depended on, but should not be collapsed into, the crisis this chapter has traced. Students who understand 587 as a moment of genuine, multicausal instability — not a simple religious triumph or a completed Soga conquest — are well prepared to see Chapter 12's statecraft as a real historical achievement, built on uncertain and still-settling foundations rather than on any predetermined outcome.
