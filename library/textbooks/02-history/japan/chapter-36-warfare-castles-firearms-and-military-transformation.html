Part VI — Sengoku Japan and Reunification
36

Warfare, Castles, Firearms, and Military Transformation

Japanese History: From Jōmon Japan to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

An army of the mid-sixteenth century did not appear on a battlefield so much as it arrived, over days or weeks, out of a landscape of roads, river crossings, requisitioned rice, borrowed pack horses, and thousands of individual decisions about loyalty, pay, and survival. Long before two forces exchanged arrows, bullets, or spear thrusts, a daimyō's officials had already fought a quieter campaign of muster rolls, granaries, and marching orders — one this chapter treats as no less a part of "warfare" than the clash of arms itself.

Chapter 35 examined how Sengoku daimyō built the territorial governments — the taxation systems, retainer bands, castle-centered administrations, and domain law codes — that made sustained warfare possible in the first place. This chapter turns from that political architecture to the practice of war itself: how armies were composed, mustered, armed, fed, and moved; how fortifications evolved from simple hilltop refuges into elaborate defensive complexes; how firearms entered an already-changing military system; and how commanders coordinated increasingly large forces across a landscape of mountains, rivers, and rice paddies that shaped what was tactically possible.

It is tempting to tell the story of Sengoku warfare as a story about a single object: the matchlock arquebus, said to have arrived at Tanegashima in 1543 and to have rendered centuries of mounted samurai combat obsolete within a generation. That story is not false so much as radically incomplete. Firearms mattered, and this chapter gives them serious attention. But they arrived in the middle of a longer, broader transformation already under way — armies were growing larger, infantry organized around spear-carrying ashigaru were becoming more important, fortifications were becoming more elaborate, and the logistical demands of campaigning were becoming more sophisticated, well before firearms became widespread on Japanese battlefields. Guns intensified and accelerated changes that territorial state-building, described in Chapter 35, had already set in motion.

This chapter also asks students to think about war as a problem of organization as much as a problem of courage or weaponry. An army that could not be fed dissolved regardless of how bravely its soldiers fought. A castle that could not be provisioned fell regardless of how thick its walls were. A daimyō who could not translate tax revenue into marching columns, ammunition, and fortified strongpoints could not compete, however skilled his individual retainers. Understanding Sengoku warfare therefore requires attending to muster rolls and grain requisitions as much as to swords and matchlocks.

At the same time, this chapter resists flattening a century of warfare into a single, uniform "Sengoku way of war." Practices varied enormously by region, by decade, and by the specific terrain, climate, and political circumstances a given daimyō faced. A mountain domain in Kai or Echigo, dependent on narrow valley roads and seasonal snow, fought differently than a domain built around the open plains and river networks of the Kantō or the trade-rich corridors of the Kinai region near Kyoto. Firearms spread unevenly, and different daimyō integrated them into their forces at different paces and in different ways. Readers should therefore treat every generalization in this chapter as describing broad tendencies across an uneven and regionally varied century, not a single fixed template applied uniformly across the archipelago.

Finally, this chapter introduces one of the most heavily mythologized battles in Japanese history, the 1575 Battle of Nagashino, as a case study not only in Sengoku combined-arms tactics but in the dangers of accepting a clean, dramatic historical narrative without examining the evidence and the later retellings that shaped it. Nagashino has often been presented as the moment massed firearms proved decisive over cavalry once and for all. The truth, as this chapter explains, is considerably more complicated — and considerably more instructive about how historians should evaluate famous, frequently repeated stories about technological revolution.

By the chapter's end, students should understand Sengoku warfare not as a simple contest of weapons or a parade of famous commanders, but as a system: armies drawn from an expanding and increasingly organized population of fighters, sustained by the administrative structures examined in Chapter 35, equipped with a widening range of weapons old and new, sheltered and supplied by fortifications of growing sophistication, and constrained throughout by the unglamorous but decisive realities of logistics.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how the territorial state-building examined in Chapter 35 enabled larger, more sustained, and more organized warfare during the Sengoku period.
  2. Describe the changing composition of Sengoku armies, including the roles of mounted samurai, retainers, and ashigaru infantry.
  3. Trace the process by which political and economic authority was converted into mustered field armies.
  4. Compare bows, spears, swords, and firearms as battlefield weapons, and assess how their combined use changed over the century.
  5. Explain the introduction of the arquebus to Japan and its adoption, adaptation, and domestic manufacture.
  6. Evaluate claims that firearms alone revolutionized Japanese warfare, distinguishing gradual adaptation from sudden transformation.
  7. Trace the development of Sengoku fortifications from early mountain castles to larger, more elaborate defensive complexes.
  8. Analyze the logistical demands of sustained campaigning, including provisioning, transport, and seasonal constraints.
  9. Assess regional variation in Sengoku warfare using selected campaigns and sieges as case studies.
  10. Interpret the Battle of Nagashino historiographically, distinguishing evidence from later, simplified retellings.
  11. Connect military transformation during the Sengoku period to the process of reunification examined in later chapters.
  12. Avoid common misconceptions about ashigaru, swords, and the supposedly instantaneous impact of firearms in Japanese warfare.

  2. How did the composition of Sengoku armies change over the sixteenth century, and why?
  3. What administrative and social mechanisms turned political authority into an army in the field?
  4. How did firearms fit into, rather than replace, an already-changing system of weapons and tactics?

Historical Context

Chapter 35 traced the emergence of the Sengoku daimyō as territorial rulers who combined castle-centered administration, retainer organization, cadastral surveys, taxation, and domain law into functioning regional states. That chapter deliberately stopped short of explaining how these institutions translated into military force on campaign. This chapter picks up precisely at that point: the same daimyō governments whose taxation systems, retainer registers, and castle networks Chapter 35 described are the governments whose armies, fortifications, and logistics this chapter now examines.

It is worth recalling briefly why sustained warfare of this scale had become possible at all. The breakdown of centralized Ashikaga authority, discussed in Part V, had left political power fragmented among competing regional lords no longer restrained by an effective central government or a unified military hierarchy. Where earlier medieval warfare had often involved relatively small bands of mounted warriors engaged in seasonal, limited campaigns, the Sengoku period saw territorial rulers with the administrative capacity to mobilize much larger populations, sustain campaigns over longer periods, and invest heavily in fortification and equipment. This chapter explains the military consequences of that capacity.

Students should also recall that the Sengoku period was not a single, uniform century of warfare but an extended, regionally uneven process spanning roughly the late fifteenth century through the political reunification examined in Chapters 39 through 41. Military practices that were standard in the 1560s differed considerably from those of the 1490s, and practices in one region often differed from those in another. This chapter traces general patterns of change while remaining attentive to that unevenness.

Section 36.1

Warfare in a Fragmented Political Landscape

Chapter 35 described how Sengoku daimyō converted fragmented Ashikaga-era authority into functioning territorial governments equipped with cadastral surveys, tax assessment, retainer registers, castle-centered administration, and domain law. This chapter takes those institutions as given and asks a different question: what did they make possible on the battlefield? The short answer is that they made warfare bigger, more sustained, and more organized than it had generally been in earlier medieval Japan, without changing overnight the underlying social order from which armies were drawn.

Warfare in a fragmented political landscape without a single overarching military authority tended, almost by definition, to be frequent. Neighboring daimyō pressed against one another's borders more or less continuously, contesting river valleys, mountain passes, and productive agricultural land. But frequency alone does not explain why campaigns grew larger and more sustained over the course of the sixteenth century. That growth depended directly on the administrative capacity Chapter 35 described: a daimyō who could assess land productivity, register retainer obligations, and store surplus grain in a fortified capital could field a larger army for a longer campaign than one who could not. Military transformation and administrative transformation were, in this sense, two faces of the same underlying process.

This connection matters for how students should read the rest of this chapter. Every development examined here — the growing importance of ashigaru infantry, the spread of firearms, the elaboration of fortifications, the sophistication of logistics — ultimately traces back to the same territorial governments whose formation Chapter 35 already explained. This chapter does not repeat that administrative story. It explains its military consequences.

Section 36.2

Samurai, Ashigaru, and the Changing Composition of Armies

Popular depictions of Sengoku warfare often center on the mounted samurai, sword in hand, engaged in individual combat. The reality of sixteenth-century Japanese armies was considerably more collective, and increasingly centered on organized infantry. Mounted retainers — samurai bound to a daimyō or to an intermediate lord by hereditary or contractual ties, often supported by land grants or stipends — remained an important and prestigious component of Sengoku armies throughout the period, providing shock action, mobility, and, crucially, the officer corps that commanded larger units of foot soldiers. But by the mid-sixteenth century, a growing proportion of most daimyō's fighting strength consisted of ashigaru, foot soldiers organized, armed, and increasingly drilled to fight in formation rather than as individual duelists.

It is important to resist a common oversimplification: the idea that ashigaru were simply untrained peasant mobs thrown into battle as expendable filler, in contrast to a professional samurai class. The reality was more layered. Some ashigaru were indeed drawn from the peasantry, mobilized on a seasonal or emergency basis through the same land-based obligations Chapter 35 described. But many others were more or less permanently attached to a daimyō's military establishment, equipped with standardized weapons, and organized into units under samurai officers who drilled them in coordinated spear or firearm tactics. Over the course of the century, the trend was toward greater organization, more consistent equipment, and more deliberate battlefield coordination of ashigaru units — not toward their remaining a disorganized rabble. The growing military importance of ashigaru reflected the same administrative maturation discussed in Section 36.1: a daimyō capable of assessing land productivity and mobilizing manpower systematically could field, arm, and increasingly train much larger infantry forces than had been common in earlier medieval warfare.

Alongside mounted retainers and ashigaru, Sengoku armies often included other categories of fighters: local warrior households loosely attached to a regional lord, warriors without settled lord-vassal ties sometimes described as ronin, and in some cases armed religious communities or locally mobilized villagers defending their own land. The precise mixture varied by domain, by campaign, and by decade. Students should therefore think of a Sengoku army not as a single uniform type of force but as a layered combination of core retainer bands, organized ashigaru units, and more loosely attached auxiliary fighters, all coordinated — with varying degrees of success — under a daimyō's command structure.

This changing composition had significant tactical consequences, discussed further in Sections 36.4 and 36.6: larger, more organized infantry forces made possible the massed use of spears and, later, firearms in coordinated formation, a very different mode of combat from the individual mounted engagements more characteristic of earlier medieval warfare.

Historical Vocabulary
Ashigaru

Ashigaru ("light feet") were foot soldiers who formed a growing and increasingly organized component of Sengoku daimyō armies over the course of the sixteenth century. Unlike the mounted samurai retainer, whose status typically derived from hereditary attachment to a lord and, often, land tenure, ashigaru status and composition were more varied: some were drawn from the peasantry on a seasonal or emergency basis, while others became more permanently attached to a daimyō's military household, equipped with standardized spears, bows, or, later, firearms, and organized into units under samurai command.

Students should avoid two opposite misconceptions. The first is imagining ashigaru as simply untrained, disposable peasant conscripts — a caricature that understates the organization, equipment, and coordinated tactics many ashigaru units achieved by the mid-to-late sixteenth century. The second is imagining ashigaru as a modern professional standing army in the contemporary sense — a status and level of institutionalization ashigaru forces did not reach until later, more centralized reorganizations associated with reunification, discussed in Chapters 39 through 41. The historically accurate picture lies between these extremes: an infantry force of growing size, organization, and tactical importance, recruited and equipped through the expanding administrative capacity of Sengoku territorial government.

Section 36.3

Mobilizing an Army

Between a daimyō's decision to campaign and an army's arrival on a battlefield lay a substantial process of mobilization that depended directly on the administrative institutions Chapter 35 described. Land surveys and tax assessments, commonly expressed during this period in terms of cash-equivalent land value known as kandaka rather than the later, more standardized kokudaka productivity measure that would be consolidated nationally through Toyotomi Hideyoshi's cadastral surveys discussed in Chapter 40, allowed a daimyō's officials to calculate how many men, horses, and how much equipment a given retainer or locality was obligated to furnish as gun'yaku, or military service obligation. A summons to mobilize — transmitted in writing through documents historians call gunzei saisokujō, orders directing designated warriors and their contingents to take the field — was sent down through a chain of intermediate lords and local officials, each responsible for mustering their own assigned contingent.

Retainer bands, whose organization Chapter 35 examined as a political structure binding vassals to their daimyō, became on campaign the basic building blocks of the field army: a senior retainer's contingent, itself composed of the retainer's own dependents, hired auxiliaries, and ashigaru under his command, joined similar contingents from other retainers to form the daimyō's overall force. Command hierarchy on campaign therefore mirrored, in large part, the political hierarchy of retainer obligation examined in the previous chapter — though skilled ashigaru commanders and specialist officers could also hold significant battlefield authority regardless of hereditary status.

Once mustered, contingents were organized and identified using a range of visual and administrative devices: banners (sashimono) worn on the back of individual soldiers to identify their unit and lord, larger command banners marking a commander's position, and signaling devices including drums, conch-shell horns, and flags used to transmit orders across a battlefield too large and too noisy for spoken commands alone. Messengers moved between contingents and command headquarters, and increasingly formalized march orders and camp discipline governed how a large army moved and encamped without collapsing into disorganized chaos — a discipline this chapter's discussion of logistics in Section 36.8 treats as no less important than tactical skill.

This mobilization process is the direct bridge between Chapter 35's political and administrative institutions and this chapter's account of battlefield mechanics: without functioning tax assessment, retainer registration, and a chain of command capable of transmitting orders reliably, none of the tactical developments discussed in the sections that follow would have been possible on the scale the Sengoku period eventually achieved.

Section 36.4

Weapons before and alongside Firearms

Long before the arquebus arrived, Japanese warriors fought with an established repertoire of weapons that continued to matter throughout, and indeed beyond, the Sengoku period. The bow (yumi), a long asymmetrical weapon requiring years of training to use effectively from horseback or on foot, remained an important missile weapon throughout the sixteenth century, valued for its range and, in skilled hands, its accuracy, even as firearms spread. The spear (yari), relatively simple to manufacture and, compared to the bow, comparatively quick to train large numbers of ashigaru to use in massed formation, became an increasingly central infantry weapon as army sizes grew, since disciplined ranks of spearmen could present a formidable defensive and offensive wall largely independent of years of individual training. Polearms such as the naginata, historically significant in earlier medieval warfare, continued to see use, particularly among some warrior households and in defensive contexts, though the straight thrusting spear increasingly dominated massed infantry formations.

The sword (katana), whatever its later cultural prominence, functioned on the Sengoku battlefield primarily as a secondary or close-quarters weapon rather than the primary instrument of infantry or cavalry combat that popular culture often suggests. Swords mattered enormously as symbols of status, as finely crafted objects, and as backup weapons when spears, bows, or firearms were unavailable or exhausted, but the battlefield itself was dominated by missile weapons and polearms used in coordinated group formation, not by sword-wielding duels between individual warriors. Students encountering later popular or cinematic depictions of Sengoku combat should recognize that these often reflect much later cultural reinterpretation — some of it associated with Tokugawa-era and modern martial culture discussed in later chapters — rather than sixteenth-century battlefield reality.

Mounted combat continued throughout the period, though its tactical role shifted as infantry forces grew larger and more organized. Mounted retainers provided mobility, shock action against disorganized or retreating forces, and reconnaissance, but massed cavalry charges against organized, spear-armed infantry formations — let alone firearm-equipped positions — became increasingly costly as the century progressed, a development discussed further in Section 36.6. The tactical combination of missile weapons (bow and, later, firearm), close-order spear infantry, and mounted retainers used for flanking, pursuit, or shock at decisive moments defined much of mid-to-late Sengoku battlefield practice, well before firearms became widespread and largely unchanged in its basic logic afterward.

Section 36.5

The Arquebus Comes to Japan

According to the conventional account, in 1543 a Chinese vessel carrying Portuguese traders was driven by a storm to the island of Tanegashima, off the southern coast of Kyushu, where the local lord acquired matchlock firearms from the visitors and had Japanese smiths begin reproducing them. This chapter treats 1543 as the conventional date associated with the weapon's introduction rather than as a precisely and independently verified single moment; the broader story of Portuguese maritime trade, the individuals involved, and the wider Nanban cultural and commercial contact that followed belongs primarily to Chapter 37, which examines European contact, trade, and Christianity in full. This chapter's concern is narrower: what happened to Japanese warfare once the weapon existed within the archipelago.

The matchlock arquebus, known in Japan as teppō (and by other regional and period-specific terms, including a name derived from Tanegashima itself), was a firearm ignited by a slow-burning match lowered mechanically into a pan of priming powder. It fired a lead ball with considerably greater penetrating power at short-to-medium range than a bow, and, importantly, required substantially less individual training to achieve basic battlefield competence than the years of practice demanded by effective mounted or foot archery. This combination — high penetration and a comparatively low training threshold — made the weapon attractive to daimyō seeking to arm the growing ashigaru infantry forces discussed in Section 36.2 without the extended training investment archery required.

What followed was not dependence on continued imports but rapid domestic reproduction. Japanese smiths, particularly in commercial centers such as Sakai, near Osaka, and Kunitomo, in Ōmi Province, began manufacturing matchlocks within years of the weapon's introduction, adapting European design to available materials and manufacturing techniques and, in some respects, improving aspects of the weapon's construction. By the mid-to-late sixteenth century, several regions of Japan possessed substantial domestic firearms manufacturing capacity, and individual daimyō competed to acquire, produce, and equip their forces with growing numbers of matchlocks. This rapid indigenous adaptation is itself historically significant: it demonstrates a sophisticated metalworking and manufacturing base already present in Sengoku Japan, capable of absorbing and reproducing new technology quickly rather than remaining dependent on a foreign supply.

Historical Vocabulary
Teppō

Teppō, literally "iron cannon" or "iron gun," is the general Japanese term for the matchlock arquebus introduced to Japan in the conventional 1543 account and subsequently manufactured domestically. The weapon is also sometimes called by a name derived from Tanegashima, the island associated with its introduction. A matchlock ignites its powder charge using a slow-burning match mechanically lowered into a priming pan, a comparatively simple mechanism that Japanese smiths were able to reproduce and adapt rapidly.

Students should be careful not to treat the appearance of the teppō as, by itself, a self-explanatory cause of military revolution. As Sections 36.6 and the Historical Debate feature below discuss, the weapon's battlefield significance depended on how it was integrated into existing infantry organization, fortification, and tactics — a process of adaptation that unfolded over decades rather than instantly.

Section 36.6

Firearms and Battlefield Adaptation

The arquebus possessed real tactical advantages, but also significant limitations that shaped how, and how quickly, it was integrated into Sengoku armies. A matchlock's rate of fire was considerably slower than a skilled archer's rate of shooting, since loading required measuring powder, ramming a ball and wadding down the barrel, priming the pan, and preparing the burning match — a sequence that, even with practice, took appreciably longer than nocking and loosing an arrow. Matchlocks were also vulnerable to wet weather, since damp powder or an extinguished match rendered the weapon useless, a serious constraint during Japan's rainy seasons and humid climate. Effective range, while the weapon's penetration at shorter distances exceeded that of the bow, was in some respects more limited than long-range archery, meaning the two missile weapons were not simply interchangeable but suited to somewhat different tactical roles.

Japanese commanders and ashigaru units addressed the slow reload problem primarily through organizational rather than purely technological means. By the late sixteenth century, Japanese forces demonstrably employed forms of alternating or staggered fire — arranging gunners so that some ranks discharged their weapons while others reloaded, sustaining a more continuous rate of fire than any individual gunner could achieve alone. Such practices are securely documented, for instance, among Japanese forces during the 1590s invasions of Korea. The precise development, timing, and organization of these techniques earlier in the century, however — and in particular their specific use at any single famous engagement such as Nagashino, discussed in Section 36.9 — remain genuinely debated among historians, and students should be cautious of textbook or popular accounts that describe a fixed, formally drilled "three-rank volley system" as an established fact of the 1570s. What can be said with confidence is that Japanese commanders across the later Sengoku period experimented with organizational solutions to the matchlock's slow reload time, and that this experimentation represents the kind of tactical adaptation this chapter emphasizes throughout: firearms became effective not simply by existing, but by being incorporated into disciplined unit organization, itself a continuation of the growing infantry coordination already under way before firearms arrived, as discussed in Section 36.2.

Firearms were also integrated with, rather than substituted for, existing weapons and fortification. Gunners were frequently positioned behind field fortifications — wooden palisades, earthen embankments, or improvised barriers — that protected them during the vulnerable reloading process while allowing them to fire on approaching infantry or cavalry. Spear-armed ashigaru continued to provide close-quarters defense against forces that closed the distance before gunners could inflict maximum damage, and mounted retainers continued to provide flanking action, pursuit of a broken enemy, and shock action where circumstances favored it. The resulting combined-arms system — missile fire (bow and matchlock), close-order spear infantry, field fortification, and mounted maneuver, all coordinated through the command and mobilization structures discussed in Section 36.3 — represented the mature Sengoku military system of the later sixteenth century, a system to which firearms contributed a significant but not solitary role.

It is equally important to note what firearms did not immediately or completely do. Bows did not disappear; they remained in use throughout the period and well beyond it, valued for range and versatility in circumstances unfavorable to matchlocks. Swords and spears did not become obsolete; close-quarters combat remained a real and frequent feature of Sengoku battles. Mounted retainers did not vanish from the battlefield; cavalry continued to serve important tactical functions even as massed frontal cavalry charges against prepared firearm and spear positions became increasingly costly. The picture that emerges from careful study of the evidence is one of gradual, uneven, and creative adaptation — daimyō and commanders experimenting with how best to combine old and new weapons under the specific conditions of terrain, manpower, and opponent they faced — rather than a single dramatic moment in which firearms swept away an older way of war.

Section 36.7

Castles, Fortifications, and Siege Warfare

Chapter 35 examined the castle primarily as an administrative center: the seat of a daimyō's government, the hub of retainer organization, and the symbolic center of territorial rule. This section returns to the castle from a different angle, examining its military function as a defensive work and the object of siege warfare — and tracing how that military function changed dramatically over the course of the Sengoku period.

Early and mid-Sengoku fortifications were typically mountain castles, or yamashiro, built on defensible high ground using the terrain itself as the primary defensive asset. Construction relied heavily on earthworks — cut ditches, raised embankments, and terraced levels called baileys (kuruwa) arranged along a hillside or ridge — supplemented by wooden palisades, gates, and watchtowers rather than extensive stone walls. These fortifications were relatively quick to construct, well suited to the limited resources of earlier Sengoku daimyō, and effective at slowing an attacking force forced to climb narrow, exposed approaches under fire from defenders above. Students should resist the temptation to picture these early fortifications through the image of the large, stone-walled castles associated with the later reunification period, examined in Chapters 39 through 41; the elaborate, multi-story stone keep is a product of the later sixteenth century and after, not a feature of typical early-to-mid Sengoku fortification.

As the century progressed and daimyō governments became more capable of mobilizing labor, resources, and skilled construction workers — a direct consequence of the administrative maturation discussed in Chapter 35 and Section 36.1 — fortifications grew larger, more elaborate, and increasingly incorporated stone construction, particularly for foundations, retaining walls, and gate structures, alongside continued use of earthworks and timber. Castle complexes expanded to include multiple concentric baileys, more sophisticated gate and approach designs intended to expose attackers to defensive fire from multiple angles, and, in some cases, moats supplied by diverted rivers or springs. This gradual elaboration reflected both growing resources and the escalating scale of the warfare fortifications were designed to withstand.

Siege warfare itself involved a range of methods short of direct assault: blockade, intended to starve a garrison into surrender by cutting off supply and reinforcement; negotiation, sometimes resulting in a garrison's surrender in exchange for safe conduct or incorporation into the besieging daimyō's service; and, where direct assault was attempted, coordinated efforts to breach walls or gates, scale defensive works, or exploit weaknesses in the fortification's design. Because a fortified position with an adequate garrison and supply could resist direct assault for a considerable time, and because direct assault against strong fortifications was typically costly in casualties, blockade and negotiated surrender were frequently preferred to storming a well-defended castle outright — a preference that connects directly to the logistical themes examined in Section 36.8, since a successful blockade itself required the besieging army to sustain its own supply over an extended period while denying supply to the defenders.

Section 36.8

Logistics: Feeding, Moving, and Sustaining Armies

No dimension of Sengoku warfare more directly connects battlefield outcomes to the territorial administration examined in Chapter 35 than logistics — the unglamorous but decisive work of feeding, arming, and moving an army large enough to matter. An army of several thousand men, together with the horses, porters, and pack animals needed to support it, consumed enormous quantities of rice, other foodstuffs, fodder, and water every single day it remained in the field, whether marching, besieging, or waiting. A daimyō's capacity to sustain a campaign therefore depended directly on the tax assessment, requisitioning authority, and administrative reach that Chapter 35 described as the foundation of Sengoku territorial government.

Provisioning an army involved several interlocking systems. Granaries maintained at castle towns and forward staging points stored surplus rice collected through regular taxation, providing a baseline supply for planned campaigns. Requisitioning from villages along a marching route, whether through negotiated purchase, compulsory levy, or outright seizure, supplemented stored supplies, particularly during longer campaigns that outran what could be carried from the outset — a practice that placed real and sometimes severe burdens on the rural population living along contested borders and campaign routes, a theme this chapter's Everyday Life feature examines further. Porters and pack animals, mobilized alongside fighting men through the same muster obligations discussed in Section 36.3, carried food, ammunition, replacement weapons, and equipment that soldiers could not practically carry themselves.

Transport infrastructure mattered enormously. Roads connecting a daimyō's core territory to the frontier or campaign theater determined how quickly and reliably supplies and reinforcements could move; mountainous terrain, poor road conditions, river crossings without adequate bridges, and seasonal flooding or snow could all delay or halt a campaign as effectively as an enemy army. Where rivers or coastal waters were navigable, water transport offered a valuable alternative to overland porterage for moving bulk supplies, though its usefulness varied enormously by region and by the specific geography of a given campaign.

Seasonal constraints shaped campaigning throughout the Sengoku period. Planting and harvest seasons drew heavily on the same rural manpower daimyō depended on for both ashigaru levies and porterage, creating pressure to campaign in the gaps between agricultural demands rather than during peak planting or harvest periods. Winter weather, particularly in mountainous or northern domains, could close roads and passes entirely for months at a time, a constraint that shaped the seasonal rhythm of campaigns such as the repeated Takeda-Uesugi confrontations discussed in Section 36.9. Sustained, multi-season campaigning — increasingly common by the later sixteenth century — represented a significant logistical achievement, made possible only by the administrative capacity to plan, store, and move supplies well beyond what an army could carry on its own.

Ammunition and equipment logistics grew more complex as firearms spread. Sustaining a firearm-equipped force in the field required continuous supplies of gunpowder, lead shot, and match cord, alongside replacement weapons and repair capacity — additional demands layered onto the already substantial burden of feeding and moving an army, and additional evidence that the spread of firearms intensified, rather than simplified, the logistical challenge of Sengoku warfare.

Everyday Life

An Army Eats Before It Fights

Behind every famous Sengoku battle lay a much larger and far less celebrated undertaking: the daily work of feeding, moving, and equipping thousands of men. A village along a campaign route might find its stored rice requisitioned, its able-bodied men and draft animals conscripted as porters, and its fields trampled or seized for temporary camps — burdens that fell disproportionately on the rural population regardless of which side ultimately prevailed. A porter's daily experience involved carrying heavy loads of rice, ammunition, or equipment over difficult terrain, often for wages that were modest at best and sometimes under outright compulsion, exposed to the same weather, disease, and danger as fighting men but without a fighting man's status or, frequently, his protection.

For the soldiers themselves, campaigning meant long periods of marching and waiting punctuated by brief, intense combat; the daily reality of most campaign days involved securing food and water, maintaining equipment, building or improving field fortifications, and enduring weather and terrain rather than fighting battles. Understanding this everyday texture of campaigning — grain requisitioned, roads marched, camps built and broken, porters straining under loads — is essential to understanding why logistics, not simply weapons or tactics, so often determined which daimyō could sustain the kind of prolonged, large-scale warfare this chapter describes.

Section 36.9

Battle, Siege, and Regional Variation

The general patterns traced in this chapter — growing infantry organization, the gradual integration of firearms, evolving fortification, and increasingly sophisticated logistics — played out differently across the archipelago's varied regions and among its many competing daimyō. This section examines selected case studies not as a comprehensive battle-by-battle narrative of the Sengoku period, a task better suited to specialized military history than to a single textbook chapter, but as illustrations of how the chapter's broader themes appeared in specific, well-documented circumstances.

The repeated confrontations between Takeda Shingen of Kai and Uesugi Kenshin of Echigo, including the several battles fought on or near the plain of Kawanakajima between 1553 and 1564, illustrate the constraints of mountain-domain warfare and the difficulty of achieving decisive victory even between two of the most capable commanders of the period. Both domains depended on narrow valley roads and were subject to severe seasonal weather that limited the campaigning season, discussed in Section 36.8's treatment of logistics. Repeated campaigning over more than a decade produced tactically significant engagements without permanently resolving the underlying territorial rivalry, a pattern that should caution students against assuming that Sengoku warfare consistently produced quick, decisive outcomes. The political relationship between the Takeda and Uesugi houses, and their place within the broader Sengoku political landscape, belongs to Chapter 35 and later chapters; this chapter's interest lies specifically in what the Kawanakajima campaigns reveal about the operational and logistical realities of mountain-domain warfare.

The 1575 Battle of Nagashino, fought in Mikawa Province between the combined forces of Oda Nobunaga and Tokugawa Ieyasu and the Takeda army under Takeda Katsuyori, offers a different kind of case study — one that illustrates both the mature combined-arms system described in Section 36.6 and the risks of accepting a simplified historical narrative uncritically. The battle involved firearms deployed alongside field fortifications and spear-armed infantry against Takeda assaults that included mounted warriors, in terrain that constrained mounted maneuver; the familiar popular image of a unified mass cavalry charge repeatedly smashing head-on into a fixed gun line is itself part of the simplified later narrative this chapter treats with caution, and modern scholarship has questioned that traditional reconstruction of the battle alongside the traditional volley-fire story. The battle's outcome favored Nobunaga and Ieyasu's forces, and the engagement has since become, in much later popular retelling, the emblematic proof that massed firearms alone had rendered traditional cavalry warfare obsolete. This chapter's Historical Debate feature below examines that claim critically, showing that the evidence supports a considerably more complicated picture involving terrain, field fortification, combined-arms coordination, and command decisions, not firearms operating in isolation.

Sieges of increasingly elaborate fortifications, a growing feature of the later Sengoku period as the fortification developments described in Section 36.7 matured, illustrate the logistical themes of Section 36.8 in concentrated form: a besieging army had to sustain itself in the field, often for weeks or months, while denying supply to a defended position whose garrison might hold out as long as its own stores lasted. Such sieges rewarded daimyō with strong administrative capacity for provisioning and organization over those relying on short, seasonal campaigns alone, reinforcing this chapter's central argument that military transformation depended fundamentally on the territorial state-building examined in Chapter 35.

Section 36.10

From Sengoku Military Competition toward Reunification

By the later sixteenth century, the cumulative effect of the developments traced in this chapter — larger and more organized armies built around ashigaru infantry, the widespread adoption and domestic manufacture of firearms integrated into combined-arms tactics, increasingly elaborate fortifications, and the logistical sophistication needed to sustain longer campaigns — had raised the resources and organizational capacity required simply to remain competitive among Japan's regional daimyō. A lord who could not assess and collect taxes effectively, register and mobilize retainers reliably, manufacture or acquire firearms in quantity, fortify strategic positions adequately, and sustain an army logistically over an extended campaign was at an increasing disadvantage against rivals who could do all of these things at scale.

This rising threshold of military and administrative capacity favored daimyō able to combine the institutions Chapter 35 described with the military practices this chapter has examined most effectively and at the largest scale — a dynamic directly relevant to the process of political reunification examined in Chapters 39 through 41, in which Oda Nobunaga, Toyotomi Hideyoshi, and Tokugawa Ieyasu each built upon, refined, and ultimately transcended the Sengoku military system this chapter describes. None of these leaders invented Sengoku warfare's essential components from nothing; each inherited an already-transformed system of organized infantry, integrated firearms, elaborated fortification, and mature logistics, and applied it with growing scale and increasing political centralization.

The chapters that follow trace two further developments essential to completing this picture. Chapter 37 examines the Portuguese traders, Jesuit missionaries, and broader Nanban commercial and cultural contact only briefly introduced here in connection with the arquebus, situating firearms within the wider story of European maritime contact with Japan. Chapter 38 turns to a very different but related theme — how aesthetic culture, particularly tea practice, became entangled with elite Sengoku politics. Chapters 39 through 41 then follow Nobunaga, Hideyoshi, and Ieyasu as they used, and ultimately transformed, the military inheritance this chapter has described to achieve the political reunification of Japan.

Primary Source Window

A Later Hōjō Military-Service Order, 1581

Type: Chakutō sadamegaki (military-service assessment order) · Creator: Issued in the name of Hōjō Ujinao to the retainer Ikeda Magosaemon-no-jō · Date: Tenshō 9, seventh month, 24th day (1581) · Repository: Held among Later Hōjō house documents and described by the Odawara Digital Museum

Context: The Later Hōjō, a daimyō house controlling much of the Kantō region, administered one of the Sengoku period's most systematically documented territorial states, and their surviving archive includes numerous chakutō sadamegaki — written orders specifying exactly what military service a named retainer owed the house based on the assessed kandaka value of his landholding, discussed in Section 36.3. This particular order, dated the twenty-fourth day of the seventh month of Tenshō 9 (1581) and addressed to the retainer Ikeda Magosaemon-no-jō, itemizes the composition of the armed contingent he was required to furnish when summoned: a specified mix of mounted men, spear-bearers, bowmen, and other equipped soldiers, totaling fifty-six people in all.

Paraphrase of the order's substance (not a verbatim translation): The document assesses Ikeda Magosaemon-no-jō's landholding in kan and, on that basis, itemizes the categories and numbers of armed men he is obligated to provide when mustered for Later Hōjō service — mounted retainers, spearmen, archers, and other troop types — specifying the total contingent at fifty-six people.

Analysis: A document of this kind makes concrete, for a single named retainer, exactly the process described more generally in Section 36.3: kandaka assessment converted directly into a specified gun'yaku obligation, itemized by troop type rather than left to informal custom. The order demonstrates that by 1581 the Later Hōjō administration could specify not merely a vague expectation of military support but a precise combined-arms contingent — cavalry, spear infantry, and archers together — expected from a single retainer's holding, direct evidence for the administrative sophistication underlying the army-building process this chapter has traced throughout.

Questions for students:

  1. What does the itemization of specific troop types suggest about how Later Hōjō administrators thought about army composition?
  2. Why might a daimyō house have wanted such assessments recorded in writing and addressed to a named individual rather than announced only verbally?
  3. What can a document like this tell historians that a narrative battle account cannot?
  4. What questions about Ikeda Magosaemon-no-jō's actual campaign experience does this order not answer?
Historical Debate

Did Firearms Revolutionize Japanese Warfare?

The question: Did the introduction and spread of the matchlock arquebus fundamentally revolutionize Japanese warfare, rendering earlier weapons and tactics obsolete, or did it become one significant element within a broader, longer-term military transformation already under way?

Interpretation A — The firearms-revolution narrative: A once-common account, closely associated with popular retellings of the Battle of Nagashino, holds that the introduction of massed matchlock volley fire in 1575 proved decisively that firearms had made traditional cavalry-centered samurai warfare obsolete, marking a sharp and dramatic turning point after which Japanese warfare changed fundamentally and quickly.
Interpretation B — The gradual-adaptation view: Many historians examining the fuller evidentiary record argue that firearms were integrated gradually and unevenly into an already-changing military system over decades, alongside continued and substantial use of bows, spears, and mounted retainers, and that Nagashino itself is better understood as a case of successful combined-arms coordination involving field fortification, terrain, and infantry discipline than as a simple demonstration of firearms superiority in isolation. On this view, later retellings — some produced considerably after the battle itself — simplified a more complex engagement into a cleaner, more dramatic story of technological revolution.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on the same body of evidence concerning Nagashino and the broader spread of firearms, but differ in how much weight they give to later narrative retellings, how they interpret the role of field fortification and terrain relative to firearms alone, and how they read the continued prominence of bows, spears, and cavalry throughout the following decades.

Why it matters: How historians resolve this question shapes how the entire century of Sengoku military change examined in this chapter should be understood — as a story dominated by a single technological turning point, or as a story of cumulative organizational, administrative, and tactical adaptation in which firearms played an important but not solitary role.

Student task: Identify one piece of evidence from this chapter that supports the gradual-adaptation view and explain why historians have grown cautious about the firearms-revolution narrative specifically in connection with Nagashino.

Cause and Effect

State-Building and Military Transformation

Long-term causes

The breakdown of centralized Ashikaga authority left political and military power fragmented among competing regional daimyō, discussed in Part V, creating sustained conditions of territorial rivalry across the archipelago.

Short-term causes

The territorial administrative institutions examined in Chapter 35 — cadastral survey, taxation, retainer registration, castle-centered government — matured over the sixteenth century, providing the resources and organization needed to mobilize larger, better-supplied armies.

Effect

Armies grew larger and more organized around ashigaru infantry; firearms were adopted and manufactured domestically; fortifications became more elaborate; and logistics grew correspondingly more sophisticated to sustain longer campaigns.

Long-term consequence

The rising threshold of administrative and military capacity required for competitiveness favored the daimyō who could combine these elements most effectively, setting the stage for the process of political reunification examined in Chapters 39 through 41.

Historical complexity: Military transformation did not proceed independently of political and administrative development; each reinforced the other, so that no single cause — not firearms, not taxation, not any one commander's genius — can adequately explain the century's military change on its own.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Muromachi to Sengoku Warfare

What Changed

  • Armies grew significantly larger and more sustained, supported by maturing territorial administration
  • Organized ashigaru infantry became a much more central component of fighting strength relative to mounted retainers alone
  • Firearms were introduced and rapidly manufactured domestically, adding a new missile weapon to the existing repertoire
  • Fortifications evolved from simple earthwork mountain refuges toward larger, more elaborate, partly stone-built complexes
  • Logistics became more systematized, enabling longer and more sustained campaigns across multiple seasons

What Stayed the Same

  • Continued reliance on bows, spears, and swords alongside any newer weapons
  • Continued importance of mounted retainers for mobility, flanking, and shock action
  • Continued dependence of military capacity on land-based taxation and retainer obligation
  • Continued vulnerability of campaigns to terrain, weather, and seasonal agricultural demands

Why the pattern matters: Treating Sengoku military change as a continuous, cumulative process rather than a single sharp break — whether located at the introduction of firearms or elsewhere — better reflects the evidence and prepares students to evaluate similar claims of sudden technological revolution in later chapters.

Multiple Perspectives

Six Views of a Campaign

A mounted retainer: Military status and family standing depended on maintaining horse, armor, and equipment appropriate to hereditary obligation, and on providing effective leadership over the ashigaru contingents increasingly placed under a retainer's command in the field.
An ashigaru spearman: Service might be seasonal or more permanent, equipment increasingly standardized and issued rather than self-supplied, and battlefield survival dependent above all on maintaining formation discipline alongside fellow spearmen rather than individual heroics.
A village porter: Conscription into army service as a porter meant heavy loads, difficult terrain, and danger without the status or protections extended to recognized fighting men, and often meant a village's stored food and draft animals were requisitioned regardless of the hardship this created at home.
A castle defender: Holding a fortified position under siege meant careful rationing of stored supplies, constant vigilance against blockade, assault, or negotiated betrayal, and awareness that the outcome might depend as much on how long supplies lasted as on the skill of the defenders.
A gunsmith in Sakai or Kunitomo: Rapid demand for matchlocks from competing daimyō created new commercial opportunity, requiring smiths to adapt an imported design to available materials and to scale up production methods capable of equipping growing ashigaru forces.
A daimyō's logistics official: Success depended on accurately assessing what a campaign would require in food, fodder, ammunition, and transport, coordinating requisition from villages along the route, and ensuring that an army's fighting strength was never undermined by hunger, exhaustion, or supply failure before a single battle was fought.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how the territorial governments described in Chapter 35 translated into the practice of Sengoku warfare: larger and more organized armies, evolving weapons and tactics, more elaborate fortifications, and increasingly sophisticated logistics. The chapter's governing argument has been that no single factor, and certainly no single weapon, adequately explains this century-long military transformation. Instead, administrative capacity, changing army composition, weapons adaptation, fortification development, and logistical organization developed together, each reinforcing the others.

Sengoku armies grew to include a much larger proportion of organized ashigaru infantry alongside mounted samurai retainers, a shift driven by the administrative capacity of territorial daimyō to assess, register, and mobilize manpower at increasing scale. Mobilization itself depended directly on the taxation, retainer registration, and command structures examined in Chapter 35, converted into muster rolls, banners, and marching columns through an increasingly formalized process.

Weapons before firearms — the bow, the spear, and the sword — continued to matter throughout the period, and the chapter has been careful to correct the common misconception that the sword dominated Sengoku battlefield combat. The arquebus, introduced in the conventional 1543 Tanegashima account and rapidly manufactured domestically at centers such as Sakai and Kunitomo, added a significant new missile weapon, but one integrated gradually into an already-changing combined-arms system of spear infantry, field fortification, and mounted maneuver rather than one that swept away existing practice overnight.

Fortifications evolved substantially over the century, from earthwork-and-palisade mountain castles well suited to the resources of earlier Sengoku daimyō toward larger, more elaborate, and increasingly stone-incorporating complexes reflecting growing administrative capacity and escalating military scale. Siege warfare, frequently resolved through blockade or negotiated surrender rather than costly direct assault, connected fortification directly to the logistical themes the chapter has emphasized throughout.

Logistics received sustained attention as a central, not peripheral, theme of this chapter. Feeding, arming, and moving an army in the field depended on granaries, requisitioning, porterage, transport infrastructure, and seasonal timing, all of which placed real burdens on the rural population and all of which depended directly on the administrative reach Chapter 35 described. Sustained, multi-season campaigning represented a genuine logistical achievement possible only for the most administratively capable daimyō.

Case studies including the repeated Takeda-Uesugi campaigns at Kawanakajima and the 1575 Battle of Nagashino illustrated both the regional variation of Sengoku warfare and the need for historiographical caution when evaluating famous, frequently retold engagements. Nagashino in particular demonstrated the risks of accepting a simplified narrative of firearms-driven revolution without examining the fuller evidence for combined-arms coordination, terrain, and field fortification.

By the century's later decades, the cumulative weight of these developments had raised the threshold of administrative and military capacity required for daimyō to remain competitive, setting the direct stage for the process of political reunification examined in the chapters that follow. Oda Nobunaga, Toyotomi Hideyoshi, and Tokugawa Ieyasu inherited, rather than invented, the transformed Sengoku military system this chapter has traced.

Students should carry forward from this chapter both its specific content — army composition, weapons, fortification, and logistics — and its broader methodological lesson: that dramatic, popularly repeated historical narratives, such as the firearms-revolution story associated with Nagashino, deserve careful, evidence-based scrutiny rather than uncritical acceptance, a lesson with direct relevance to the historiographically contested chapters that lie ahead in this textbook.

Key Terms

Ashigaru
Foot soldiers who formed a growing, increasingly organized component of Sengoku armies over the sixteenth century.
Bushi
A general term for warriors, including mounted samurai retainers bound to a daimyō or intermediate lord by hereditary or contractual ties.
Teppō
The Japanese term for the matchlock arquebus, introduced in the conventional 1543 Tanegashima account and rapidly manufactured domestically.
Matchlock
A firearm ignition mechanism using a slow-burning match lowered into a pan of priming powder.
Yari
The spear, an increasingly central massed-infantry weapon well suited to relatively rapid training of large ashigaru forces.
Yumi
The Japanese bow, an important missile weapon throughout the Sengoku period despite the spread of firearms.
Katana
The sword, functioning primarily as a secondary and close-quarters weapon and status symbol rather than the dominant infantry battlefield weapon.
Naginata
A polearm weapon with a curved blade, used in earlier medieval warfare and continuing in more limited use during the Sengoku period.
Muster
The process of assembling an army from retainer contingents and levied manpower according to assessed obligation.
Sashimono
A banner worn on the back of an individual soldier, used to identify unit and lord amid the confusion of battle.
Yamashiro
A mountain castle built using earthworks, ditches, and palisades on defensible high ground, characteristic of earlier and mid-Sengoku fortification.
Kuruwa
A bailey, or terraced defensive level, within a castle complex.
Siege
Military operations, including blockade, negotiation, and assault, directed against a fortified position.
Logistics
The organization of supply, transport, and provisioning necessary to sustain an army in the field.
Requisition
The compulsory or negotiated collection of food, animals, or equipment from a local population to supply a campaigning army.
Combined arms
The coordinated use of multiple weapon and troop types—missile weapons, spear infantry, cavalry, and fortification—within a single tactical system.
Field fortification
Temporary defensive works, such as palisades or earthen barriers, constructed to protect troops, particularly gunners, during battle.
Kandaka
A cash-equivalent land-value assessment used by many Sengoku daimyō to calculate taxation and military service obligation, distinct from the later, nationally standardized kokudaka system consolidated through Hideyoshi's surveys, examined in Chapter 40.
Gun'yaku
The military service obligation — men, horses, and equipment — owed by a retainer or locality according to assessed landholding.
Gunzei saisokujō
A written order summoning designated warriors and their contingents to mobilize for campaign.
Volley technique
The organizational practice of alternating or staggering the fire of ranks of gunners to sustain a more continuous rate of fire despite the matchlock's slow individual reload time; securely documented by the 1590s, though its precise development and use in earlier, more famous engagements remain debated.
Historiography
The study of how historians have interpreted and reinterpreted the past, relevant here to evaluating later retellings of battles such as Nagashino.
Ronin
Warriors without settled attachment to a lord, sometimes mobilized as auxiliary fighters in Sengoku armies.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has carried the territorial state-building introduced in Chapter 35 into its military consequences: the armies, weapons, fortifications, and logistics through which Sengoku daimyō competed for survival and advantage across a fragmented archipelago. It has shown that Sengoku warfare cannot be understood as a story about a single weapon, a single battle, or a single commander's genius, but must instead be understood as a system in which administrative capacity, army organization, weapons technology, fortification, and logistics developed together over decades.

The chapter's treatment of the Battle of Nagashino matters beyond its immediate subject. It demonstrates that even a well-documented and frequently discussed historical event can accumulate a simplified, dramatic popular narrative that outruns what the underlying evidence actually supports, and that careful historians must be willing to complicate such narratives even when they are widely repeated and appealing. Students should carry this lesson of historiographical caution into every subsequent chapter of this textbook, particularly those addressing other famous battles, technological turning points, and moments of celebrated individual achievement.

The chapter has also planted an important seed for the rest of Part VI: the recognition that the military capacity Oda Nobunaga, Toyotomi Hideyoshi, and Tokugawa Ieyasu would each draw upon in the chapters ahead was not their own invention, but the product of a century of cumulative Sengoku military transformation—organized ashigaru infantry, adapted firearms, elaborated fortification, and mature logistics—developed by dozens of competing daimyō across the archipelago before any one of them achieved reunification.

Students who understand Sengoku warfare as a genuine system of interacting institutions and practices, shaped by administrative capacity as much as by weapons or battlefield courage, are well prepared to move forward into Chapter 37, where the broader story of Portuguese trade, Christianity, and Nanban cultural contact reveals how the same era's overseas connections extended well beyond the arquebus alone.
