Part II — Yamato and the Formation of Ancient Japan
8

The Emergence of the Yamato Polity

Japanese History: From Jōmon Japan to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

A sword, corroded almost beyond recognition after more than fifteen hundred years underground, was excavated from a burial mound in the Kantō plain in 1968. A decade later, during conservation work in 1978, X-ray examination revealed something no keyhole tomb by itself could ever say: words. Inlaid in gold across both faces of the blade — 115 characters in all — was a short inscription naming a lineage, describing service to a ruler styled "Great King," and recording, however tersely, a claim about political relationships that a mounted warrior's grave goods could only imply.

Chapter 7 examined the archaeological evidence of the Kofun age: the enormous keyhole-shaped tombs, the grave goods that marked out a small elite from everyone else, the horse trappings and iron weapons that spoke of a newly mounted warrior aristocracy, and the widening gap between powerful regional chiefs and the communities who labored to build their monuments. That chapter asked what the tombs could show about hierarchy, wealth, and regional competition. It could not, on its own, answer a different and more difficult question: what political institutions, if any, connected these chiefs to one another? Was there a recognized paramount ruler, and if so, what could that ruler actually command? Did elite families organize themselves into anything more durable than temporary alliances of convenience? This chapter takes up that question directly.

Between roughly the middle of the fifth century and the end of the sixth, the network of powerful chiefs visible in the archaeological record of Chapter 7 developed into something historians can describe, with real but limited confidence, as a political order centered on the Kinai region — the area around the Nara and Osaka basins — and organized around a paramount ruler, a small number of extraordinarily powerful hereditary families, formal titles of rank and service, and a set of ritual practices that bound rulers, elites, and the wider community of local chiefs together. Historians call this order the Yamato polity, after the place name most closely associated with its ceremonial and residential center. The label is a modern historian's convenience, not a name the participants themselves necessarily used in a fixed, consistent way, and it should not be imagined as describing a state with settled borders, a permanent capital, a standing bureaucracy, or uniform administration across the archipelago. It describes, instead, a genuine and increasingly durable concentration of political, military, and ritual authority — one still built substantially on personal relationships, inherited status, and negotiated cooperation among powerful families rather than on the kind of written law codes and salaried officials that would appear only generations later.

This chapter draws on three very different kinds of evidence, and much of its historical argument depends on keeping them carefully distinct. Archaeology — the tombs, grave goods, and settlement patterns already introduced in Chapter 7 — continues to matter, but this chapter adds two further bodies of evidence that Chapter 7 could not use. The first is a small number of contemporary indigenous inscriptions, cut or inlaid onto swords and mirrors, that preserve the actual words, or something close to them, used by people living within this developing political order to describe their rulers, their lineages, and their relationships of service. The second is the dynastic history of Liu Song China, which recorded diplomatic missions sent across the sea by a succession of rulers the Chinese called the kings of Wa, and preserved something of the titles those rulers claimed for themselves in dealing with the continental great power. Neither source is unproblematic. Inscriptions are laconic, sometimes damaged, and open to more than one reading. Chinese dynastic histories reflect a Chinese diplomatic and administrative framework, not a neutral window onto internal Wa politics. But taken together with the archaeological record, they allow historians to say something concrete about how a fifth- and sixth-century paramount ruler in the Kinai region was actually described, in the ruler's own time, by people who served that ruler and by a foreign court that dealt with that ruler directly.

This chapter deliberately withholds material that belongs to later chapters. It will not treat Buddhism's arrival, which transforms the political and religious landscape and is examined in full in Chapter 10. It will not narrate the rivalry between the Soga and Mononobe families in the detail Chapter 11 provides, though those families' predecessors and forerunners appear here in their earlier, more limited roles. It will not describe Prince Shōtoku's statecraft, which belongs to Chapter 12, nor the Taika reforms and the mature ritsuryō legal state, which belong to Chapter 13. Readers should understand the political order described in this chapter as an important and necessary stage on the way toward the centralized imperial state of the seventh century — but not yet that state itself, and not a state whose eventual outcome was already guaranteed or foreseeable to the people living through it.

It is worth pausing, too, on what this chapter is not permitted to assume. The great chronicles of early Japanese history, the *Kojiki* and the *Nihon Shoki*, were compiled in the early eighth century, two hundred years or more after most of the events this chapter describes, under the patronage of a court with strong political reasons to present its own ancestors as legitimate, continuous, and divinely sanctioned rulers stretching back into deep antiquity. Those chronicles preserve genealogies, titles, and narratives that later readers, and sometimes later historians, have used to reconstruct fifth- and sixth-century politics. This chapter uses such material cautiously and only where independent, contemporary evidence — inscriptions, archaeology, or foreign records — can corroborate or usefully constrain it. Where the chronicles provide the only surviving account of a person or event, that fact will be stated plainly, and students should read such passages as later court tradition rather than as contemporary fifth-century record.

By the chapter's end, students should be able to describe how kingship, clan organization, formal rank, ritual authority, and regional alliance combined to turn a Kofun-period network of chiefs into a recognizable, if still uneven and negotiated, Yamato court — and should be equipped to ask, with appropriate caution, when historians can reasonably begin using the word "Yamato" at all, and how much real central authority that word implies.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how the Kofun-period chiefly hierarchy examined in Chapter 7 developed into a more institutionally recognizable Yamato-centered political order.
  2. Describe the office and meaning of the paramount ruler known as ōkimi, and distinguish this role from the later, more fully institutionalized title of tennō.
  3. Analyze the fifth-century Chinese dynastic records concerning the "Five Kings of Wa" as evidence for diplomatic titles, continental recognition, and Yamato's place within a wider East Asian political order.
  4. Interpret the Inariyama and Eta Funayama sword inscriptions as direct, contemporary evidence for political language, lineage, and service relationships within the emerging Yamato order.
  5. Explain the uji as hereditary elite kin groups or corporate lineages, and distinguish them from modern surnames or strictly biological clans.
  6. Describe the kabane system of hereditary rank and explain why it should not be understood as a fully standardized bureaucratic hierarchy from its origins.
  7. Assess the relationship between political authority and ritual practice in the emerging Yamato court, without projecting later, fully developed Shinto institutions backward onto the fifth and sixth centuries.
  8. Evaluate the unevenness of Yamato influence across different regions of the archipelago and the role of alliance, marriage, exchange, and coercion in extending that influence.
  9. Trace the emergence of specialized service groups and court-centered households as an early, limited form of institutional administration.
  10. Distinguish among archaeological evidence, contemporary inscriptions, foreign dynastic records, and later Japanese chronicles as different, unevenly reliable categories of historical evidence for this period.
  11. Avoid describing the fifth- and sixth-century Yamato polity as a unified nation-state or its ruler as an "emperor" in the later institutional sense.
  12. Contextualize the Yamato polity's development as a foundation for, rather than an outcome equivalent to, the centralized imperial state examined in later chapters.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How did a network of powerful Kofun-period chiefs become a more recognizable, Kinai-centered political order with a paramount ruler, elite clans, and formal titles?
  2. What can the Chinese dynastic records of the Five Kings of Wa tell historians about Yamato's diplomatic claims and standing — and what do they not tell us about internal Wa politics?
  3. What do the Inariyama and Eta Funayama sword inscriptions reveal about how contemporaries described their ruler, their lineage, and their own service?
  4. What were uji and kabane, and how did they organize relationships among elite families and between elite families and the paramount ruler?
  5. In what ways was fifth- and sixth-century political authority inseparable from ritual and claims of sacred legitimacy?
  6. How uniform or uneven was Yamato influence across different regions of the archipelago, and what methods extended or limited that influence?
  7. What early institutional and administrative features — service groups, craft specialists, court households — began to appear before the mature ritsuryō state?
  8. When, if ever, can historians responsibly begin speaking of a "Yamato state," and how centralized should we imagine that political order to have been?

Historical Context

Chapter 7 traced the emergence, from roughly the mid-third through the sixth centuries, of the great keyhole-shaped tomb mounds (kofun) that gave the period its name, together with the elite hierarchy those tombs made visible: a small number of extraordinarily large tombs concentrated in the Kinai region, surrounded by progressively smaller tombs belonging to lesser but still substantial regional elites, and beneath them the largely invisible mass of the population whose labor built the mounds but who left no comparable monuments of their own. That chapter also introduced the horse gear, iron weaponry, and imported prestige goods that marked the rise of a mounted warrior aristocracy and the intensification of contact with the Korean Peninsula. Chapter 6, still earlier, described the late Yayoi world of competing regional chiefdoms, the Chinese accounts of the land of Wa, and the figure of Himiko, whose third-century polity of Yamatai remains geographically disputed and cannot be securely identified with the later Kinai-centered order this chapter examines.

This chapter picks up directly from the archaeological hierarchy Chapter 7 established and asks what political institutions, if any, stood behind it. The tombs by themselves can show relative wealth, access to iron and horses, and a widening gap between elite and commoner; they cannot by themselves demonstrate that a single ruler was recognized across a wide area, that elite families organized themselves according to consistent principles, or that anything resembling formal rank or office existed. For those questions, historians must turn to two further bodies of evidence largely unavailable to Chapter 7: contemporary inscriptions produced within the archipelago itself, and the dynastic histories of continental China, which recorded diplomatic contact with a succession of rulers in Wa during the fifth century. Both kinds of evidence begin to appear in meaningful quantity only from the fifth century onward, which is one reason historians generally place the clearer emergence of a recognizable Yamato political order in that century and the one that followed, even while acknowledging that the Kinai region's rise to regional preeminence had begun earlier, within the period Chapter 7 examined.

Students should also keep in mind the wider East Asian setting. During the fourth, fifth, and sixth centuries, the Korean Peninsula was divided among competing kingdoms — Goguryeo in the north, and Baekje and Silla further south, alongside smaller polities such as the Gaya confederacy — while successive dynasties in China, including the Liu Song dynasty examined below, claimed a position of cultural and diplomatic preeminence across the wider region. Wa rulers' diplomatic missions to China, and Wa's shifting relationships with Baekje, Silla, and Gaya, formed an important part of the context in which Yamato kingship developed, though the fuller treatment of these continental relationships, migration, and cultural exchange is reserved for Chapter 9. This chapter introduces only what is necessary to understand the diplomatic evidence for Yamato kingship itself.

Section 8.1

From Kofun Hierarchy to Yamato Polity

Chapter 7 left students with a striking archaeological picture: enormous keyhole-shaped tomb mounds concentrated in the Nara and Osaka basins, dwarfing tombs elsewhere in the archipelago, surrounded by a hierarchy of progressively smaller mounds belonging to lesser regional elites, and accompanied by grave goods — iron weapons, horse trappings, imported bronze mirrors, and eventually gilt-bronze crowns and ornaments — that testified to real wealth, real military capability, and a real and widening gap between a small elite and the population at large. That evidence, however, is fundamentally silent about political organization in a specific and important sense: a tomb can show that its occupant was powerful, but it cannot, by itself, say whether that power was recognized by other powerful people as belonging to a single ranked order, whether elite families understood themselves as serving a common ruler, or whether anything like formal titles, ranks, or offices existed to organize relationships among them.

This chapter asks precisely that question, and answers it with evidence Chapter 7 did not have available: inscriptions produced by people living within the political order itself, and diplomatic records produced by a foreign court dealing directly with its rulers. Historians use the term "Yamato polity" to describe the political order that this evidence reveals taking shape across the fifth and sixth centuries — a Kinai-centered order built around a paramount ruler, a small number of extraordinarily powerful hereditary families, systems of rank and titled service, and ritual practices that bound rulers and elites together. The label itself deserves a word of caution. "Yamato" was, in this period, primarily a place name associated with the Nara basin and, by extension, with the ceremonial and residential center of the paramount ruler's household; it was not yet a settled name for a state with fixed borders, nor did the political order's own participants necessarily use a single, consistent term to describe the whole system in which they operated. Historians apply "Yamato polity" retrospectively, as an analytical convenience, in much the same way that "Kofun period" is a modern label for an archaeological era rather than a term the period's inhabitants used for themselves.

No single founding date and no clean territorial boundary can be established for this political order, and readers should resist the temptation to look for either. The Kinai region's tomb-building elite had already achieved regional preeminence, in the archaeological sense Chapter 7 described, by the fourth century. What changes across the fifth and sixth centuries, on the evidence this chapter will present, is not simply the size of tombs or the wealth of grave goods, but the appearance of a vocabulary of kingship, service, lineage, and rank that allows historians to speak, with appropriate caution, of an institutionally recognizable court rather than merely an archaeologically visible hierarchy of chiefs. That transformation — from chiefly hierarchy to institutionally described court — is the specific historical development this chapter traces.

Section 8.2

The Great King and the Meaning of Kingship

Contemporary evidence from this period does not use the title tennō, "heavenly sovereign." Secure contemporary evidence for that later title belongs by at least the late seventh-century reigns of Tenmu and Jitō, and proposed earlier uses of it remain debated among specialists; this textbook reserves tennō for the more fully developed institution examined beginning in Chapter 12. For the fifth and sixth centuries, the indigenous sword inscriptions examined in Section 8.4 instead attest the title 大王, conventionally read in this context as ōkimi, "great king" or "great sovereign." This is a distinct and separate body of evidence from the Chinese dynastic records examined in Section 8.3, which independently describe rulers of Wa and record the Chinese diplomatic and military titles — "King of Wa," various generalships — that the continental court granted them; the Chinese sources do not themselves attest the domestic Yamato title ōkimi. Historians nonetheless use ōkimi, drawn from the indigenous evidence, for the paramount ruler of this period, precisely to avoid importing the later, more institutionally elaborate connotations of tennō into a period whose evidence does not support them.

What can be inferred about the nature of this kingship? Several strands of evidence converge, cautiously, on a similar picture. Diplomatically, as Section 8.3 will show, the Wa kings who sent embassies to Liu Song China claimed extensive, indeed strikingly ambitious, titles of authority — describing themselves in terms that asserted command over a wide range of territories and peoples, including areas of the Korean Peninsula where their actual military position was, at best, contested. Epigraphically, as Section 8.4 will show, the Inariyama inscription preserves a hereditary service lineage, while the Eta Funayama inscription records the service of an individual attendant; together they show that relationships of service to a ruler styled "Great King" had currency among elites far beyond the immediate Kinai core, suggesting that this title, and the relationships of service it implied, extended well outside the royal household itself. Archaeologically, the continued concentration of the very largest tombs in the Kinai region, alongside a broader diffusion of keyhole-tomb construction and certain prestige goods to allied regional elites, suggests that access to and association with the paramount ruler's court conferred real status that regional elites sought to display in their own burial practices.

None of this evidence, however, demonstrates that the ōkimi possessed anything resembling later bureaucratic sovereignty — the power to issue binding law across a defined territory, to appoint and remove officials at will, or to tax a settled population through a standing administrative apparatus. What the evidence more plausibly supports is a paramount ruler whose authority rested on a combination of ritual precedence, military capability, control over prestige goods and their distribution, diplomatic standing with the continental powers, and a network of personal and family relationships with other powerful lineages — relationships that had to be actively maintained, renewed, and sometimes fought for, rather than simply inherited as an automatic and permanent possession. Kingship in this period, in other words, was real, but it was also negotiated, contingent, and dependent on continued elite cooperation in ways that later, more fully institutionalized imperial authority would not be, at least in principle.

Historical Vocabulary
Ōkimi

Ōkimi ("great king" or "great sovereign") is the title historians generally use for the paramount ruler of the fifth- and sixth-century Yamato polity, based directly on the character pair 大王 ("Great King") attested in the contemporary Inariyama sword inscription. The Chinese dynastic records examined in Section 8.3 are a separate line of evidence: they describe rulers of Wa using Chinese diplomatic and military titles rather than attesting the domestic term ōkimi itself. Ōkimi should be distinguished from the later title tennō ("heavenly sovereign"). Secure contemporary evidence for tennō belongs by at least the late seventh-century reigns of Tenmu and Jitō, a period of much more developed imperial ideology, court ritual, and administrative institutions examined beginning in Chapter 12; proposed earlier uses of the title remain debated among specialists.

Using ōkimi rather than tennō for this period is not a matter of stylistic preference. It reflects a deliberate historical distinction: the fifth- and sixth-century paramount ruler's authority rested on a different, less institutionally elaborated foundation than the later imperial office, and applying the later, more familiar title backward risks implying a continuity and a level of centralized sovereignty that the contemporary evidence does not support.

Section 8.3

The Five Kings of Wa and the Chinese Record

Some of the most important evidence for a paramount ruler in Wa during the fifth century comes not from within the archipelago at all, but from the dynastic history of the Liu Song dynasty, which governed southern China from 420 to 479. This is not, it should be stressed, the earliest external evidence for Wa altogether — Chinese records already report Wa embassies as far back as the first and second centuries CE, and the third-century *Wei zhi* provides extensive testimony concerning Himiko and Yamatai, examined in Chapter 6. What the Liu Song material provides is the principal fifth-century evidence for a specific, later sequence of rulers. An earlier Wa embassy is recorded under the preceding Eastern Jin dynasty in 413, before the Liu Song court had even been established. The *Song shu*, the official history of the Liu Song dynasty, then records a series of diplomatic embassies sent by rulers of Wa to that court between 421 and Bu's petition in 478. Two further notices, dated 479 and 502, record additional promotions of Bu's military title by the newly founded Southern Qi and Liang dynasties respectively; these later notices are investiture or promotion records tied to dynastic founding rather than necessarily documented new embassies, and should not be treated as equivalent in kind to the earlier Liu Song missions. Chinese sources name the sequence of Wa rulers using characters conventionally read, in modern reconstructions, as San, Chin, Sai (or Sei), Kō, and Bu; historians refer to this sequence collectively, somewhat informally, as the Five Kings of Wa.

These embassies were not simply expressions of goodwill. Each mission sought formal titles and recognition from the Chinese court, participating in a diplomatic system, sometimes called the tributary system, in which peripheral rulers acknowledged Chinese imperial preeminence in exchange for titles that could be used to bolster their own standing, both diplomatically and, quite plausibly, domestically among rival regional elites. The most detailed and historically significant of these episodes concerns the Wa ruler recorded in 478 as petitioning the Liu Song court under the name "Bu." According to the *Song shu*, Bu's petition described his predecessors' military campaigns in extravagant terms, claiming subjugation of numerous polities within the archipelago and asserting authority extending to parts of the Korean Peninsula, and requested an extensive package of military titles, including a title asserting authority over Baekje, Silla, and other regions of the peninsula. The Liu Song court granted Bu a somewhat reduced version of the requested titles — recognition as king of Wa and general commanding forces in a defined but more limited set of territories, notably without formal recognition of authority over Baekje, with which the Chinese court maintained its own separate diplomatic relationship.

What should students draw from this record, and what should they be careful not to draw from it? On one hand, the petition and the response together demonstrate that a Wa ruler by 478 possessed sufficient organization, resources, and diplomatic sophistication to mount an ambitious continental diplomatic campaign, and that this ruler's claims to authority extended, at least rhetorically, well beyond the Kinai core to encompass much of the archipelago and, in aspiration if not always in acknowledged fact, parts of the Korean Peninsula as well. The very scale of the claim indicates that whoever held the Wa kingship by this date commanded a political and military position substantial enough to make such a claim plausible to a continental audience, even if not every element of the claim was accepted. On the other hand, historians must be careful about what this evidence does not show. Chinese recognition of a title tells us what the Chinese court was willing to grant for its own diplomatic purposes; it does not tell us how much practical authority the Wa king actually exercised over the various regional chiefs the petition claimed to have subjugated, nor does it describe the internal political structure of Wa in any detail. The petition is, moreover, a claim made by the petitioner, filtered through Chinese diplomatic and historiographical convention, and its rhetorical exaggeration for diplomatic effect cannot be entirely ruled out. Historians therefore treat the Five Kings of Wa evidence as valuable but partial: it establishes that a paramount kingship existed and sought international standing, without establishing precisely how centralized, uniform, or secure that kingship's authority was within the archipelago itself.

The identification of "Bu" with a specific ruler named in later Japanese chronicles is itself a matter of scholarly inference rather than direct statement in either source. Many, though not all, historians identify Bu with the ruler later remembered in the *Kojiki* and *Nihon Shoki* as Yūryaku — an identification that gains support, as the next section explains, from a separate and independent line of evidence: a royal name that appears in a contemporary indigenous inscription.

Section 8.4

Inscribed Swords and the First Indigenous Political Voices

The Chinese dynastic record, valuable as it is, still reports Wa politics from outside and at second hand. For a contemporary, indigenous account of political relationships within Wa itself — however brief — historians turn to a small and precious body of epigraphic evidence: inscriptions inlaid onto the blades of iron swords, then buried with their owners in elite tombs. Two inscriptions in particular have proven decisive for understanding the political vocabulary of the fifth-century Yamato order: the Inariyama sword, recovered from a tomb in present-day Saitama Prefecture in the Kantō region, bearing a 115-character inscription inlaid in gold; and the Eta Funayama sword, recovered from a tomb in present-day Kumamoto Prefecture, far to the southwest in Kyushu, bearing a 75-character inscription inlaid in silver.

The Inariyama inscription, examined in detail in the Primary Source Window below, records an eight-generation lineage and describes its commissioner Owake's family as serving a ruler referred to by a title read as "Great King," in a context associated with the royal name Wakatakeru. The Eta Funayama inscription, though more damaged and in places harder to read with confidence, names an individual attendant, Murite, and records his personal administrative service to a paramount ruler, without preserving an equivalent multi-generation genealogy. Together, these two inscriptions do something the archaeological evidence of Chapter 7 could not: they demonstrate, in words chosen by contemporaries rather than inferred from grave goods, that elite service relationships to a paramount ruler styled "Great King" were articulated across regions as geographically distant from one another as the Kantō plain and central Kyushu; Inariyama additionally demonstrates the importance of inherited service lineage in expressing that relationship.

This geographic spread matters enormously for how historians assess the reach of Yamato political authority in the later fifth century. A political vocabulary of kingship and service that appears only in the immediate vicinity of the Kinai core would suggest a much more geographically limited order than one that appears, in broadly similar terms, in both the Kantō region and Kyushu. The Eta Funayama and Inariyama inscriptions, precisely because they come from opposite ends of the areas where elite tomb-building was well established, provide meaningful evidence that some version of Yamato political language and some form of acknowledged service relationship to a paramount ruler had currency across a wide swath of the archipelago by this period — even though, as Section 8.8 will stress, this evidence of shared political vocabulary should not be mistaken for evidence of uniform administrative control.

The identification of the royal name Wakatakeru, preserved in the Inariyama inscription, with the ruler later remembered in Japanese chronicle tradition as Yūryaku, and by extension with the Wa king "Bu" recorded in the Chinese *Song shu*, is among the more celebrated pieces of detective work in early Japanese historical scholarship. The phonetic correspondence between "Wakatakeru" and the reading of characters associated with Yūryaku's name in later tradition is genuinely suggestive, and if the identification is correct, it allows historians to connect three independent bodies of evidence — Chinese diplomatic record, indigenous inscription, and later chronicle tradition — around a single historical figure, considerably strengthening confidence in the outline of later fifth-century Yamato kingship. Students should nonetheless treat this identification as a strong and widely, though not universally, accepted scholarly interpretation rather than an established, unquestionable fact. The inscriptional evidence for Wakatakeru's title and lineage relationships stands on its own regardless of the identification's ultimate certainty; the additional claim that this ruler is the same person named "Bu" in Chinese sources and Yūryaku in later Japanese chronicles is an inference, however well supported, and should be presented to students as such.

Section 8.5

Uji: Powerful Clans and the Structure of Elite Politics

The Inariyama inscription describes its bearer Owake's status in terms of an eight-generation lineage, tracing named ancestors who, the inscription states, served as leaders of sword-bearing attendants before Owake himself served the Great King. The Eta Funayama inscription provides a different, complementary kind of evidence: it names an individual, Murite, and records his service to the Great King as a tensōjin, an administrative attendant, without preserving an equivalent multi-generation genealogy. Taken together, these two inscriptions still point toward the importance of inherited lineage and hereditary service in defining elite standing during this period, even though only Inariyama directly documents the depth of a service lineage across generations. This emphasis on inherited lineage and service points toward one of the central organizing structures of Yamato-period elite politics: the uji. The fuller reconstruction of the uji-kabane system as a whole draws not only on these two inscriptions but also on later textual and chronicle evidence, used with the caution this textbook has emphasized throughout.

An uji is best understood as a hereditary elite kin group, or corporate lineage, whose members shared a claimed common ancestry, a collective name, and often an association with a particular function — military service, ritual performance, craft production, or administration — or with a particular territory. It is important for students to resist two tempting but misleading modern analogies. An uji was not simply a family surname in the modern sense; it was a corporate political and social unit whose membership, leadership, and internal organization could be considerably more fluid, contested, and functionally defined than a modern surname implies. Nor was an uji necessarily a strictly biological clan uniting only direct blood relatives; uji could incorporate dependents, subordinate lineages, and affiliated households under a common name and a common claimed ancestry, some of which may have reflected political incorporation as much as literal descent.

By the fifth and sixth centuries, a relatively small number of uji had achieved extraordinary prominence in association with the Yamato court. Later tradition and historical reconstruction associate the Ōtomo chiefly with military and court guard functions; the Mononobe, whose fuller political role and eventual conflict with the Soga family are examined in detail in Chapter 11, are likewise associated in later tradition with military functions and, increasingly, with certain ritual responsibilities; and the Katsuragi, based in the Kinai region itself, are described as exercising substantial regional power and, through marriage alliances with the royal lineage, considerable influence at court. These specific functional assignments rest more heavily on later textual tradition and scholarly reconstruction than on the kind of direct, contemporary inscriptional evidence examined in Section 8.4, and should be understood accordingly. Other uji were associated with particular crafts, with the management of the royal household's storehouses and treasuries, or with specific regional territories from which they drew their name and their local authority. This chapter introduces these leading uji only to the extent necessary to explain the structure of elite politics in this period; their detailed rivalries, and the political and religious conflicts that would eventually reshape the court order altogether, belong to Chapter 11, following the introduction of Buddhism in Chapter 10.

The uji structure mattered because it gave the paramount ruler's court a durable framework for organizing relationships with powerful families beyond the immediate royal household. Rather than dealing with an unstructured mass of individually powerful chiefs, as the archaeological evidence of Chapter 7 might on its own suggest, the ōkimi's court increasingly operated through recognized, named, hereditary corporate groups, each with an established (if sometimes contested) function, status, and relationship to the ruler. This did not eliminate rivalry among uji — indeed, competition among powerful lineages for royal favor, status, and access to resources remained a constant feature of Yamato politics, and would eventually produce the serious conflicts examined in later chapters — but it did provide a shared vocabulary and structure within which that rivalry took place.

Section 8.6

Kabane, Rank, and Service

Closely associated with the uji structure was a system of hereditary titles known as kabane. A kabane was a title attached to a particular uji, indicating that lineage's general status, function, and proximity to the paramount ruler. Titles that appear in the evidence for this period and the generations immediately following include omi, associated with lineages of high status often linked to the royal family through marriage or shared ancestry claims; muraji, associated with many of the leading service-oriented uji, including, in later tradition, families such as the Ōtomo and Mononobe; and kimi, associated in some cases with regional chiefly lineages whose local authority predated, and continued alongside, their incorporation into the Yamato order.

Students should approach the kabane system with real caution about how standardized and centrally administered it actually was during the fifth and sixth centuries. The clearer, more systematic kabane hierarchy familiar from later sources — most famously reorganized under a formal eight-rank system attributed to court reform late in the seventh century — represents a considerably later stage of institutional development than the period this chapter examines. During the fifth and sixth centuries, the evidence suggests something more gradual and less uniform: a growing but still unevenly applied vocabulary of hereditary titles, whose precise meaning, ranking relative to one another, and mechanism of bestowal likely varied over time and by region, rather than a single settled bureaucratic system administered consistently from a central authority.

What the kabane system does demonstrate, even in this earlier and less standardized form, is that status among elite families was becoming something that could be named, claimed, and — at least in principle — granted or withheld by association with the paramount ruler's court, rather than resting solely on independently held local power, as may have been closer to the case for many regional chiefs in the period Chapter 7 examined. This represents a genuine, if partial and uneven, step toward the kind of formalized rank and office that would become considerably more elaborate under the seventh-century reforms examined in Chapter 13. It should not, however, be mistaken for a developed civil service in which appointment, promotion, and removal operated according to consistent, written, centrally enforced rules. Kabane status in this period remained heavily bound up with inherited lineage, existing power, and personal relationships to the ruler and to other powerful families, rather than functioning as an impersonal bureaucratic hierarchy.

Historical Vocabulary
Uji and Kabane

Uji were hereditary elite kin groups or corporate lineages, often associated with a particular function, territory, or form of service, whose members shared a collective name and a claimed common ancestry. Kabane were hereditary titles attached to particular uji, indicating general status and relationship to the paramount ruler's court.

Students should avoid two common misunderstandings. First, uji were not equivalent to modern family surnames; they were political and social corporate units whose membership and boundaries could be more fluid and functionally defined than a surname implies. Second, the fifth- and sixth-century kabane system was not yet the more standardized, centrally administered rank system of later centuries; it should be understood as an emerging, unevenly applied vocabulary of status rather than a fully developed bureaucratic hierarchy.

Section 8.7

Royal Authority, Ritual, and Sacred Kingship

Political authority in the emerging Yamato order cannot be separated from ritual practice and claims of sacred legitimacy. The paramount ruler's standing rested not only on military capability, control over prestige goods, and diplomatic recognition, but also on a ritual role that connected the ruler to ancestral spirits, to agricultural fertility, and to the maintenance of proper relationships between the human and divine or spirit worlds — however those relationships were understood in this period, a matter on which the surviving contemporary evidence remains frustratingly thin.

Archaeological evidence — including ritual objects recovered from tombs and from certain sites associated with elite ceremonial activity, and the continued, though evolving, symbolic importance of bronze mirrors and other regalia-like objects inherited in part from the practices Chapter 7 described — indicates that ritual performance and access to prestigious ceremonial objects remained closely tied to elite, and especially royal, status throughout this period. Later chronicle tradition, though it must be used with the caution appropriate to a source compiled centuries afterward, preserves accounts of royal rites connected to agricultural cycles, ancestral commemoration, and specific sacred sites, some of which plausibly reflect practices with genuine roots in this earlier period, even if the chronicles' specific narrative details about particular rulers and events cannot be taken as contemporary record.

Historians must resist two opposite temptations here. The first is to assume that because later, more fully developed Shinto institutions eventually organized much of this ritual activity into a more systematic framework, something equivalent to that later, institutionally elaborate Shinto tradition already existed in the fifth and sixth centuries. It did not, in the developed institutional sense examined in later chapters; ritual practice in this period was almost certainly more localized, more closely tied to specific lineages, cult sites, and sacred places, and less systematically organized than the later tradition might suggest. The second temptation is to dismiss ritual authority as a mere ornament to "real," more narrowly defined military and diplomatic power. Archaeological evidence and later tradition together suggest, instead, that ancestral and agricultural ritual contributed to royal legitimacy alongside military capability, control over prestige goods, and diplomatic recognition. The exact fifth- and sixth-century royal ritual portfolio, however, cannot be securely reconstructed from the surviving evidence, and claims about precisely what would have happened to a ruler's standing had particular rites gone unperformed go beyond what the evidence can support; this remains a matter of reasonable historical inference rather than demonstrated fact. Sacred kingship, in this qualified sense, functioned as one strand among several that together sustained the ōkimi's contested and continually renewed authority.

Section 8.8

Regional Elites, Alliances, and Uneven Yamato Influence

The geographic spread of Yamato political vocabulary demonstrated by the Inariyama and Eta Funayama inscriptions should not be mistaken for evidence of uniform territorial administration. The mechanisms by which Yamato influence extended beyond the Kinai core were varied, and they left considerable regional power in the hands of local elites rather than replacing that power with direct central rule.

Marriage alliance was among the most important of these mechanisms. Powerful regional families, and powerful Kinai uji such as the Katsuragi, extended and consolidated influence through marriage relationships with the royal lineage and with one another, creating networks of kinship obligation that reinforced political cooperation without requiring direct administrative control. Exchange of prestige goods — including the very swords whose inscriptions provide this chapter's most direct evidence, along with bronze mirrors, horse trappings, and other valued objects — served a similar function, binding regional elites to the Yamato court through gift relationships that acknowledged status and expected loyalty without erasing local authority. Military cooperation, including joint campaigns and, at times, coerced submission of resistant local chiefs, extended Yamato's reach by force where alliance and exchange proved insufficient, though the specific campaigns described in later chronicle tradition — including the extensive conquests claimed in King Bu's petition to the Liu Song court — must be treated with appropriate caution regarding their scale and historicity.

The regional case of Kibi, a powerful polity in what is now Okayama Prefecture, illustrates this unevenness clearly. Kibi's elite constructed tombs among the largest in the archipelago outside the Kinai core itself, and possessed a distinctive material culture and evident independent resources, even as Kibi's elite also participated, in some periods more and in others apparently less cooperatively, in the wider Yamato-associated order. Later chronicle tradition preserves accounts of tension and even conflict between Kibi and the Yamato court, alongside accounts of cooperation and alliance; whatever the precise historical details, the persistence of a powerful, distinctively self-directed regional elite in Kibi throughout much of this period demonstrates that "Yamato expansion" did not mean the disappearance of independent regional power.

Similar patterns of negotiated, uneven relationship, rather than direct administrative incorporation, likely characterized Yamato's connections with elite communities in northern Kyushu, whose position astride the diplomatic and commercial routes to the Korean Peninsula gave them independent strategic importance and, at times, independent diplomatic initiative; with elite communities in eastern Honshu, including the Kantō region where the Inariyama sword was ultimately deposited; and with communities in other regions whose relationship to the emerging Yamato order is more thinly documented. Students should understand "the expansion of Yamato influence," the phrase used in this chapter's guiding purpose, as describing this uneven, negotiated, multi-method process of alliance, exchange, marriage, and selective coercion — not as describing the steady extension of a uniform central government's direct administrative control across the archipelago, which would not exist in anything like a mature form until the institutional developments examined in Chapter 13 and beyond.

Section 8.9

Court, Households, and the Beginnings of Administration

Alongside the uji and kabane structures examined above, the fifth and sixth centuries also show the gradual emergence of more specialized personnel performing particular tasks for the paramount ruler's household or for powerful uji. In the fifth century itself, the evidence points to a somewhat looser arrangement of individual service, sometimes discussed by historians as the hito system — a designation reflected in titles such as jōtōjin, attested in the Inariyama inscription, and tensōjin, attested in the Eta Funayama inscription, both denoting particular kinds of attendance or administrative service to the Great King rather than membership in a fully organized corporate group. From roughly the late fifth century and increasingly across the sixth, this looser pattern of individual service developed into the more formally organized be, hereditary occupational or service groups encompassing craft specialists — including metalworkers, weavers, and potters — military retainers, and administrative or record-keeping personnel, some of whom, particularly those associated with writing and record-keeping, are closely connected to the immigrant scribes and craftsmen from the Korean Peninsula examined in Chapter 9.

This developing service structure represented a genuine, if still limited and gradually maturing, form of institutional specialization. Rather than every task the royal household required being performed by generalized dependents or by members of the ruler's own family, particular groups became associated, often hereditarily, with particular specialized functions — a pattern that parallels, and likely reinforced, the broader hereditary logic of the uji and kabane structures already examined. Storehouse management, in particular, deserves attention: the growing scale of the paramount ruler's household, and its role in receiving, storing, and redistributing prestige goods, tribute, and resources drawn from allied regional elites, required increasingly organized administrative arrangements, even in the absence of writing systems fully adapted to the Japanese language, a development still in its early stages during this period and treated further in Chapter 9.

It is important for students to understand these developments as genuinely significant without overstating their institutional maturity, and without assuming that a fully organized be system was already in place throughout the fifth century. The individual service attendants of the fifth century and the more formally organized be groups of the later fifth and sixth centuries together represent real steps toward the kind of organized, differentiated administrative apparatus that the mature ritsuryō state, examined in Chapter 13, would eventually establish on a far more comprehensive and formally codified basis. But they remained, in this earlier period, closely tied to personal service to specific royal or elite households, organized substantially along hereditary lines, and dependent on continuing relationships of loyalty and obligation rather than on impersonal, written administrative law. The Yamato court of the sixth century was becoming more institutionally differentiated than the chiefly network Chapter 7 described, but it had not yet become a bureaucratic state in the fuller sense that later chapters will examine.

Section 8.10

A Polity Becoming a State

By the end of the sixth century, the political order centered on the Kinai region had become considerably more durable, hierarchical, and institutionally recognizable than the network of powerful, archaeologically visible chiefs described in Chapter 7. A paramount ruler, described by contemporaries and by foreign observers alike using a consistent vocabulary of kingship, stood at the center of a court increasingly organized through hereditary uji, marked by titled kabane rank, and served by specialized be groups managing craft production, military service, and administrative tasks. Ritual authority, inseparable from more narrowly defined military and diplomatic power, reinforced the ruler's standing among the elite families whose continued cooperation that standing depended upon. And Yamato's influence, extended through marriage, exchange, alliance, and selective coercion rather than uniform administrative control, reached — unevenly, and without eliminating independent regional power — from the Kantō plain to Kyushu.

This was, by any reasonable historical standard, a genuine political achievement, and a substantial development beyond the chiefly hierarchy of the Kofun age examined in Chapter 7. It was not, however, the centralized imperial state that would emerge from the seventh-century reforms examined in Chapter 13, nor should students imagine the sixth-century Yamato court as simply an earlier, smaller-scale version of that later state, waiting only for time and growth to become it. The transformation from the negotiated, uji-and-kabane-organized court of the sixth century to the law-code-governed, more uniformly administered imperial state of the eighth century involved genuine institutional innovation, much of it prompted by continental models, political crisis, and deliberate reform — developments this textbook examines in the chapters immediately following this one.

The final question this chapter leaves open, and that the next several chapters will begin to answer, is what happens when a court already organized around kingship, hereditary clans, ritual authority, and expanding regional alliance encounters a new set of continental religious ideas, institutional models, and political vocabularies. Chapter 9 examines Yamato's deepening relationship with Korea and the continental world — the migration, craftsmen, writing, and technology that flowed into the archipelago through this period's diplomatic and commercial connections. Chapter 10 examines the arrival of Buddhism and the controversy it provoked. Chapters 11 through 13 examine the clan rivalry, court reform, and eventual construction of a fully codified imperial state that followed. The Yamato polity described in this chapter is the foundation on which all of that later transformation was built — a foundation real and consequential in its own right, but still, in the sixth century, a court of negotiated alliances and hereditary service rather than a centralized administrative state.

The Inariyama Sword Inscription

Creator: Unknown metalworker or workshop, commissioned by Owake, a member of an elite service lineage · Date: the inscription dates itself to a xin-hai (辛亥) cyclical year, conventionally and now predominantly identified as 471 CE; 531 CE was historically proposed as an alternative and remains a minority view · Type: 115-character gold-inlaid inscription on an iron sword, recovered from the Inariyama tomb, Saitama Prefecture

Context: The sword was excavated from the Inariyama tomb in 1968. For a decade afterward its heavily corroded surface obscured the inscription entirely; only during conservation work in 1978 did X-ray examination reveal 115 gold-inlaid characters distributed across both faces of the blade, 57 on one side and 58 on the other. The inscription traces an eight-generation lineage and describes its commissioner, Owake, and his ancestors as having served a ruler identified by the title "Great King" (大王), in association with the royal name Wakatakeru.

Paraphrase of the inscription's content (not a verbatim translation): The inscription traces Owake's ancestors across eight generations, stating that they served as leaders of sword-bearing attendants. Owake himself states that he served the Great King Wakatakeru at the Shiki court and assisted in matters of governance, and that he had this sword made to record the basis, or merit, of his family's long service. The inscription does not state that the sword was given to Owake by the Great King as a royal gift.

Analysis: This inscription is exceptionally valuable precisely because it is contemporary and indigenous: unlike later chronicle tradition, it was not compiled generations afterward under the political needs of a later court, and unlike the Chinese dynastic record, it was not produced by a foreign court describing Wa from outside. It shows, in words chosen by people living within the political order itself, that lineage, inherited status, and service to a ruler bearing a specific royal title were understood as central to elite identity in this period. At the same time, the inscription is brief, its precise wording and the identity of some named individuals remain subjects of scholarly debate, and it describes only one family's relationship to the royal court — it cannot, by itself, establish how widely or evenly this pattern of service applied across the archipelago's many regional elites.

Historical Debate

When Can We Legitimately Speak of "Yamato"?

The question: At what point in this long process of political development can historians responsibly begin using the term "Yamato polity," and what evidence should be decisive in answering that question?

Interpretation A — An early, archaeologically grounded starting point: Some historians emphasize the Kinai region's regional preeminence, visible archaeologically from the third and fourth centuries in tomb scale and the wide distribution of certain prestige goods, and argue that a recognizably Yamato-centered order — even if not yet institutionally elaborate — can reasonably be dated to this earlier period, since the region's political preeminence, once established, provided the essential foundation for everything that followed.
Interpretation B — A later starting point grounded in institutional and textual evidence: Other historians argue that the term "Yamato polity" should be reserved for the period from which contemporary institutional evidence — the sword inscriptions, the Chinese diplomatic record of the Five Kings of Wa, and the emerging uji and kabane vocabulary — actually survives, placing the meaningful emergence of a recognizable polity, as opposed to a merely preeminent chiefdom, in the later fifth and sixth centuries.

Evidence: Both positions draw on the same underlying archaeological sequence described in Chapter 7, but differ in how much institutional weight to place on regional preeminence alone, as opposed to the additional, later-appearing evidence of kingship vocabulary, diplomatic titles, and elite service structures.

Why it matters: How historians answer this question shapes how they describe the pace and nature of early Japanese state formation, and shapes how cautiously later chapters can describe continuity between the Kofun-period chiefly hierarchy and the institutionally developed court of the sixth century.

Historical Debate

How Centralized Was Yamato Authority?

The question: Given the evidence examined in this chapter, how much real, practical authority should historians attribute to the fifth- and sixth-century ōkimi over regional elites across the archipelago?

Interpretation A — A direct-rule or proto-state model: Some historians, drawing especially on the extensive titles claimed in King Bu's petition to the Liu Song court and on the wide geographic spread of Yamato political vocabulary shown by the Inariyama and Eta Funayama inscriptions, argue that the Yamato court by the later fifth century exercised a meaningful degree of direct authority over much of the archipelago, functioning as an early, if still developing, state rather than merely an alliance network.
Interpretation B — A paramount-chief or negotiated-alliance model: Other historians argue that the same evidence is better explained by a model of paramount chieftaincy resting on negotiated alliance, marriage, exchange, and selective coercion, in which regional elites such as Kibi retained substantial independent power and the ōkimi's authority depended on continued elite cooperation rather than enforceable central command.

Evidence: Both positions draw on the diplomatic titles claimed and granted in the Chinese record, the sword inscriptions' evidence of service relationships, and the archaeological evidence for continued regional elite power, but weigh the significance of claimed titles against the evidence for persistent regional independence differently.

Why it matters: This question shapes how historians understand the relationship between the sixth-century Yamato court and the far more centralized, law-code-governed state that would emerge from the seventh-century reforms — whether that later state represents a natural extension of already-substantial central authority, or a genuine and difficult transformation of a much more loosely organized alliance system.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From Kofun Chiefs to Yamato Court

What Changed

  • An archaeologically visible hierarchy of chiefs became describable, through inscriptions and diplomatic records, in terms of a paramount ruler bearing a consistent title, "Great King"
  • Elite families increasingly organized themselves through named, hereditary uji associated with specific functions, rather than through more informal or purely locally based chiefly authority
  • Status became expressible through titled kabane rank, adding a vocabulary of formal standing to existing relationships of wealth and military power
  • Individual service attendance (hito) gave way, from the late fifth century onward, to more organized, hereditary be service groups performing craft, military, and administrative functions
  • Yamato rulers pursued formal continental diplomatic recognition, seeking and receiving titles from the Liu Song court that had no clear precedent in the earlier Kofun-period evidence examined in Chapter 7

What Stayed the Same

  • Political authority remained personal, relational, and negotiated rather than impersonal and bureaucratically enforced
  • Powerful regional elites, such as those in Kibi, retained substantial independent power and resources
  • Access to prestige goods and control over their distribution continued to mark and reinforce elite status, much as in the Kofun period
  • Ritual and claims of sacred legitimacy remained inseparable from political authority
  • No standing, salaried, centrally directed bureaucracy existed; service and status remained substantially hereditary and personal

Why the pattern matters: Treating this period as a gradual, uneven institutional development — rather than either a simple continuation of Kofun-period chieftaincy or an early version of the later imperial state — allows students to appreciate both the genuine achievement of fifth- and sixth-century Yamato kingship and the real institutional work that seventh-century reform would still have to accomplish.

Cause and Effect

How a Chiefly Network Became a Court

Long-term causes

The Kinai region's accumulated regional preeminence in wealth, tomb-building capacity, and access to continental prestige goods, established across the fourth century and examined in Chapter 7, provided the underlying resources from which a more institutionally elaborate court order could develop.

Short-term causes

Intensifying diplomatic engagement with Liu Song China, competitive pressures among Kinai and regional elites for status and access to the paramount ruler, and the practical need to organize growing numbers of specialized craftspeople, military retainers, and administrative dependents all encouraged the development of more formal titles, lineage structures, and service arrangements during the fifth and sixth centuries.

Effect

A paramount ruler bearing a recognized title, supported by hereditary uji, titled kabane rank, and specialized be service groups, presided over a court whose political vocabulary and, to a real if uneven extent, whose influence extended across much of the archipelago.

Long-term consequence

This institutionally developing but still negotiated and uneven court order provided the essential political and social foundation on which the more centralized, law-code-governed imperial state of the seventh century would eventually be constructed, following the additional transformations examined in Chapters 9 through 13.

Historical complexity: The development from chiefly network to Yamato court was not a smooth, inevitable progression toward the later imperial state; it involved continuing competition among powerful families, uneven regional cooperation, and contingent diplomatic and military circumstances whose outcomes were not predetermined.

Everyday Life and Environment

Service, Craft, and the Court Household

Although the surviving evidence for this period concentrates heavily on rulers, elite lineages, and diplomatic titles, the functioning of the emerging Yamato court depended on the labor of many people whose individual names and experiences did not survive in any inscription or chronicle. Craftspeople attached to be service groups produced the iron swords, gold inlay, horse trappings, and other prestige goods whose distribution reinforced elite status throughout this political order; without their specialized skill, the very objects this chapter uses as evidence — the Inariyama and Eta Funayama swords among them — would not exist. Agricultural communities across the Kinai region and beyond produced the surplus that supported elite households, funded tomb construction, and sustained the growing administrative and ceremonial demands of an increasingly organized court.

Military retainers, some organized through be service groups and some through more informal ties to particular uji, provided the armed capability that backed diplomatic claims and, at times, enforced submission on resistant regional chiefs. Household dependents managed the practical business of storing, recording, and redistributing tribute, prestige goods, and resources drawn from allied elites across the archipelago — work that, in its own modest way, anticipated the far more elaborate administrative apparatus of the later ritsuryō state. Understanding these everyday dimensions of court life, even in outline, helps prevent this chapter from reducing the Yamato polity's emergence to a story exclusively about kings, diplomatic titles, and elite lineages, and reminds students that every prestige good, every tomb, and every diplomatic mission rested on the labor of people whose individual experience the surviving evidence, for this period, cannot fully recover.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced how the archaeologically visible hierarchy of Kofun-period chiefs, examined in Chapter 7, developed across the fifth and sixth centuries into a more institutionally recognizable political order historians call the Yamato polity: a Kinai-centered court organized around a paramount ruler, hereditary elite clans, systems of formal rank, and ritual authority, whose influence extended, unevenly, across much of the Japanese archipelago.

Central to this chapter's argument has been the introduction of two bodies of evidence largely unavailable to Chapter 7's archaeological approach: contemporary indigenous inscriptions, preserved on the Inariyama and Eta Funayama swords, and the Chinese dynastic record of diplomatic missions sent by the Five Kings of Wa to the Liu Song court. Together, these sources allow historians to describe the paramount ruler of this period using a title, ōkimi, that contemporaries themselves used, and to trace, with appropriate caution, the geographic reach and diplomatic ambitions of fifth-century Yamato kingship.

The chapter has explained how elite politics in this period came to be organized through uji, hereditary corporate lineages associated with particular functions, territories, or forms of service, and through kabane, titles indicating status and relationship to the royal court — while cautioning against imagining either institution as fully standardized or bureaucratically administered at this early stage. It has also traced the emergence of specialized be service groups performing craft, military, and administrative functions, representing a genuine, if still limited, step toward institutional differentiation.

Political authority in this period, the chapter has argued, cannot be separated from ritual practice and claims of sacred legitimacy, even though the fully developed institutional Shinto tradition of later centuries did not yet exist. Royal standing depended on a combination of ritual precedence, military capability, control over prestige goods, diplomatic recognition, and continuously renewed relationships with powerful elite families — a form of authority that was real but negotiated, contingent, and dependent on elite cooperation, rather than automatically and permanently secured.

The chapter has been equally attentive to the limits of Yamato influence. Regions such as Kibi retained substantial independent power even while participating, unevenly, in the wider Yamato-associated order. Marriage alliance, exchange of prestige goods, and selective military cooperation or coercion — rather than uniform administrative control — extended Yamato's reach from the Kantō plain to Kyushu, as the geographic spread of the sword inscriptions demonstrates.

Throughout, the chapter has insisted on careful distinctions among different categories of evidence: archaeology, which shows relative wealth and hierarchy; contemporary inscriptions, which directly attest to political vocabulary and relationships; Chinese dynastic records, which report Yamato's diplomatic claims and standing from outside; and later Japanese chronicles, compiled centuries afterward, which preserve court tradition that must be used with appropriate caution rather than treated as contemporary record.

The chapter has consistently avoided describing the fifth- and sixth-century Yamato order as a unified nation-state, its paramount ruler as an "emperor" in the later institutional sense, or its kabane and be systems as fully developed bureaucratic institutions. It has instead presented a genuine, consequential, but still developing political order — one considerably more institutionally recognizable than the Kofun-period chiefly network, but not yet the centralized, law-code-governed imperial state that would emerge from seventh-century reform.

This chapter's findings are not incidental to later Japanese history. The kingship, clan structure, ritual authority, and administrative beginnings examined here provided the essential political and social foundation on which the transformative developments of the following chapters — continental connections and migration, the arrival of Buddhism, intense clan rivalry, and eventually comprehensive institutional reform — would be built.

Students who understand the Yamato polity as a genuine but still negotiated and uneven political achievement — supported by real evidence, subject to real scholarly debate, and clearly distinguishable from the more centralized imperial state of later centuries — are well prepared to move forward into Chapter 9's examination of Yamato's deepening relationship with Korea and the continental world.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Ōkimi
"Great king" or "great sovereign," the title historians use for the paramount ruler of the fifth- and sixth-century Yamato polity, distinct from the later title tennō.
Yamato polity
The modern historian's label for the Kinai-centered political order that developed across the fifth and sixth centuries, organized around a paramount ruler, hereditary elite clans, formal rank, and ritual authority.
Kinai
The region encompassing the Nara and Osaka basins, the geographic core of the emerging Yamato political order.
Five Kings of Wa
A conventional label for a succession of Wa rulers recorded chiefly in the Liu Song dynastic history as sending diplomatic embassies to that court between 421 and 478, with an earlier 413 embassy under the Eastern Jin and later promotion notices recorded by the Southern Qi (479) and Liang (502).
Song shu
The official dynastic history of the Liu Song dynasty, containing the principal fifth-century external record of Wa kingship and diplomatic titles, though not the earliest Chinese evidence for Wa altogether.
Wakatakeru
A royal name preserved in the Inariyama sword inscription, plausibly identified by many scholars with the ruler later remembered as Yūryaku and with the Wa king "Bu" recorded in Chinese sources.
Uji
Hereditary elite kin groups or corporate lineages, often associated with a particular function, territory, or form of service, sharing a collective name and claimed common ancestry.
Kabane
Hereditary titles attached to particular uji, indicating general status and relationship to the paramount ruler's court; examples include omi, muraji, and kimi.
Be (service group)
Specialized groups organized around particular crafts, forms of labor, or administrative tasks performed in service to the court or to powerful uji.
Inariyama sword inscription
A 115-character gold-inlaid inscription on an iron sword from a tomb in Saitama Prefecture, recording an eight-generation lineage and service to a ruler titled "Great King," associated with the name Wakatakeru.
Eta Funayama sword inscription
A 75-character silver-inlaid inscription on a sword from a tomb in Kumamoto Prefecture, Kyushu, naming the attendant Murite and recording his service to a paramount ruler, demonstrating the geographic spread of Yamato political vocabulary.
Sacred kingship
The inseparability of political authority from ritual practice and claims of ancestral or divine legitimacy in this period, without the fully developed institutional Shinto framework of later centuries.
Kibi
A powerful regional polity in present-day Okayama Prefecture whose large tombs and distinctive material culture illustrate the persistence of independent regional elite power.
Liu Song dynasty
The southern Chinese dynasty (420–479) that received diplomatic missions from Wa kings and recorded their titles in the Song shu.
Tributary diplomacy
A diplomatic system in which peripheral rulers, such as the kings of Wa, acknowledged Chinese imperial preeminence in exchange for titles and recognition.
Baekje, Silla, Gaya
Kingdoms and confederacies of the Korean Peninsula whose shifting relationships with Wa shaped diplomatic and military claims examined in this chapter.
Kojiki / Nihon Shoki
Early eighth-century court chronicles containing genealogies and narratives about earlier rulers that must be treated as later court tradition rather than contemporary record.
Ōtomo, Mononobe, Katsuragi
Among the leading uji associated with the fifth- and sixth-century Yamato court, linked to military, ritual, and regional political functions respectively.
Epigraphic evidence
Evidence in the form of inscriptions, such as the sword inscriptions examined in this chapter, distinguished from archaeological grave goods and from later textual tradition.
Negotiated authority
A model of political power resting on continually renewed alliance, ritual legitimacy, and elite cooperation rather than enforceable, impersonal bureaucratic command.
Anachronism
The error of applying a later concept, identity, or category to an earlier period; relevant here to describing fifth-century rulers as "emperors" or the Yamato order as a unified nation-state.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has carried the archaeological hierarchy introduced in Chapter 7 into its first fully institutional chapter: the emergence of a paramount kingship, hereditary elite clans, formal rank, and ritual authority that historians describe, with appropriate caution, as the Yamato polity. It has shown that even with evidence as thin and fragmentary as two sword inscriptions and a handful of diplomatic exchanges recorded by a foreign court, historians can reconstruct a genuine and evidence-based account of how political authority in the archipelago became more institutionally recognizable — while remaining honest about how much this evidence still leaves uncertain.

The chapter's careful distinction among archaeology, indigenous inscription, foreign diplomatic record, and later chronicle tradition matters beyond this chapter's immediate subject. It demonstrates a method of critical source evaluation that students should carry forward into every subsequent chapter of this textbook, particularly those addressing periods, such as the Nara and early Heian court examined in Part III, where later literary and chronicle tradition threatens to overwhelm the more limited contemporary record.

The chapter has also planted an important seed for the rest of the book: the recognition that Japanese political centralization was neither instantaneous nor uniform, but proceeded through negotiated alliance, uneven regional cooperation, and genuine institutional innovation over generations. This complexity anticipates the textbook's continuing insistence that "Japan" as a centralized state, no less than "Japan" as a unified people, is a historical construction built gradually and unevenly — not a timeless category that can be projected back onto the fifth and sixth centuries.

Students who understand the Yamato polity as a genuine political achievement, grounded in real if limited evidence and clearly distinguished from the later imperial state, are well prepared to move forward into Chapter 9, where Yamato's deepening relationship with Korea, migration, craftsmen, writing, and continental technology reveals another crucial dimension of the same broader story of early Japanese state formation.
