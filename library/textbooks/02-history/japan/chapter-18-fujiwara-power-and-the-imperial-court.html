Part III — Nara and Heian Japan

Fujiwara Power and the Imperial Court

Japanese History: From Jōmon Japan to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

In the year 1018, according to the diary of one aristocrat who was present, a banquet was held to celebrate a daughter's elevation to the rank of empress. Her father, the most powerful man at court, is said to have composed a poem comparing his own fortune to the full moon, lacking nothing. Whether or not the moment happened exactly as it was later remembered, it captures the puzzle of this chapter: how could a man who was not the emperor, and who never claimed to be, stand so evidently at the center of the court?

Chapter 17 ended with the new capital of Heian-kyō (modern Kyoto) established and the ritsuryō machinery of government reorganized around it. This chapter asks the question that Chapter 17 deliberately left open. If Heian-kyō was the political center, who actually exercised power within that court, and how did aristocratic families come to dominate government without displacing the emperor as the source of political legitimacy? The answer given by generations of historians has been a single family name: Fujiwara. But that name, taken alone, conceals as much as it explains. The Fujiwara were not one institution, they did not seize the throne, and their dominance was not a plan carried out across centuries. It was a historical outcome, produced gradually and contested at nearly every stage.

The central mechanism was kinship. Through the marriage of daughters to emperors and crown princes, Fujiwara men became maternal relatives of reigning and future sovereigns. Because elite households in this period often raised children in close relationship with the mother's family, a prince might grow up in the house of his maternal grandfather. When such a prince became emperor, that grandfather was extraordinarily well placed to advise him, to control who reached him, and to speak in his name when he was too young to rule. From the middle of the ninth century, this position was formalized in two titles, sesshō (regent for a child or minor emperor) and kampaku (chief adviser to an adult emperor), which this chapter will define with care because they are often misunderstood.

The chapter's principal claim is paradoxical, and it will be tested repeatedly. The Fujiwara became extraordinarily powerful because they did not abolish imperial authority. Their system worked by placing themselves beside, around, and maternally behind the emperor. Imperial legitimacy and Fujiwara political power therefore reinforced one another, even while individual emperors, retired emperors, rival Fujiwara branches, other aristocratic families, bureaucrats, and imperial women resisted, bargained with, and sometimes outmaneuvered particular Fujiwara leaders. Fujiwara power was never simply the emperor's weakness. It was a way of exercising the emperor's authority.

The chapter's chronological frame runs from roughly 806, the year of Emperor Kanmu's death and the beginning of the succession problems that followed, to 1068, the accession of Emperor Go-Sanjō, whose lineage did not fit the usual pattern of regent-dominated sovereignty. This is the period historians often call the age of "regent politics" (sekkan seiji), a label that is useful shorthand but one this chapter will examine critically. Within it, we will trace the settlement of early Heian succession disputes, the machinery of government, the branching of the Fujiwara family, marriage politics, the creation of regent government under Fujiwara no Yoshifusa and Fujiwara no Mototsune, the limits placed on Fujiwara control by emperors such as Uda and Daigo, the consolidation of the Northern House, the career of Fujiwara no Michinaga, and the everyday work of aristocratic bureaucracy and ceremony.

Students should keep in mind the narrowness of this world. The court aristocracy amounted to a small fraction of the archipelago's population, perhaps a few thousand people who held rank and office in the capital, and the documents they left reflect their own concerns. Farmers, artisans, provincial communities, Emishi and other frontier peoples, and the majority of women appear in these sources only at the edges, if at all. Chapter 16 examined some of those peoples, and Chapter 20 will turn to what was happening beyond and beneath the capital as estates, provincial magnates, and armed households altered the balance between center and region. This chapter, by contrast, is about the inner political life of the court itself. It should therefore be read as an account of one important arena of power, not of Japan as a whole.

The evidence for this arena is unusually rich in one respect and unusually partial in another. Court aristocrats kept diaries recording appointments, ceremonies, illnesses, and conversations, and official histories and legal compilations survive. But these sources were written by and for the people who benefited from the system, and later tales celebrating Fujiwara glory were composed after the fact. The chapter therefore treats ceremony as government, treats accusations of conspiracy critically, and treats later narratives of Fujiwara triumph as interpretations to be tested rather than as transparent records. It connects directly to the chapters around it: Chapter 17 built the political center, this chapter explains how power operated within it, and Chapter 19 will examine the gendered, literary, and aesthetic world that this same court produced.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how the early Heian succession disputes, especially the Kusuko Incident of 810, shaped the political balance between reigning and retired emperors and secured the permanence of Heian-kyō.
  2. Describe how the Heian court governed through both ritsuryō offices and extra-statutory institutions such as the Chamberlain's Office and the kebiishi.
  3. Analyze why ceremony, rank, precedent, and access to the sovereign functioned as mechanisms of government rather than decorative ritual.
  4. Distinguish the Fujiwara as a family of competing branches from the anachronistic image of a single, unified political machine.
  5. Explain how marriage, maternal kinship, and household residence patterns created political influence, while recognizing the agency of imperial consorts and mothers.
  6. Differentiate sesshō from kampaku, and trace the formation of regent government under Yoshifusa and Mototsune.
  7. Evaluate the limits on Fujiwara control, using the reigns of Uda and Daigo and the exile of Sugawara no Michizane.
  8. Assess critically the accusations and outcomes of succession crises such as the An'na Incident of 969.
  9. Interpret the career of Fujiwara no Michinaga, including the offices he actually held and the sources that shaped his later reputation.
  10. Compare older and newer historical interpretations of whether the Fujiwara "ruled Japan."
  11. Contextualize the accession of Go-Sanjō in 1068 as a sign of the dependence of Fujiwara power on imperial marriage and succession.
  12. Connect the political structure of the tenth- and eleventh-century court to the developments treated in Chapters 17, 19, and 20.

Historical Context

The court that this chapter examines inherited an ambitious institutional design. As earlier chapters explained, the ritsuryō state of the seventh and eighth centuries was built on law codes borrowed and adapted from Tang China: a hierarchy of court ranks, a Council of State (Daijō-kan) presiding over eight ministries, provincial governors appointed from the capital, and a system of taxation and household registration meant to tie the countryside to the center. Chapter 13 traced the making of this order and Chapter 14 the building of the Nara capital, Heijō-kyō. Chapter 15 examined the entanglement of the court with Buddhist temples, and the political tensions surrounding monastic influence, including the ambitions of the monk Dōkyō, helped persuade Emperor Kanmu to abandon Nara. Chapter 17 then described the move to Nagaoka and finally to Heian-kyō in 794 and the reorganization of government that accompanied it.

The Fujiwara were already a prominent family by the time of that move. The name was granted to Nakatomi no Kamatari, an adviser to the future Emperor Tenji, near the end of his life in the seventh century, and his son Fujiwara no Fuhito was a leading figure in the codification of law and in the early eighth-century court. Fuhito's four sons founded the four "houses" (ke) of the family, a branching examined in Section 18.3. Fuhito's daughters also married into the imperial line, so that marriage between the imperial house and the Fujiwara was not an invention of the ninth century. What changed later was the scale, regularity, and institutional consequence of the pattern.

Two features of the Heian political environment deserve emphasis at the outset. First, court society was organized by rank and office to an extraordinary degree. A person's position in the hierarchy determined where he or she could stand, whom he or she could address, which offices were available, and what income and privileges followed. Access, in other words, was a political resource. Second, the emperor remained the sole source from which rank, office, and legitimate authority flowed. No aristocrat, however powerful, could appoint himself, and no family ever tried to remove the imperial line and replace it with its own. The tension between a small circle of families who controlled the practical operation of the court and a sovereign who alone conferred its authority is the subject of this chapter.

Students should also understand what this chapter does not cover. The estates (shōen) that increasingly supported aristocratic households, the provincial magnates and armed families who rose in the countryside, and the military power of great monasteries belong to Chapter 20. Women's literature, the vernacular kana script, The Tale of Genji, and the aesthetics of court life belong to Chapter 19. The retired-emperor government that emerged in the late eleventh century (insei) may be briefly foreshadowed here but is not narrated. The chapter's task is narrower and, for that reason, deeper: to explain how politics worked at the court between about 806 and 1068.

Section 18.1

After Kanmu: Succession and the Political Settlement of the Early Heian Court

Emperor Kanmu died in 806, leaving a capital that was only twelve years old and a court still adjusting to the costs of building it. His eldest surviving son, Heizei, took the throne and pursued a program of administrative reduction, trimming offices and tightening oversight of provincial government. Heizei's reign was short. In 809 he fell ill and abdicated in favor of his younger brother, who became Emperor Saga, and then withdrew to the old capital at Heijō (Nara). The arrangement looked like an orderly transfer of power. Within a year it had become a crisis, and the way it was resolved shaped the political culture of the Heian court for generations.

The crisis is traditionally called the Kusuko Incident (Kusuko no hen) after Fujiwara no Kusuko, a court lady of the Shikike branch of the Fujiwara who was a favorite of the retired Heizei and who held a senior post in the palace service. Some modern scholars prefer the alternative name Heizei Retired Emperor's Incident, on the grounds that the traditional label directs attention to a woman and her brother rather than to the retired emperor whose authority was at stake. In 810 Heizei announced that the capital should return to Heijō, and he began to issue orders as though he still held sovereign power. Saga responded decisively. He moved against Kusuko's brother, Fujiwara no Nakanari, who was killed, Kusuko died by poison, Heizei took Buddhist vows, and the crown prince, Heizei's son, was removed from the succession. Saga's own line was secured.

Historians read the episode in several ways. On the surface it was a succession dispute between two brothers, one retired and one reigning, each with supporters. But it also settled a larger question: whether Heian-kyō was to be a permanent capital or one more temporary seat that a change of ruler might abandon. Kanmu's move had been contested, and Nara retained supporters among families and temples with long roots there. The outcome made a return to Nara politically unthinkable. It also revealed that an emperor's abdication did not necessarily mean his disappearance from politics. A retired sovereign retained prestige, property, attendants, and a claim on the loyalty of courtiers. The tension between reigning and retired emperors would recur across the Heian period and, as Chapter 20 will show, eventually become a governing system in its own right.

The incident also illustrates the role of the Northern House of the Fujiwara in the emerging order. Around the time of the crisis Saga created a Chamberlain's Office (kurōdo-dokoro) to handle confidential documents and transmit imperial wishes directly, and the early appointees included Fujiwara no Fuyutsugu of the Northern House and Kose no Notari. Fuyutsugu, whose family stood to gain from the sidelining of the Shikike branch, became a trusted official of the reigning emperor. The Fujiwara did not rise as a bloc. One branch's advantage often came at another's expense, and Kusuko and Nakanari were themselves Fujiwara. This point will matter in Section 18.3.

Saga's long reign (809–823), and his retirement as a respected senior figure until his death in 842, helped stabilize the new capital. Saga's court adapted government to Heian-kyō through compilations of supplementary regulations and the use of new offices. A notable measure was his decision to remove many of his numerous sons and daughters from the imperial family and to grant them the surname Minamoto, turning princes and princesses into subjects. This reduced the cost of supporting a large imperial kin group and created a new category of high-status nobles whose origins lay in the throne. Minamoto men would hold senior ministerial posts across the ninth and tenth centuries, and as later sections show, they sometimes competed with the Fujiwara, as in the case of Minamoto no Takaakira.

Saga abdicated in 823 in favor of Junna and lived as a retired emperor who continued to exert influence, and when Junna in turn abdicated in 833, the succession passed to Saga's son Ninmyō. It was after Saga's death in 842 that the Jōwa Incident occurred, in which conspirators were accused of plotting to move the crown prince Tsunesada, a son of Junna, out of the succession. Those accused were punished, Tsunesada was deposed, and Ninmyō's son Montoku became crown prince. Because Montoku's mother was a daughter of Fuyutsugu, the incident advanced the fortunes of Fuyutsugu's line and of his son Yoshifusa, then rising in office. Historians debate how much of the alleged plot was real, but the important observation is structural: succession disputes were fought partly through accusations, and those who benefited from the outcome wrote much of the surviving record.

Section 18.2

How Did the Heian Court Govern?

Before the Fujiwara can be understood, students need the machinery in which they worked. On paper, the Heian court preserved the ritsuryō structure described in earlier chapters. At its head stood the emperor. Below him, the Council of State (Daijō-kan) coordinated the eight ministries, and it was led by a Chancellor (Daijō daijin), a Minister of the Left (Sadaijin), a Minister of the Right (Udaijin), and, from the later eighth century, an Inner Minister (Naidaijin), followed by counselors (dainagon, chūnagon, sangi). Every official held a rank from a graded scale, and office and rank were meant to correspond. Provincial governors (kokushi) were appointed from the capital, and the court's decisions reached the provinces through official orders.

In practice, the court did not discard this system. It governed through offices and procedures layered alongside it. Historians describe the new institutions as extra-statutory (ryōge no kan), meaning "outside the codes," because they were not provided for in the original ritsuryō statutes. The Chamberlain's Office, created in the early ninth century, is the best-known example. Its staff, the chamberlains (kurōdo), served at the emperor's side, controlled the flow of documents to and from him, and handled matters of confidence. Over time the office and its heads gained influence out of proportion to its modest beginnings, because access to the sovereign was itself a form of political authority. The kebiishi, a police and judicial body originally concerned with maintaining order in the capital, grew into an important institution that handled investigations, prosecutions, and the enforcement of court rulings, gradually absorbing functions of older offices.

Historical Vocabulary
Ryōge no kan (extra-statutory office)

Ryōge no kan refers to offices created outside the framework of the ritsuryō codes (ryō), which is why the phrase means literally "offices beyond the statutes." Examples include the Chamberlain's Office and the kebiishi, and, in a sense, the roles of sesshō and kampaku themselves.

The concept matters because it corrects a common misunderstanding. The court did not abandon the ritsuryō state in favor of something different. It continued to use ranks, ministries, and codes, while adding practical institutions that were more flexible and closer to the emperor. Students should therefore avoid describing the Heian government as a "collapse" of the earlier system. It was a layering, in which formal structures remained the language of legitimacy and new institutions did much of the work.

The daily deliberation of state affairs also changed. Major decisions had once been framed in the formal Council of State, but by the tenth century a great deal of business was conducted at meetings of the highest-ranking nobles, called jin no sadame after the guard station where such councils were held. Petitions and reports from the provinces reached the court, senior officials debated them with reference to precedent (senrei), and the outcome was communicated as an imperial decision. Precedent had enormous authority. Because so much depended on how a given matter had been handled before, aristocrats prepared themselves by studying and recording prior cases, and family expertise in ritual and administrative practice became a form of capital passed from father to son.

Historical Vocabulary
Kugyō (senior nobles)

Kugyō is a collective term for the highest-ranking court nobles: the ministers, counselors, and others of the third rank and above. This small group, numbering perhaps a few dozen at any given time, took part in the deliberations that decided major appointments and policy. Below them stood a much larger group of officials of fourth and fifth rank, some of whom were permitted to enter the emperor's residential hall and thereby gained access to the sovereign.

The distinction between kugyō and lesser officials structured life at court. Membership in the kugyō depended on family standing as well as ability, and it was largely concentrated in a handful of lineages. Students should not treat the term as equivalent to "cabinet." The kugyō deliberated but did not act as a modern ministerial body, and their authority derived from rank and imperial appointment.

Ceremony must be treated as government, not decoration. Audiences with the emperor, the New Year's banquets, annual observances tied to the calendar, enthronement rites, the awarding of ranks and offices, the placement of officials according to precedence, and the formal reception of envoys were the means by which hierarchy was made visible and enforced. Who stood where, who was allowed to speak, who held a particular ritual role, and who was excluded were political facts. A man who lost his place in a ceremony might lose his standing at court, and disputes over ritual precedence could occupy senior officials for months. Because ceremonial competence was expected of nobles, the ability to perform correctly, and to record and teach the correct forms, was a real political asset. Chapter 19 will explore the aesthetic dimension of court life. Here, it is enough to observe that appointments, rank, and ceremony were the ordinary working parts of the system in which Fujiwara power operated.

Section 18.3

The Fujiwara Were a Family, Not a Single Political Machine

It is tempting to speak of "the Fujiwara" as one institution pursuing a continuous plan from the seventh century onward. The evidence does not support this. The name was conferred on Nakatomi no Kamatari near the end of his life, and it descended through his son Fuhito, whose four sons became the founders of four houses: the Southern House (Nanke) under Muchimaro, the Northern House (Hokke) under Fusasaki, the Ceremonial House (Shikike) under Umakai, and the Capital House (Kyōke) under Maro. The eighth century saw all four brothers die in the smallpox epidemic of 737, a reminder that family fortunes were vulnerable to accident, and the branches fared differently in subsequent decades.

By the early Heian period the Northern House had emerged as the most successful, in part through the careers of men such as Fuyutsugu and his son Yoshifusa, and in part through good fortune and shrewd marriage. But other Fujiwara branches continued to hold offices and to compete. The Kusuko Incident, for example, was in part a conflict involving the Shikike branch. Even within the Northern House, brothers, uncles, and cousins competed for the senior offices and for the ability to marry daughters into the imperial line. Some of the most bitter struggles in the later tenth century were between sons of the same father, as Section 18.7 will show.

The Fujiwara also competed with families whose fortunes rose and fell across the period. The Tachibana, Ōtomo, Ki, and other older lineages were represented at court, and the Minamoto, created by Saga's decision and its successors, produced ministers who were of imperial descent. Scholarly families such as the Sugawara and Ōe rose through learning in Chinese letters and law. These families were not equal in resources or influence, but the point is that Fujiwara dominance was historically produced, not predetermined. It required continual success in specific contests over specific offices, marriages, and successions.

The concept of the "house" is important here. Aristocratic lineages were not corporations in the modern sense, but they held property, transmitted expertise, and kept records that tied them to particular roles. A leading man served as head of the house, often called by a later term such as chōja, and controlled resources such as household lands, retainers, and connections. The concentration of such resources in the Northern House made it a formidable base for political activity, and later chapters will show how it extended into the countryside through estates. But the head of a house was still an individual who could fail, die young, or lose an argument, and the history of this period reads as a series of such contingencies.

Section 18.4

Marriage Politics and the Power of Maternal Kin

The engine of Fujiwara influence was marriage. To understand why, students must first understand how elite households worked in this period. Among the court aristocracy, marriage often did not involve a wife moving permanently into her husband's household. A man might visit his wife in her family's residence, or the couple might live in her parents' house for years, and children were frequently raised there, by their mother's kin. The pattern varied by period, rank, and circumstances, and historians debate how uniform it was, but it was common enough among the aristocracy to shape politics. A young prince born to a Fujiwara consort might grow up in the mansion of his mother's father, surrounded by maternal relatives who oversaw his education, protected his interests, and shaped his understanding of court.

When that prince became emperor, especially as a child, his maternal grandfather and uncles stood in a uniquely strong position. They knew the emperor personally, they could control who was admitted to his presence, they could advise him on appointments, and they could act on his behalf when he was too young to govern. Their status as maternal kin (gaiseki) gave them a legitimate claim to participate, grounded in kinship rather than in office alone. The strategy therefore required Fujiwara men to marry their daughters to emperors or crown princes and then to ensure that those daughters bore sons and that those sons were designated as heirs.

It is crucial not to describe Fujiwara women simply as pawns moved on a marriage board. Imperial consorts and empresses, and the emperor's mothers, had their own households, retinues of attendants, property, religious patronage, and channels of information. They participated in decisions about succession and appointments, and their views mattered to fathers and brothers. Some women acted with real independence, supporting or opposing their male relatives, as the case of Empress Dowager Senshi in the 990s will show. Women were also constrained: their marriages were arranged, their success was measured by the birth of sons, and they could be sidelined if they failed to produce an heir or if their fathers lost influence. Chapter 19 will develop the literary and cultural lives of court women. Here it is enough to establish that they were political actors and that Fujiwara strategy depended on their participation.

Cause and Effect

Marriage Alliance and Regency

Long-term causes

Court society tied political access to rank and proximity to the sovereign; elite marriage and residence patterns often raised children in the mother's household; the Fujiwara had a long record of imperial marriages and a stock of experienced officials.

Short-term causes

The removal of rivals in succession disputes such as the Jōwa Incident; the availability of daughters of marriageable age; the succession of child emperors, who needed a guardian.

Event or transformation

A Fujiwara daughter marries a crown prince or emperor, bears a son who is named heir, and grows up in the maternal grandfather's household. When the child becomes emperor, the grandfather acts as regent or chief adviser.

Immediate effects

The maternal grandfather controls access to the sovereign, influences appointments, and shapes court decisions. Rival branches compete for the next imperial marriage.

Long-term consequences

The Northern House becomes durable at the center of court politics; regency becomes an established institution; the imperial line is preserved even as it is managed.

Historical complexity

No marriage guaranteed an emperor. Daughters could fail to bear sons, sons could die young, emperors could resist their in-laws, and rival families could arrange competing marriages. The system worked only when several contingencies aligned.

Section 18.5

Yoshifusa, Mototsune, and the Creation of Regent Government

The institutional center of the chapter is the creation of regent government. Its architect was Fujiwara no Yoshifusa, a son of Fuyutsugu. Through the 840s and 850s Yoshifusa rose steadily, benefiting from the Jōwa Incident and from marriage. His daughter Akirakeiko became a consort of Emperor Montoku, and she gave birth to a son in 850, Prince Korehito, who was named crown prince as an infant, an unusual step that placed the child at the center of the succession. When Montoku died in 858, the boy, aged eight or nine, was enthroned as Emperor Seiwa. Yoshifusa, his maternal grandfather, took charge of government on the child's behalf.

This was a landmark. In earlier times a regent (sesshō) had been a member of the imperial house acting for a young or female sovereign. In tradition, Prince Shōtoku had been such a figure for Empress Suiko, a role Chapter 12 examined critically. Yoshifusa was the first man outside the imperial family to occupy the position. When Seiwa acceded in 858, Yoshifusa, already Daijō daijin, exercised the functions of regency in practice. The surviving chronicle records the explicit imperial command authorizing him to conduct the affairs of state in 866, so sources and modern accounts differ over whether his regency should be dated from 858 or from its formal articulation in 866. What is clear is that from the late 850s the governing of the empire during Seiwa's minority rested with Yoshifusa, and that court society accepted the arrangement.

Historical Vocabulary
Sesshō and Kampaku

Sesshō designates a regent who acts on behalf of an emperor who is a child or otherwise unable to rule. Kampaku, sometimes translated "chief adviser" or "regent for an adult emperor," designates a figure who oversees documents and advises a sovereign who has reached adulthood. Both titles carried extraordinary influence, but neither meant shōgun, and neither was a prime minister in the modern parliamentary sense. Neither replaced the emperor, and neither could appoint the sovereign, though those who held them heavily influenced succession.

The distinction matters for reading sources correctly. A sesshō in effect exercised the sovereign's powers, issuing decisions in his name. A kampaku, by contrast, had to work with an adult emperor who might have views of his own, and the relationship could be negotiated or contested. Students should also note that the two offices were not always continuously held: there were intervals with neither, and one man could hold the offices in succession, as Michinaga's son Yorimichi did.

After Yoshifusa's death in 872, his adopted heir and nephew Fujiwara no Mototsune became the leading figure of the Northern House; his securely attested regency as sesshō begins with Yōzei's accession in 876, when Seiwa abdicated and the new emperor was a child of about nine. Mototsune's relationship with Yōzei deteriorated as the young emperor grew older. Chronicles portray Yōzei as violent and unfit, and in 884 Mototsune took part in his removal and in the enthronement of Prince Tokiyasu, then in his mid-fifties, as Emperor Kōkō. Students should treat the chronicle accounts of Yōzei's conduct with care, since they were written by those who benefited from the outcome. What is clear is that a leading Fujiwara had helped depose one sovereign and choose another, without claiming the throne himself, and that Kōkō was expected to rely on Mototsune. In this period Mototsune's role as chief adviser to an adult emperor took shape, and it was later understood as the origin of the kampaku.

The authority of this new role had to be negotiated. The Akō controversy of 887–888 shows how. When Kōkō died and Emperor Uda succeeded, Uda's court issued an edict confirming Mototsune in his position and using the term akō, a title with classical Chinese associations. A scholar, Tachibana no Hiromi, argued that the term was purely honorary and carried no administrative duties. Mototsune, taking offense, withdrew from government, and business stalled. The dispute was resolved when Uda had the edict rewritten and Hiromi was censured. The episode is instructive because it shows a Fujiwara leader defending his authority through a contest over the wording of an imperial document, and because it shows that even a young emperor could initially resist. It also reveals how far law, classical learning, and the precise language of offices mattered in the exercise of power.

The creation of regent government did not end the presence of emperors in politics. It changed how their authority was exercised: through a senior kinsman who mediated between the sovereign and the state. That mediation made both sides dependent on one another. The emperor needed a capable guardian to manage the court, and the regent needed an emperor to give his decisions legitimacy. The consequences of this mutual dependence are explored in the remainder of the chapter.

Section 18.6

Emperors, Bureaucrats, and Limits on Fujiwara Control

A smooth narrative of Fujiwara rise would be misleading. When Mototsune died in 891, Emperor Uda did not appoint a successor as kampaku. Uda, whose own mother was not a Fujiwara and who had spent part of his early life outside the direct line of succession, governed with a group of officials that included Fujiwara but also men of other origins. He consulted scholars, gave prominence to talented bureaucrats, and maintained close personal control of the chamberlains. His reign (887–897) is remembered as a period of unusually active imperial participation. In 897 Uda abdicated in favor of his son Daigo, later took Buddhist vows, and remained an influential senior figure for decades.

Daigo's long reign (897–930) has been idealized in later tradition as a model era of imperial rule. Court-sponsored projects of the period, including the compilation of the law supplement Engi-shiki and the completion of a national history, Nihon Sandai Jitsuroku (901), reflect a government that took its documentary and legal tasks seriously. The later nostalgia for the Engi era should be treated as an interpretation, since later aristocrats used the memory of Daigo's reign to argue for their own visions of good government. But the fact that the period could be remembered this way indicates that Fujiwara dominance was not continuous and that emperors could preside over administratively energetic courts. A similar interval followed the death of Fujiwara no Tadahira in 949, when the office of regent or chief adviser remained vacant for about eighteen years during the reign of Emperor Murakami.

The career of Sugawara no Michizane illustrates the pressures of the period. Michizane (845–903) belonged to a family of scholars and specialists in Chinese literature, the Sugawara, who held teaching positions at the court's university and served as advisers on Chinese learning. He was not a commoner. He was a high-ranking aristocrat and bureaucrat whose family had held office for generations. Under Uda he rose rapidly, holding posts including that of chamberlain and later senior offices, and in 894 he was appointed head of an embassy to Tang China. He advised that the mission be reconsidered given conditions in China, and the embassy was not sent, a decision often treated as the end of the Tang missions. Michizane's own diplomatic and scholarly reputation was considerable, and his daughter married into the imperial family, into the household of a son of Uda.

Under Daigo, Michizane became Minister of the Right in 899, while Fujiwara no Tokihira, Mototsune's son, held the office of Minister of the Left. In 901 Michizane was accused of plotting to replace Daigo with another imperial prince, the husband of his daughter, and was demoted and sent to Dazaifu in Kyushu as a nominal deputy. He died there in 903. The episode is often told as the destruction of a talented outsider by jealous Fujiwara, and this story deserves to be resisted. Michizane was already among the highest officials. The clash involved succession politics, rivalry between Michizane and Tokihira, and the balance among networks of support at court. Whether Michizane truly plotted is impossible to establish, and the sources are those of the victorious side. What the incident shows is that the court's most senior figures were vulnerable to accusation, and that a scholar's rise depended on patronage as well as talent. Michizane's afterlife is a different story: after a series of misfortunes at court, including deaths and disasters that contemporaries came to attribute to his vengeful spirit, he was honored and eventually venerated as the deity Tenjin, a religious development later chapters will encounter.

The importance of bureaucratic learning deserves emphasis without turning into a discussion of literature, which Chapter 19 will handle. Advancement in the civil service depended on training in Literary Chinese, knowledge of law and administrative precedent, mastery of ritual, and skill in composing documents. Families such as the Sugawara and Ōe specialized in these skills, and their expertise made them valuable to emperors and regents alike. A capable bureaucrat could therefore be an ally of the throne against a dominant family, or a partner of that family, depending on circumstances. This complexity is why the picture of a simple contest between "the emperor" and "the Fujiwara" must be replaced by an account of shifting alliances among emperors, retired emperors, Fujiwara branches, Minamoto and other nobles, and scholar-officials.

Section 18.7

The Northern Fujiwara Consolidate Power

The middle of the tenth century saw the Northern House recover and consolidate its position. Fujiwara no Tadahira, Tokihira's brother, served as regent for the child Emperor Suzaku from 930 and as kampaku from 941, and his authority was widely accepted. After his death in 949 the court entered the interval of Murakami's more direct governance. But Tadahira's sons, Saneyori and Morosuke, built a foundation for a longer-lasting dominance. Morosuke's daughter Anshi became a consort of Murakami and the mother of Emperors Reizei and En'yū, and this strengthened the branch descending from Morosuke. In 967, when Reizei was enthroned, Saneyori became kampaku.

The next decades were shaped by repeated marriages, child emperors, maternal-grandfather relationships, control of senior offices, and the marginalization of rivals. A physical development also mattered: after a fire in 960 damaged the palace, emperors increasingly resided in the mansions of aristocrats, often maternal relatives, a practice noted in the Geography Study in Section 18.4. Living in a Fujiwara mansion strengthened the ties between the emperor and his maternal house and gave that house closer control over access.

The An'na Incident of 969 illustrates how such competition was resolved. Minamoto no Takaakira, a son of Emperor Daigo, had risen to the position of Minister of the Left. He was accused of involvement in a plot to depose the crown prince, Morihira, later En'yū, and to place another prince, Tamehira, who was Takaakira's son-in-law, on the throne. Takaakira was demoted and exiled to Dazaifu, and Reizei abdicated in favor of En'yū, then about ten years old, for whom Saneyori became sesshō. Takaakira was later pardoned, but the effect was to remove the last major Minamoto challenger of the older generation from the top ranks and to leave the Northern Fujiwara without a comparable rival.

Historians interpret the incident with caution. The accusation of conspiracy came from those who profited, and the record does not allow a confident judgment of whether a plot existed, or how serious it was. Some scholars emphasize that the incident was less a Fujiwara purge than a sequence of events in which several actors, including anxious officials and informants, contributed. The safest conclusion is that the accusation was politically decisive whatever its truth, and that students should treat the phrase "conspiracy uncovered" as the official account rather than as an established fact.

Section 18.8

Michinaga and the High Point of Fujiwara Power

The narrative climax of this chapter is the career of Fujiwara no Michinaga (966–1028). A warning about accuracy is needed from the start: Michinaga is often called "the kampaku," but he was not simply that. He dominated government through other offices and relationships and served for a short period, in 1016–1017, as sesshō. The conventional association of his name with regency, encouraged by the later title of his diary, should not erase the actual offices he held.

Michinaga was the fifth son of Kaneie, and his early prospects were not those of a certain leader. His brothers Michitaka and Michikane held the regency in turn, and both died in 995, apparently in an epidemic, within weeks of one another. Michinaga's rise depended on a contest with his nephew Korechika, Michitaka's son, who enjoyed the favor of Emperor Ichijō and of Ichijō's consort Teishi, Michitaka's daughter. Michinaga had a crucial supporter: his sister, Empress Dowager Senshi, the emperor's mother, who is recorded as pressing Ichijō to grant Michinaga authority to review documents before they reached him. This granted Michinaga a role called nairan ("inner inspection"), which gave him a supervisory position over documents without the title of regent. In 996 Korechika and his brother were disgraced after an incident in which Korechika's followers were involved in an attack on a retired emperor, and both were sent from the capital.

Michinaga then applied the marriage strategy at extraordinary scale. In 999 he placed his daughter Shōshi in Ichijō's household, and in 1000 she was raised to the rank of empress (chūgū) while Teishi was given a separate title, so that for the first time two consorts held the rank of empress at once, an innovation that reflects Michinaga's control of ceremonial rank. Shōshi bore two sons, both of whom became emperors: Go-Ichijō and Go-Suzaku. Michinaga's other daughters married other emperors and princes, including Kenshi to Emperor Sanjō, Ishi to Go-Ichijō, and Kishi to Go-Suzaku. As a result, Michinaga was father-in-law or grandfather to several emperors and heirs at the same time.

His relationship with Sanjō shows that the system had limits. Sanjō, who reigned from 1011, was not Michinaga's grandson, and he had ideas about how to govern. Sanjō suffered from failing eyesight, and Michinaga pressed for his abdication in 1016 in favor of Michinaga's grandson Go-Ichijō, then a child. Sanjō's son Atsuakira was named crown prince but resigned in 1017 under pressure, and another of Michinaga's grandsons was named in his place. The episode reveals both the extent of Michinaga's leverage and the fact that he had to exercise it through a sequence of negotiations and pressures on individuals. Michinaga received nairan authority in 995 and became Minister of the Right later that year; in 996 he became Minister of the Left, a post he held until early 1017. He served as sesshō from 1016 until transferring the regency to Yorimichi in 1017. Late in 1017 he was appointed Daijō daijin (Chancellor), resigned that office in 1018, and took Buddhist vows in 1019.

How did Michinaga's power work? Historians identify several elements that reinforced one another. First, daughters and imperial grandchildren gave him the maternal position. Second, senior court office, especially his tenure as Minister of the Left from 996 to early 1017, gave him enormous influence in the deliberations of the highest nobles. Third, he controlled a large share of appointments, including provincial governorships, which were valuable sources of income and which court nobles sought through patronage. Fourth, his wealth, household networks, and connections enabled him to reward followers and support ceremonies. Fifth, he enjoyed access to emperors and imperial women, and the mansion where he lived, the Tsuchimikado, was frequently used as a residence by emperors and empresses. Sixth, he could arrange ceremonial precedence, so that rituals visibly confirmed his status. His construction of the temple Hōjōji from 1019 was both an act of devotion and a display of his resources.

The court's business under Michinaga was not confined to family affairs. In 1019 raiders from across the sea, known in Japanese sources as the Toi, attacked Tsushima, Iki, and northern Kyushu. The Dazaifu administration, under the leadership of Fujiwara no Takaie, organized a defense, reported to the capital, and received the court's response. The episode reminds students that Kyushu was the empire's gateway to the continent and that the court's decisions reached distant places through the same networks of rank and appointment. It also shows that the court's foreign relations were then limited: the great embassies to China had ended, and contact with the continent was managed largely by Dazaifu and by merchants.

Primary Source Window

A Court Diary and the "Full Moon" Banquet of 1018

Creator: Fujiwara no Sanesuke, a senior noble and diarist · Date: 1018 · Type: Court diary (kanki), the Shōyūki, a private record kept over many decades

Context: Court aristocrats kept diaries in Literary Chinese so that they and their descendants would have a record of correct procedure, precedent, and events. Michinaga's own diary, later called the Midō Kanpakuki, covers roughly 998 to 1021 and records appointments, ceremonies, illnesses, and family events. But the best-known account of a banquet marking the elevation of Michinaga's daughter Ishi as empress in the tenth month of 1018 comes from the Shōyūki of Sanesuke, who was present. Because Michinaga is not known to have recorded the poem in his own diary, the tradition of the poem rests substantially on Sanesuke.

Paraphrase (not a verbatim quotation): The diarist reports that at the celebration Michinaga asked him to respond to a poem that Michinaga had composed, in which Michinaga compared his own situation to that of the full moon, without any part missing. The diarist says he declined to answer with a poem of his own and proposed instead that the assembled guests chant Michinaga's verse together, which they did.

Analysis: The passage tells historians that political authority and social celebration were not separate. A banquet honoring an empress's investiture was also a display of a family's position, and a poem was a way of making a claim. The diary also shows the diarist's own position: he is portrayed as a careful observer who chooses how to respond. Historians should note limitations. The entry reflects a single witness's account, the poem's wording may have been affected by later copying and by the reputation the verse acquired, and the diary does not tell us how Michinaga's rivals, other women of the court, or people outside the aristocracy understood the moment. The poem has often been treated as a legendary emblem of Fujiwara triumph, but a responsible reading treats it as evidence of self-presentation and of a diarist's choice about what to record, not as a summary of Michinaga's power.

Questions for students:

  1. What does this source say happened at the banquet, and who is the narrator?
  2. Who created the diary and for what likely purpose, and why might a court noble record such an event?
  3. What can historians learn from the passage about the relationship between political power, family, and ceremony?
  4. What can historians not establish from this source alone, including the poem's exact wording and the views of people not at the banquet?
Section 18.9

Bureaucracy, Ceremony, and the Everyday Work of Aristocratic Government

Any account of the court that concentrates only on emperors and regents will miss the daily activity that kept it going. What did aristocratic government actually do? Much of its work consisted of receiving and processing petitions, deciding appointments, conferring ranks, adjudicating disputes over land and office, granting exemptions from taxes to favored institutions, drafting and issuing official orders, and organizing the annual cycle of rites and festivals. The court also received reports from the provinces, handled matters of foreign contact through Dazaifu, and responded to disasters and epidemics with prayers, ritual, and relief measures.

Appointments were the central currency. At regular sessions, senior nobles determined who would be given which office, and these decisions were recorded in documents that formed the basis for later precedent. Provincial governorships mattered greatly. A governor (kokushi) collected taxes in the province, and since the court allowed governors considerable latitude, the office could be lucrative. Middle-ranking aristocrats often depended on such appointments for income, and they sought them through relationships with senior nobles. Michinaga and other powerful figures thus distributed offices to followers, who in turn supported them. This chapter treats provincial office as a political resource in the capital, and leaves to Chapter 20 the story of how landholding, provincial magnates, and armed households changed conditions in the countryside.

Documents and precedent structured everything. An official act required correct form. A ceremony required correct sequence. Nobles trained their sons in the detailed knowledge of procedure, and they kept diaries and family records to preserve it. Households became repositories of specialized expertise: one family might be known for a certain office, another for a particular rite, and a third for legal opinions. Disputes over precedence could be intense, since a slight in seating or sequence could signify a change in status. To dismiss such concerns as trivial is to misunderstand the system. In a government run largely by rank and access, ritual correctness was how authority was recognized.

The bureaucracy operated within a set of dependencies. Officials depended on senior nobles for advancement, senior nobles depended on the emperor for authority, and the emperor depended on the officials for the functioning of government. Income arrived through stipends attached to rank and office, through gifts, and through lands. The relationship between the court and the provinces, which Chapter 16 examined for the earlier period, continued to matter. Provincial governors were responsible to the court, and the court's decisions on appointments and taxation set the terms within which provincial elites operated. Frontier regions such as the northeast, home to Emishi communities and their descendants, and Kyushu, with its foreign connections, remained matters of concern that were managed through appointments and reports. But the sources of this chapter are the records of the capital, and the perspectives of provincial populations largely remain outside it.

Section 18.10

Why Fujiwara Power Depended on the Emperor

Michinaga died in 1028, and his son Yorimichi (992–1074) inherited the position. Yorimichi served as regent under Go-Ichijō, Go-Suzaku, and Go-Reizei, holding the office of kampaku from 1019 in most of the succeeding decades and resigning in 1067 in favor of his younger brother Norimichi. His long tenure suggests stability. But his generation also revealed the fragility of the system. Yorimichi's own marriage strategy did not produce an imperial son who could become emperor. Go-Reizei, the son of Go-Suzaku and Michinaga's daughter Kishi, reigned from 1045 to 1068 and died without a son. The pipeline that had produced Ichijō, Go-Ichijō, and Go-Suzaku had run dry.

Yorimichi is remembered also for the Byōdō-in, a villa at Uji that he converted into a Buddhist temple in 1052. Its Phoenix Hall, completed in 1053, has been treated as an emblem of aristocratic religious and aesthetic ambition. The timing has a specific context: the court and its Buddhist advisers believed that 1052 marked the start of the age of the decline of the Buddhist teaching (mappō), and aristocrats turned increasingly to devotions such as those of Pure Land Buddhism. This religious and cultural life will be taken up by Chapter 19 and, in different form, by Chapters 20 and 26. For the political story, the point is that even the most successful Fujiwara leaders were embedded in the same religious and ceremonial culture as the emperors whom they served.

In 1068 Emperor Go-Sanjō took the throne. His father, Go-Suzaku, was a maternal grandson of Michinaga through Empress Shōshi; his mother was Imperial Princess Teishi (later Yōmeimon-in), a daughter of Emperor Sanjō and Michinaga's daughter Kenshi. Go-Sanjō therefore descended from Michinaga on both parental sides, but his maternal grandfather was Emperor Sanjō, whom Michinaga had pressured out of the throne, rather than a Northern Fujiwara regent. Go-Sanjō had been crown prince for decades and was an adult of ability and independent views, and the regent Norimichi remained in office until 1075. The accession did not suddenly destroy Fujiwara power. Fujiwara nobles continued to hold high office for centuries. But it exposed how much the earlier system depended on a continuing supply of imperial marriages and births, and it opened the way for emperors and retired emperors to build sources of authority less dependent on maternal kinship. Go-Sanjō's measures to review estate holdings, and the government of retired emperors that followed his son Shirakawa's abdication, belong to Chapter 20 and beyond. This chapter stops here.

The central conclusion is a paradox. The Fujiwara became extraordinarily powerful because they did not abolish imperial authority. Their power was constructed from the emperor's own legitimacy, through kinship, offices, and ceremony. This is why it lasted, and it is also why it was vulnerable to changes in the succession that they could not control. Individual emperors resisted, Uda and Daigo pursued their own aims, Sanjō defied Michinaga, and Go-Sanjō ended the pattern of maternal regency. But the institution of the throne was never threatened by the Fujiwara, and its sacred status was, if anything, reinforced by a system that made it the source of every rank and appointment. The relationship was one of mutual dependence, not of a strong family overpowering a weak throne.

Historical Debate

Did the Fujiwara Rule Japan?

The question: Older narratives spoke of "regent politics" (sekkan seiji) as a period in which the Fujiwara controlled the state and reduced emperors to figureheads. More recent scholarship has questioned this picture. How should historians define "ruling" in a court whose authority rested on the emperor?

Interpretation A — Regents dominated powerless emperors: Emphasizing the succession of regents, the removal of emperors such as Yōzei, Kazan, and Sanjō, and Michinaga's control of appointments, this view holds that from the ninth century emperors were largely dependent on Fujiwara guardians, and that real power lay with the Northern House.
Interpretation B — Negotiated authority within an imperial system: Emphasizing the reigns of Uda, Daigo, and Murakami, the vacancies in the regency, the rivalries among Fujiwara branches, the role of retired emperors and imperial mothers, and the fact that the Fujiwara's authority derived from the emperor, this view holds that power was shared and negotiated, and that emperors retained real agency.

Evidence: Both positions draw on the same court diaries, chronicles, and records of appointments. They differ in how they weigh moments of Fujiwara pressure against intervals of imperial initiative, and in how far they trust later narratives that celebrate or condemn Fujiwara dominance.

Why it matters: Whether the Fujiwara "ruled" affects how historians understand imperial legitimacy, how they explain the later rise of retired-emperor government and warrior power, and how they interpret the long survival of the throne. Many scholars now see the two positions as overlapping rather than opposed, with the balance varying by reign.

Student task: Choose one reign discussed in this chapter and argue whether Interpretation A or B better explains it, citing at least two pieces of evidence and noting one source-related limitation.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From the Ritsuryō State to the Aristocratic Court

What Changed

  • Extra-statutory offices, such as the Chamberlain's Office and the kebiishi, took on major roles
  • Regency by a non-imperial relative became an accepted institution
  • Precedent, family expertise, and maternal kinship shaped political influence more than the formal codes
  • Emperors increasingly lived in aristocratic mansions, and imperial marriage became a central political mechanism

What Stayed the Same

  • The emperor remained the sole source of rank, office, and legitimate authority
  • The ranks, ministries, and Council of State continued to be the formal language of government
  • Chinese-style learning, ritual, and documents remained the tools of administration

Who experienced change most directly: Senior nobles and officials in the capital felt these shifts most sharply, since their opportunities depended on new offices and marriages. Who experienced greater continuity: Most of the archipelago's population, including farmers and provincial communities, had little direct contact with court politics, though taxation and appointments reached them indirectly.

Why the pattern matters: It shows that the Heian government did not collapse or transform overnight, but was reworked through layering, which helps explain both its durability and the later pressures described in Chapter 20.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has asked who actually exercised power within the Heian court between roughly 806 and 1068, and how aristocratic families could dominate government without displacing the emperor as the source of legitimacy. Its central answer is that Fujiwara dominance was neither a seizure of the throne nor an uninterrupted family dictatorship. It developed gradually through imperial marriage, maternal kinship, control of succession, regency, appointments, court office, residence patterns, and ceremonial access, and it always depended on proximity to the sovereign.

The early Heian settlement laid the foundation. The succession disputes following Kanmu's death, especially the Kusuko Incident of 810, confirmed that Heian-kyō would remain the capital and showed that an abdicated emperor could remain politically significant. Saga's reign stabilized the court and introduced new institutions, including the Chamberlain's Office, and his decision to convert many imperial children into Minamoto subjects created a new high nobility of imperial descent.

The court governed through a layering of old and new. The ritsuryō ranks, ministries, and Council of State continued to supply the language of legitimacy, while extra-statutory offices such as the chamberlains and the kebiishi, councils of senior nobles, and the authority of precedent did much of the practical work. Ceremony was part of government: audiences, appointments, rank, and precedence made hierarchy visible and enforceable, and mastery of ritual and documents was a form of political capital.

The Fujiwara were a family of competing branches, not a single machine. The Northern House prevailed over the Southern, Ceremonial, and Capital Houses in part through success in marriage and office, and within the Northern House brothers and cousins competed bitterly. The family also contended with Minamoto, Tachibana, Ōtomo, Ki, and scholarly houses such as the Sugawara. Their dominance was produced, not predetermined.

Marriage was the engine. Because elite households often raised children with the mother's kin, a Fujiwara maternal grandfather was uniquely placed to advise and speak for a young emperor. Imperial consorts and mothers were not mere pawns: they held households, property, and influence, and they took part in decisions about succession, though they were also constrained by expectations about bearing heirs. No marriage guaranteed an emperor, and the strategy required many contingencies to align.

Regent government was created by Yoshifusa, who acted for the child Seiwa from 858, and extended by Mototsune, who advised adult emperors in a role later called kampaku. The Akō controversy showed that the meaning and authority of this role had to be negotiated. The vocabulary distinction is essential: sesshō served a child or minor emperor, kampaku an adult one, and neither meant shōgun, prime minister, or replacement emperor.

Limits on Fujiwara control were real. Uda and Daigo governed actively and used non-Fujiwara officials, Murakami's reign saw no regent for nearly two decades, and the exile of Sugawara no Michizane in 901 illustrates factional competition among high aristocrats, not a simple morality tale. Later, the An'na Incident of 969 removed a leading Minamoto minister, though the sources on conspiracy must be read as the accounts of the winners.

Michinaga's career marks the high point. He was not simply "the kampaku," but a dominant figure who used nairan powers, senior ministerial office, appointments, wealth, access, and an extraordinary set of imperial marriages, and who served briefly as sesshō. The Toi raid of 1019 reminds us that the court's business reached the frontier through Dazaifu, and the "full moon" banquet illustrates how power and celebration were intertwined, though it rests on a single diarist's account.

The daily work of the system rested on middle-ranking officials, provincial appointments, documents, and precedent. Appointments were the currency of patronage, and provincial governorships were a political resource in the capital, an entry point to themes Chapter 20 will develop. Women managed households and family relations that supported this world, though the sources rarely record them.

The system's dependence on the emperor became visible after Michinaga. Yorimichi's long regency continued the pattern, but his generation failed to secure a Fujiwara-born imperial heir, Go-Reizei died without a son, and in 1068 Go-Sanjō acceded with a lineage outside the usual regent-generating pattern. The Fujiwara became powerful because they worked through the throne, and imperial legitimacy and Fujiwara power reinforced one another even as individual emperors resisted. This understanding prepares students for Chapter 19's study of the court's literary and social world and for Chapter 20's examination of forces outside and beneath it.

Key Terms

Sekkan seiji
"Regent politics," the traditional label for the period of Fujiwara regency; useful shorthand but questioned by historians who stress negotiated authority.
Sesshō
A regent acting for a child or minor emperor; first held by a non-imperial person by Yoshifusa.
Kampaku
The chief adviser to an adult emperor, developing from Mototsune's role; not a shōgun or modern prime minister.
Daijō-kan
The Council of State, the top body of the ritsuryō administration, headed by the Chancellor and the Ministers of the Left and Right.
Kugyō
The senior nobles of the third rank and above who took part in high-level court deliberations.
Ryōge no kan
Extra-statutory offices created outside the ritsuryō codes, such as the Chamberlain's Office.
Kurōdo-dokoro
The Chamberlain's Office, created in the early ninth century to handle confidential documents and communication with the emperor.
Kebiishi
A police and judicial body that grew from a capital-security office into an important court institution.
Jin no sadame
Meetings of senior nobles, named for the guard station where they were held, in which major matters were deliberated.
Senrei (precedent)
Prior practice that carried great weight in appointments, rituals, and legal decisions.
Gaiseki
Maternal relatives of an emperor, whose kinship gave them a claim to political participation.
Hokke (Northern House)
The branch of the Fujiwara descended from Fusasaki that became dominant in the Heian court.
Minamoto
A surname given to imperial children removed from the imperial house beginning under Saga, creating a nobility of imperial descent.
Jōkō / retired emperor
An emperor who has abdicated but retains prestige and influence.
Kusuko Incident (810)
A confrontation between the reigning Saga and the retired Heizei over the capital's location and authority, resolved in Saga's favor.
Jōwa Incident (842)
A purge after Saga's death that removed a crown prince and advanced the Northern Fujiwara.
Akō controversy
A dispute of 887–888 over the wording of an edict granting Mototsune authority, showing that regent power was negotiated.
An'na Incident (969)
The exile of Minamoto no Takaakira after an alleged plot, which consolidated Northern Fujiwara dominance.
Nairan
The authority to inspect documents before they reached the emperor, held by Michinaga before he served as regent.
Satodairi
A "village palace": an aristocratic mansion used as the emperor's residence, especially after palace fires.
Kokushi
A provincial governor appointed from the capital; an office valued as a source of income and patronage.
Kanki (court diary)
A diary in Literary Chinese kept by nobles to record events and precedent.
Dazaifu
The administrative headquarters of Kyushu, a gateway to the continent and a place of exile.
Mappō
The "latter age" of Buddhist decline, believed by many at court to have begun in 1052.
Chūgū / kōgō
Titles of empress; the rare simultaneous granting in 1000 illustrates Michinaga's control of ceremonial rank.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a political order can be dominated by some and legitimated by another. The Heian court did not resolve the tension between imperial legitimacy and practical power by choosing one; it built a system in which each depended on the other. That system was neither a corruption of the ritsuryō state nor a simple continuation of it, but a reworking that used kinship, ceremony, and new offices to manage the throne's authority.

The chapter also shows how the historical record can shape interpretation. The accounts that survive were written by and for aristocrats, and later tales celebrated Fujiwara achievement. Reading them critically, distinguishing established events from accusations, legends, and celebratory memory, is the skill this chapter has asked students to practice.

The developments traced here shaped what followed. The habit of governing through the emperor rather than in place of him, the importance of retired emperors, and the reliance on marriage and succession all continued in altered form. At the same time, the court's absorption in its own ceremonial and family world left largely unexamined the changes that were occurring in the provinces and monasteries, which Chapter 20 will address. And the refined life of the court described in this chapter formed the setting for the literature that Chapter 19 will explore.

Finally, students should remember how narrow this arena was. The Heian court governed a large archipelago whose peoples, from Kyushu to the northeast frontier, experienced the court's decisions mainly at a distance. Understanding how power worked in the capital is essential, but it is one part of the larger history of the archipelago and the societies that developed within and around it.
