Part VII — Tokugawa Japan
50

Learning, Rangaku, Reform, and the Tokugawa Underworld

Japanese History: From Jōmon Japan to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

In 1771 a group of physicians in Edo watched the dissection of an executed criminal’s body and compared what they saw with the plates of a Dutch anatomy book. Three years later, their collaborative translation appeared in print. The episode is famous, but it was only one point in a much larger story: by the early nineteenth century, Tokugawa society was reading, studying, measuring, arguing, borrowing, and, on its margins, gambling and trading beyond the reach of official regulation.

Beyond that episode lay a wide intellectual landscape. By the early nineteenth century, children in many towns and villages learned to read and use the abacus, printers and lending libraries supplied books to readers in the cities, scholars argued over how to read the Confucian classics and the ancient Japanese texts, and a few worked out Dutch medical and astronomical books with limited language and few tools.

The same decades saw repeated attempts by the bakufu and domains to repair their finances and discipline their subjects, famines that exposed the limits of those attempts, a brief armed rising in Osaka led by a former police official, and the punishment of scholars who criticized foreign policy. At the margins of official society, gamblers and fair vendors worked in the gaps left by a thin and divided system of policing. This chapter asks how these developments fit together, and why a regime so adaptive in some ways was increasingly strained in others.

The evidence differs by subject. School regulations, booksellers’ catalogues, and printed texts document learning; edicts and officials’ memoranda document reform; magistrates’ records, village petitions, and later legend document the underworld. Each source has its own viewpoint, and the chapter notes whose voices are missing. Earlier chapters supplied background on status (43), village protest (44), popular entertainment (46), religion (47), controlled foreign exchange (48), and crafts (49), and Chapter 51 begins the diplomatic crisis that follows.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why literacy and schooling expanded in Tokugawa Japan and why historians hesitate over precise literacy rates.
  2. Compare terakoya, domain schools, and private academies.
  3. Analyze how publishing and censorship shaped the circulation of knowledge.
  4. Distinguish Confucian learning and kokugaku as intellectual and political developments.
  5. Trace how foreign knowledge reached Japan through Nagasaki and was translated, as in the Kaitai shinsho.
  6. Assess the achievements and limits of rangaku, medicine, astronomy, and mapping.
  7. Compare the Kyōhō, Kansei, and Tenpō reforms.
  8. Connect famine, debt, and unrest to questions of Tokugawa legitimacy.
  9. Distinguish documented bakuto and tekiya activity from later romanticized legend.
  10. Evaluate whether the Tokugawa order on the eve of 1853 was stagnant or adaptive.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How did expanding schooling and commercial printing change who could participate in intellectual life?
  2. Why did a regime that patronized learning also restrict some kinds of it?
  3. What did Tokugawa scholars learn from foreign books, and how did they adapt it?
  4. Why did reforms repeatedly address symptoms of fiscal and social strain without resolving their causes?
  5. How reliable are policing records and later legends as evidence for bakuto and tekiya?
  6. In what sense were the bakuto and tekiya antecedents of later organized crime, and in what sense is that connection misleading?
  7. Did learning and reform strengthen or weaken Tokugawa authority?
  8. How should historians describe a regime that was adaptive yet increasingly strained?

Historical Context

By the mid-eighteenth century the Tokugawa settlement described in Chapter 42 had endured for well over a century. The shogunate (bakufu) and some 250 domains (han) governed a society divided into legally distinct status groups, as Chapter 43 showed, while Chapter 45 traced the commercial economy that grew up around sankin-kōtai, castle towns, and the cities of Edo, Osaka, and Kyoto. Samurai were salaried stipend-holders living in towns, and commoners increasingly managed trade, credit, and local administration.

Two features of this world matter here. First, government ran largely on documents, from village registers to domain edicts, so reading, writing, and calculation had practical value across classes. Second, official controls were real but thinly staffed, especially outside the main cities. Where administration was thin, informal arrangements filled the gap. Chapter 48 explained that foreign contact was regulated through Nagasaki, Tsushima, Satsuma/Ryukyu, and Matsumae; this chapter follows what scholars did with the information those channels delivered.

Section 50.1

Knowledge, Authority, and Change in Tokugawa Japan

Why did a society that restricted foreign contact, ranked people by birth, and censored books produce so much new knowledge, and why did the same society struggle to resolve its fiscal and social problems? The question frames this chapter. Tokugawa Japan was neither a closed world waiting to be opened nor a smoothly adaptive one. It was a place where schooling, print, scholarship, and commerce expanded under a government that patronized some kinds of learning, feared others, and relied on thin administration.

Three fields are examined together. Learning and the circulation of knowledge, from village schools to Dutch anatomy, widened who could take part in intellectual life. Government reform, from the Kyōhō to the Tenpō era, shows an elite trying to repair its fiscal and moral order. The informal world of gamblers, fair vendors, and local policing shows where official authority was weakest and who bore the cost of its limits. The same people appear across these fields: physicians who were also translators, village headmen who were also readers and sometimes bakuto’s neighbors, magistrates who relied on informants.

The chapter covers roughly 1720 to the early 1850s and stops before the diplomatic crisis assigned to Chapter 51. The sources differ by field: school regulations, booksellers’ catalogues, and printed texts for learning; edicts and officials’ memoranda for reform; magistrates’ and village records, along with later legend, for the underworld. Each carries a particular viewpoint, and the chapter notes it.

Section 50.2

Literacy, Numeracy, and the Expansion of Reading

The uses of literacy differed by status and occupation. Samurai were expected to read Chinese-style texts (kanbun) and the classical Confucian books, and to handle the documents of domain and bakufu administration. Letters across all classes were commonly written in a standard epistolary style (sōrōbun), which made it a shared skill of correspondence. Merchants needed to keep account books, read contracts, and write shop rules and household precepts (kakun), and primers such as shōbai ōrai taught the vocabulary of commerce. In the countryside, village headmen kept land registers, tax records, and copies of petitions, and the huge quantity of surviving village documents (mura monjo) is itself evidence of working literacy among local elites.

Historians distinguish reading, writing, and numeracy, because they did not always go together. Many people could read simple texts without writing fluently, and writing a formal letter was a more demanding skill than recognizing common characters. Reading was taught through model-letter primers (ōrai-mono) that doubled as vocabulary books for particular occupations, and many farmers owned or borrowed practical texts, among them agricultural manuals such as Miyazaki Yasusada’s Nōgyō zensho (1697). Artisans and shopkeepers used pattern books and price lists. For most people literacy was selective and tied to the tasks of their work, not a general mastery of Chinese learning.

Numeracy had an equally practical base. The abacus (soroban) spread through commerce, and Yoshida Mitsuyoshi’s Jinkōki (1627) was an influential arithmetic text. A Japanese tradition of mathematics (wasan), associated with Seki Takakazu (died 1708), developed alongside it, and geometry problems were sometimes inscribed on wooden tablets (sangaku) dedicated at shrines and temples, a sign that mathematical learning had a public, even devotional, audience. Skill with figures was necessary for merchants, village officials who assessed tax, and craftsmen, which helps explain the demand for schooling among commoners.

Women’s literacy varied with status, region, and household. Daughters of samurai and wealthy commoners were often taught to read and write, and to compose letters, and conduct manuals such as Onna Imagawa and the widely circulated Onna daigaku were used in teaching girls, though they also promoted a model of obedience whose real influence on daily life is debated. Diaries and letters by women survive, but in smaller numbers than those by men. Children’s learning usually began around the age of seven, and boys were more likely than girls to continue.

Estimating literacy rates is difficult. Signatures on documents may distinguish those who wrote their names from those who used a seal or mark, but the surviving documents come disproportionately from literate households and better-documented regions. Evidence from school enrollment, from commemorative monuments to teachers (fudezuka) that pupils erected, from book ownership, and from lending records points the same way, but none gives a national figure. Estimates associated with the historian Ronald Dore suggested that by the end of the period roughly 40 percent of boys and 10 percent of girls had some formal schooling. These are rough estimates of school attendance, not measurements of literacy proficiency, and later scholars have questioned them and stressed regional variation. A safer conclusion is that practical literacy was widespread in towns and commercialized villages and more limited in remote areas, and that it grew over the period.

The written language itself reinforced these divisions. Documents mixed Chinese-derived characters with the kana syllabaries, and formal and informal registers differed, so a farmer who could read a village notice in plain script might still be unable to follow a legal text or a scholarly commentary. Literacy was therefore a matter of degree, and a household might hold several levels of skill, from the headman who drafted petitions to the child who had learned only a few hundred characters.

These patterns mattered for the chapter’s larger argument. A society in which headmen, merchants, and many artisans could read and calculate could also circulate petitions, read printed books, and take part in the debates described in later sections. Literacy gave commoners tools to bargain with officials, but it also gave officials a way to govern through documents. Both effects were present, and neither depended on formal schools alone.

Section 50.3

Terakoya, Domain Schools, and Private Academies

Terakoya (also called tenaraijo, “handwriting places”) were small private schools for commoner children, taught by a master (shishō) who might be a priest, a samurai without a post, a doctor, a village official, or a woman. Pupils usually entered around the age of seven and stayed for some years. Students began with writing practice, then read the model-letter primers (ōrai-mono), and often learned the abacus. There was no fixed curriculum or national standard, and parents paid fees in cash or goods, often on festival days. The number of terakoya rose markedly in the nineteenth century, especially in towns and prosperous villages.

Domain schools (hankō) were founded by daimyō to train retainers’ sons. Examples include the Meirinkan in Chōshū (1718), the Jishūkan in Kumamoto (1755), the Nisshinkan in Aizu (1803), and the Kōdōkan in Mito (1841). The curriculum centered on Confucian classics, writing, history, and martial arts, and some added medicine, mathematics, or Dutch learning. Attendance was often required for samurai boys, and examinations or promotions in some domains rewarded diligence. Founding these schools was part of a broader effort to train capable administrators in the reform era described in Sections 50.12–50.14.

Private academies (shijuku) were led by individual scholars and survived on reputation and fees. The Kaitokudō in Osaka, founded in 1724 by merchants and later recognized by the bakufu, combined Confucian study with open-minded inquiry, and its scholars included the rationalist Yamagata Bantō. Hirose Tansō began teaching in Hita, Bungo, in 1805 and opened the academy known as Kangien in 1817. Its sandatsuhō principle set aside status, age, and previous learning at admission, and a monthly ranking (gettanhyō) placed students by results. In Osaka, Ogata Kōan’s Tekijuku (1838) taught Dutch medicine and attracted students from many domains, including the later writer Fukuzawa Yukichi. Academies thus created networks that crossed domain and status lines.

Teaching methods were largely individual. In a terakoya the master set each pupil a model to copy, corrected brushwork, and moved children on at their own pace, so a room might hold pupils of several ages at different stages. In domain schools, students recited and read aloud passages of the Confucian classics (sodoku) before turning to commentary, and teachers lectured to groups. The contrast is a useful reminder that the schools served different purposes: one trained people in practical skills for work, the other in the moral and administrative tradition of a ruling class.

Access depended on status, wealth, and place. A wealthy commoner or village headman could pay for his sons’ and sometimes daughters’ education, whereas many poor children attended only briefly or not at all, since their labor was needed. Girls attended terakoya in some cities in considerable numbers, and women taught in them, though what girls learned often emphasized sewing, etiquette, and letters. Samurai boys’ schooling was tied to hereditary rank, but talent could count, especially in private academies. Chapter 43 explained how status law channeled people along inherited lines, and education softened this only at the margins.

Regional variation was substantial. Edo, Osaka, and Kyoto had many schools, and commercial regions such as the Kantō and Kinai were well supplied. Remote and poorer regions had fewer, and schooling in Hokkaido and among Ainu communities was not part of this system. The Ryukyu Kingdom trained its scholar-officials in Shuri and Kumemura through its own institutions, including Chinese-derived traditions, and not through the Tokugawa domain-school system; Chapters 32–33 treat that tradition. Even within a domain, a school in the castle town might have little connection to the villages.

The significance of these institutions lies partly in their breadth and partly in their diversity. They produced readers, officials, physicians, and teachers. Some later institutions of the Meiji school system built on their buildings, teachers, and habits of learning, as Japanese educational histories stress, but students should not treat Tokugawa schooling as a seamless prelude to Meiji universal education. The earlier system was voluntary, uneven, and shaped by status.

Everyday Life and Society

Reading, Calculating, and Gambling

For many families, a child’s time at a terakoya meant learning to read letters and use the abacus, skills useful for shopkeeping or village business. A town dweller could borrow novels or manuals from a lending library, and a traveler might meet itinerant sellers at a festival or lose money at a roadside dice game. These ordinary encounters connect the chapter’s intellectual and informal worlds.

Section 50.4

Publishing, Booksellers, Reading Markets, and Censorship

Commercial woodblock printing took off in Kyoto in the early seventeenth century, after brief experiments with movable type, and spread to Osaka and Edo. Carvers cut text and illustrations on wooden blocks, which could be stored and reprinted, an economical choice for a script with thousands of characters. Publishers (hanmoto) held rights to particular titles within trade associations, and booksellers formed associations (hon’ya nakama) in the three cities. Distribution relied on shops, traveling sellers, and exchange among booksellers. Nagoya’s Eirakuya was a major regional house, and provincial publishing was otherwise limited, centered on temples and domain schools.

The range of books was wide. Besides novels and picture books, which Chapter 46 treated, the catalogues included dictionaries, primers, Confucian commentaries, agricultural manuals, medical books, almanacs, maps, travel guides, and encyclopedias such as Terajima Ryōan’s Wakan sansai zue (1712). Chinese books arrived through Nagasaki and were reprinted in Japan, often with reading marks to help Japanese readers. Print thus shaped the intellectual world as much as the schools, since it allowed scholars’ work to reach readers who had never met them.

Lending libraries (kashihon’ya) made books accessible. A reader paid a small fee for a loan, and traveling lenders carried books to customers’ doors. A record of 1808 lists 656 lending-book operators in Edo. This system suited a society with rising literacy but limited disposable income, and it meant that a single printed copy could be read by many people. Women and children were among the customers, though the evidence for individual readers is thin.

Censorship developed gradually. A bakufu ordinance of 1721 recognized the booksellers’ associations and made them responsible for policing new publications. It required publishers’ names, and where possible authors’, on books, helped protect publishers’ claims to their printing blocks, and forbade works that criticized Tokugawa rule, discussed the shogun’s family, or promoted unorthodox ideas. Regulation, commercial organization, and censorship were closely tied: the rules that protected a publisher’s property also made him answerable for what he printed. Christian works were banned. The Kansei and Tenpō reforms tightened controls, and individual authors were punished, such as Hayashi Shihei in 1792 for writings on coastal defense. Enforcement was inconsistent. It depended on officials’ vigilance and on the associations’ willingness to police their own members, and authors found ways around it by publishing anonymously or circulating manuscripts.

Print and intellectual change were closely linked. The scholarly debates of Section 50.5 and Section 50.6 depended on printed texts: Itō Jinsai’s and Ogyū Sorai’s followers spread their ideas through published commentaries, and Motoori Norinaga’s Kojiki-den appeared in printed installments between 1790 and 1822. Medical and scientific books reached practitioners far from Nagasaki. Printing also made it possible for scholarship to become a livelihood, since teachers could sell books to their students and others.

Cost and format shaped readership. Books were priced by size and quality, and many scholarly works were printed in small runs. A publisher who held the blocks could reprint a successful title cheaply, and blocks were sometimes sold or leased to other houses, which spread popular works beyond their first market. Rental fees were low enough that a clerk, a craftsman, or a villager’s son could read widely without buying. Travel guides, almanacs, and practical manuals appear to have had the widest audience, while specialized scholarship reached a narrower public of students and teachers, many of whom were themselves connected to schools and academies.

The picture should not be romanticized. Printing was costly, many people in remote regions had little access, and the books that survive overrepresent the genres that were widely reprinted. Records of publishers and the catalogues of booksellers help historians estimate what was available, but not who read what. The history of print in Tokugawa Japan is therefore best read as a story of expanding access that was uneven in region, class, and gender.

Section 50.5

Confucian Learning and Political Thought

Confucian thought was the dominant framework of Tokugawa political and moral discourse, yet it was never a single doctrine. Hayashi Razan (1583–1657) served Tokugawa Ieyasu and his successors, and his descendants kept the Hayashi family at the head of the shogunal scholarly establishment. In 1690 Tokugawa Tsunayoshi had a Confucian temple moved to Yushima in Edo, and in 1691 Hayashi Nobuatsu was appointed head of the associated school. The Hayashi tradition favored Zhu Xi learning, which held that a rational moral order (ri) underlay nature and society and which gave the hierarchies of ruler and subject, parent and child, and husband and wife a natural basis.

Not all Confucians accepted Zhu Xi. Followers of Wang Yangming, such as Nakae Tōju and Kumazawa Banzan, stressed intuitive moral knowledge and its realization in action. Yamazaki Ansai’s school gave Zhu Xi learning a strongly religious and moral cast. Scholars of ancient learning (kogaku) argued that later commentary had obscured the original texts. Yamaga Sokō reinterpreted Confucian ethics for the samurai in a peaceful age. Itō Jinsai, a Kyoto merchant’s son who taught commoners and samurai alike at his academy, emphasized benevolence (jin) and the ordinary relations of daily life over metaphysics.

Ogyū Sorai (1666–1728) pushed the critique further. He argued that the sages of antiquity had created institutions, rites, and laws, and that good government depended on those institutions and on rulers’ practical abilities, not on individual moral cultivation alone. He studied ancient texts closely and insisted on reading them in the language of their time. In his policy writings, addressed to Yoshimune, he proposed that samurai be brought back to the land or otherwise freed from dependence on merchants, and he criticized the commercial economy for undermining the warrior class. His arguments shaped debates about reform, even where his proposals were not followed.

Other thinkers addressed the problems of the commercial age from different angles. Arai Hakuseki advised the bakufu in the Shōtoku period (1709–1716) on finance, diplomacy, and ceremonial. Dazai Shundai, a student of Sorai, accepted that the samurai’s dependence on commerce was a fact and argued that the state should manage trade. Merchant-backed academies such as the Kaitokudō sponsored critical inquiry; Yamagata Bantō’s rationalism challenged supernatural beliefs. In Mito, the compilation of the Dai Nihonshi, begun under Tokugawa Mitsukuni in 1657, developed a historical scholarship that later loyalists used.

The disagreements mattered because they shaped how people understood social hierarchy, education, and reform. Zhu Xi learning supported a moral view of government in which ruler and officials should cultivate virtue and subjects should accept their stations. Sorai’s school supported institutional solutions, such as changes in finance or administration. Wang Yangming’s emphasis on action inspired some critics of officials, including, as Section 50.13 described, Ōshio Heihachirō. Debates over the proper balance between morality and institutions recur in the reforms of Section 50.12 to Section 50.14.

Confucian scholars also worked as employees. Many served daimyō as advisers and teachers (jusha), held stipends, and depended on patrons, which shaped what they could say about government. A scholar who criticized the policy of his own lord risked his position, and this is one reason that the most pointed criticism often came from private teachers or from writers outside the official establishment.

The bakufu patronized Confucian learning but also tried to define its orthodoxy. The Kansei restriction of 1790, discussed in Section 50.11, directed the Hayashi school to teach Zhu Xi doctrine, and in 1797 the school became the shogunal academy (Shōheizaka Gakumonjo). This reflects a tension that runs through the chapter: the regime wanted learning that supported order and also drew on thinkers who criticized the way it governed. Chapter 47 treated Confucianism as a religious and ethical tradition. Here it is important as political thought, and as one of the main languages in which Tokugawa subjects argued about what good government required.

Section 50.6

Kokugaku, Classical Scholarship, and Japanese Antiquity

Kokugaku, usually translated “national learning” or “native studies,” was not a single movement but a set of scholarly projects centered on the language and literature of early Japan. Its roots were philological. Keichū (1640–1701), a Buddhist priest, produced a major commentary on the Man’yōshū, working with commissions from Tokugawa Mitsukuni, and sought to recover the meaning of ancient words through close reading. His method, grounded in evidence from early texts and not later interpretation, was the foundation for what followed.

Kada no Azumamaro (1669–1736), a Shinto priest at Fushimi Inari in Kyoto, is traditionally regarded as an early figure in the movement. A proposal to found a school of classical studies survives under his name, traditionally dated 1728, but it appears to have been drafted and not submitted to the shogunate, so the document should not be confused with the founding of a school. Kamo no Mabuchi (1697–1769) of Hamamatsu studied the Man’yōshū and praised its vigorous, direct style as a lost ancient simplicity. He criticized what he called Chinese-mindedness (karagokoro) and held that the Japanese of the early period had expressed themselves naturally. His students included merchants, priests, and samurai in Edo and elsewhere.

Motoori Norinaga (1730–1801), a physician in Matsusaka who studied medicine in Kyoto, became the movement’s most influential scholar. After a meeting with Mabuchi in 1763 he began the commentary on the Kojiki that he completed in 1798, the Kojiki-den. He argued that the Kojiki should be read in the language of its own time and that its accounts of the kami should be accepted as they stood, not explained away by Chinese philosophical categories. He also wrote on the Tale of Genji, which he valued for expressing mono no aware, a sensitivity to the emotional depth of things, as a counter to moralistic readings.

Norinaga’s views drew criticism. The writer Ueda Akinari debated him over the status of the sun goddess and the reliability of ancient accounts, and Confucian scholars disputed his rejection of Chinese learning. The disagreement shows that kokugaku was one position among many in an active intellectual field. Its scholars used rigorous textual methods, comparing manuscripts and analyzing grammar and phonology, and some of the philological findings they reached remain useful to modern scholars even though the interpretive claims behind them do not.

Hirata Atsutane (1776–1843), from Akita, took the movement in a more religious direction. Claiming to be a posthumous disciple of Norinaga, he wrote on the world of the kami and the afterlife, arguing that the dead entered an unseen realm governed by Ōkuninushi. He drew on Chinese, Buddhist, and even Christian sources while rejecting foreign teachings in principle. His writings found a following among village headmen and wealthy farmers (gōnō) in the countryside, who valued a learning that connected them to local shrines and the land. In 1841 the bakufu ordered him to leave Edo and stop publishing.

The movement’s social base was wide. Norinaga taught from his house in Matsusaka and, by correspondence, many students elsewhere, among them physicians, merchants, shrine priests, and village headmen, and several hundred names appear on his rolls of students. This network spread the scholarship beyond the capital and the castle towns, and it is one reason that kokugaku can be studied as a social as well as an intellectual history. Women also took part, as poets, though they appear less often in the records of scholarly circles.

Kokugaku should not be read as a unified, modern nationalism. Its scholars worked on literature, language, and religion, and their politics varied; Norinaga was a physician who also lectured for the Kii domain, not a political activist. But the movement’s ideas could be put to political use, and later loyalist writers in the Bakumatsu period and the Meiji state drew selectively on Norinaga’s and Atsutane’s views, as later chapters show. Chapter 47 treated the religious side of Tokugawa Shinto; the point here is that kokugaku’s significance lay as much in textual scholarship and in the debate it started as in its later uses.

Section 50.7

Nagasaki, Dutch Interpreters, and the Circulation of Foreign Knowledge

Nagasaki was the one place where European books and instruments entered Japan in regular quantity, but what made it a channel of knowledge was the people who worked within it. Chapter 48 described the regulated trade at Dejima and at the Chinese quarter established in 1689. This section asks how information left Nagasaki: who learned Dutch or Chinese, how books circulated, and why scholars elsewhere cared.

Hereditary interpreters (tsūji) of Dutch and Chinese stood at the center. Families such as the Nishi, Motoki, and Yoshio held posts across generations. Their work was commercial and diplomatic, but some became scholars. Motoki Ryōei (1735–1794) translated Western astronomy and geography, and Yoshio Kōgyū (1724–1800) belonged to the circle around the Kaitai shinsho. Their Dutch was largely the language of trade, which limited what they could read in scientific texts and shaped what translators later attempted.

Physicians attached to the Dutch post were a second channel. Engelbert Kaempfer (in Japan 1690–1692) and Carl Peter Thunberg (1775–1776) wrote about Japan and exchanged knowledge with Japanese colleagues; Shizuki Tadao’s translation of part of Kaempfer’s work in 1801 introduced the word sakoku. Philipp Franz von Siebold, who arrived in 1823, taught Japanese students at Narutaki outside Nagasaki from 1824. Students traveled to Nagasaki to study and buy books. The Dutch head’s journey to Edo, annual and later every four years from 1790, brought scholars to visit the Dutch lodging, in Edo the Nagasakiya.

Several institutions governed this traffic. Dutch shipping was limited by an authorized quota, reduced to one vessel a year in 1790 (a quota, not a record of arrivals), and Chinese ships came more often; cargoes were inspected under the Nagasaki magistrates. Imported Chinese-language books were screened by book inspectors (shomotsu aratame yaku), an office the Mukai family held hereditarily, to exclude Christian writings. Dutch books reached Japan through a related but distinct commercial and supervisory channel. Chinese books arrived in large numbers, while Dutch books were few. Japanese obtained them through gifts from Dutch officials, purchases arranged by interpreters, and orders placed by the bakufu, and scarcity made copying by hand the usual means of circulation. Interpreters were employed by the Nagasaki authorities and paid stipends. Their dealings with the Dutch were supervised, and outsiders, including scholars, normally visited Dejima only with permission and with interpreters present.

Interpreters also produced official information. When Dutch ships arrived, the Dutch head supplied a report on world affairs (Oranda fūsetsugaki), which interpreters translated for the bakufu, and a similar series summarized reports from Chinese ships. The bakufu relied on these for news of events such as the Opium War. Knowledge moved from Nagasaki to Osaka, Kyoto, and Edo along the same routes as trade and travel, carried by students, physicians, retainers on duty, and booksellers.

Policy shaped access. Around 1720 Yoshimune relaxed the ban on imported books not concerned with Christianity, and in 1740 he directed Aoki Konyō and Noro Genjō to study Dutch. Chinese learning remained central, since medicine rested on Chinese texts such as the Honzō kōmoku, and Chinese ships brought books, including Chinese translations of European works. Knowledge therefore arrived by two linguistic routes that overlapped, and neither displaced the other quickly.

Edo and Osaka served as hubs. Ōtsuki Gentaku (1757–1827), a student of Maeno Ryōtaku and Sugita Genpaku, opened his school Shirandō in Edo in 1786, and its members held gatherings that became known as the Dutch New Year from 1794. Copyists and booksellers circulated manuscript translations, which often moved outside the print market because of regulation. The scholars involved were mostly physicians, interpreters, and retainers of modest rank, not an official elite, and they chose fields of practical use: medicine, astronomy, geography, botany, and military technology. Dutch learning had little to say about European law, religion, or literature, and the Japanese engagement with it was selective from the start.

Section 50.8

Rangaku, Anatomy, and the Difficulties of Translation

Rangaku, “Dutch learning,” began as a set of individual efforts. In 1754 Yamawaki Tōyō observed a dissection in Kyoto and concluded that the organs did not match the standard Chinese descriptions; his findings were published in 1759. The better-known episode came in 1771, when Sugita Genpaku (1733–1817), a physician of Obama domain, Maeno Ryōtaku (1723–1803) of Nakatsu domain, and others watched the dissection of an executed woman at Kozukappara in Edo and compared what they saw with a Dutch anatomical atlas. They concluded that the Dutch book was accurate, and they decided to translate it.

The book was the Dutch version (Ontleedkundige Tafelen, 1734) of a German work by Johann Adam Kulmus. Maeno, who had learned some Dutch from Aoki Konyō, was the strongest linguist, although his name does not appear among the credits printed in the book, and Sugita led the project, joined by Nakagawa Jun’an and Katsuragawa Hoshū, a shogunal physician’s son who helped with revision. None was fluent. They worked out meanings from the plates, from the Dutch-Chinese-Japanese equivalences they could find, and by coining terms where none existed. The result, Kaitai shinsho, was published in 1774 with illustrations by Odano Naotake, a painter from Akita domain.

Sugita’s memoir Rangaku koto hajime, written decades afterward, dramatizes the struggle and made the translation a founding story. Historians value it but read it as retrospective, written by an elderly man with an interest in how the project would be remembered. Accounts say the translators sought official cover before publication by presenting a copy to the shogun through the court physician Katsuragawa Hosan, Hoshū’s father. That sequence rests on later accounts and not on the strongest archival sources, so it is reported here as probable and not established; the publication of 1774 and its collaborators are independently documented.

The effect on medicine was gradual. Most practitioners kept using Chinese-derived (kanpō) medicine, and the kōhō school developed distinctive Japanese clinical approaches through renewed study of classical Chinese medical texts, especially the Shanghan lun, independently of the European anatomical tradition. Ōtsuki Gentaku completed a revision, Jūtei kaitai shinsho, in 1798, but it was not published until 1826, so a corrected text circulated among scholars for decades before reaching print. By the early nineteenth century, physicians such as Ogata Kōan, who opened his Tekijuku in Osaka in 1838, taught Dutch medicine to large numbers of students; Section 50.9 treats vaccination. European anatomy became one authority among several, whose strength lay in its method of direct observation, and the differences among medical traditions persisted into the Meiji period.

Section 50.9

Medicine, Natural History, Experiment, and Practical Science

Medical practice in Tokugawa Japan was plural. Chinese-derived medicine (kanpō) supplied the main texts, diagnostic ideas, and drugs. Within it the kōhō (ancient-formula) tradition, which predated Yoshimasu Tōdō (1702–1773) but of which he was a leading exponent, favored clinical observation and classical prescriptions over speculative theory. Tokugawa Japan lacked a uniform nationwide system of medical licensing. Physicians commonly trained through families, apprenticeships, and private schools, although some domains regulated medical practice, and Saga introduced examinations and physician licensing in 1851. Patients chose among physicians as well as priests, midwives, and folk healers. Domains employed physicians, and a physician’s standing depended on his teacher, his patrons, and his results.

Because most drugs came from plants, minerals, and animals, medicine pushed scholars toward natural history. Imported drugs were expensive, and the bakufu encouraged domestic cultivation, including medicinal plants at its Koishikawa garden. The study of materia medica (honzōgaku) began from the Chinese Honzō kōmoku and compared its descriptions with Japanese specimens, and scholars such as Ono Ranzan (1729–1810) extended the work through field observation. Hiraga Gennai (1728–1780) organized drug and product exhibitions from 1757 that drew collectors from many regions, and later displayed a Dutch electrostatic generator. He died in prison after a violent quarrel, on the 18th day of the twelfth month of An’ei 8 (24 January 1780 Gregorian), so his death is given as 1779 or 1780 depending on the calendar.

Dutch-mediated knowledge entered at particular points: anatomy and surgery, as Section 50.8 showed, and later instruments and vaccination. It did not replace Chinese-derived practice. Many physicians combined them, studying a Chinese-derived internal medicine and adding Dutch-derived surgical technique, and differences among schools were as much about teachers and lineages as about doctrine.

The surgeon Hanaoka Seishū (1760–1835) of Kii shows this combination clearly. Trained in Kyoto in both Chinese-derived medicine and Dutch-derived surgery, he developed an anesthetic, Mafutsusan, whose name recalls an ancient Chinese anesthetic. It was a mixture of plant drugs, reportedly including a datura species and aconite. On the 13th day of the tenth month of Bunka 1 (14 November 1804 Gregorian; the familiar “13 October” carries the Japanese month and day over without conversion) he removed a breast tumor from a patient under this anesthetic, an operation often cited as preceding the first European use of general anesthesia in the 1840s. His achievement drew on Chinese-derived pharmacology and Dutch-derived surgery. Its circulation beyond his lineage was limited because the formula was kept within his school, but within it his teaching and surgical practice were extensive, and his pupil Kamada Gendai recorded anesthetic procedures in 1839. The ingredients are known from later accounts, and reconstructing a historical formula from them leaves uncertainty about amounts and preparation, so the recipe should not be treated as established.

Medical knowledge was not the same as medical availability. Vaccination illustrates the difference. Cowpox vaccination was known by report before 1849, and earlier attempts to introduce it failed. After Otto Mohnike’s successful introduction at Nagasaki in 1849, Ogata Kōan and colleagues established a vaccination clinic in Osaka later that year (the Jotokan), and its spread depended on networks of doctors, domain support, and the willingness of parents to accept it. Most patients relied on household remedies, temples, and local practitioners, and fees limited access to trained physicians.

Craft traditions also supplied scientific and technical instruments, though not medical ones. Kunitomo Ikkansai (1778–1840), a gunsmith, made telescopes and air guns, and Tanaka Hisashige (1799–1881) built mechanical clocks, adapting European principles to local skills, which Chapter 49 treated as craft history. Taken together, these cases show expanding practical knowledge among physicians, craftsmen, and scholars across status lines, with uneven links among Chinese, Japanese, and Dutch-mediated traditions. They do not add up to a scientific revolution.

Section 50.10

Astronomy, Geography, Cartography, and New Ways of Measuring the World

Calendars mattered to government, because the calendar fixed rites, agricultural advice, and the legitimacy of those who issued it. Shibukawa Shunkai produced the first Japanese-compiled calendar (the Jōkyō calendar) in 1685, and the bakufu established an astronomical bureau soon after. The Hōreki calendar of 1755 proved flawed, and the Kansei calendar, compiled chiefly by Takahashi Yoshitoki and Hazama Shigetomi with others, promulgated in 1797 and implemented in 1798, drew on both Chinese and European astronomy. The Tenpō calendar, implemented in 1844 under Shibukawa Kagesuke, relied more heavily on European methods, including the work of Lalande. Motoki Ryōei adopted heliocentric ideas in 1792 and Shizuki Tadao introduced Newtonian physics in the early 1800s, though these ideas circulated within a small scholarly community.

Geographic knowledge had practical and strategic value, especially as Russian and other ships approached the northern coasts. Inō Tadataka (1745–1818), a merchant and village headman from Sawara who retired and studied astronomy under Takahashi Yoshitoki, began surveying in 1800 with an expedition along the Ezo coast. He received bakufu authorization and some limited help at first but bore much of the cost himself. In later surveys the bakufu took over financing, and the work became an official project.

His method was traverse surveying. Teams measured distances along connected routes, mostly coasts and main roads, with measuring chains or ropes, took bearings with compasses, and recorded the results as chains of linked lines. Because errors accumulate along such a traverse, Inō corrected it with astronomical observations of latitude from selected points. The maps, completed in 1821 after his death under Takahashi Kageyasu’s supervision and prepared at three scales, were the product of sustained collaboration. They were remarkably accurate for their time, particularly along the coasts, while the interior was less thoroughly surveyed.

Mapping also involved the north. Mamiya Rinzō (1775–1844), who had trained with Inō, explored Karafuto (Sakhalin) in 1808–1809 and showed that it was an island; his work depended on the knowledge and cooperation of Ainu and other local people along the route, a reminder that frontier geography rested on indigenous guides. Because maps had strategic value, access was restricted, and in 1828 the discovery that Siebold had obtained copies led to the arrest of Takahashi Kageyasu, who died in custody in 1829.

Section 50.11

Official Learning, Intellectual Dissent, and Political Criticism

The bakufu patronized learning but also policed it. In 1790 the Kansei restriction directed the Hayashi educational establishment to teach Zhu Xi doctrine, and in 1797 the school became the shogunal academy; the order was aimed at the Hayashi establishment and did not eliminate other schools. Domains and teachers continued to offer other approaches, and the policy shows the regime’s wish to define orthodoxy at its own institution.

Scholars who linked foreign knowledge to criticism of policy faced greater danger. In 1825 the bakufu had ordered coastal authorities to drive away foreign ships. In 1837 the American merchant ship Morrison, which carried Japanese castaways home, was fired on at Uraga and Kagoshima under that order. The incident set off debate among those with knowledge of the West. Watanabe Kazan (1793–1841), a painter and senior retainer of Tahara domain, wrote Shinkiron, which criticized the policy but circulated only privately. Takano Chōei (1804–1850), a physician and translator, wrote Yume monogatari (1838), an anonymous dream narrative that argued against the use of force.

In 1839 the bakufu arrested a number of scholars in the affair called Bansha no goku. Torii Yōzō, a Confucian official who opposed Western learning, played a leading role in bringing the charges. Kazan was placed under house arrest in his domain and killed himself in 1841. Takano was sentenced to life imprisonment, escaped during a fire in 1844, and died in 1850 when police found him. The punishments were intended to deter unauthorized comment on foreign policy.

The sources for the affair are mostly official records of the investigation and the writings of the men involved, and the officials’ accounts and the scholars’ later reputations tell different stories. Torii Yōzō and the officials around Mizuno Tadakuni treated Western learning as a source of political danger, and Watanabe Kazan and Takano Chōei, who wrote on different grounds and in different ways, were treated together as a group.

The affair is often presented as a clash between enlightened scholars and a backward regime, but the picture is more complicated. Many officials shared the interest in foreign knowledge, and the bakufu itself employed translators and used Dutch-derived information in its astronomical bureau and for coastal defense. The distinction the regime drew was between knowledge it controlled and criticism it did not. Learning flourished as long as it stayed within acceptable limits, and the limits tightened as concern about foreign ships grew.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Knowledge and Governance

What changed: Schools multiplied, print expanded, European learning entered, and reform ideas diversified. What remained continuous: Status hierarchy, the rice-based tax system, the structure of bakufu–domain relations, and official limits on foreign contact. Who experienced change most directly: Urban commoners, scholars, and physicians. Who experienced greater continuity: Many rural households and lower-status groups. Why it matters: Change accumulated within a framework that stayed legally stable, which helps explain both the regime’s resilience and its vulnerability.

Section 50.12

The Kyōhō and Kansei Reforms

When Tokugawa Yoshimune, formerly lord of Kii domain, became shogun in 1716, the bakufu was running chronic deficits. Its revenue came chiefly from the land tax on its own estates (tenryō), collected in rice, while its expenses, including retainers’ stipends, were rising and were themselves paid in rice that brokers had to convert to cash. The commercial economy made this arrangement awkward. Rice tended to fall in price relative to other goods, so samurai living on fixed stipends saw their purchasing power erode; contemporaries summarized the problem by saying that rice was cheap and everything else dear.

Yoshimune’s strategy combined thrift with revenue. He issued repeated orders for frugality and, in 1722, introduced the agemai levy, under which daimyō handed the bakufu a small proportion of their rice revenue in return for a shorter required residence in Edo under sankin-kōtai; the measure was dropped in 1730 once finances had improved. He encouraged the opening of new fields (shinden), sometimes with merchant capital, and moved land-tax collection away from annual inspection of the harvest (kemi), which invited bargaining and bribery, toward fixed rates based on average yields (jōmen). Fixed rates gave the bakufu predictable income and rewarded villagers who raised productivity, but in bad years they sharpened hardship, and village petitions against them recur in mid-century records. Tax receipts rose, though the size of the gain varied by region and is debated.

Yoshimune also changed how government worked. The tashidaka system of 1723 let men of modest hereditary stipend hold senior offices by receiving a salary supplement for the duration of their service, widening the pool of talent. A suggestion box (meyasubako) invited subjects to submit proposals from 1721. Ōoka Tadasuke, as Edo city magistrate, oversaw the organization of ward fire brigades (1720) and the Koishikawa public clinic (1722), and the legal compilation Kujikata osadamegaki appeared in 1742. In 1719 courts were told to stop hearing many civil suits over debts between private parties (aitai sumashi rei), which relieved indebted samurai at the expense of lenders. Yoshimune tried to steer rice prices by supervising the Dōjima rice exchange in Osaka, which he recognized in 1730, and by a recoinage in 1736 intended to raise prices. Around 1720 he relaxed the ban on foreign books not concerned with Christianity, a step noted in Section 50.7.

The results were mixed. Finances improved, but the Kyōhō era also saw the 1732 famine in western Japan, associated with insect damage to rice, and in 1733 a crowd in Edo attacked the shop of a rice dealer, an early large urban disturbance. The reform treated price and revenue problems as questions of administration and discipline; it did not alter the underlying tax base.

Between Yoshimune and the next reform came the ministry of Tanuma Okitsugu (senior councillor 1772–1786, losing office in 1786), who sought revenue from commerce by licensing guilds and monopolies, collecting fees, and promoting development projects. Critics, then and later, charged him with encouraging corruption, and the Tenmei famine and urban riots of the 1780s discredited his government. The famine and the 1787 riots, which followed his fall, helped consolidate the transition. Matsudaira Sadanobu, Yoshimune’s grandson and lord of Shirakawa, who had organized relief in his own domain, took office in 1787 as chief senior councillor.

The Kansei measures aimed at relief, finance, and discipline. Communities were directed to stock granaries (shasō). In 1789 an edict (kien-rei) cancelled debts that bakufu retainers had contracted with rice brokers (fudasashi) through 1784 (Tenmei 4) and set revised repayment terms for loans from 1785 (Tenmei 5) onward; the order concerned these specified loans between retainers and brokers, not private debts throughout Japan. This helped samurai households but made lenders wary, and the bakufu had to support credit afterward. The 1790 Return-to-Village order offered travel funds to migrants in Edo, and a work camp on Ishikawajima (ninsoku yoseba) gave vagrants and released offenders training in crafts. In Edo, wards were ordered to set aside part of their savings from ward expenses, and the resulting fund, run through a town office (machi kaisho), provided relief and low-interest loans. In education, the 1790 restriction required the Hayashi establishment to teach Zhu Xi doctrine, the shogunal school was upgraded in 1797, and retainers faced examinations. Publishing controls led to punishment of the writers Santō Kyōden and Hayashi Shihei.

Sadanobu resigned in 1793 amid friction within the government and widespread unpopularity. The two reforms differed in constituency: Yoshimune relied on rural taxation and some merchant capital, while Sadanobu prioritized samurai relief and urban order and placed costs on brokers and townspeople. Both sought to restore balance between a stipended class and a market economy without changing the tax system itself. Neither was wholly successful or wholly futile; each bought time and set precedent for the next.

Section 50.13

Famine, Fiscal Crisis, and Challenges to Tokugawa Authority

The Tenmei era (1781–1789) brought repeated cold, wet summers that damaged rice crops, especially in the northeast (Tōhoku), where rice cultivation was already near its climatic limit. The eruption of Mount Asama in 1783 added to the disruption: ash and atmospheric effects probably deepened the cooling, though the famine had several causes, including weather, the region’s dependence on rice, and the policies described below. The famine is conventionally dated 1782–1788. Mortality was highest in northern domains such as Hirosaki, where records describe severe depopulation, though figures are uncertain and the surviving accounts, domain records and later narratives, differ.

Weather alone does not explain why the famine became a crisis. Domains collected taxes in rice but also sold rice through Osaka and Edo to raise cash for debts and for the costs of sankin-kōtai. In poor years officials sometimes continued to ship rice out, leaving small reserves, while merchants held stocks for higher prices and granaries proved inadequate. Moving grain between regions was slow and costly. In 1787 crowds in Osaka and Edo attacked rice dealers over several days, and the weakness of the official response helped complete the political transition away from Tanuma’s supporters, who had already lost their leader in 1786. Regional contrast mattered: the southwest was far less affected in the Tenmei years than Tōhoku, yet the west had suffered in the Kyōhō famine of 1732, so no region was permanently secure, and the burden of each crisis fell unevenly on city dwellers, tenants, and day laborers.

The Tenpō famine followed poor harvests beginning in 1833 and reached its worst in 1836, after a cold, wet summer, with consequences continuing into 1837. This chapter therefore dates it approximately 1833–1837, distinguishing the major crop failures from the continuing effects of high prices, hunger, and disease. Tōhoku again suffered most, but central Japan was also affected. Disturbances in 1836 included a large rising in Kai province and another in Mikawa. The bakufu opened granaries, pressed wealthy households to contribute rice, and set up relief huts in Edo, while domain relief varied widely in scale and effectiveness.

Ōshio Heihachirō (1793–1837) was a senior police officer (yoriki) in the Osaka East Magistrate’s office, known for integrity and for exposing corruption. He retired in 1830 and taught at a private academy, the Senshindō, where he followed the Wang Yangming school of Confucian thought, which stressed the unity of moral knowledge and action. During the famine he petitioned the magistrate to open granaries and press wealthy merchants to sell or lend rice. The request was refused, and Osaka’s authorities were criticized for continuing to send rice to Edo while local people went hungry. According to traditional accounts, Ōshio sold his books to fund payments to the poor.

On the 19th day of the second lunar month of Tenpō 8 (25 March 1837 in the Gregorian calendar), he and a band of followers, including former students, minor officials, and villagers, attacked officials’ premises and burned the houses of wealthy merchants. A manifesto denounced officials and merchants and appealed to Heaven’s judgment, and the rebels promised to distribute rice and money. Fires spread and destroyed a substantial part of the city. The rising was suppressed within about a day. Ōshio hid for roughly forty days and killed himself when discovered, on the 27th day of the third lunar month (1 May 1837 Gregorian). At Kashiwazaki in Echigo, Ikuta Yorozu (生田万), a kokugaku scholar influenced by Hirata Atsutane, led another short-lived rising the same year. His surviving correspondence of April 1837 shows that he knew of Ōshio’s actions and proclamations, and historians regard him as influenced by the Osaka uprising, although the two risings were not coordinated.

The Osaka uprising had no program for governing, but its significance was political. A former official trained in Confucian moral philosophy, specifically the Wang Yangming school, publicly accused magistrates and merchants of failing the obligations of benevolent rule, and the bakufu feared imitation. The episode also exposes a structural problem. Samurai stipends were paid in rice, merchants controlled credit and distribution, and villages paid taxes and produced the crop. When harvests failed, each link was strained and officials’ fiscal needs competed with relief. The poor of the cities and the tenant and day-labor households of the countryside bore the heaviest burdens. Chapter 44 treats village protest in depth; here the point is how recurring crises raised questions about the legitimacy of Tokugawa authority.

Section 50.14

The Tenpō Reforms and Domain Experiments

Mizuno Tadakuni, a senior councillor since 1834, took charge of policy after the death of the retired shogun Ienari in early 1841. The Tenpō reforms responded to inflation, fiscal strain, disorder after the famine, and growing anxiety about foreign affairs, sharpened by news of the Opium War. Mizuno explicitly looked to Yoshimune and Sadanobu as models.

Austerity and moral discipline came first. Orders banned luxury goods, costly food, and elaborate clothing and hairstyles, and set rules for entertainers. In 1842 Edo’s theaters were ordered to relocate to the northern outskirts at Saruwaka-chō, unlicensed prostitution was suppressed, and the authors Tamenaga Shunsui and Ryūtei Tanehiko were punished. Prints of actors and courtesans were restricted. These measures cut employment in the entertainment trades. Chapter 46 treated the pleasure quarters; here they illustrate how far administrative reach could extend into daily life.

In late 1841 the bakufu ordered the dissolution of the wholesalers’ associations known as kabu nakama and barred new ones. Mizuno’s advisers blamed the associations’ monopolies for high prices and believed free entry would lower them; orders to reduce prices and rents accompanied the measure. In fact the associations did more than restrict competition. They vetted members, set quality standards, enforced contracts, collected debts, regulated credit among members, coordinated shipping and warehousing, and paid fees to the authorities. They also linked producing regions to metropolitan wholesalers. Abolition allowed new merchants to enter but disrupted credit and distribution, and goods reaching Edo declined. Prices did not fall as intended, and in 1851 the bakufu began to authorize reorganized trade associations again.

Two further measures met resistance. In 1843 the Return-to-Home-Village order (hito-gaeshi-rei) sought to send migrants in Edo back to their villages. Enforcement was difficult, since many migrants had no land or family to return to, and villages were reluctant to take them back. In mid-1843 the land-transfer order (agechi-rei) directed that holdings near Edo and Osaka be transferred to the bakufu in exchange for lands elsewhere, to strengthen finances and defense around the two main cities in view of the foreign threat. Daimyō and bakufu retainers did not wish to give up productive land, and villagers feared a heavier tax burden. The order was withdrawn in the autumn of 1843, and Mizuno was dismissed.

Domains pursued their own reforms, driven by their own debts. In Satsuma, debts had reached approximately 5 million ryō, and the domain, under the official Zusho Hirosato from the 1830s, negotiated an interest-free, 250-year repayment schedule with its creditors, expanded the sugar monopoly in the Amami Islands, and extended trade through Ryukyu. The burden of the sugar regime fell heavily on Amami islanders, who were required to deliver sugar at fixed terms. Zusho died in 1848 after the Ryukyu trade drew bakufu scrutiny. In Chōshū, following a major rising in 1831, Murata Seifū from 1838 cut expenditure and used the domain’s role in the Shimonoseki port trade to raise revenue. His 1843 thirty-seven-year repayment scheme dealt specifically with the public and private debts of domain retainers, with the domain assuming obligations to creditors; it provoked opposition and was replaced in 1844. In Saga, Nabeshima Naomasa from 1830 reduced the bureaucracy and retinue, enforced austerity, promoted porcelain, lacquer, and tea for sale, and in 1842 declared a ten-year suspension of certain agricultural debts and tenant-rent obligations. A measure of 1845 sought to separate farming from commercial occupations, while the more direct redistribution of land (the kinden policy) belongs to the 1850s and later, beyond this chapter’s main chronology.

These cases differed because the domains’ problems and resources differed: Satsuma drew on Ryukyu and Amami, Chōshū on port commerce and debt management, and Saga on retrenchment and agriculture. Some reforms strengthened domain finances and administration, but often at the expense of subordinate groups, and many other domains reformed with little success. It would be anachronistic to treat these experiments as preparation for the Meiji Restoration, which depended on political events yet to unfold.

Section 50.15

Bakuto: Gambling, Mobility, and Informal Networks

Gambling was prohibited in Tokugawa law yet common: dice and card games were played at festivals, in inns, in temple precincts, and in private houses. Laws punished those who hosted games more severely than those who played, with penalties ranging from fines and confinement to banishment, and in grave cases death. Enforcement was uneven. Bakuto (also bakuchi-uchi) were professionals who made their living from organizing and playing games, as distinct from ordinary people who gambled occasionally.

A bakuto leader ran games in a house or temporary venue, supplying the place, the dice or cards, and trusted attendants, and took a fee from the stakes (terasen). He lent money to players at interest and relied on followers (kobun) to collect debts. The relationship between leader (oyabun) and followers was modeled on a family, with obligations of protection and loyalty. Followers included younger sons without land, displaced villagers, former servants, and unemployed men, and the leader provided lodging and food. Debt was central. A loser became obliged to the host, and indebtedness could draw villagers into labor, theft, or violence.

Bakuto appear in many regions but are best documented in the Kantō. Several conditions favored them there. Sericulture and trade put cash into the countryside of Kōzuke, Musashi, and Kai; highways and post-towns brought travelers; and jurisdiction was divided among bakufu lands, domains, and the estates of bakufu retainers, so that a man wanted in one place could cross a boundary into another. After the famines of the 1780s and 1830s the number of people who had left villages or lost their registration (mushuku) grew. Leaders sometimes allied with local headmen or wealthy farmers whose protection or tolerance mattered; other headmen and villagers petitioned against them.

Gambling also touched households. Losses could force families to sell land or send members into service to pay debts, and village rules of the period commonly forbade young men from gambling, which suggests that communities regarded it as a threat to order and to the household economy. Temple and shrine precincts, where crowds gathered, were common venues, and the tolerance of local institutions or officials was often decisive for whether a game continued.

Authorities did respond. The Kantō patrol established in 1805, described in Section 50.17, and the village leagues organized after 1827 were aimed in part at gamblers and vagrants. Arrest and sentencing records and village petitions complaining of gambling and violence show that bakuto were a documented problem and not merely a legend. Rival leaders could also clash: Iioka Sukegorō and Sasagawa Shigezō, both real figures in Shimōsa, led rival groups whose followers fought a major clash in August 1844; Sasagawa was killed in 1847. The episode was later dramatized in the Tenpō suikoden tales, which mix these events with invention. Ōmaeda Eigorō of Kōzuke (1793–1874) is another documented example of a bakuto leader whose career spanned decades.

Kunisada Chūji (1810–1851 by Gregorian reckoning), born into a farming family in Kōzuke, became a gambler with a following, was involved in violence, and fled the authorities. In 1842 he broke through the Ōto checkpoint, an offense documented in official and local records, and remained a fugitive for years. After his arrest he was executed by crucifixion at Ōto on the 21st day of the twelfth month of Kaei 3, which corresponds to 22 January 1851 in the Gregorian calendar though it is usually given as 1850. The outline of his life as a wanted gambler is supported by official records, but stories that he protected the poor or shared money during the Tenpō famine come largely from storytelling and drama after his death.

Meiji and later storytellers in kōdan and rōkyoku (naniwabushi), novelists, and filmmakers built the figure of the chivalrous outlaw, a gambler who upheld a code of honor and defended the weak. Historians treat these works as evidence for later attitudes and entertainment tastes rather than for Tokugawa realities. For the Tokugawa period itself the evidence is official records, village petitions, and judicial compilations, all of which reflect the viewpoint of those who policed or suffered from gamblers. What bakuto thought of themselves is much harder to recover.

Historical Vocabulary
Bakuto and Tekiya

Bakuto were gamblers and gambling organizers. Tekiya (yashi in Tokugawa usage) were itinerant sellers at fairs and festival markets. The two categories overlapped in some places but were different occupations, and later writers often merged them. Neither term meant “yakuza” in the modern sense, which developed later and is the subject of Chapter 87.

Section 50.16

Tekiya: Itinerant Peddlers, Festival Markets, and Territorial Authority

Tokugawa towns and villages held periodic fairs and festivals tied to temples and shrines. On festival days (ennichi) and at seasonal markets such as Edo’s year-end markets, vendors sold food, toys, tools, plants, ornaments, and medicines, and entertainers drew crowds. Precincts like Asakusa in Edo and riverside entertainment areas such as Ryōgoku were especially busy. The seller who followed fairs from place to place was often called a yashi, the older term; tekiya became common later, especially after the Meiji period, and is used here for convenience.

Itinerant vending required little capital, and many sellers were ordinary commoners from poor households or from villages with surplus labor, including women. Medicine sellers often combined sales with performances such as demonstrations and storytelling, and authorities watched them because remedies could be harmful or fraudulent. Peddlers were an important part of the commercial economy, carrying goods to places without shops, and they appear in many records simply as sellers.

Space was scarce on festival days. Temple and shrine precincts belonged to institutions that regulated their use, charging rents or requiring permission, and vendors’ groups developed leaders who allocated positions, collected fees, settled disputes, and excluded unauthorized sellers. The term nawabari, originally the marking out of a plot with rope, came to describe a leader’s claimed territory. These arrangements could amount to orderly market management. But a leader’s power to exclude could also be used coercively, through extortion of fees, violence against rivals, or toleration of cheating.

Relations with authorities varied. Town officials regulated markets and vendors, shrines and temples had their own arrangements with local groups, and some vendors’ leaders cooperated with officials or supplied information. Authorities could also restrict or ban sellers, as the Tenpō reforms did with certain trades and stalls. Documentation is limited, and it is difficult to tell how far leaders were formally recognized in particular places; practice differed between cities and between regions.

Festivals were also the high point of the commercial year for many small sellers. Food stalls, sellers of seasonal goods, and street performers appear in guidebooks and woodblock prints, which show the sights but not the arrangements behind them, and temple and shrine records of fees and permissions are among the few sources that document how space was granted. Women and children appear among sellers in images and records, though they are less visible in accounts of leaders and disputes.

Several distinctions matter. A fee collected under a shrine’s authority is different from extortion, and a group allocating stalls is different from a criminal association. Tokugawa vendors should not be read through the organizations of the twentieth century. Later organizations sometimes claimed ancient lineages, but such claims are generally unsupported. The leader–follower model was not unique to vendors; it resembled arrangements among artisans, performers, and other occupational groups, which is one reason the model could later be adopted by criminal organizations.

Many fairground occupations, including street performance, begging, and some vending, drew people classified by law as hinin or otherwise outside the main status system. In Edo and Osaka, hereditary headmen administered outcaste groups. Danzaemon headed the Edo-area eta hierarchy, while Kuruma Zenshichi, a leading hinin headman in Asakusa, operated within Danzaemon’s wider authority. The two organizations could nonetheless dispute occupational rights and jurisdiction over activities such as performing and begging, and those disputes appear in the records. Historians must avoid equating status with criminality. The marginalization of these communities was produced by status law and official assignment of tasks (see Chapter 43), and their members were not a criminal population; duties such as guarding prisons were imposed through discriminatory institutions and were not a chosen criminal career. Because fairs depended on crowds, disputes over stalls, hygiene, fire risk, and public order regularly drew in town and temple officials, and that official attention is why vendors appear in the record at all. Evidence for ordinary vendors and marginalized communities is thin, which is another reason students should treat the image of the fairground boss with caution.

Section 50.17

Crime, Policing, Marginalization, and the Origins of Underworld Traditions

There was no unified police force in Tokugawa Japan. Edo had two town magistrates (machi-bugyō), North and South, who served in alternate months, each with about 25 senior assistants (yoriki) and 100–120 constables (dōshin), roughly 250–290 officers across the two offices. Their principal jurisdiction was the town population, not all of Edo’s roughly one million inhabitants, and only some of these officers conducted criminal investigations. Ward officials and neighborhood groups bore much of the burden, and five-household groups (gonin-gumi) made neighbors collectively accountable. Magistrates also employed informants (meakashi, later called okappiki), often men with contacts in the underworld, whose position blurred the line between law enforcement and the people being policed.

In the countryside, villages were led by headmen, and bakufu lands were administered by intendants (daikan) with small staffs. Jurisdiction was divided among bakufu lands, domains, and the estates of bakufu retainers, and agents could not freely pursue suspects across borders; cooperation required requests and negotiation. Highway checkpoints (sekisho) controlled travel, but those who knew local routes could evade them. In 1805 the bakufu created the Kantō torishimari shutsuyaku, a patrol under its finance magistrates, to operate across jurisdictions in the eight Kantō provinces. It was a response to concern about vagrants, gamblers, and rural disorder and was assisted by locally recruited guides.

In 1827 the Bunsei reform organized villages into protective leagues (yoseba kumiai-mura), cooperative rural administrative structures and not independent police departments, which shared responsibility and costs for maintaining order and reporting offenders. Results were mixed. Villages bore the cost, patrols lacked resources, and reliance on local intermediaries meant that informal bargains were common. Some officials tolerated gamblers or used them as informants, which could mean cooperation, corruption, or both.

Punishments included fines, flogging, banishment, tattooing in some regions, and execution, and the state assigned tasks in the penal system to people of outcaste status. Hinin and eta organizations were required to guard prisons, assist at executions, handle corpses, and apprehend or manage vagrants and beggars. Vagrants without registration could be absorbed into hinin groups. This history is part of the story of marginalization told in Chapter 43. It also shows how status law could create categories of people who were both policed and made to police.

Criminal procedure was regulated. Investigators were expected to review evidence, and sentences followed written precedent and the bakufu’s legal codes, such as the Kujikata osadamegaki, with higher officials reviewing serious cases. Formal torture was legally regulated, limited to particular serious cases in which evidence existed but the suspect would not confess, and required authorization; it was comparatively rare. Other coercion in interrogation also occurred, and confessions recorded in trial files must be read critically. Kyoto and Osaka had their own magistrates, and each domain maintained its own officers and rules, so practice differed widely. A gambler’s experience in Edo, a Kantō village, and a domain town could therefore differ greatly.

The evidence has strong limits. Judicial compilations of precedents, magistrates’ trial records, village petitions, and domain records show offenses, punishments, and categories, but they were written to justify authority, and confessions could be obtained under coercion. They tell little about the internal life of groups. Many records have been lost. Later memoirs by former gamblers and their descendants and histories written by organizations narrate lineages and rituals such as cup exchanges that cannot be verified, and they often project later practices backward.

Tokugawa informal organizations contributed models and vocabulary to later criminal groups: the leader–follower relationship, claims to territory, gambling revenue, and control of markets, along with a stock of stories. But the modern yakuza emerged in different conditions. The abolition of the status system, the creation of new police forces in the 1870s, new laws, industrial labor recruitment in ports, mines, and construction, and political and economic change after Meiji shaped them, and the postwar years reshaped them again. Chapter 58 covers the transformation of martial traditions and identities in Meiji, and Chapter 87 treats organized crime after 1945. Tokugawa bakuto and tekiya are therefore antecedents, not an unbroken ancestry.

Section 50.18

The Tokugawa Order on the Eve of Crisis

By the early 1850s the Tokugawa regime governed a society that had become more literate, more commercial, and more argumentative than the one its founders had organized. Schools and printing reached many commoners. Scholars debated the nature of good government and the character of the ancient past, translators and surveyors had learned to measure and describe the world in new ways, and local societies from Edo’s fairs to Kantō villages managed their affairs partly outside official control.

The regime had real capacity to adapt. It patronized useful knowledge, relaxed some book restrictions, supported surveying, repeatedly reorganized finances, and tolerated much that fell short of open criticism. Domains such as Satsuma, Chōshū, and Saga restructured their finances and administration, although often at heavy cost to subordinate groups. Yet the three great reforms of the era each ended in partial failure, because they addressed prices, discipline, and debt without changing a tax system based on rice in a commercial economy. Famines exposed those weaknesses, and Ōshio’s rising showed that criticism could come from within the official class.

The bakufu also showed that it could punish learning that touched on foreign policy. That is the point at which the chapter’s themes converge. A government that depended on knowledge of the outside world, and on the loyalty of educated subordinates, had also begun to treat discussion of that world as dangerous. Neither the regime’s adaptability nor its strains should be read as inevitable steps toward collapse, which depended on events that Chapter 51 begins to narrate: the arrival of foreign ships determined to force a change in Japan’s external relations.

Historical Debate

Did Scholarship and Reform Strengthen or Undermine Tokugawa Authority?

The question: Did the expansion of learning and repeated reform preserve the regime or erode it?

Interpretation A — Strengthened: Education supplied capable officials, foreign learning improved medicine and mapping, and reforms adapted policy, extending the regime’s life.
Interpretation B — Undermined: New ideas and knowledge of the outside world generated criticism, fiscal strain went unresolved, and reform failures weakened confidence.

Evidence: Both rely on school records, reform edicts, and cases like Bansha no goku and Ōshio’s uprising. Why it matters: It shapes whether 1853 is seen as the sudden collapse of a stagnant order or the test of a strained but adaptive one. Many scholars hold that both processes occurred.

Chapter Summary

Tokugawa Japan was a society of expanding learning. Practical literacy and numeracy spread unevenly but widely through terakoya, domain schools, and private academies, and commercial woodblock printing, lending libraries, and booksellers’ associations carried texts across regions under rules that protected publishers’ property while making them answerable for content. Estimates of schooling are rough, and regional and gender differences were large.

Competing intellectual traditions offered rival languages for thinking about government and the past. Zhu Xi orthodoxy, Wang Yangming learning, ancient learning, and Ogyū Sorai’s institutional realism disagreed about whether order rested on moral cultivation or on sound institutions. Kokugaku began as philology, in Keichū, Mabuchi, and Norinaga, and took a more religious turn with Hirata Atsutane; it was never a single nationalism.

Foreign knowledge entered mainly through Nagasaki, mediated by hereditary interpreters, Dutch physicians, and Chinese books, and spread through scholars’ networks in Edo and Osaka. The Kaitai shinsho of 1774 is the best-known product, but anatomy, medicine, natural history, astronomy, and mapping all advanced. Chinese-derived frameworks remained dominant, and European learning was added as a competing authority, not a replacement.

The regime governed through repeated reform. Yoshimune’s Kyōhō program, Sadanobu’s Kansei measures, and Mizuno’s Tenpō reforms each addressed fiscal strain and social disorder without changing the rice-based tax system, and famines, debt, and Ōshio’s rising tested their limits. Domains such as Satsuma, Chōshū, and Saga pursued their own reforms, often at heavy cost to subordinate groups such as Amami islanders.

The state also policed learning that touched foreign policy, as in the Bansha no goku of 1839 and the Siebold affair of 1828–29, while employing the same kinds of expertise in its own astronomical bureau and coastal defense.

Alongside official society, bakuto and tekiya operated in gambling houses, markets, and fairs. Documentary evidence comes largely from magistrates’ and village records, and later legend, as in the stories of Kunisada Chūji and the Tenpō suikoden, exaggerated their coherence. Policing was thin and divided among jurisdictions, and outcaste communities were assigned penal duties by law without being criminal populations. These groups were antecedents of later organized crime, not its unbroken ancestors.

Taken together, the evidence shows a regime that could adapt and a society that was changing faster than its institutions. The chapter ends before the diplomatic crisis of Chapter 51, which would make those strains consequential.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter asks students to hold two pictures of Tokugawa Japan together: a society that read, translated, measured, and experimented, and a regime that struggled to pay its way and to govern the margins. Neither picture is complete alone, and the chapter’s sources, from school regulations to magistrates’ files, were each created by someone with a particular purpose.

Several threads continue into Part VIII. Habits of schooling and reading, networks of physicians and scholars, and knowledge of the outside world shaped how people interpreted the crisis of the 1850s, and the structure of domain finances and administrative experiments shaped what domains could do. The informal economies of gambling and fairs, and the status law that assigned penal duties to outcaste communities, did not disappear with the regime, although they were reorganized.

Students should ask, as they move to Chapter 51, which of these developments the people of the 1850s would have recognized as relevant, and which are visible only in hindsight. The Tokugawa order was neither doomed nor secure, and understanding why requires attention to the people who wrote, learned, taught, governed, gambled, and sold in it.
