Hunger, Homelessness, Rationing, and the Yami-ichi

Japanese History: From Jōmon Japan to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

A ration card could be valid while its promised rice was late. How did people survive when legality and the next meal no longer reliably coincided?

At a distribution counter in Tokyo in 1946, possession of a ration card did not guarantee that rice would arrive on the expected day. Elsewhere in the city, people exchanged clothing for food, repaired roofs with scavenged materials, and searched station markets for goods unavailable in ordinary shops. Such activities were not merely the background to political occupation. They were the central work of keeping households alive. The war had officially ended, but the practical boundary between emergency and peace was still being negotiated at tables, kitchens, railway platforms, and government counters.

In the first postwar years, the material consequences of defeat reached far beyond the damage visible in photographs. A household might have an intact room but lack fuel, income, or a reliable food delivery. Another might own a ration entitlement yet have no registered address at which to receive it. Farms could produce grain that did not reach urban consumers, while city families carried their remaining possessions into the countryside to barter for provisions. To understand the emergency, students must distinguish production from distribution, legal prices from actual costs, and the existence of food from a particular person’s access to it.

The black markets known as yami-ichi became some of the most recognizable spaces of the postwar city. They sold food, clothing, repairs, and secondhand goods around stations and on war-damaged land. Their role was contradictory. They enabled trade and employment where ordinary channels were inadequate, yet they also rewarded some traders, disadvantaged poor buyers, and sometimes involved coercion or diversion from regulated supplies. The presence of an illegal transaction did not tell observers whether its purpose was household survival, commercial profit, or exploitation.

The differences among people were immense. A farmer with retained food, a widow with two children, a returning soldier seeking work, a Korean resident with uncertain legal status, a disabled worker, and an unaccompanied child did not possess equal resources. The conditions of housing, health, family networks, and transport affected the strategies available to each. Women performed much of the work of household provisioning, but men, children, and elders also worked, bartered, or improvised livelihoods. The chapter avoids depicting everyone as either heroic survivors or victims without choices.

The food emergency had a political history. The Japanese state maintained rationing and procurement rules partly because abandoning controls could allow the wealthy to absorb scarce goods. Yet the state could not consistently supply its own legal entitlements. Occupation authorities held supreme power but relied on Japanese ministries and local governments for distribution. The Food May Day demonstration of May 1946 expressed public anger, while Judge Yamaguchi’s much-discussed death in 1947 dramatized the difficulty of obeying food controls when they could not always secure life. Legal obligation and material necessity were not reliably aligned.

Historical evidence includes GHQ monthly summations, Australian diplomatic reports, U.S. policy papers, Japanese newspaper translations, nutrition surveys, Shōwakan photographs, welfare and municipal records, and later scholarly studies. Such records require careful interpretation. A food-import forecast is not a delivered shipment; a calorie average is not every person’s diet; a photograph of stalls is not evidence about the price or origins of every good sold; and a welfare count does not include everyone in need. Combining different sources makes the unequal experience of scarcity more visible.

This chapter builds on Chapter 84’s discussion of constitutional and social reform but asks how rights and public responsibility operated amid hunger and homelessness. It does not repeat the campaigns that destroyed Japanese cities, the empire-wide story of repatriation, or the institutional structure of GHQ. It also keeps the detailed histories of occupation-era sexual politics, organized crime, economic restructuring, and the Korean War in their assigned later chapters. Its subject is the organization of ordinary survival from 1945 through the late 1940s.

By around 1950, greater imports, recovered transport, welfare legislation, housing construction, and altered commercial arrangements had reduced some of the worst emergencies. But improvement occurred unevenly, and the city that emerged was not simply the city that had existed before the war. Temporary markets affected permanent shopping areas; household barter and private assistance left memories of inequality; the state acquired new welfare obligations; and people debated what compliance with law meant when law failed to secure basic provisions. This chapter follows those changes without treating eventual economic growth as predetermined.

Historical Context

The social emergency had roots in the wartime mobilization system. The state used controls over rice and other staples to direct scarce food toward the armed forces, industry, and civilian populations. Production suffered from labor shortages and limited fertilizer, while maritime shipping losses disrupted imports and distribution. Those conditions did not disappear when fighting stopped; the dismantling of the Japanese empire made prewar and wartime supply relationships even harder to reconstruct.

Urban areas faced a separate material crisis. Incendiary bombing and wartime demolition had destroyed residential and commercial districts, while evacuees and demobilized people needed places to return. JACAR’s reconstruction exhibition records roughly 9.7 million persons affected and around 2.3 million households or dwellings affected under contemporary damage classifications, including wartime building clearance. These are historical damage aggregates, not an exact count of people still homeless at a later date.

Chapter 81 explained the overseas collapse and return of soldiers and civilians. Chapter 82 explained why SCAP exercised authority through Japanese institutions. Chapter 84 explained the formal shift toward democratic government, labor rights, land reform, and social rights. Those developments created a new political setting but did not solve immediate shortages. Administrative continuity and foreign oversight had to coexist with public expectations of a better material future.

Agricultural and urban histories must be read together. Farms required urban goods and official payments that often failed to preserve value, while cities depended on regular shipments from producing areas. Food control was intended to reduce inequitable access, but the difference between planned and actual delivery encouraged private exchange. The rural–urban divide ran through the crisis without creating uniformly prosperous farmers or uniformly starving city residents.

The main chronological center of this chapter is 1945–1948, with selected developments to 1950. The focus is the crisis of subsistence, housing, and informal work, not the broader institutional account of the Reverse Course or the later Korean War economy. Surviving official reports provide dated estimates, while photographs, municipal histories, and family testimony reveal how emergency conditions varied among communities.

Section 85.1 · Division I — Defeat, Ruin, and the Food Emergency

After Surrender: Ruined Cities, Lost Homes, and Broken Urban Services

Japan's surrender ended organized warfare against the Allied powers but did not restore the ordinary conditions of urban existence. Across Tokyo, Yokohama, Osaka, Kobe, Nagoya, and many regional centers, people confronted a landscape in which familiar streets had disappeared, workplaces were unusable, water and power were unreliable, and the surviving buildings were already crowded. There was no single postwar scene: an intact neighborhood could stand beside an incinerated district; a relatively undamaged rural town could receive families whose former city addresses no longer identified a habitable house. The destruction described in Chapter 80 had therefore acquired a second history, measured not in bomb tonnage or casualties but in the ability to sleep indoors, obtain food, reach a station, and keep a household together. The return of peace did not automatically produce an address at which to receive it.

Physical devastation had several origins. Incendiary air raids had destroyed residential and commercial districts, but the wartime government had also demolished buildings to create firebreaks, and military requisitioning had redirected construction materials. At surrender, residents of some apparently empty lots had not fled a bomb; their dwellings had been removed as part of a defensive policy. The distinction matters for reconstruction statistics and for claims to compensation or replacement space. Official estimates of buildings destroyed or households displaced often group together unlike losses, and later housing-shortage figures measure demand rather than the number of houses directly damaged. The historian must therefore specify whether a number counts structures, dwelling units, households, or people affected. A family's problem, meanwhile, was not only that a roof was missing. Water pipes, drains, latrines, shop premises, tramlines, fire services, and routes to schools and clinics were interdependent.

The loss of residence could start a chain of administrative difficulties. Food distribution required registration and some degree of local organizational continuity; missing papers, displacement, or an improvised living arrangement might complicate access. Evacuated children and relatives had to be located or recalled, often through a damaged railway system. The person who had worked near a station could no longer necessarily reach that station from a new shelter, while a factory whose exterior survived might lack electricity, fuel, orders, or machinery. The absence of goods in legitimate shops reflected these material disruptions as well as regulation and inflation. Destruction changed the geography of employment and family support, making a familiar city less navigable precisely when reliance on informal help increased. It was easier for some households to move into an existing relative's dwelling than for others to find any stable refuge.

Ruined districts did not remain motionless. People gathered scrap wood and sheet metal, repaired rooms inside damaged structures, put up shacks and barracks, or opened counters along walking routes. Owners returned to plots, tenants asserted prior occupancy, and newcomers sought shelter in available space. Local officials faced competing demands: clear dangerous ruins, recover streets, allocate temporary housing, regulate unauthorized building, and avoid evicting residents who had nowhere else to go. Municipal decisions were often provisional because construction resources were inadequate. The emergency also altered what counted as a desirable site. Land near a station was attractive to vendors and workers but contested by road planners and landlords; a plot suitable for a simple stall might be the only workable shelter for a family. A broad map of reconstruction conceals these successive claims to the same limited ground.

The social divisions of the wartime city survived into its ruins. A household with savings, relatives in the countryside, a functioning employer, or ties to an established neighborhood association had options that a newly widowed parent or displaced family might lack. Tenants and small shopkeepers often had weaker claims to reconstruction resources than owners. Repatriates arriving from former imperial territories added to the pressure for rooms and jobs, although their journeys and the fall of the overseas empire belong chiefly to Chapter 81. Veterans and evacuated workers were not interchangeable populations: their benefits, occupations, papers, and networks differed. Nor were all those in need formally registered as homeless. Families squeezed into a single room could escape homelessness counts while still suffering dangerous overcrowding. This is why a history of shelter must examine occupancy and access, not only completed housing units.

Urban destruction was also visible through evidence produced for different purposes. Occupation aerial photographs and municipal damage maps recorded built form; welfare offices recorded applicants; newspapers narrated shortages and public disorder; oral histories remembered the work of finding a home. None offers a complete demographic account. Photographs of empty fields of rubble may make cities appear depopulated, although people were walking through them, returning, and building. Administrative records often exclude unregistered residents. In the coming years roads, rails, market buildings, and permanent houses would reappear, yet that outcome cannot be projected backward onto 1945 as a guaranteed recovery. The immediate historical question was how surviving infrastructure and social relationships could support life when the ordinary built environment no longer did.

Section 85.2

The 1945 Harvest and the Geography of Food Shortage

The postwar food emergency had been prepared by years of agricultural strain. War mobilization deprived villages of able-bodied labor, drew animals and equipment into military uses, and diverted fertilizer, machinery, and transport toward urgent state priorities. Crop production depended upon local irrigation, soil, seasonal labor, and supplies of nitrogenous fertilizer that could not be replaced simply by ordering farmers to work harder. The difficult growing conditions and poor harvest of 1945 magnified these inherited weaknesses. Fisheries faced depleted vessels and gear, fuel shortages, restricted movement, and disrupted distribution. Rice was culturally central and administratively significant, but calories also came from barley, wheat, sweet potatoes, vegetables, fish, and a shifting array of substitutes. A household's ability to obtain any of these depended on where it lived and what it could exchange.

The geographical distinction between producing and consuming areas was decisive. Cities concentrated industrial and service workers who ordinarily purchased food through networks linking farms, ports, warehouses, wholesalers, railways, and neighborhood shops. Farms produced food but also required fertilizer, fuel, clothing, tools, and other urban goods. Wartime transport damage and postwar fuel shortages weakened this reciprocal arrangement. A railway delay could leave edible grain in a producing district while an urban ration counter remained empty. Rice collected into an official store was not yet rice consumed by a child in Yokohama. Likewise, grain remaining on a farm was not necessarily surplus available for immediate transfer. It might be seed, household subsistence, animal feed, or a reserve against the uncertainties of another harvest.

The breakdown of empire mattered because Japan could no longer rely on the same external food and raw-material flows. Prewar and wartime economic systems had linked the home islands to Korea, Taiwan, Manchuria, Southeast Asia, and other territories through unequal patterns of production and extraction. After defeat, the Japanese state lost control over these networks and lacked independent shipping and foreign exchange. An account claiming that Japan simply lost an empire-sized granary would nonetheless overstate the regularity of wartime deliveries, which had already been disrupted by submarine warfare and shipping losses. The chronology is therefore one of cumulative scarcity: wartime damage constrained supply before August 1945, while defeat eliminated imperial command over resources and complicated the international arrangements required to replace them. Food deficits emerged from a changed political economy as well as a poor crop.

Regional and occupational experiences varied sharply. Some families had access to home gardens, farm relatives, coastal fishing, or small plots; others lived in dense neighborhoods where food had to be carried in at considerable cost. A rural household might produce vegetables yet lack rice, fertilizer, or cash. Hokkaido and northeastern districts faced different crops, climates, shipping dependencies, and seasonal constraints from western Honshu and Kyushu. Farmers with sufficient land and stores could negotiate from strength in barter transactions; land-poor cultivators and agricultural wage workers were often more exposed. Workers paid in devaluing currency could be disadvantaged against those holding tangible goods. Thus a national aggregate could describe a serious shortage without indicating who went hungry first or who could avoid shortages through access to land.

Official estimates deserve close scrutiny. Harvest forecasts were assembled from administrative reports produced under a system of procurement quotas, so officials, cultivators, and intermediaries had different incentives to report or contest quantities. Estimates of available food could assume an unrealistically smooth delivery from producer to consumer. The same apparent deficit looked different if calculated as rice alone, staple-grain calories, all food energy, or commercially marketed output. Furthermore, claims made in 1946 about the coming months were forecasts made amid anxiety, not final counts. Contemporary Australian diplomatic communications observed both the danger of urban famine and the significant role of hoarding, black-market distribution, and earlier consumption patterns. That document is important evidence of how policymakers interpreted scarcity, but its figures should not be treated as a universal household survey.

The food emergency therefore raises a geographical problem rather than only a question of crop size. Where was grain grown, what routes carried it, who had the right or power to keep it, and what official or informal mechanisms determined its final destination? One of the chief sources for answering these questions is the GHQ/SCAP monthly series of non-military activity summaries, whose food tables distinguished available stocks, imports, and distribution. Their usefulness comes partly from recording the administration's attempts to measure a moving crisis. Their limits are equally instructive: a tonnage table cannot establish whether food reached people on time or whether a household's total diet included supplies purchased outside the official system. The most illuminating history joins the food balance sheet to the journey of food from field to table.

Section 85.3

Rationing on Paper and at the Distribution Counter

Food rationing did not begin with surrender. The wartime state had already created a controlled distribution structure for staple grains and other necessities, an arrangement intended to stabilize supplies, prices, and public order. Under occupation the structure survived because replacing it immediately would have risked a still greater crisis. Households remained connected to registration systems, distribution points, and local officials charged with delivering allotted quantities. The ration could be expressed in standard measures and converted into calories for administrative planning, but the theoretical allotment was not identical to food physically received. Ration cards were therefore both instruments of entitlement and reminders of the state's limited capacity to honor its promise. A family's registered right to rice could coexist with a nearly empty cupboard.

The system required several transfers. Farmers were to deliver controlled amounts to collection agencies, which moved stocks through prefectural and central channels, warehouses, transport services, and retailers or ration counters. Each stage depended on fuel, functioning vehicles, paperwork, honest reporting, and the cooperation of people with alternative uses for the grain. Shortfalls did not always arrive as a formal announcement of a permanent reduction. They often appeared as a delay, a substitute grain, a smaller delivery, or conflicting information about the next allocation. Citizens could therefore spend long periods waiting or repeatedly visiting a counter without obtaining enough food. Government policy was most visible to them not in an office memorandum but in the difference between a due date and the actual arrival of a bag of grain.

Measured delays made the gap unmistakable. Later analyses of contemporary Tokyo records identify a rise in average rice-ration delivery delays from about 3.7 days in April 1946 to about 18.9 days in June. Those numbers represent average days late for a particular location and commodity, not the number of days people went entirely without food, and they are not interchangeable with a national calorie estimate. Households might possess substitute staples, receive charity, trade possessions, cultivate vegetables, or purchase grain illegally while waiting. Still, a delivery delay nearing three weeks made the ration system unreliable as the sole basis of subsistence. GHQ's May 1946 summation identified the Tokyo–Yokohama area's lag as especially serious, confirming that officials recognized an acute urban administrative crisis rather than merely a shortage in popular imagination.

Historical Vocabulary

Entitlement, Delivery, and Consumption

Ration entitlement is the amount a registered person is officially due under a distribution scheme. Delivery means the quantity and date actually supplied. Consumption includes officially distributed goods plus food obtained by gardening, direct exchange, private buying, and help from others. A 1946 delay of nineteen days is not synonymous with nineteen days without any calories; likewise an entitlement cannot be treated as a measure of diet when deliveries were late.

Prices deepened the problem. The official ration price was regulated and therefore could appear affordable on paper, while food obtainable immediately through private channels often cost far more. A worker's nominal wage was affected by rapid inflation and uncertain employment. The distinction between money income and purchasing power became a daily concern, especially for those without barterable goods. Factory workers, clerks, widows, and disabled veterans might have a legal claim to provisions but little cash to secure alternative supplies. Different households also consumed different amounts: infants, growing adolescents, laborers doing heavy physical work, and sick adults did not have identical nutritional needs. An administrative per-person allowance could not account fully for such differences, and a fixed ration offered little protection against unequal access to supplementation.

Local organization was essential but complicated. Neighborhood associations that had helped carry wartime mobilization were changing under occupation policy, yet local officials and informal leaders continued to know where people lived and how deliveries could be organized. Evacuated residents returning to the city, households that had lost documents, homeless persons, and people moving between jobs could be especially difficult to include. Urban poverty was therefore not simply the absence of food but also the absence of a recognized place within distribution procedures. The contrast with rural households was not absolute: those without land or status in their village could encounter similar obstacles. Even where rules said allotments were equal, differences in registration, delivery, local influence, and the means to complain produced unequal outcomes.

For historical interpretation, three distinct kinds of evidence must be kept separate: official entitlements, actual official deliveries, and total consumption. Ration regulations establish the first; distribution ledgers and monthly reports help establish the second; nutritional surveys and household accounts offer partial views of the third. A family diary describing a barter trip is not a statistical sample, but it can reveal why recorded ration deliveries underestimate the extent of informal exchange. Conversely, an administrative report may reveal the depth of delays beyond what one surviving memoir can show. The ration counter thus stood at the junction of law, infrastructure, social hierarchy, and improvisation. Its weakness did not make official distribution irrelevant. It made the ability to supplement that system one of the decisive determinants of survival.

Section 85.4

Producers, Procurement Quotas, and Rural–Urban Bargaining

The food crisis was experienced differently at the receiving counter and in the producing village. Officials sought to secure grain from agricultural households through compulsory procurement quotas at controlled prices. The premise was that a state confronting mass urban need could not leave the supply of staple grains entirely to voluntary sales. Yet the agricultural economy was itself disrupted. Farmers required seed, family provisions, fertilizer, labor, tools, cloth, footwear, and fuel, and the official prices offered for grain were often poorly aligned with the cost or availability of what farmers needed to buy. Inflation made payment in currency less attractive than retaining grain that could be consumed or exchanged. A policy that appeared fair to hungry city residents could therefore seem coercive or economically irrational to cultivators.

The quota system was rooted in wartime administration, but it became more controversial after surrender. Farmers who had endured years of requisitioning and labor shortages expected the end of fighting to improve their circumstances. Instead, collection officers continued to demand deliveries, sometimes under intense pressure from the occupation and Japanese ministries concerned about disorder in cities. Administrative agencies had to estimate harvests and set obligations, a task made contentious by variation in field quality and household needs. A landowner with extensive acreage, a tenant cultivator, and a small farmer working marginal soil did not face identical opportunities. Assertions that farmers as a group were uniformly prosperous obscure rural indebtedness, dependency, and the shortages of nonfood essentials that encouraged informal exchange.

Barter altered the balance between towns and countryside. City residents arrived with clothing, blankets, utensils, jewelry, machinery parts, or other goods that had become more valuable relative to cash. A farmer might decline an official price but accept a usable coat or fabric, while a household in the city could exchange valuable possessions merely to obtain a few meals. The transaction might be mutually advantageous in immediate terms while reinforcing structural inequality. People with expensive possessions or family connections could secure food more easily than those who had already sold everything. Farmers possessing little surplus were not always in a position to make such exchanges, and some resented visitors who assumed the countryside was uniformly well fed. The result was not a single free market but a shifting series of relationships conditioned by local resources, kinship, policing, and relative desperation.

Reports of hidden stocks sometimes demonstrated successful enforcement, but the meaning of a 'hoarded' quantity depended upon who made the determination and what the farmer intended for it. In an economy with uncertain future deliveries, retaining grain for household subsistence or the next planting season could be prudent even while urban consumers needed it urgently. This does not mean all private withholding was defensible. Some traders exploited scarcity for profit, and some large producers benefited from their privileged position. The historical task is to distinguish these circumstances rather than assigning moral virtue to every rural household or dishonesty to every official collector.

Land reform provides essential background without becoming the principal subject here. During 1946 and the following years, new legislation began transferring land from landlords to cultivators, as explained in Chapter 84. Changes in formal ownership did not instantly reorganize the current harvest, repair railways, or eliminate the conflict between official procurement and private exchange. Tenants newly expecting greater security still needed seed and food; former landlords could retain influence through credit and local networks. Local agricultural committees and delivery agencies operated in a society where old authority and new legal rights overlapped. Farmers could support greater control over their land while resisting the state's continued claim to its output. These simultaneous attitudes underline why democratization and food scarcity must be studied together but not collapsed into one narrative.

Rural–urban conflict became a political problem because neither side could solve the emergency alone. Cities needed farmers' deliveries; farms needed urban manufactured goods and working transportation. Officials could threaten penalties, but coercion could not manufacture fertilizer or make an inflated currency trustworthy. Private barter might feed a household, yet it could deprive the ration system of supplies intended for people unable to trade. The question of fairness consequently ran through every link of the exchange. What proportion of the harvest should farmers keep, at what price should they deliver the rest, and how should a government balance food security against the property and labor of producers? The answers changed as imports and harvests improved. In the meantime, procurement remained an arena in which state authority, household survival, and rural bargaining power continuously met.

Section 85.5

SCAP, Imported Grain, and the Politics of Emergency Food Relief

Japan's government could not supply enough food through domestic collection alone, and foreign imports required the cooperation of the occupying power. Under SCAP, Japanese ministries continued to assemble estimates, request shipping, and organize distribution, but they lacked independent control of international procurement and transport. American officials had to consider Japan's emergency alongside worldwide postwar grain scarcity, military shipping requirements, the needs of other occupied territories, and domestic political pressure not to reward a defeated enemy. These were not merely logistical calculations. Food shortages threatened disease, unrest, strikes, and the credibility of occupation governance. Preventing catastrophe was therefore a humanitarian objective and an instrument of political stabilization.

Official alarm became pronounced during the spring of 1946. The GHQ monthly summaries described severe urban shortages, especially in Tokyo–Yokohama. In a May 6 Australian diplomatic cable, Ball reported estimates suggesting that a large share of Tokyo's food consumption the preceding December had come through the black market, while discussing forecasts of severe shortages for the months ahead. Such statements are revealing precisely because they are situated and provisional: they record how an Allied representative understood the danger and interpreted American responses, not the result of a definitive nationwide audit. Forecasts of the tonnage required or calories likely available should not be reported as quantities actually delivered. They were arguments within the politics of relief.

Imports increased but did not immediately restore a normal diet. Deliveries of wheat, flour, maize, and other staples had to be transported from ports to inland communities and allocated through ration systems that were already unreliable. Imported foods also changed the composition of diets, sometimes substituting for rice in ways that required new cooking practices and distribution arrangements. The United States supplied emergency relief under arrangements later associated with Government Aid and Relief in Occupied Areas, or GARIOA; the Japanese Ministry of Foreign Affairs dates the beginning of food and medical assistance under that program to July 1946. This program should not be confused with voluntary relief shipments organized by private organizations, which followed different funding and distribution channels. Nor should later economic rehabilitation assistance be retroactively described as identical to the emergency food imports of 1946.

Section 85.6 · Division II — Bodies, Households, Shelter, and Childhood

Hunger in the Body: Malnutrition, Illness, and Unequal Risk

Food scarcity became visible in bodies, not merely in government ledgers. Underconsumption reduced strength and concentration, slowed recovery from illness, and made physical work harder to sustain. Shortages of animal protein, fats, and specific vitamins could injure health even when a person obtained enough bulk from grains or tubers to feel temporarily full. Tuberculosis and other infections interacted with weakened nutrition and overcrowded living conditions; a sick worker might lose wages while needing more food and warmth. Children faced risks associated with growth, and mothers with insufficient diets could have difficulty feeding infants. Yet the record does not support a picture in which every person in Japan was equally malnourished. The significant historical question is whose food supply fell below what standard, during which season, and through which combination of economic and personal circumstances.

Nutrition surveys begun under occupation supervision offer a more systematic view than isolated recollections. The first Tokyo survey was undertaken late in 1945, and sampling expanded to other cities and adjacent rural areas in 1946. Investigators examined food consumption and signs of deficiency, often repeating surveys seasonally. The studies were imperfect: households could misreport privately acquired food, measurement methods changed, and selected urban or rural populations were not automatically representative of everyone in Japan. Still, the surveys revealed sharp disparities. A later historical analysis of those records reports an average of about 1,352 kilocalories per person per day among surveyed Tokyo ward residents in May 1946, compared with roughly 2,076 among surveyed farmers in Tokyo; by August the urban figure had risen to about 1,828. These are dated survey estimates for defined populations, not fixed national ration levels or universal experiences.

The figures help explain why nominally similar entitlements could produce unequal welfare. Rural families had direct access to garden produce or agricultural output, while salaried urban families relied much more heavily on official distribution and outside purchases. The same research records the particular hardship of railway employees, whose surveyed family food intake could remain below that of other urban groups despite their work's strategic importance. Family size, household composition, and income mattered: one surviving wage earner might support many relatives, and widows could have fewer safe earning opportunities. A person with a garden, an uncle who farmed, or enough clothing to barter had a degree of protection unavailable to others. Differences among social groups were neither simply biological nor moral; they reflected control of food, cash, networks, transport, and time.

Public-health authorities responded to nutritional deterioration by collecting evidence, allocating medical supplies, and attempting to protect hospitals and vulnerable institutions. Occupation medical records describe shortages of food, medicines, and personnel, but they also document organized surveys and the gradual rebuilding of health centers. Hospitals could not create a normal diet for patients by administrative order. Their work depended upon supplies available for general distribution and sometimes on specifically allocated stocks. Emergency nutrition programs aimed to reduce harm, yet many households still turned to private exchange to acquire food. This was especially consequential for pregnant women, infants, elderly residents, and people with tuberculosis. The difficulty of attributing any individual death to hunger rather than an interacting illness is a reason for medical precision, not for minimizing the effects of deprivation.

Food insecurity could also change social relations inside a household. Adults sometimes reduced their portions to feed children, while adolescents doing paid or informal work required considerable energy. Household decisions were rarely recorded with the precision of a health survey, yet diaries, memoirs, and oral histories describe constant calculations about portions, fuel, and where the next meal could be obtained. The physical work of cooking substitutes mattered: sweet potatoes, flour, or unfamiliar grain required pots, heat, water, and time, all sometimes scarce. A food parcel that looked adequate in calories could be less useful to a family without safe cooking facilities. Health and hunger therefore have to be connected to housing and urban services, not treated as an isolated medical problem.

The contrast between urban and rural diets also cautions against broad claims about collective memory. Later narratives of starving cities are supported by abundant evidence, but the distribution of suffering varied over time. May 1946 was not nutritionally identical to November; Tokyo was not identical to a Hokkaido farming district; a family able to purchase private food was not identical to one dependent on delayed rations. Scientists of the occupation produced averages and graphs because they were attempting to identify these differences and allocate relief. Historians need to read their reports alongside testimonies that show what averages conceal. Hunger was at once a measurable problem of intake and an unequal experience shaped by the organization of postwar society.

Section 85.7

Going to the Countryside: Barter, Journeys, and Household Improvisation

For many urban households, the country became an essential extension of the food supply. People carried clothing, bedding, household utensils, and whatever remained of family valuables to relatives, acquaintances, or unfamiliar farming communities in hopes of exchanging them for grain and vegetables. Japanese recollections of these journeys often describe them as trips to find food rather than as commercial speculation. Their logic was stark: regulated prices were meaningless if the ration was delayed, and money bought little when private sellers preferred physical goods. A coat that had once marked respectability could become a means of obtaining rice. The exchange revealed how rapidly war and inflation had unsettled ordinary understandings of property, savings, and social status. Items acquired over years might be consumed as economic reserves within months.

The journey itself required labor and risk. Railway services were overcrowded or interrupted, passes and inspections could complicate travel, and rural destinations might require walking considerable distances after the train ended. People had to decide how much they could carry, where to sleep, and whether a farmer would accept the goods offered. The exchange sometimes depended on kinship or longstanding urban–rural relationships; households without such connections were disadvantaged. Women frequently performed critical provisioning work while caring for dependents, but surviving records should not reduce all barter to a fixed female role. Men who had lost jobs, youths helping their families, and elderly relatives also participated. Some travelers faced police searches because the movement of controlled grains outside authorized channels was restricted. A journey undertaken to feed a family could involve a prohibited transaction and the threat of confiscation.

Barter did not produce the same outcome for everyone. A family with fine textiles, tools, or furniture might negotiate a quantity of food that a poorer household could not approach. Farmers who had already exchanged away their surplus or faced local shortages could refuse. Exchange rates differed among places and weeks, and the perceived value of objects depended on what rural families lacked. An imported item or a good pair of shoes might be valuable where replacements were impossible. Inflation encouraged direct exchange because cash lost purchasing power quickly. Yet barter could itself intensify inequality, transferring the remaining possessions of impoverished urban residents to households with food stocks. A transaction could be voluntarily agreed upon and still arise from severely unequal circumstances.

The boundary between barter, private sale, and black-market distribution was fluid but analytically important. A householder exchanging a kimono for several measures of rice was not operating the same enterprise as a trader buying many sacks for resale in a station market. Both could violate food controls, but their organization, scale, incentives, and social consequences differed. Family-to-family exchange often depended upon trust and reputation, while commercial intermediaries required capital, storage, transport, and mechanisms of enforcement. Such intermediaries could help move food from producers to consumers but could also profit from the shortage. The question of legality did not track this entire range of behavior. One prohibited act could keep a child fed, while another could exploit many buyers.

Urban provisioning also involved activities less dramatic than the countryside journey. People planted vegetables in available plots, collected edible plants, queued at official counters, took paid or informal employment in exchange for food, and pooled supplies with relatives or neighbors. Small acts of mutual assistance were often crucial, particularly where official welfare was inadequate. Newspapers and later recollections sometimes present these acts as evidence of extraordinary resilience. The word is useful only if it does not make structural hardship disappear. A family's ability to improvise was constrained by health, mobility, property, age, networks, and the duties of care. Not everyone could travel or carry goods, and not every person who stayed in the city was insufficiently resourceful. The frail and those without dependents willing to help could be most exposed to the breakdown of regular distribution.

Household diaries and oral histories are especially revealing here because official statistics generally count quantities delivered or sold rather than hours spent obtaining them. But recollections can also reshape the past. Later narrators may emphasize ingenuity or sacrifice and downplay humiliating exchanges, illegality, or assistance received from strangers. Historians must compare such accounts with ration records, contemporary press reports, police files, and evidence from rural villages. What emerges is a picture of households as active economic organizers. They allocated labor, stored goods, assessed risk, cultivated social relations, and moved resources across space. The postwar economy was rebuilt partly from these small decisions made before stable wages, prices, and supplies had been restored.

Section 85.8

Where to Sleep: Homeless Families, Temporary Housing, and Reconstruction

Food was urgent, but shelter could determine whether other forms of recovery were possible. Air raids and wartime demolition had destroyed extensive residential districts, while returning evacuees, discharged soldiers, and repatriated civilians sought accommodation in places already overcrowded. Temporary settlement took many forms: a family repaired one room in a damaged house, relatives divided their living space, someone occupied a barracks-like structure erected from salvaged materials, or several households crowded into a property designed for far fewer residents. A family might possess land but lack roof tiles, timber, glass, or permission to build. Another might have construction skills but no legal claim to a plot. The shortage therefore involved both the number of dwellings and the rights, materials, and relationships necessary to occupy them.

Conditions varied among cities and neighborhoods. Houses in some districts survived where nearby factories and streets had been destroyed; other areas were nearly empty of usable structures. Temporary living near an employment center could be attractive even in severe discomfort, while a larger space on a distant edge of the city might be impractical if trains were unreliable. The return of office workers, merchants, students, and families placed additional strain on surviving housing. Public buildings, institutions, and unused rooms were sometimes requisitioned or adapted, creating disputes about who should have priority. Occupation forces also required facilities for their own use, though the varied character of requisition must be documented locally rather than assumed for every neighborhood. Reconstruction was thus a negotiation among private claims, public plans, and military needs.

The supply of housing was constrained by controls governing materials, capital, rents, and land. Wartime scarcity and postwar policy limited the availability of timber, nails, cement, and other necessities. Even when officials announced a reconstruction program, builders needed labor and finance. Some households built without normal commercial arrangements, relying on family labor or material exchange. Housing historian Hiroshi Ono emphasizes that late-1940s reconstruction cannot be understood simply as a peacetime market shortage. Residential space was created and divided through regulated access to materials and land, and social connections shaped who could occupy rooms. The role of kinship and informal rental arrangements was historically significant because many people survived in housing that was neither a new public apartment nor an ordinary market transaction.

Housing allocation could expose unequal power. Owners retained valuable claims to land even after buildings had burned; tenants might have to negotiate a return or accept an improvised subdivision. Employers could influence access to company quarters; public officials decided eligibility for emergency assistance; families with connections could find accommodation ahead of strangers. Women heading households, war widows, those with disabilities, and people without stable employment often encountered special obstacles. Returning repatriates might have had no surviving network in the home islands, but their experiences differed according to prior residence, occupation, and family ties. Former colonial subjects and minority residents faced another set of challenges involving documentation and social discrimination. An undifferentiated category of 'the homeless' obscures these overlapping inequalities.

The physical conditions of temporary housing shaped health. Overcrowding complicated sanitation, cooking, ventilation, and disease prevention. A family waiting for a ration delivery might have nowhere secure to store grain; a household with food but no working stove faced different difficulties. Children needed safe places to sleep and study, while adults required rest to return to paid work. The absence of an address could hinder registration with welfare and distribution offices. Housing and food were therefore complementary necessities, not separate administrative sectors. Public-health officials needed to understand why people lived in dangerous conditions even when warned about disease; the answer was often that no practical alternative existed. Rebuilding sanitation and reliable utilities could matter as much to survival as new walls and roofs.

Photographs of wooden shacks or crowded rooms often illustrate the era, but they should not define the entire experience. Some residents lived in repaired prewar houses, others in makeshift districts whose appearance later changed rapidly, and still others in rooms made available by social networks. Maps of permanent construction can hide those temporary arrangements. Municipal records may document permits and relief housing while missing self-built structures; oral histories reveal improvisation but are selective in their memories. The crucial analytic point is that urban reconstruction began as the creation and allocation of usable domestic space long before the completion of official plans. This process helped determine who could work, attend school, receive food, and participate in the civic reforms explained in Chapter 84. A new political order was being installed in a society whose members often lacked a secure place to live.

Section 85.9

Street Children, War Orphans, and the Limits of Protection

Children without secure adult protection were among the most exposed people in postwar Japan. Bombing, evacuation, military deaths, poverty, family separation, and institutional disruption left some children without a parent or guardian, while others had living relatives unable to house or feed them. Railway stations and underground passages became places where children could sleep, encounter travelers, find odd jobs, and search for food. Ueno Station in Tokyo acquired particular historical significance as a location associated with such children, though similar situations existed beyond Tokyo. The terms applied to them in contemporary newspapers and welfare records—often translated as 'vagrant children' or 'street children'—were administrative and moral labels, not neutral descriptions of every child's family history or conduct.

Street survival involved work and ingenuity. Children shined shoes, carried belongings, salvaged cigarette ends or scrap, begged, sold small goods, and sometimes committed theft. Some were drawn into exploitative labor or abusive arrangements; some survived through mutual help with other children or intermittent support from adults. There were considerable differences of age, gender, health, and experience among them. A child temporarily separated from relatives was not in the same position as a child permanently orphaned; a teenager able to work was not in the same situation as a younger child struggling to obtain meals. Characterizing all these children as criminals reproduces a contemporary prejudice that often helped justify roundups rather than adequate care.

Official figures require particularly careful reading. A 1948 administrative survey counted categories of war-affected children whose definitions and totals cannot be used interchangeably with a count of children actually living on the streets. The fact that a child had lost a parent, returned from overseas, or entered an institution did not necessarily make that child a homeless railway-station resident. Conversely, a child could be effectively abandoned while falling outside a narrowly defined orphan category. The historical record includes partial welfare counts, photographs, institutional registers, newspaper accounts, and later oral histories; each reveals different populations. It is better to explain these categories and their uncertainty than to assert a sensational national total of street children unsupported by a consistent census.

Authorities increasingly treated the problem as one of public order as well as welfare. Police and welfare officers conducted operations to remove children from stations and streets, sometimes using coercive methods. A study of wartime orphanage records reports that hundreds of street children were taken into Tokyo institutions during operations in June and July 1946, while some children later escaped or departed. These details show why institutional intake figures do not equal the number of children safely rehabilitated. Children could avoid official shelters if conditions were poor, family contact was lost, or they feared losing their freedom. Institutions themselves had suffered bombing, personnel shortages, and reductions in support during wartime. A government could identify a problem through roundup campaigns without yet possessing sufficient places or resources to solve it.

Welfare reform proceeded gradually. Japan's Ministry of Health and Welfare established specialized child-welfare administration and the Child Welfare Law was enacted in 1947, with major provisions implemented around the start of 1948. The law formed part of a broader commitment to protect children's welfare, not merely to remove visible children from stations. Yet legal reform did not instantly produce well-staffed facilities, sufficient foster homes, or reliable tracing of relatives. Public assistance, school meals, health services, and housing policy all affected whether children could remain safely with adults. Charitable and religious organizations also participated, but their resources and practices varied. The success of child welfare has to be assessed through children's later safety and opportunities, not simply through new institutional titles.

The voices of children are among the most difficult to recover. Photographs made by adults can preserve their presence while leaving their own understanding invisible. Welfare files usually record an official assessment, and later memoirs come disproportionately from those whose experiences survived into a narratable adult life. Historians should therefore avoid treating a single photograph as a complete biography, or a fictional depiction as administrative evidence. The Shōwakan archive's 1946 photographs of children at Ueno are valuable dated records, but they cannot reveal the subjects' full family histories or futures. Their significance is partly that they make visible people whom large-scale accounts of constitutional reform and economic recovery can leave outside the narrative. The measure of reconstruction was also whether such children could acquire food, protection, education, and a place within a stable community.

Section 85.10 · Division III — The Yami-ichi and the Informal Economy

What Was a Yami-ichi? From Wartime Controls to Postwar Street Commerce

The Japanese term yami-ichi is commonly rendered as 'black market,' but the translation can conceal a range of commercial forms. Yami referred to transactions outside official regulations, while ichi evoked a market. During and after the war, many foodstuffs, textiles, fuel, and other essentials were subject to price and distribution controls. Trading without authorization, selling at prohibited prices, or moving controlled goods through unofficial channels could therefore be illegal even when the goods themselves were ordinary necessities. Yet the spaces described as black markets were not uniformly secret or hidden. In cities they often appeared in conspicuous public locations, including plazas, station approaches, and cleared lots, where crowds could buy, sell, and gather within sight of the police. Their visibility exposes the tension between formal prohibition and the impossibility of fully enforcing controls amid widespread scarcity.

These markets did not arise from nothing on August 15, 1945. Unofficial exchange existed during the war, and Japanese urban commerce already included street stalls, peddlers, small independent shops, and covered market corridors. Postwar conditions expanded the demand for goods outside controlled distribution and created open sites on land damaged by bombing or wartime clearance. The disappearance of many established retailers and their stocks made low-cost temporary commerce especially attractive. Sellers could begin with a mat, table, or makeshift shelter; some later joined groups that organized stalls, passageways, rent, cleaning, and security. The distinction between spontaneous exchange and an organized market is important. What began as a small gathering could become a planned row of stalls or a more durable shopping district within a few years.

Architectural research has challenged an older image of the black market as merely a short-lived abnormality. Kōsei Hatsuda's study of Tokyo markets places postwar stalls in relation to prewar retail and street vending. Masakazu Ishigure and Hatsuda's analysis of a map of emerging markets in Tokyo documents differences among commercial sites and their internal organization. These studies suggest that markets should be understood as part of the city's changing built environment. Their shops, paths, and clusters affected pedestrian circulation and later rebuilding decisions. A market was a place where merchandise passed between hands, but it was also a social and spatial institution that connected vacant land, railway traffic, local politics, and the demand for neighborhood services.

Historical Vocabulary

Yami-ichi, Informal Trade, and Prohibited Sales

Yami-ichi literally refers to dark or unofficial markets, typically called black markets. Informal economy is broader: exchange or work outside regular reporting or licensing arrangements. Some transactions were forbidden by price and food-control rules; some stalls sold otherwise lawful goods; others received partial permission. Do not infer that every vendor belonged to a criminal organization or that every informal transaction was equally exploitative.

One difficulty is that names and legal categories shifted. A 'free market,' a newly established market, a row of temporary shops, and a strictly illegal stall could overlap without being identical. Some vendors acquired partial permissions; others were repeatedly moved or raided. A shop might sell regulated food alongside legal goods, while a person might engage in informal transactions outside a permanent shop. Describing every seller in a station district as a criminal would erase licensed commerce and ordinary work; describing all activity as benign survival would erase exploitation and black-market profit. The historian should ask what was traded, under which rule, by whom, and at what time. Legal status could change as municipalities issued new ordinances and occupation authorities altered enforcement priorities.

The markets also reflected an emergency of money and employment. Demobilization, factory closures, the loss of overseas business, and the erosion of salaries brought people into trading who had never operated a shop. A small stock of goods could be acquired through barter, salvage, previous occupational connections, or resale. Other traders had access to far larger stocks and could profit from their ability to transport goods or negotiate with suppliers. The dividing line between necessity and commercial advantage was not fixed. A widow selling household belongings might return the next week to resell food, while a former shopkeeper could attempt to restore a legitimate business through temporary stalls. The markets were thus avenues into a changed economy, but they did not distribute opportunity equally.

Their prominence also made them symbols. Newspapers could present market crowds as evidence of official incompetence, moral decline, ingenuity, or practical liberation from wartime restrictions. Occupation photographs frequently framed stalls and buyers as characteristic sights of the defeated country. But an image of a crowded market should not be taken as proof that every buyer could afford what was offered or that the goods were abundant in the rest of the city. The market's presence signaled a demand for immediate goods and a gap between regulation and daily life. Its persistence suggests that the restoration of commerce required more than a police decision. Stable supply, usable currency, premises, public trust, and legitimate distribution had to be rebuilt before unofficial trade would lose its everyday attraction.

Section 85.11

Markets as Urban Places: Shinjuku, Shinbashi, Ueno, and Beyond Tokyo

Postwar markets clustered where people and goods could meet. Railway stations offered routes to rural suppliers, commuting workers, returning evacuees, and shoppers willing to travel in search of necessities. Open spaces created by bombing or demolition provided room for temporary stalls. In Tokyo, Shinbashi, Shinjuku, Ueno, Ikebukuro, and other station districts became important points in the informal economy. Similar sites emerged in Osaka, Kobe, Nagoya, and numerous regional cities, although their scale, organization, and relationship to local government differed. The geography of the markets was therefore inseparable from the wartime destruction of buildings and the surviving routes of movement. A station forecourt could become an economic center because rail access mattered more immediately than the orderly commercial plans drawn up before or after the war.

Shinbashi supplies a documented visual case. A photograph catalogued by the Shōwakan archive shows market trading in 1946 near the station; the archive notes that a Shinbashi market was closed on July 22, 1946, even though stalls continued in the vicinity. This distinction demonstrates why closure cannot automatically be equated with the disappearance of commerce. The legal organization or a particular row of stalls might be removed while vendors reappeared nearby. Photographs taken at different dates can capture a sequence of temporary structures, not one unchanging market. Students examining a station image should note railway access, the arrangement of stalls, the crowd, any surviving buildings, and the viewpoint of the photographer. Those details reveal more about spatial organization than a generic caption stating that Japan had black markets.

At Shinjuku, the area around the station developed several market forms. Research on the Wadagumi market traces how its physical organization and participants changed during reconstruction. Entrepreneurs and stall operators laid out passages and retail spaces, while shifting plans for streets and rebuilding created conflict over occupation and eventual relocation. The history of one market site can include informal beginning, organized construction, regulation, and replacement by other commercial uses. It is therefore misleading to place all late-1940s station commerce into a single frozen photograph. The 'market' often became a series of spatial arrangements, some short-lived and others leaving durable traces in the neighborhood. Its transformation was an aspect of urban reconstruction rather than an obstacle wholly separate from it.

A broader Japanese architectural study examined municipal histories from one hundred cities that had populations exceeding roughly forty-five thousand before the war and identified evidence of black markets in nearly all of them. Market architecture had a practical logic. Early sellers used tables, mats, and movable goods, minimizing the cost of entry and risk of forced removal. Organized stall corridors could use screens or simple roofs to establish semi-permanent pitches, while later wooden barracks or shop rows offered storage and more predictable trade. Some developments mixed retail at the front with living quarters, eating places, and services behind. Such arrangements blurred the distinction between workplace and home in cities where both were scarce. Buyers experienced these places as routes through which to seek food, used clothing, repairs, news, and perhaps a meal. To planners, the same structures could obstruct road widening, sanitation, and land readjustment. Neither perspective by itself captures their historical function.

The spatial history of markets also requires attention to what photographs cannot show. A crowded view near a station does not reveal the supply chain that brought rice from a village, the rent paid for a pitch, or the restrictions faced by a vendor who had no recognized papers. Maps may include organized markets but omit individual traders who moved constantly. Official plans might portray a market site as empty land available for a future street, while the people trading there regarded it as their livelihood. Reading maps, photographs, and municipal records together makes the changing occupation of city space visible. The yami-ichi helped determine the routes and rhythms of the postwar city as much as it responded to the ruins that war had left behind.

Section 85.12

Goods, Prices, Exchange, and the Cost of Survival

A black-market stall did not necessarily sell a single category of contraband. Food was central, but households also sought clothing, fuel, utensils, medicines, tools, secondhand objects, and practical services. The disappearance of ordinary retail stocks and the breakdown of shipping encouraged small-scale resale and repair. Some commodities reached markets from farm households or private workshops; others came through diversion from regulated distribution, salvage, or illicit disposal of official stores. The provenance mattered legally and morally. A family bartering a bowl for food was engaged in a different transaction from an intermediary who diverted supplies intended for hospital patients. Yet both existed within an economy where legal channels often failed and where the price of immediate delivery could exceed the value of an official entitlement.

Price comparisons are central to understanding who benefited. Official food prices were controlled, but black-market prices reflected scarcity, risk, transport costs, bargaining power, and inflation. A state could publish a low lawful price without guaranteeing a product at that price. A merchant who could obtain stocks therefore possessed an advantage over a buyer whose wages were set or paid in currency rapidly losing value. Workers, pensioners, and unemployed people were often exposed to the difference between nominal income and actual purchasing power. Rural producers with food could sometimes obtain more valuable goods through private exchange than through an official sale. The resulting transfer of resources was not merely a matter of individual dishonesty; it followed from the misalignment of controlled prices, available supplies, and the economic needs of households.

Different kinds of informal transaction should be distinguished. Barter moved goods directly between people without a money price; private sale involved currency outside the official system; resale could pass goods through several intermediaries before reaching the final consumer. The more links involved, the more storage, transport, credit, and risk could shape price. An urban buyer might know only the stall's final price, not the original producer's payment. Some traders profited greatly, but many vendors operated on narrow margins and faced confiscation or eviction. The historical record rarely permits a single profit figure for 'the black market.' Location, commodity, legal exposure, and available capital all mattered. The costs borne by the seller were not the same as the hardships borne by the buyer.

Historical Debate

Were the Yami-ichi a Lifeline, an Exploitative Market, or a New Urban Institution?

Interpretation A — Survival institution: Informal exchange moved food and provided work when controls failed. Household and railway histories emphasize improvisation and immediate need.

Interpretation B — Source of inequality: High prices, diversion of rationed goods, powerful organizers, and unequal access to cash could make shortages worse for those least able to buy.

Interpretation C — Urban reconstruction mechanism: Architectural research shows that market layouts, stall associations, and sites became part of durable urban commercial change rather than a fleeting wartime anomaly.

Contemporary observers attempted to quantify the informal economy. The Australian representative Ball reported in May 1946 that an estimate placed forty percent of Tokyo's food consumed in December 1945 as black-market purchases. This is evidence of what an Allied diplomat believed or was told about Tokyo at a specific date, not a definitive measurement of all Japanese commerce. A later observer might use a different denominator—household expenditure, food weight, rice purchases, or calories consumed—and obtain a very different share. Such comparisons are useful only when the unit and population are identified. Inflation likewise requires dated price series: 'ten times the official price' is meaningless without a commodity, date, and place. Rather than inventing a universal premium, the chapter should explain why the legal and actual price systems diverged.

A market could be a place of information and social connection as well as exchange. Traders learned where goods might arrive; customers learned which sellers were reliable; informal credit or repeated dealings could help those without immediate cash. Trust was valuable because enforcement was inconsistent and quality could be uncertain. The same networks could exclude outsiders or permit exploitative practices. Goods might be adulterated, sellers could misrepresent quantity, and buyers could lack any practical avenue of complaint. Market rules and associations sometimes provided predictable arrangements that official law had failed to secure, but these private forms of order should not be romanticized. Their beneficiaries were often those able to negotiate, move goods, or enforce agreements.

The economic significance of the yami-ichi extended beyond immediate nutrition. Informal trade kept some goods circulating, offered income to workers displaced from wartime production, and supported the emergence of new retail clusters. It also made the official distribution system harder to sustain when supplies were diverted, raised the effective cost of living for those dependent on private purchases, and encouraged criticism of government credibility. Some buyers could condemn black-market profiteering while returning to buy food from the same stalls. Their behavior was not necessarily contradictory: it showed the difference between judging a system collectively and meeting an immediate personal need. Rebuilding a stable economy required addressing this tension through supply, purchasing power, lawful commercial outlets, and confidence that official rules could be followed without sacrificing survival.

Section 85.13

Vendors, Women, Repatriates, and Informal Work

Informal market activity offered work to people whose former occupations had collapsed. Demobilized soldiers, displaced factory workers, clerks without functioning offices, small merchants, returning settlers, and other civilians faced scarce formal jobs and uncertain wages. Many lacked the capital or permits needed to reopen a conventional business. Buying and reselling small quantities of goods, repairing clothing or tools, preparing food, carrying baggage, and operating makeshift stands allowed them to begin earning with limited equipment. Some had prewar trading experience; others learned through necessity. The occupations ranged from occasional household exchanges to full-time commercial ventures. Calling every participant an 'entrepreneur' would erase coercion and deprivation, while calling every participant a 'criminal' would deny the everyday economic work involved.

Women were central to the household economy and to commerce. Widows, married women supplementing declining household income, unmarried workers, and women caring for children often balanced shopping, barter journeys, and paid or informal work. Their efforts cannot be reduced to a sentimental story of endurance. Access to capital, safe transport, childcare, and social networks shaped what they could do. Some operated stalls or small services, while others exchanged household possessions or produced goods in domestic workshops. The market could provide a route to earning money outside a conventional employer, but it also exposed participants to harassment, police action, and precarious income. Gendered expectations sometimes made women's work less visible in official occupational records even though their contributions sustained households.

Repatriated civilians faced distinctive obstacles. People returning from former colonies and occupied areas could arrive with few possessions and without a preexisting position in the labor market. Chapter 81 described the scale and trauma of their return; here the concern is what earning a living required after arrival. Some had administrative or commercial skills transferable to trading, while others lacked local networks or usable credentials. Their participation in markets should not be inferred from a single image of a crowd. The extent and form of repatriate work varied among cities, generations, and families. Nevertheless, the relative openness of informal commerce could help individuals excluded from established employment, and specialist research on nationwide yami-ichi formation recognizes repatriates among the groups involved in their organization.

Employment also existed outside visible station stalls. People transported goods, repaired bicycles, sorted used clothing, cooked meals, stored merchandise, prepared makeshift structures, and cleaned market corridors. Children and adolescents sometimes performed marginal services, but the absence of secure legal protections made such work risky. Artisans attempted to restart production with salvaged tools; women sewed or washed; people with contacts arranged introductions between urban buyers and rural producers. These activities link the yami-ichi to the broader informal economy, which includes work and exchange outside regular records but not necessarily every form of criminal enterprise. A historian studying wages and factory production alone would miss much of this labor. A more comprehensive economic history considers household production, intermittent jobs, and the time spent obtaining necessary goods.

Access to the market was unequal. A vendor with a stable pitch, trusted supplier, and enough stock to weather confiscation could develop a business; a person selling the last family blanket had no similar reserve. Groups controlling valuable station locations could collect fees or exclude competitors. Such controls create the transition to Chapter 87's history of criminal networks and coercion, but the present chapter should not substitute a history of gangs for an account of how ordinary exchange worked. Most market participants cannot be classified merely by the existence of some coercive organizers. It is necessary to distinguish people who traded, people who owned or controlled market structures, and those who extracted payments by force or threat. The categories could overlap, but they should not be presumed identical.

Informal work also helped remake social status. Former office employees could sell food from wooden stalls; people who had been privileged under empire might find themselves dependent on manual labor; women could become primary earners; and returning soldiers might seek any occupation that supplied meals. Postwar society did not simply move from 'military' to 'civilian' roles overnight. The market was one site where hierarchies and identities were renegotiated under pressure. The continuing demand for small services also shows why commercial normalization was gradual. Even after factories resumed operations, household earnings, prices, housing costs, and access to lawful shops remained uncertain. Market workers had to weigh their fragile livelihoods against the changing prospects of licensed commerce and the threat of municipal clearance.

Section 85.14

Former Colonial Subjects, Migration, and Unequal Access to Markets

The markets of occupied Japan were shaped by the collapse of empire and by the continued presence of Korean, Taiwanese, Chinese, and other residents. Some had been brought to Japan through colonial labor mobilization or other imperial institutions; others had migrated earlier for work, education, trade, or family reasons. Their legal and political circumstances changed unevenly after August 1945. Koreans and Taiwanese were no longer ordinary imperial subjects in the same sense as before, yet occupation and Japanese authorities did not immediately establish a stable nationality and residency settlement. People faced repatriation decisions, restrictions, discrimination, and new opportunities in a rapidly changing urban economy. Treating them as a homogeneous group would misrepresent these distinct histories and their different relationships to the former empire.

Participation in informal markets sometimes offered a livelihood when regular employment and administrative recognition were uncertain. Street commerce could be entered with relatively small capital, and communities formed mutual-assistance and commercial networks. The historical record also documents disputes over market space, taxes, regulation, and competing claims to trade. These conflicts were shaped by prior colonial inequalities and by the ambiguity of legal status under occupation. The presence of a Korean or Taiwanese vendor in a black-market account does not make that vendor criminal by identity, and a newspaper's ethnicity-focused account may reveal the author's prejudices more clearly than the actual circumstances of a dispute. A critical history must ask whose testimony was recorded and what police, occupation, and community records can independently establish.

A scholarly study of postwar Tokyo markets identifies negotiations among Japanese market organizers and Chinese and Korean community representatives in spring 1946, including discussion of unauthorized sales and self-regulation. These accounts show that parties were not merely fighting for control; they also attempted to establish mutually intelligible rules in spaces where official government was ineffective or contested. The same study discusses later violent incidents, including the July 1946 Shibuya confrontation, as part of the wider struggle over market organization and status. Such events require careful chronological and evidentiary treatment. They cannot support claims about the conduct of an entire ethnic community, nor should they erase the colonial violence and wartime discrimination that shaped relationships after liberation.

The category 'liberated people' is itself historically revealing. SCAP communications sometimes recognized former colonial populations as liberated from Japanese rule while also allowing authorities to treat particular people as enemy aliens under specified circumstances. That ambiguity complicated policing and jurisdiction, and it affected opportunities for work and residence. Japanese institutions continued to act through their own legal categories even as Allied commands issued overriding instructions. These conditions differed from those of citizens whose nationality and voting rights were more stable. The longer history of nationality and legal membership belongs partly to Chapter 84, but its everyday consequences were felt when someone attempted to obtain work, rent a stall, open a shop, or contest an arrest. Economic inclusion could be partial and precarious despite formal political language about liberation.

Market participation also interacted with broader migration. Many Koreans left Japan after the war, while others remained because of family, livelihood, or the turmoil on the peninsula; Chinese and Taiwanese residents confronted different pathways. Returning Japanese settlers and soldiers competed for housing and employment in the same cities. Market relations could therefore bring together individuals whose recent histories included colonial privilege, forced labor, liberation, dispossession, and repatriation. The resulting tensions cannot be explained simply as ethnic hostility or greed. They involved scarce land, regulated goods, uncertain law, and memories of empire. Nor can individual experiences be reduced to national categories: class, gender, local ties, political affiliation, and existing commercial experience shaped market choices within each community.

No single source should be treated as a neutral transcript. Comparing them can illuminate why the postwar city was not an ethnically uniform space and why the distribution of economic opportunities after defeat was tied to the unresolved status of former imperial populations. The chapter's account of survival would be incomplete if it discussed only Japanese citizens, but equally incomplete if it turned minority residents into symbols of illegality. Their individual and collective experiences demonstrate how the end of empire continued to shape the market economy of the home islands.

Section 85.15

Crackdowns, Toleration, Markets, and Municipal Authority

The black markets posed an immediate dilemma for police and municipal governments. Food control and price regulations formally prohibited many of the transactions that occurred in public station districts. Officials could order a raid, confiscate goods, arrest sellers, or clear unauthorized structures. Yet the same officials knew that legitimate distribution failed to supply many residents, and that suppressing every informal outlet might intensify hardship. Enforcement therefore varied across time and place. Some markets operated with visible tolerance or partial permission; others were repeatedly raided or relocated. The apparent contradiction was not simply administrative incompetence. The city was trying to reconcile public health, transport, property claims, police authority, commercial recovery, and emergency subsistence under conditions in which no single policy could satisfy all demands.

The Shōwakan photographic archive records police action against market trading in Shinbashi in the surrender period, and later photographs document enforcement against unauthorized rice or prices. Such images establish that officials intervened and that the markets were publicly visible. They do not by themselves explain the grounds for each search or identify every object seized. Documents concerning licensing, confiscation, and arrests are needed to reconstruct the regulatory process. Some actions targeted controlled foodstuffs; others responded to obstruction of roads, sanitation concerns, or unauthorized occupation of land. Conflating these reasons produces an oversimplified picture of a state uniformly hostile to trade. Conversely, an image of a well-organized stall row does not prove that its operators enjoyed a secure legal right to remain.

Municipal rebuilding policies created a second form of pressure. Temporary markets occupied sites that city planners sometimes designated for streets, squares, transportation improvements, or permanent buildings. Removing stalls could serve a recognized public objective while destroying the income of people who lacked another occupation. Market organizers tried to negotiate, relocate, or convert temporary structures into more durable commercial premises. Some traders had rights based on leases or recognized permissions; others possessed only an informal claim supported by continued use and local relationships. Redevelopment disputes thus show how urban reconstruction was also a conflict over which economic activities would be acknowledged as legitimate and whose work would receive space in the new city.

From Wartime Controls to Postwar Commerce

Continuities: Food controls, ration registration, street vending traditions, producer–consumer tensions, and reliance on local relationships survived 1945.

Changes: The scale and visibility of station markets grew, new groups entered informal trade, welfare institutions expanded, commercial plots were rebuilt, and supply routes slowly stabilized.

The boundaries between public and private regulation also blurred. Stall associations could organize cleaning, rent payments, passageways, and collective demands. Such arrangements sometimes made markets easier for officials to supervise and for customers to navigate. They could also become mechanisms for extracting money or excluding less powerful sellers. The presence of coercion or racketeering is relevant, but a sustained analysis of postwar organized crime belongs to Chapter 87. Here it is enough to demonstrate why city authorities sometimes bargained with market representatives rather than addressing each individual trader. In the absence of sufficient police personnel and lawful retail capacity, negotiated order could appear more practicable than blanket prohibition, even when it left unequal powers within a market intact.

Occupation governance added further complexity. SCAP pursued food controls, public order, and economic normalization, but often left everyday enforcement to Japanese ministries and local authorities. American personnel could regard visible trading as evidence of instability while also recognizing that it was feeding people. Administrative decisions had to balance controls with the wider goal of preventing starvation and disorder. This complexity helps explain why laws, orders, and practice diverged. A prohibition could be selectively enforced; a market might be officially closed while selling continued nearby; and the same city could expand relief while increasing police action against unauthorized supplies. The fact that officials were inconsistent does not make the legal framework meaningless. It shows the difficulty of governing during a material emergency.

The market's longer-term fate depended upon changes beyond police power. More regular supplies, improved incomes, official shops, transport repairs, land readjustment, and new development altered the attractiveness and location of informal commerce. Some vendors moved into legal premises, others remained precarious, and some disappeared from the recorded economy. A simple narrative that black markets were 'eliminated' by a crackdown overlooks their transformation into new commercial forms. Architectural studies of Tokyo and municipal histories across Japan reveal that traces of these markets continued to structure neighborhoods after the emergency's peak. The yami-ichi was an improvised answer to institutional failure, but the way it was regulated and transformed helped determine the distribution of opportunity in the rebuilding city.

Section 85.16 · Division IV — Protest, Relief, and Uneven Recovery

The Food May Day of May 19, 1946: Hunger as Political Protest

Hunger was not only a private hardship. By spring 1946, discontent over shortages had entered the public streets, giving ordinary economic needs an explicitly political form. On May 19 demonstrators gathered in Tokyo in an event commonly remembered as the Food May Day. Labor activists, political organizations, and residents protested the failure of official distribution and demanded more effective measures from the government. The demonstration followed months of delayed rations and public discussion of the risk of starvation. Unlike the clandestine or household-level transactions described earlier in the chapter, a mass demonstration made food policy a visible question of governmental responsibility. The newly reopened political sphere allowed citizens to express grievances more openly than during the wartime years, but the emergency gave those grievances exceptional urgency.

The protest was shaped by a changing institutional setting. The occupation had instructed the Japanese authorities to permit greater freedom of association and expression, while SCAP still held overriding powers. Political parties and labor unions were reorganizing, and public meetings attracted people whose immediate concerns were wages, ration delays, and food prices. It would be misleading to portray the protest simply as an ideological maneuver by a single party, because the widespread food crisis created a base of genuine material grievance. It would be equally misleading to ignore how organized political groups attempted to turn the crisis into pressure on the cabinet. The demonstration united demands that were shared with disagreements over who should lead the response and how far confrontation with occupation authorities could proceed.

The event included placards and slogans directed at government responsibility. The Placard Incident became a notable controversy because a protest message aimed at the emperor exposed continuing limits on permissible political expression; the legal and political repercussions outlived the immediate food protest. The exact wording of particular slogans should be quoted only from a verified contemporary source, not reconstructed from memory or later paraphrase. The incident is significant because the constitutional status of the emperor was changing, but not all officials or residents agreed on how criticism of the imperial institution could be expressed. Public hunger therefore intersected with the remaking of political authority described in Chapters 82–84 without requiring those chapters' full institutional narrative to be repeated.

MacArthur responded on May 20 with a public statement warning that mass disorder would not be permitted to continue. The National Diet Library's occupation chronology records both the May 19 Food May Day and the following day's statement. These two dated entries are useful evidence of the tension within occupation democratization: demonstrators could mobilize and voice demands, but the supreme occupation authority reserved the power to define the boundary between lawful protest and unacceptable disorder. A proclamation about public order, however, cannot establish the intentions of every participant or the specific conditions faced by their households. Newspaper reports, union records, photographs, police accounts, and occupation communications need to be compared to reconstruct how the event was organized and understood.

Food demonstrations also posed a practical question. Even if a cabinet conceded that rations were inadequate, it still had to obtain grain and transport it. Protest could alter priorities, force public explanation, or expose the inadequacy of official assurances, but political pressure did not directly increase harvests. It could, however, influence decisions about procurement, imports, wages, and who was held responsible for delivery failures. Officials faced the possibility that hunger would destabilize the occupation system itself. The concern was not wholly cynical: unrest could disrupt railways and offices essential to distribution. Yet the use of order as a reason to constrain demonstrations also risked suppressing the voices of people whose claims reflected real deprivation.

The Food May Day illustrates how the problem of subsistence became a problem of legitimacy. A government could insist that people comply with food regulations, but the force of that insistence weakened when official channels did not reliably feed them. Citizens who had endured wartime mobilization were now asked to trust renewed authorities while the basic necessity of eating remained uncertain. The protest did not end the shortage, and its participants did not all share one political program. Its historical significance lies in the convergence of hunger, the reopening of mass politics, and the limits set by foreign military rule. It revealed that the restoration of civic rights had meaning only in a society whose members also demanded the material ability to live.

Section 85.17

Relief, Public Assistance, School Meals, and Community Support

Emergency welfare was not an afterthought to food and housing: it was one of the ways the Japanese state and occupation authorities attempted to make survival possible for people unable to purchase or barter enough necessities. Relief could involve temporary lodging, bedding, food, clothing, medical aid, and assistance to families without regular incomes. Local offices, charitable groups, schools, hospitals, and neighborhood networks were all important, but they differed in resources and authority. A widow might need both a ration entitlement and cash assistance; a homeless child needed protective care and an address; a disabled worker might require medical treatment as well as food. These overlapping needs challenged programs organized around separate administrative categories. The success of relief depended on actual supplies and trained personnel, not simply on the recognition of need.

Government records illustrate the scale of intervention while also revealing its limits. Japan's Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare retrospectively reports that in 1946 approximately 3.17 million people received forms of emergency support, or about 4.2 percent of the population. The figure reflects recorded relief participation under the categories used at the time and should not be treated as a total count of everyone hungry or homeless. Many people endured hardship without entering formal programs, while some received help from multiple sources. The Public Assistance Law of 1946 established an early statutory foundation for relief, preceding the fuller revision of the public-assistance system in 1950. The legal principle of minimum protection thus emerged during the emergency, but the ability to provide it was limited by budgets, available commodities, local offices, and social expectations about dependence.

Relief to children was especially significant. In addition to institutions for orphans and street children, school-based feeding could reach households through a familiar public setting. Wartime school meals had become difficult to maintain, and after defeat the program was resumed and extended in stages with foreign food contributions and Japanese administrative work. Skim milk powder and other imported supplies later became recognizable aspects of school nutrition. It is important not to date the entire postwar school-lunch system to one delivery or confuse the emergency measures of the late 1940s with the School Lunch Law of 1954, which formalized a later framework. A school meal could improve a child's nutrition and regular attendance, but the benefits depended on school access, cooking facilities, staff, and local distribution.

Community support was often less visible in official reports. Neighbors shared cooked food, relatives housed displaced people, employers assisted workers, and religious or charitable organizations gathered supplies. Mutual aid was not an adequate substitute for a functioning state. Its uneven distribution sometimes reinforced precisely the inequalities emergency welfare was intended to address.

The expansion of social assistance was part of the longer reconstruction of governmental legitimacy. Chapter 84 explained the Constitution's recognition of social rights, but rights became consequential through offices, programs, budgets, and the capacity to supply real goods. Welfare authorities learned to survey nutrition, classify needs, and construct public-health and child-protection systems while responding to immediate emergencies. The experience also showed the limits of emergency improvisation: administrative categories could exclude people, temporary measures could be underfunded, and distribution could fail even when policy was benevolent. Relief after defeat thus linked a basic human need for food and shelter to the institutional development of postwar public responsibility.

Section 85.18

Judge Yamaguchi and the Ethics of Illegal Food Purchase

One of the most frequently retold stories of the postwar food crisis concerns Yamaguchi Yoshitada, a judge of the Tokyo District Court who died in October 1947 after refusing to purchase food through illegal channels. The basic case is documented in Japanese biographical and legal-historical accounts, as well as in contemporary international reporting. Yamaguchi worked in a judicial system that prosecuted violations of economic regulations; he considered it inconsistent for a judge to break those rules personally. As shortages continued, he and his household faced serious nutritional hardship. His death became an emblem of the tension between obedience to law and the practical necessity of acquiring food. Its importance derives not from statistical representativeness, but from the stark moral problem it posed to a society dependent on widespread prohibited exchange.

The account must be handled carefully. A Japanese reference-library record identifies Yamaguchi's dates as November 16, 1913, to October 11, 1947, and states that he refused black-market rice after the war. Medical and historical narratives describe severe malnutrition together with tuberculosis and physical weakening. It is therefore too simple to describe the cause solely as immediate death from a single missed ration. The sequence combined prolonged nutritional deprivation, illness, work, and the pressures of a household with dependents. Contemporary reports include statements attributed to Yamaguchi and his widow, but wording reproduced through English journalism should not be treated as a checked translation of an original Japanese diary without further collation. The documented facts are already sufficient to raise a difficult historical question.

Yamaguchi's conduct was unusual precisely because many people believed obedience to the food-control laws was impossible without risking their health. Other judges, officials, wage earners, and families bought food privately while continuing to respect the law in principle. The contradiction was not a simple opposition between good citizens and criminals. The government promised rationed necessities while repeatedly failing to supply them on schedule; prosecutions treated unauthorized trade as an offense even when controlled goods were otherwise unavailable. A judge responsible for imposing penalties could see personal compliance as a professional obligation. A parent responsible for feeding children might see the same behavior as intolerable. A historian's task is to reconstruct those competing obligations rather than pronounce a universal moral verdict.

Everyday Life and Society

Judge Yamaguchi Yoshitada and the Price of Legal Compliance

Yamaguchi (1913–1947), a Tokyo District Court judge involved in economic-control cases, refused black-market food and died on October 11, 1947 after severe nutritional deprivation and illness. His biography is documented by Japan’s National Diet Library cooperative reference service and discussed by Matsuyama University’s law faculty.

The case raises a problem rather than providing a simple moral answer. A judge could believe that applying a law required obeying it personally. A parent could believe that feeding a family took precedence when the official ration failed. Medical accounts discuss malnutrition together with tuberculosis and debility; the case should not be used to invent a national death rate.

The case also reveals the unequal costs of principled compliance. Yamaguchi possessed legal standing and an identifiable place in the public record, while many people who suffered quietly from inadequate diets were not memorialized. His death became famous partly because a judge's refusal placed the legal system's own contradictions on display. It should not eclipse anonymous widows, street children, elderly people, or workers who lacked the resources to purchase privately whether they wished to obey the law or not. Nor should it suggest that every person refusing black-market food would necessarily die. The outcomes depended on health, official allotments, family support, local supplies, and the length of shortage. The exceptional case illuminates a broader system without standing in for every participant.

Yamaguchi's story was quickly transformed into moral commentary. Some writers praised unwavering integrity; others questioned whether the law deserved such obedience when its operation endangered life. These interpretations belong to the history of memory and legal thought. They can tell us how postwar observers understood an episode, but they may also simplify the competing demands on a judge, spouse, and parent. Historical analysis should preserve the distinction between what Yamaguchi reportedly did, what medical evidence establishes, and what later audiences made of his decision. This distinction is especially important in a textbook: an illustrative biography should provoke inquiry rather than become a sentimental substitute for explaining ration shortages and economic controls.

At its core the episode asks what makes law legitimate in an emergency. A state may regulate scarce food to prevent inequitable distribution, but its capacity to enforce rules depends partly on public confidence that compliance is possible and fair. If the official system cannot provide minimum subsistence, citizens may regard prohibited trade as necessary rather than shameful. Yet unrestricted private trade can also direct food toward those with money and away from those without it. Neither solution is automatically just. The case of Yamaguchi makes this conflict personal while the rest of the chapter shows its collective dimensions. It is a window into the moral instability of an economy in which legal compliance and survival no longer reliably coincided.

Section 85.19

1947–1950: From Emergency Exchange toward a Changed Urban Economy

Recovery from the worst food shortages was gradual, uneven, and vulnerable to new pressures. Better harvests, larger or more regular imports, the repair of transportation, and administrative changes improved some supplies after the acute emergency of 1946. Yet people continued to experience inflation, delayed or substituted rations, inadequate housing, and uncertain wages. The problems of 1947 were not simply a repetition of those of 1945: the new Constitution had taken effect, welfare and child-protection legislation was developing, and market arrangements had become more organized in many places. At the same time, workers and households still depended on unofficial exchange when lawful retail failed them. The transition therefore should not be described as a clean break between chaotic black markets and an orderly modern economy.

Supply networks changed through a combination of public and private action. Repairs to railways, ports, factories, storage facilities, and urban shops improved the movement of goods. Foreign relief shipments helped address deficits, while agriculture and domestic manufacturing gradually adjusted to peacetime conditions. Producers had incentives to sell through official channels when prices and available manufactured goods became more predictable; city residents had less reason to make barter journeys if stores could supply necessities. These changes were interconnected. A repaired railway could carry both lawful grain shipments and commercial merchandise; a stable local shop could offer credit and service where temporary stalls had once dominated. The restoration of commerce therefore depended on infrastructure and institutional trust as much as on police enforcement.

Inflation remained a serious obstacle. Households with fixed incomes could find that money wages lost purchasing power even as products became more available. The late-1940s stabilization effort, culminating in more stringent fiscal and monetary policies, altered the economy but also imposed costs on workers and struggling businesses. Detailed discussion of the Dodge Line and the wider Reverse Course belongs in Chapter 89. For Chapter 85, the central point is how stabilization affected the everyday choices of consumers and vendors. Lower inflation could improve the usefulness of wages and official prices, but tighter conditions could also threaten employment and credit. The effect on a household varied according to its job, savings, debts, and access to goods. 'Recovery' could describe a government statistic while particular families remained insecure.

Housing reconstruction likewise advanced unevenly. Some temporary markets were cleared, relocated, or transformed into regular shopping streets as municipal land readjustment and construction proceeded. Not all informal trade disappeared. Urban reconstruction did not merely replace an emergency economy with a former arrangement; it created new patterns of property, pedestrian movement, and work.

Social welfare changed the prospects for vulnerable people, though unevenly. The 1946 public-assistance arrangements, the 1947 child-welfare framework, school-based nutrition programs, and expanding health surveys contributed to a more organized system of protection. These institutions did not immediately overcome the disadvantages of families without secure homes or work. A street child could remain outside schooling, and a widow might continue to struggle with rent and food costs. The persistence of need helps explain why later welfare reforms were necessary. Community support and household labor also remained important even as official programs grew. The distinction between emergency aid and durable social rights was still being worked out through administrative practice.

By around 1950, many of the conditions that had made conspicuous black markets indispensable were changing, but no single year ended the experience of postwar scarcity. The Korean War would soon influence Japanese industry and employment, a separate development treated in Chapter 90. The present chapter ends before those later effects become its narrative center. Its conclusion is more precise: the immediate survival economy created after defeat was progressively reshaped by imports, reconstruction, welfare, commercial regulation, and economic stabilization, but households did not experience these changes at equal speed. The yami-ichi and other forms of informal exchange were not merely stains on a recovered city's history. They were among the arrangements through which people lived long enough to participate in reconstruction, even as their inequalities and conflicts exposed the limits of official government.

Section 85.20 · Division V — Historical Synthesis and Review

Survival after Defeat: Hunger, Law, the Informal Economy, and Reconstruction

The surrender of August 1945 ended the fighting of Japan's imperial war but did not bring immediate security to civilians in the home islands. Damaged houses and transportation, a weak agricultural harvest, limited access to imported food, inflation, and disrupted administration meant that obtaining a meal or a place to sleep could require as much organization as rebuilding a formal institution. The crisis cannot be reduced to a single harvest failure or one government mistake. It arose from overlapping problems of production, delivery, income, property, and the collapse of the wider imperial economy. These problems were not distributed evenly: geography, employment, family connections, health, gender, and legal status all affected exposure to shortage.

The rationing system embodied the state's continuing promise to allocate necessities. It also revealed the distance between a promised entitlement and a delivered good. Urban households could hold ration cards while facing long delays; rural producers could be compelled to surrender grain at prices that did not obtain needed manufactured goods. Neither side of the exchange operated outside history or social obligation. Official controls were meant to prevent the richest from absorbing all supplies, but their breakdown encouraged precisely the private bargaining they were designed to constrain. Without reliable food collection, transport, storage, and distribution, the legality of an allotment could not secure its nutritional value.

Households responded with labor and improvisation. Barter trips, gardens, shared rooms, repair work, small trading, and cooperation among relatives helped sustain people who had few official options. Yet resourcefulness was unequally distributed because the ability to improvise depended on resources: goods to trade, strength to travel, time, social networks, and access to land. The elderly, chronically ill, newly displaced, poor, and children without reliable adults could be least equipped to use the informal economy. The experience of street children and the gradual development of public welfare make clear that rebuilding society required institutional responsibilities extending beyond individual survival strategies.

The yami-ichi became an especially visible expression of this conflict. It was neither a uniformly benevolent market nor merely a criminal underground. Markets moved necessities and offered livelihoods while also allowing profiteering, unequal advantage, coercive organization, and the diversion of controlled goods. Their location near stations and on damaged urban land linked the emergency to the long-term reconstruction of cities. Trading spaces changed from movable stalls into organized corridors and sometimes permanent commercial properties. In that process they helped transform urban geography even as officials struggled to regulate or remove them. Former colonial subjects, repatriates, women, and unemployed workers participated under circumstances shaped by differing resources and political status.

Public protest and legal disputes demonstrated that hunger was also a question of legitimate government. The Food May Day of May 1946 connected basic provisioning to political accountability at a moment when public expression had reopened under occupation. Judge Yamaguchi's death in 1947 exposed the difficulty of demanding obedience to food-control laws when lawful supplies were inadequate. Both episodes invite analysis beyond moral simplicity. The absence of restrictions could worsen inequality; strict enforcement could punish necessary acts. The challenge was to build institutions capable of offering a real alternative to prohibited exchange, not simply to demand that people choose between hunger and illegality.

From 1947 toward 1950, larger supplies, improved transportation, relief programs, and new forms of commerce gradually changed the survival economy. These gains were substantial but did not arrive at one time for everyone. Economic stabilization could reduce price instability while imposing new difficulties; welfare laws could create rights without ensuring that every vulnerable household received aid. Chapter 86 will examine the gendered power relationships of occupation life, Chapter 87 the organizations that exercised coercive control over some markets, and Chapter 89 the wider restructuring of the postwar economy. This chapter's central finding is that survival itself was an arena of historical change. The boundary between lawful commerce and necessary subsistence remained unstable until the material and institutional foundations of everyday life were rebuilt, and the people who crossed that boundary were also participants in the making of postwar Japan.

Chapter Summary

The end of Japan’s imperial war did not immediately restore the necessities of civilian life. War-damaged homes, broken transport, weak agricultural production, and loss of imperial supply relationships intensified a preexisting emergency. The social crisis of 1945–1948 can be explained only by examining food, shelter, income, and the ability of administrative systems to deliver goods.

Urban destruction affected far more than the appearance of cities. Lost homes, interrupted utilities, damaged workplaces, and uncertain addresses complicated access to rations, work, care, and family reunification. Temporary shelters and overcrowded rooms kept many people alive but were unevenly allocated through ownership and personal relationships.

The food shortage had important geographical dimensions. Farmers produced supplies that urban consumers could not reliably obtain; transport, storage, controlled pricing, and procurement incentives stood between field and ration counter. Agricultural households differed in resources and opportunity, just as city residents differed in cash, kin networks, and health.

Ration entitlements expressed a state promise, but actual deliveries repeatedly fell behind schedules. Contemporary reports document especially acute problems in Tokyo–Yokohama in 1946. The distance between legal provision and lived consumption made private exchange difficult to suppress and helped undermine confidence in official policy.

Nutrition surveys showed how severe and unequal the emergency was. In May 1946, surveyed Tokyo ward residents had markedly lower average food-energy intake than surveyed farmers in the same region. These observations illuminate patterns but cannot be treated as a universal measure of every family’s diet.

Households organized barter journeys, home production, repair, informal services, and mutual support. Such actions required time, strength, and possessions, so the ability to improvise was itself unequal. The story of household resilience is therefore also a story of exclusion and deprivation.

Street children and families without secure accommodation revealed particularly severe failures of protection. Institutions began to develop better child welfare, housing, and relief systems, but the existence of new laws or shelters did not immediately guarantee adequate care.

The yami-ichi emerged from these conditions as prominent commercial spaces at railway stations and on damaged land. Markets supplied goods, jobs, and contact among strangers, while raising prices, rewarding some organizers, and exposing less powerful buyers or sellers to exploitation. Their meaning cannot be reduced to criminality or benevolent subsistence.

Former colonial populations, repatriates, women, unemployed workers, and farmers entered this economy with differing status and resources. The end of Japanese empire created new legal uncertainties and conflicts, particularly for Korean, Taiwanese, and Chinese residents. Urban market histories must address those differences without stereotypes.

The Food May Day protest and Judge Yamaguchi’s death exposed the crisis as a matter of political legitimacy and ethical obligation. Food controls could serve an equitable purpose, yet legal compliance could become dangerous when rations were absent. Gradual improvements in imports, welfare, transportation, and commerce reduced some emergencies without ending hardship simultaneously for all households.

Chapter Reflection

The food emergency after Japan’s defeat forces historical analysis to move from political decisions to the material conditions that gave those decisions meaning. The restoration of a parliament, freedom of speech, and new social rights occurred in a society where many people lacked food, shelter, or secure work. These forms of history must be read together. Government legitimacy was not simply proclaimed; it was experienced at a ration counter and in the practical possibility of surviving without violating the rules.

The chapter also reveals the importance of unequal resources. Barter required possessions; travel required health and time; market vending required suppliers, space, and some protection from confiscation. Those most vulnerable were often least able to improvise. Children, families without stable housing, people with illnesses, and groups facing discrimination experienced the recovery from different positions. The existence of a market did not mean equal access to what it sold.

The yami-ichi exemplifies how emergency measures can transform the future. Some stalls disappeared, some relocated, and others formed part of new commercial districts. Meanwhile housing, welfare, and distribution systems developed in response to failures that had become publicly visible. The city’s reconstruction was therefore not simply a return to the prewar arrangement. It was shaped by temporary uses, claims to space, official regulation, and the decisions of individuals trying to keep a household alive.

Finally, the crisis highlights the unstable relationship among legality, justice, and necessity. The state was right to be concerned that unrestricted exchange could leave the poorest without food, yet it also had to make lawful provisioning reliable if it expected general compliance. The political protest of May 1946 and the individual case of Judge Yamaguchi in 1947 demonstrate how that problem reached both mass politics and personal conscience. The following chapters explore other consequences of the same occupation order, but the lesson of this chapter remains basic: the end of war is not identical to the restoration of everyday peace.
