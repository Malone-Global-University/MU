Part VIII — Bakumatsu and the Meiji Revolution
58

Bunmei Kaika: Westernization, Modern Budō, and the Reinvention of Tradition

Japanese History: From Jōmon Japan to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

In the course of a single year, 1872, the ruler of Japan was reported to have eaten beef, fire destroyed the commercial heart of Tokyo's Ginza district, a railway began carrying passengers between Shimbashi and Yokohama, and the government decided to abandon the lunisolar calendar that had organized Japanese time for over a thousand years. Few years in Japanese history compress so many changes in everyday life.

These events were not isolated curiosities. They belonged to a broad and loosely coordinated movement of the early Meiji period known as bunmei kaika, usually translated "civilization and enlightenment." The phrase named both a government aspiration and a popular fashion: the conviction that Japan should acquire the institutions, knowledge, and habits associated with the most powerful societies of the nineteenth century, and the lived experience of people who cut their hair, tried new foods, rode trains, read translated books, and rearranged their homes. Chapters 54 through 57 examined the political and institutional transformation of the Meiji state, the abolition of the domains and the samurai order, the creation of schools and the new citizen, industrial construction, and the demand for representative government. This chapter turns to a different but inseparable dimension of the same revolution: how people experienced, interpreted, resisted, and remade culture.

The central argument is that Meiji cultural change was not the simple replacement of Japanese practices with Western ones. It was selective, unequal, and contested. Japanese officials, entrepreneurs, artisans, teachers, artists, and ordinary people adopted foreign ideas, but they also adapted them, mixed them with existing customs, and redefined what counted as authentically Japanese. Some practices that came to be described as ancient and unchanging, including major elements of modern jūdō, kendō, and the organized idea of a national artistic heritage, took recognizable modern form during these decades. Tradition, in other words, was not the opposite of modernization. It was frequently one of modernization's products.

The chapter proceeds through five thematic divisions. The first examines everyday life: clothing, hairstyles, food, time, hygiene, gender, and the household. The second turns to architecture and urban space, from the rebuilt Ginza to the Rokumeikan and the new visual culture of prints and photographs. The third addresses knowledge, language, literature, music, theater, and painting. The fourth, one of the chapter's longest, traces the transformation of older martial traditions into modern budō. The fifth considers criticism of Westernization, the search for cultural balance, and the invention of modern Japanese tradition. Throughout, the chapter attends to those who experienced change differently: women and men, city and village, elite and commoner, and communities such as the Ainu, Ryukyuans, and outcaste-descended families whose position in the new national culture was not equal.

Historians reconstruct this cultural world from varied evidence: government ordinances and photographs; woodblock prints and early newspapers; memoirs and translated books; surviving buildings, including those relocated or preserved as cultural property; school songbooks; dōjō records and martial-arts manuals; and the reports of foreign visitors and residents, whose accounts are valuable but shaped by their own expectations. Each type of source preserves some voices and silences others. A government edict shows what officials wanted, not what villagers did. A print celebrating brick streets shows an ideal, not the unsold, damp, or empty buildings behind it.

The principal period is 1868 to about 1900, with limited reference to early twentieth-century developments needed to explain why some practices attained their familiar form only later. Students should be cautious about terminology. The words "jūdō," "kendō," and "budō" did not carry the same meanings in 1875 that they carried in 1925, and the labels that now organize martial arts were stabilized gradually. The chapter therefore treats names, institutions, and meanings as historical developments in their own right.

Finally, the chapter fits into the textbook's larger argument that "Japan" is a historical construction rather than a timeless essence. Meiji cultural debates, about what to borrow, what to preserve, and who counted as part of the nation, shaped the identity claims that later generations would defend, criticize, and manipulate. They also shaped how the Ainu, Ryukyuans, women, workers, and others were invited into, or excluded from, the cultural nation.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain the meaning of bunmei kaika and distinguish government policy from popular cultural adaptation.
  2. Describe how clothing, hairstyles, food, time-keeping, and hygiene changed, and why these changes varied by region, class, and gender.
  3. Analyze the Ginza Bricktown and the Rokumeikan as two different expressions of Westernization: urban reconstruction and elite performance.
  4. Distinguish hybrid giyōfū architecture from Western architecture built by foreign architects, and explain the role of Japanese craftsmen.
  5. Interpret Fukuzawa Yukichi's civilization theory as an argument about independence rather than imitation alone.
  6. Trace changes in language, literature, music, theater, and painting, including the sharpening of the nihonga/yōga distinction.
  7. Explain how the abolition of the warrior order created a crisis for martial traditions and how they were reorganized.
  8. Compare classical jūjutsu and Kōdōkan jūdō, and classical kenjutsu with the developing practices that became kendō.
  9. Assess the role of police, schools, and the Dai Nippon Butokukai in institutionalizing martial culture.
  10. Evaluate competing interpretations of Meiji Westernization as imitation, pragmatic adaptation, or cultural transformation.
  11. Evaluate the argument that some familiar "traditions" acquired their modern forms in the Meiji period.
  12. Avoid projecting twentieth-century budō terminology, institutions, and ideology onto the early Meiji decades.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did Western civilization become an attractive, and for some a threatening, model in Meiji Japan, and who decided what counted as "civilized"?
  2. Why could cutting one's hair, eating beef, or wearing shoes become politically and culturally meaningful acts?
  3. How did the experience of Westernization differ between the treaty ports and the countryside, between men and women, and among social classes?
  4. What do the Ginza Bricktown and the Rokumeikan reveal about the relationship between foreign diplomacy and domestic cultural display?
  5. In what sense was Fukuzawa Yukichi arguing for independence rather than simple imitation?
  6. How did the end of hereditary warrior status change the meaning, purpose, and organization of martial practice?
  7. To what extent are jūdō and kendō continuations of samurai-era traditions, and to what extent are they Meiji and later inventions?
  8. Why did the idea of "Japanese tradition" become so important in the same decades in which Japan was borrowing most heavily from abroad?
  9. Whose culture was treated as the national culture, and whose practices were suppressed or reclassified?

Historical Context

Chapter 51 and Chapter 52 traced how the arrival of Commodore Perry's squadron, the unequal treaties, and the opening of ports at Yokohama, Nagasaki, Hakodate, Kobe, and elsewhere brought foreign merchants, diplomats, missionaries, and advisers into Japanese life. Chapter 53 explained the overthrow of the shogunate and the creation of a new central government in the name of the emperor. The Meiji leaders who emerged from that struggle had, in many cases, seen the West at first hand. The Iwakura Mission, which left Japan in late 1871 and traveled through the United States and Europe, included senior officials and a small group of young students, among them girls sent to study abroad. Its members returned persuaded that national strength rested not only on armies and factories but on education, institutions, and the visible habits of "civilized" nations.

Japan before 1868 was not a cultural blank awaiting Western content. Tokugawa society had a sophisticated urban culture, high literacy by contemporary standards, a flourishing print industry, regional crafts, active commerce, and a learned tradition of Dutch Learning (rangaku) that had already introduced Western medicine, astronomy, and military technology (Chapter 50). The new Meiji elite therefore selected from a world they already partly knew. Their choices were shaped by the unequal treaty system, which imposed foreign legal jurisdiction and limited tariff control. Many leaders believed that displaying "civilization" would help persuade Western powers to treat Japan as an equal. This belief, more than admiration alone, explains the intensity of some early Meiji cultural policies.

The political and social setting also mattered. The abolition of domains and the samurai order (Chapter 54) removed the legal basis of warrior status; the new school system and conscription (Chapters 54 and 55) reorganized childhood and adult obligations; the construction of railways, telegraphs, and factories (Chapter 56) changed work and mobility; and the popular rights movement (Chapter 57) expanded public debate. This chapter does not retell those developments. It asks how, within them, people dressed, ate, built, wrote, performed, trained, and argued about identity. The Chapter 54 sword prohibition and Chapter 55 school regulations are referred to only where necessary to explain cultural consequences.

Students should keep three cautions in mind. First, government decrees often set permission or fashion rather than mandatory practice, and the gap between decree and behavior was large. Second, change was geographically uneven: Tokyo, Yokohama, Kobe, Osaka, and some provincial centers saw rapid visible change, while many villages changed slowly and selectively. Third, "the West" was never a single model. Britain, France, Germany, the United States, and other states offered different institutional and cultural examples, and Japanese observers chose among them for different purposes.

Division I — Bunmei Kaika and the Transformation of Everyday Life

Section 58.1

Bunmei Kaika: Civilization, Enlightenment, and the Meiji Cultural Revolution

The term bunmei kaika combined bunmei ("civilization") with kaika ("opening" or "enlightenment"). It became a slogan of the 1870s, popularized through newspapers, translated books, popular fiction, and woodblock prints. Its power came from an assumption widely shared among Meiji intellectuals and officials: that societies could be arranged along a scale of development and that the leading nations of Europe and North America stood at its summit. This was not a neutral description. It was an ideology of its time, supported by imperial expansion, and it carried a hierarchy that Japanese commentators applied both to the West and to Asia. Fukuzawa Yukichi and others used the scale to argue that Japan must advance, while later commentators would use similar language to justify Japanese claims over neighboring societies (Chapters 60 and 62).

It is important to distinguish government policy from popular adaptation. The Meiji government did promote certain changes by decree: the formal adoption of Western-style dress for official occasions, the Gregorian calendar, Western-style military and police uniforms, railways and telegraphs, and a new school system. But many of the changes that caught public attention happened through commerce, fashion, curiosity, and imitation rather than command. Restaurants selling beef, tailors making suits, publishers issuing translated manuals, and city residents wearing hats and carrying umbrellas altered everyday life without any single directive. The Meiji state encouraged some of these habits, tolerated others, and in some cases regulated them, as when Tokyo authorities issued rules about public nudity and street behavior in 1872 that responded to foreign criticism of local customs.

Why did Western civilization become influential? Several explanations overlap. The unequal treaties and the memory of Western military power created a perception of vulnerability. The Iwakura Mission's observations of industrial cities, schools, and ceremonies persuaded many leaders that "civilization" encompassed behavior and appearance as well as machinery. Merchants and intellectuals saw commercial and personal opportunity. Former samurai who lost status looked for new identities in education, bureaucracy, journalism, and enterprise. And the slogan wakon yōsai, "Japanese spirit, Western techniques," expressed a widespread hope, rarely a stable formula, that Japan could take foreign methods without losing its own character. The history of the chapter is partly the history of how unstable that hope proved.

Bunmei kaika was also contested from the beginning. Some Confucian-trained scholars distrusted the cult of novelty. Some Buddhist and Shinto figures resented religious policy and cultural mockery of older customs. Many villagers saw little immediate benefit in policies that added taxes, conscription, or school fees. The movement was strongest in treaty ports and large cities and among officials, students, merchants, and journalists. Understanding it as a metropolitan, male-dominated, and often elite-led phenomenon corrects the impression, created by many Meiji illustrations, that the whole nation changed overnight.

Historical Vocabulary
Bunmei kaika (文明開化)

Bunmei kaika, "civilization and enlightenment," was the slogan and cultural program of the early Meiji period associated with adopting Western institutions, knowledge, and customs. It was used by officials, journalists, popular writers, and publishers and appeared in the titles of popular books and prints.

Students should not treat it as a synonym for "Westernization" in a modern sense. The word bunmei had been rendered into Japanese as an equivalent of the European concept of "civilization," a concept that itself implied hierarchy. Participants disagreed over what it required: some emphasized institutions and law, others education and morals, others fashion and consumption. It was also a tool of self-presentation toward foreign powers. Its meaning shifted in the 1880s, when critics associated it with superficial imitation and emphasized "national essence" instead.

Section 58.2

Western Dress, Hairstyles, Uniforms, and the Changing Appearance of Society

Appearance communicated status, occupation, and political stance in Tokugawa Japan, and the early Meiji government sought to change it deliberately. The most dramatic change involved the male topknot (chonmage), long associated with samurai and, in modified forms, with commoner men. A government edict of 1871 permitted men to cut their hair short, and short hair, known popularly as zangiri, spread quickly among officials, students, and urban men. The emperor himself was reported to have cut his hair in 1873. A popular saying of the era linked a cropped head to the sound of "civilization," and it appears frequently in later accounts, though its exact origins and wide use are better understood as an expression of the period's mood than as a documented decree. Haircutting meant different things to different people: a mark of loyalty to the new order, an act of fashion, or a painful break with a status symbol. In the countryside, short hair spread more slowly, and some older men kept their topknots for years. Women were treated differently. The government forbade women to cut their hair in 1872, and elaborate Japanese hairstyles remained standard for women well beyond the 1870s, though new hairstyles reflecting Western fashion appeared later in the Meiji period.

Uniforms were the most thoroughly Westernized form of dress. The army and navy adopted Western-style uniforms during the Bakumatsu and early Meiji years, and the new police force, civil officials, schools, railway workers, and postal staff followed. In late 1872 the government prescribed Western-style dress for officials on formal occasions, and court dress was reorganized along European lines, extending to court women in the later 1880s as the Empress and ladies of the court adopted Western dress for public occasions. Uniforms had a particular logic. They signaled belonging to the state, replaced domain-based and status-based clothing, and made authority legible. Photographs of the emperor changed accordingly. Portraits and photographs presented him in military or Western-style dress, an image that reached many households through official distribution (see Section 58.8).

For ordinary people, the change was far less sweeping. Western clothing was expensive and unfamiliar, and tailors, cloth, and shoes were not widely available outside cities. Many men wore a mixture: kimono with a Western hat, scarf, coat, or umbrella, or a Western jacket with Japanese footwear. Women in cities and villages overwhelmingly continued to wear kimono, though new fabrics, imported yarns, and synthetic dyes changed color, price, and production. Female students in elite schools adopted the hakama skirt in the 1880s and later, a Japanese garment adapted to new conditions of movement and schooling rather than a Western import. The boundary between "Japanese" and "Western" clothing was therefore blurred, and everyday mixing was the norm. Fully Western daily clothing became common among ordinary Japanese people mainly in the twentieth century.

Footwear illustrates how cultural change linked to economic change. Boots and shoes were needed for soldiers and officials, and the new shoe industry developed in Tokyo and other cities from the early 1870s. Leather working had long been performed by communities stigmatized as outcaste under the Tokugawa status system (Chapter 43). Expanded military and consumer demand created opportunities for some of these workers and entrepreneurs, but the new demand did not remove discrimination, and the 1871 emancipation edict (Chapter 55) did not by itself change social attitudes. The same Western fashion that conferred respectability on some people therefore depended on labor that remained socially marginalized.

Appearance also became a matter of regulation and policing. Tokyo ordinances of 1872 prohibited certain forms of undress and behavior in public, partly in response to foreign critics. In Hokkaido, colonial officials pressed Ainu communities to adopt Japanese names and customs and restricted traditional practices such as men's earrings and women's tattooing, as the Kaitakushi (Hokkaido Colonization Office) sought assimilation (Chapters 48 and 62 treat the wider frontier context). Here "civilization" operated as an instrument of cultural domination as well as fashion, a reminder that bunmei kaika could be coercive when directed at groups with less power.

Section 58.3

Food, Hygiene, Time, and the Transformation of Daily Habits

Meat eating became one of the best-known symbols of bunmei kaika because it overturned a long-standing set of religious and social attitudes. For most of the Tokugawa period, the consumption of four-legged animals was discouraged by Buddhist ideas and by ritual concepts of pollution, though it was never entirely absent, and medicinal or hunters' meat eating persisted in some regions. In early 1872 the court was reported to have lifted its restrictions on meat, and the emperor's eating of beef was publicized. Advocates, including Fukuzawa, argued that meat eating would strengthen the national physique. Beef-pot restaurants (gyūnabe), forerunners of sukiyaki, spread in Tokyo and Yokohama, and Western-style dishes adapted to Japanese tastes, later called yōshoku, developed in the following decades. Opposition was real. According to commonly cited accounts, a small group of men attacked the palace in 1872 reportedly in protest at the changes at court, and many Buddhist and rural communities resisted or ignored meat eating for years.

Bread, milk, beer, coffee, and imported goods also entered the diet, though their reach was uneven. The bakery Kimuraya in Tokyo developed the sweet bean-filled anpan in the mid-1870s, a characteristic hybrid of imported technique and Japanese taste. Rice remained the foundation of most meals, and the new foods were most widely available to urban people with disposable income. Regional diets differed greatly, and many rural households continued to eat grains, vegetables, and preserved foods, not the dishes celebrated in urban prints.

Hygiene and public health formed a second front. The Meiji government built a modern public health administration, and the term eisei ("hygiene") became associated with state efforts to prevent epidemic disease. Cholera epidemics in the late 1870s and 1880s each caused very large numbers of deaths, widely cited as exceeding 100,000, and led to quarantine measures, sanitary inspection, and sometimes popular resistance, including rumors and riots in rural areas. Hygiene campaigns reached into homes and bodies: cleaning, water supply, waste, vaccination, and regulation of bathhouses and prostitution. They reflected scientific medicine, but they also expanded the state's reach into daily life and sometimes clashed with local customs.

Time itself was reorganized. The government adopted the Gregorian calendar on 1 January 1873, after decreeing the change in late 1872, abandoning the lunisolar calendar. The abrupt shortening of the final lunar month caused complaints, and the shift disrupted the ritual and agricultural rhythms tied to older festival dates. Many communities continued to observe "old calendar" dates, in some cases for generations. Government offices adopted a weekly Sunday holiday in 1876, replacing earlier rest days tied to dates with "one" and "six." The traditional system of variable "hours" that changed length with the seasons gave way to equal hours on clocks. Japan adopted a standard national time based on the 135th meridian east in 1886 (effective 1888). Together with railway timetables, school bells, and factory shifts, these changes created new habits of punctuality, though they spread unevenly and mostly through institutions rather than private life.

Everyday Life and Environment

Clothes, Clocks, and Kitchens

A Tokyo clerk in 1875 might have worn a short haircut, a Western-style jacket on top of a Japanese undergarment, and leather shoes, and then taken off the shoes at the door of a home where he ate rice, pickles, and soup on a tray. A merchant's wife in the same city may have worn the same style of kimono and hair she wore as a girl but bought imported dyed cloth. A farm family in a mountain village might have encountered new things mostly through the tax collector, the school, or the conscription notice. A woman working in a textile mill (Chapter 63) lived according to a bell and a clock but did not wear Western dress.

These combinations show the limits of the "Westernization" label. Change occurred through selective borrowing governed by cost, occupation, gender, region, and habit. The homes of ordinary people, built of wood and paper with tatami floors, remained largely unchanged, while the new items, hats, lamps, clocks, umbrellas, beef, lamps burning kerosene, entered as additions. Historians use diaries, price records, photographs, prints, and ordinance records to reconstruct these patterns, but few sources record the experience of poor rural people directly.

Section 58.4

Gender, Etiquette, Domestic Life, and the Negotiation of Modernity

Bunmei kaika rewrote expectations of manners and household life, and its effects fell differently on women and men. Elite men were expected to display fluency in Western manners: handshakes, dancing, table etiquette, and formal dress. Elite women faced new expectations of public presence. The Iwakura Mission's young women students, including Ōyama Sutematsu and Tsuda Umeko, had spent years abroad and returned with skills and perspectives unusual in Japan. Sutematsu, who married the army leader Ōyama Iwao in 1883, became a prominent figure in the Rokumeikan world and in charitable activity. Her story shows that some elite women gained visibility through the new cultural order, but within limits set by family and class.

Debates on marriage, the family, and the position of women proliferated in the 1870s and 1880s. Some Meirokusha members criticized concubinage and called for more equal marriage practices, though their proposals were limited and divided. State policy during the 1880s and 1890s increasingly emphasized women's role as managers of the household and educators of children, a model later summarized by the phrase ryōsai kenbo, "good wife, wise mother," which belongs more fully to the later chapters on women, family, and education (Chapters 55 and 64). This model borrowed both from Western domestic ideals and from Confucian traditions. It is an example of how modern Japanese gender norms were created through mixture rather than inheritance from either side alone.

The domestic sphere changed more slowly and more selectively than public display. Wealthy households and officials added Western-style reception rooms, furniture, and glass windows to otherwise Japanese houses, a pattern that allowed hosts to entertain foreign guests while living in Japanese rooms. Most families altered their homes little. Tables, chairs, and Western lighting spread in stages. Wedding ceremonies, too, were not simply replaced. The now-familiar "traditional" Shinto-style wedding in a shrine took form later, largely in the early twentieth century, after a widely publicized imperial wedding in 1900, which shows how a custom could be presented as ancient while crystallizing recently.

Etiquette became a tool of class distinction. Manuals and magazines instructed readers in correct dress, speech, and conduct, and teachers and publishers earned money by interpreting the new rules. Households aspiring to respectability studied them. Such manuals are valuable evidence of ambition and anxiety but tell historians little about actual conduct. They also show that "civilization" was a marketplace of advice rather than a single code. Women especially were both addressed as consumers and expected to represent the household's respectability.

Division II — Architecture, Urban Space, and the Visible Landscape of Modernity

Section 58.5

The Ginza Bricktown and the Reconstruction of the Modern City

On 26 February 1872 a major fire swept through the Ginza and Tsukiji area of Tokyo, destroying thousands of houses and the Tsukiji Hotel. The disaster gave the Tokyo authorities and the central government an opportunity. The area lay between the Shimbashi railway terminal, soon to open, and the foreign settlement at Tsukiji, so rebuilding it as a showcase of fireproof, orderly, Western-style modernity would impress foreign visitors and serve as a model for the capital. The Irish engineer Thomas Waters, hired by the government and already known for work on the Osaka Mint, was engaged to plan the district. The project aimed to produce wide streets, sidewalks, brick buildings with arcades, and later gas lighting. The Ginza gas lamps began operating in 1874, and street trees and tram lines followed in the next decades.

The project was only partly successful. Brick construction was costly and unfamiliar. The buildings were reported to be damp and poorly suited to Japanese ways of living and working, and many were slow to find tenants or buyers. Plans for the whole district were scaled back, and parts of it were completed in the later 1870s only after considerable delay. Merchants who had owned wooden shops before the fire were sometimes unwilling or unable to pay for brick replacements. Despite these limits, the Ginza became a powerful symbol. Prints by artists such as Hiroshige III and others depicted it as a modern street full of carriages, lamps, and fashionably dressed pedestrians. Shops sold imported and Western-style goods; newspapers set up offices; photographers opened studios. Later in the century the district developed into Tokyo's premier shopping area, with landmarks such as the clock tower of the Hattori company (1894).

The Ginza case illustrates the interplay of state ambition, commerce, and urban experience. The government intended fire prevention and display, but the district's success depended on merchants and consumers. It also illustrates how cities differed. Tokyo's Ginza was designed as a national showcase; Yokohama's foreign settlement, shaped by treaty-port life, had a more directly international character; Osaka developed as a commercial and industrial center with its own priorities; Kyoto, having lost the court to Tokyo in 1869, pursued a different strategy, organizing expositions and promoting its crafts and traditions while also modernizing industry and education. Sapporo, built with the guidance of American advisers under the Kaitakushi, was a planned colonial town, a reminder that urban modernization intersected with settlement and the dispossession of Ainu communities.

Section 58.6

Western Buildings, Hybrid Architecture, and the Changing Japanese Landscape

Much Meiji "Western architecture" was designed and built by Japanese craftsmen who interpreted foreign models in familiar materials and techniques. The Tsukiji Hotel of 1868, built for foreign guests by the carpenter-contractor Shimizu Kisuke II, mixed Western exterior forms with timber construction. Similar buildings, often called giyōfū ("pseudo-Western style"), appeared across the country: government offices, courthouses, banks, hospitals, schools, and police stations. The Kaichi School in Matsumoto, completed in 1876, combines Western-style features with traditional carving and timber techniques and is a well-known surviving example. Such buildings reveal that Japanese builders were active interpreters of foreign forms, not passive copyists, and that modern building was a field in which skill from the Tokugawa period remained valuable.

The railway stations, government offices, and hotels that rose in the 1870s were often designed with foreign advisers. The Shimbashi terminus, for example, followed designs associated with American engineers. In 1877 the government brought the British architect Josiah Conder to teach at the Imperial College of Engineering in Tokyo. Conder trained the first generation of Japanese architects, including Tatsuno Kingo, who later designed major brick buildings such as the Bank of Japan headquarters (1896) and Tokyo Station (1914). Conder also designed buildings including the Rokumeikan, discussed in the next section. Through such teachers, architecture became a profession in Japan, and Japanese architects increasingly designed Western-style buildings themselves, while later debates asked whether Japan needed an architecture of its own.

Public space changed in other ways. Schools, post offices, prefectural offices, and railway stations created new focal points in towns. They also reflected policy: as national and prefectural authorities built them, they displayed the state's presence in places where it had previously been less visible. Many such buildings were modest, and many people's daily environment remained timber houses and traditional shrines and temples. Fire, earthquakes, and later reconstruction (Chapter 71) destroyed much early Meiji architecture, and the buildings that survive are not a random sample. Historians use photographs, plans, and prints as well as surviving structures to reconstruct what has been lost.

Historical Vocabulary
Giyōfū (擬洋風) architecture

Giyōfū, "pseudo-Western style," describes early Meiji buildings that imitated Western appearance while using Japanese carpentry methods and decorative motifs. The term is a later scholarly label, and it can sound dismissive, but historians use it to describe an inventive hybrid.

It matters because it shows that Westernization involved Japanese builders interpreting foreign models. It should be distinguished from buildings designed by foreign architects and from the later, more academically Western architecture produced by Conder's students. Calling giyōfū "imitation" misses the creativity involved.

Section 58.7

The Rokumeikan, Elite Society, and the Performance of Western Civilization

The Rokumeikan was a two-story Western-style building in Tokyo, designed by Josiah Conder and opened in November 1883 under the sponsorship of Foreign Minister Inoue Kaoru. Its name was taken from a classical Chinese source. It served as a venue for receptions, dinners, and balls attended by government leaders, nobles, and foreign diplomats, with Japanese men and women in Western dress. Its purpose was diplomatic. Japan was seeking revision of the unequal treaties, which Western governments resisted partly on the ground that Japan's laws and society were not "civilized." Inoue and others believed that demonstrating fluency in Western social forms would help change this perception. Women of the elite, including Ōyama Sutematsu, were prominent organizers of events such as charity bazaars.

The Rokumeikan became a flashpoint for criticism. Conservative and nationalist observers, and some liberal critics, complained that it was extravagant and servile, a display for foreigners that diverted attention from real reform. Foreign observers sometimes responded with ambivalence or mockery; a well-known satirical drawing by the French artist Georges Bigot in 1887 depicted Japanese elites in pursuit of foreign approval, and French writer Pierre Loti described an imperial birthday ball in unflattering terms. These sources are revealing but must be read critically: they reflect foreign expectations of cultural authenticity as much as Japanese behavior. Meanwhile a lavish costume ball at Prime Minister Itō Hirobumi's residence in April 1887 drew criticism, and treaty-revision proposals that included foreign judges in Japanese courts provoked a political storm. Inoue resigned in 1887. The Rokumeikan was sold in 1890 and later served as a clubhouse for the nobility; it was demolished in 1941.

Historians disagree about the Rokumeikan's significance. Some emphasize its failure to achieve treaty revision, which was ultimately negotiated through hard diplomacy and Japan's military and legal modernization in the 1890s (Chapter 60). Others stress that it was a serious attempt at cultural diplomacy and that it helped create a model of elite sociability, including new public roles for women. Its brief career also helped shape the cultural reaction of the late 1880s, described in Section 58.18.

Section 58.8

Photography, Woodblock Prints, Advertising, and the Visual Culture of Change

Images made Meiji change visible. Kaika-e, "civilization-and-enlightenment pictures," were woodblock prints that portrayed new technologies and fashions: steam trains, brick streets, gas lamps, telegraph poles, and people in Western dress. Artists such as Hiroshige III celebrated the Ginza and the railway, often with bright colors, stylized crowds, and a degree of exaggeration that conveyed optimism. Earlier Yokohama prints (Yokohama-e) had depicted foreigners and port life. Other artists, notably Kobayashi Kiyochika, developed a light-and-shadow style in prints of Tokyo in the late 1870s and early 1880s, capturing gaslight, steam, and evening scenes in a manner influenced by Western techniques. Prints were commercial products made by publishers for urban buyers, so they show what was marketable, not an unfiltered record.

Photography was introduced in the 1850s and 1860s through pioneers including Shimooka Renjō in Yokohama and Ueno Hikoma in Nagasaki, and by the 1870s it was used for portraits, travel, and documentation. Uchida Kuichi photographed the emperor in 1872, and later official portraits, including a drawing by the Italian engraver Edoardo Chiossone completed in 1888, shaped the standard imperial image. Studios sold portraits to ordinary families as well as elites, and tourists' photographs, often staged, shaped foreign ideas of Japan, with Japanese photographers and foreign photographers both creating "typical" images for sale. Photographs are evidence that is easily mistaken for neutral, but their subjects, framing, and retouching were chosen.

Newspapers, which began as daily publications in 1871 and 1872, carried news, serial fiction, and increasingly advertisements. Advertising copy and shop signs promoted medicines, books, drapers, beef restaurants, and imported goods. The commercial culture of the Edo period continued in new forms. Older drapers adapted their businesses to new conditions, and larger retail houses prepared the ground for the department stores of the early twentieth century, which belong to Chapters 65 and 70. This visual culture is a main reason Meiji Japan can seem to modern viewers entirely Western, an impression that is misleading unless balanced with evidence of what the images left out.

Division III — Knowledge, Literature, Art, and Cultural Adaptation

Section 58.9

Fukuzawa Yukichi, Civilization Theory, and the Interpretation of the West

Fukuzawa Yukichi, born in 1835 into a lower-ranking samurai family of the Nakatsu domain and trained in Dutch and then English learning, became the best-known interpreter of the West for Meiji readers. His Seiyō jijō ("Conditions in the West," published from 1866) described Western institutions, and Gakumon no susume ("An Encouragement of Learning," 1872–1876) argued that education and individual independence were foundations of national strength. His Bunmeiron no gairyaku ("An Outline of a Theory of Civilization," 1875) was the most systematic statement of his views. He ran the school that became Keiō Gijuku and, later, a newspaper, and he never held a major government post, a choice that reflected his emphasis on private initiative.

Fukuzawa belonged to a wider intellectual community. The Meirokusha, founded in 1873 on the initiative of Mori Arinori, brought together scholars including Nishimura Shigeki, Katō Hiroyuki, Nishi Amane, Tsuda Mamichi, Nakamura Masanao, and Fukuzawa. Its members, many trained in Western learning, debated education, women's status, religion, language, and the nature of civilization in meetings and in the journal that appeared between 1874 and 1875. The group disbanded in 1875 in the wake of new press and libel regulations that restricted political writing (Chapter 57). Its members disagreed with one another, and Nakamura, translator of works including Samuel Smiles's Self-Help and John Stuart Mill's On Liberty, was influenced by both Christian and Confucian ideas. The Meirokusha was therefore a forum of competing interpretations, not a single party of Westernizers.

A central problem for these thinkers was how to modernize without surrendering independence. Fukuzawa's solution distinguished between the visible forms of civilization, such as dress, buildings, and machines, and its underlying "spirit," meaning the knowledge, independence, and public virtue of the population. Mere imitation of externals, he warned, would not produce a strong nation. At the same time he treated civilization as relative, a matter of stages through which nations passed, with Japan in an intermediate position, and he was explicit that national independence was the purpose for which civilization was valued. This is why Fukuzawa cannot be classed as a simple admirer of the West: he praised Western learning while insisting that the goal was an independent Japan. His reasoning also carried an imperialist edge, which became more pronounced in his later writing on Korea and China, a part of his legacy students should keep in view.

Section 58.10

Language, Translation, Literature, and the Making of Modern Expression

Translation was a central cultural activity of the Meiji period. Scholars and publishers rendered Western works on law, economics, science, and fiction into Japanese, and in doing so coined new vocabulary, many terms formed from Chinese characters that later spread into Chinese and Korean. Terms for concepts such as "society," "economics," and "philosophy" acquired their modern Japanese forms in this process. Translations of Smiles's Self-Help and Mill's On Liberty by Nakamura Masanao in the early 1870s were widely read, and popular writers satirized the new customs: the humorist Kanagaki Robun's Aguranabe (1871–72) poked fun at beef-pot diners and the fashions of civilization. Such texts show how the new culture was both promoted and laughed at.

Language itself became an object of reform and debate. Some proposed abolishing or limiting Chinese characters, replacing them with the phonetic kana or the Roman alphabet. Maejima Hisoka had advocated limiting characters even before the Restoration, and in the 1880s Roman-script societies promoted romanization. Mori Arinori, later minister of education, floated the idea of adopting English in the early 1870s, a proposal that was widely criticized. None of these proposals prevailed, and the mixed system of characters and kana remained. These debates reveal how deeply some reformers associated language with national modernization, and how divisive such ideas were.

The closer alignment of written and spoken Japanese, called genbun itchi ("unification of speech and writing"), developed gradually. Formal writing used a classical style distinct from speech, while popular fiction used colloquial elements. Tsubouchi Shōyō's Shōsetsu shinzui (1885–86) argued for a realistic novel focused on human psychology, and Futabatei Shimei's Ukigumo (1887–89) is often regarded as an important early experiment in colloquial prose. Others, such as Yamada Bimyō, experimented with different endings, while writers like Mori Ōgai, who had studied in Germany, continued to write in literary styles. The process took decades, and a standard colloquial written style was not established until the early twentieth century. It must therefore be understood as a gradual, contested development, not a single reform.

Translation and print also changed who could participate in cultural life. Newspapers, magazines, and cheap books reached larger audiences, and readers in cities and some villages gained access to news and ideas. Literacy, already substantial under the Tokugawa, expanded further with schooling (Chapter 55). Yet access was unequal by class, gender, and region, and censorship laws restricted political and sometimes literary expression. A shared written language also became a tool for creating a national audience, which linked language reform to the cultural construction of the nation. The Ainu and Ryukyuan languages were excluded from this national project, and later policies actively suppressed them in schools.

Section 58.11

Music, Theater, and the Adaptation of Performance Traditions

Western military music entered Japan first. Bands were organized in the army and navy, and instructors such as the British bandmaster John William Fenton trained Japanese musicians around 1869. Fenton is credited with composing an early melody, in 1870, for the text of "Kimigayo," which comes from an older poem. A new melody, composed by court musicians and harmonized by the German bandmaster Franz Eckert, replaced it in 1880, an example of selective borrowing: a Japanese text and court-musical melody in a Western harmonic setting. Military bands played at ceremonies, parades, and public parks, spreading Western instruments and scales through public performance.

The school system became the major vehicle for Western-style music. In 1879 the Ministry of Education established the Music Investigation Committee under Isawa Shūji, which worked with the American music educator Luther Whiting Mason. It produced the first school songbook in 1881, a collection of shōka (school songs) set to Western melodies, some borrowed from existing Western songs with new Japanese texts. The Tokyo Music School developed from this committee in 1887. School songs taught children shared melodies and also carried moral and civic messages, making music part of the building of a national citizenry (Chapter 55). Traditional Japanese music, such as koto, shamisen, and gagaku, continued, and tensions between Western and Japanese musical education persisted. The shift was uneven: most rural people encountered Western-style music chiefly through school.

Theater was reorganized by commercial and political pressures. Kabuki, the leading popular theater of the Edo period, faced the loss of its earlier patrons and rules, the end of Edo-period restrictions on theater locations, and new competition. Under the leadership of actors such as Ichikawa Danjūrō IX and managers such as Morita Kan'ya XII, who reopened the Shintomi-za theater in 1878, kabuki sought respectability and new audiences. Danjūrō's "living history" plays (katsureki-geki) aimed at greater historical accuracy, and some plays treated contemporary civilization-and-enlightenment themes (zangiri-mono). A theater-reform movement in 1886 argued that drama should be refined. In 1887 a performance of kabuki before the emperor at Inoue Kaoru's residence was celebrated as a milestone of the art's new prestige. Meanwhile new genres appeared, such as shinpa ("new school") drama, which emerged in the late 1880s and 1890s and addressed contemporary subjects. The Kabuki-za, opened in 1889, symbolized the new institutional setting. Kabuki's refinement and its association with a "national theater" were products of these decades and not timeless features of the form. Chapter 65 develops the later growth of film and mass entertainment.

Section 58.12

Western Painting, Nihonga, Art Preservation, and the Reinvention of Japanese Aesthetics

The concept of "fine art" itself was new. The Japanese word bijutsu was formed in the early 1870s, in connection with preparations for Japan's participation in the 1873 Vienna World Exposition, as a translation of a Western category. Before then, painting, calligraphy, lacquer, ceramics, and sculpture were understood in terms of schools, workshops, and social contexts, not as a single category of art. The Meiji state's participation in international expositions made art and craft into an export and a national representation. It also generated demand for objects judged as "Japanese" by foreign audiences, an early link between commerce and identity. At the same time, the upheavals of the Restoration, including the anti-Buddhist campaigns of 1868 and the loss of daimyo patronage, endangered temple collections and traditional workshops.

The government responded with preservation measures. In 1871 it issued an edict directing the protection of old objects, and in 1872 a survey of temple and shrine treasures was carried out. Later, in 1897, the Ancient Shrines and Temples Preservation Law gave legal form to a protective approach. The American scholar Ernest Fenollosa, who arrived in 1878 to teach at Tokyo University, became an influential advocate of Japanese painting. In an 1882 lecture to the Ryūchikai art society, he argued that Japanese painting deserved higher esteem than Western-style painting. He worked with the young official Okakura Kakuzō and, in 1884, with others, examined sacred images at Hōryūji. Their efforts, with those of Kanō Hōgai and Hashimoto Gahō, helped shape a modern painting often called nihonga.

Western-style painting (yōga) had an independent path. Students such as Takahashi Yuichi trained in oil techniques, and the government established the Technical Fine Arts School (Kōbu Bijutsu Gakkō) in 1876 with Italian instructors, including Antonio Fontanesi. In the 1880s, however, official favor shifted against yōga amid the cultural reaction described in Section 58.18, and the school closed in 1883. The Tokyo Fine Arts School, planned in 1887 and opened in 1889 with Okakura as a leading figure, initially taught Japanese art only; a Western painting department was added in 1896 under Kuroda Seiki, who had studied in Paris. In 1898 Okakura left the school and founded the Japan Art Institute with colleagues. These episodes show that the categories "Japanese-style" and "Western-style" were sharpened through institutions, exhibitions, and rivalry, and that nihonga was not simply a continuation of earlier painting. It drew on Kanō-school and other earlier traditions but also incorporated new ideas of perspective, subject, and national significance.

The preservation and display of art also created a national heritage. Imperial museums collected and presented objects as the legacy of the nation, selecting what counted. Some works were elevated, while others, such as folk crafts and popular prints, were undervalued, a hierarchy later challenged by movements such as Mingei (Chapter 72). Ainu and Ryukyuan material culture was collected and displayed as ethnographic or regional material within this national framework, not as part of its own artistic history. Students should therefore treat Meiji art institutions as both achievements of preservation and acts of definition.

Historical Vocabulary
Nihonga (日本画) and yōga (洋画)

Nihonga ("Japanese painting") and yōga ("Western-style painting") are categories that became fixed in the Meiji period. Yōga designates oil and related painting in a Western manner. Nihonga names a modern painting based on Japanese materials and traditions such as mineral pigments, ink, and silk, but adapted and reorganized in the context of national art institutions.

The distinction matters because it shows how art was divided into "native" and "foreign" camps in a period when artists borrowed freely. Nihonga should not be read as unchanged continuity with Edo painting; it was a modern formation shaped by Fenollosa, Okakura, exhibitions, and teaching institutions.

Division IV — From Classical Martial Arts to Modern Budō

Section 58.13

The Crisis of Samurai Martial Traditions after the Restoration

Martial arts under the Tokugawa were taught in schools called ryūha, each with its own techniques, lineage, and transmission rules, in domain academies and private dōjō. The Tokugawa order had turned most samurai into administrators, and swordsmanship, spearmanship, archery, and unarmed grappling arts (jūjutsu and related forms) had become partly disciplines of physical and moral cultivation as well as martial skill. By the late shogunate, some schools had adopted protective equipment and bamboo practice swords, and the bakufu's Kōbusho (founded 1856) had promoted instruction. These practices were not a single "samurai code" but a diverse set of institutions. Students should avoid a timeless image of the samurai (Chapter 21 and Chapter 43 trace the changing warrior role).

The Restoration and the abolition of the domains and the warrior status order (Chapter 54) placed martial schools in crisis. Domain patronage ended, stipends were commuted and then eliminated, the right to wear swords was prohibited in 1876, and conscription replaced hereditary military service. Many instructors lost income, students declined, and some teachers turned to other work. Contemporary accounts and later memoirs describe a period in which classical martial arts seemed obsolete. The government's emphasis on Western-style drill and weapons reinforced this impression. The loss was not only economic but conceptual: with hereditary warrior service gone, what were martial arts for?

Survival strategies varied. Some masters taught privately, taught to police or schools, or moved into other trades. Some schools continued small-scale instruction in the provinces. Others sought public audiences. In 1873 the swordsman Sakakibara Kenkichi organized paid public sword exhibitions (gekiken kōgyō), in which skilled fencers staged matches before ticket-buying crowds in Tokyo, an example of martial skill adapting to the commercial entertainment market. Critics saw such shows as a degradation of the martial tradition; supporters saw them as a way to preserve skills and support teachers. The episode shows how quickly martial culture had to find new institutions and justifications.

The Satsuma Rebellion of 1877, in which former samurai rose against the government and were defeated by a conscript army (Chapter 54), is a dividing line. It demonstrated the military superiority of the modern army and ended large-scale warrior armed resistance. It also created an opening: government police units used trained swordsmen against rebel forces, and the experience encouraged officials to consider sword skills for police and for physical and moral training. The classical schools did not simply vanish; they were reinterpreted, sometimes by teachers who adapted techniques to new settings. The next sections follow the two best-documented paths of reorganization: jūdō and kendō.

Section 58.14

Kanō Jigorō and the Founding of Kōdōkan Jūdō

Kanō Jigorō was born in 1860 in Mikage, in present-day Hyōgo Prefecture, and came to Tokyo as a boy. A small young man of scholarly background, he studied English and German, entered the Tokyo school system, and graduated from Tokyo Imperial University (then the Tokyo University) in 1881. As a student he sought training in jūjutsu, the grappling and throwing arts that had declined sharply. He studied under teachers of the Tenjin Shin'yō-ryū, notably Fukuda Hachinosuke and later Iso Masatomo, and then under Iikubo Tsunetoshi of the Kitō-ryū, drawing on multiple traditions. Kanō's biography shows that the founder of modern jūdō was himself a product of the new education: a university graduate and teacher who looked at martial training through the lens of pedagogy.

In 1882 Kanō opened a small dōjō, the Kōdōkan, at the Eishōji temple in Tokyo's Shitaya district, with a few students and a modest practice space. He reorganized what he had learned. He emphasized randori, free practice in which partners attempted throws and holds under rules, and kata, prearranged forms that preserved technical principles. He de-emphasized or restricted techniques considered dangerous for free practice, such as strikes and certain joint locks, so that students could train vigorously with less risk of injury. He applied systematic teaching methods and a graded ranking system, and he stressed moral and educational aims: the cultivation of body, mind, and character. Later, in the early twentieth century, he articulated principles summarized as maximum efficient use of energy and mutual welfare and benefit. These formulations belong mainly to later decades and should not be read back into 1882.

The new name, jūdō ("the way of gentleness"), signaled a conceptual shift from jūjutsu ("the art of gentleness") toward a method of education. Kanō was not alone in using such language, and the line between old and new was not absolute. Kōdōkan's growth was helped by contests with other jūjutsu schools, including a widely told account of a Metropolitan Police tournament in the mid-1880s in which Kōdōkan fighters performed strongly. Such stories are part of the movement's historical memory and are best treated as traditional accounts requiring caution. Kanō also served as an educator, including as headmaster at the Tokyo Higher Normal School from 1893, and he worked to bring jūdō into schools. In 1911 the Ministry of Education permitted jūdō and kendō as optional elements in middle-school physical education, and they became compulsory in 1931, developments reaching beyond the chapter's period but showing how Meiji innovation led to institutional adoption.

Historical Vocabulary
Jūjutsu, jūdō, randori, and kata

Jūjutsu is a general term for a range of unarmed and short-weapon grappling arts practiced by many separate schools. Jūdō, as established by Kanō Jigorō at the Kōdōkan from 1882, was a reorganized system that emphasized throwing and ground techniques, graded training, and educational purpose. Randori is free practice under rules; kata are prearranged forms that transmit technique and principle.

The distinction between jūjutsu and jūdō is real but not a clean break. Kanō drew from existing ryūha, and some schools used the term jūdō before him. Historians also caution against describing classical jūjutsu as a single "ancient art." Many jūjutsu schools were themselves relatively recent Tokugawa creations. The achievement of Kōdōkan lay in its pedagogical system, institutions, and later international spread.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Classical Martial Traditions and Their Modern Successors

What Changed

  • The social base moved from hereditary warrior households to students, police, schoolboys, and urban practitioners.
  • Purposes shifted from battlefield preparation and domain service toward education, physical culture, discipline, and later national moral training.
  • Organization shifted from secretive lineages toward graded ranks, shared rules, competitions, and national institutions.
  • Rules of safety, free practice, and standardization shaped technique.

What Stayed the Same

  • Many techniques, forms, and teachers derived from older ryūha.
  • The dōjō, teacher-student relations, ritual etiquette, and emphasis on character cultivation persisted, though often re-articulated.
  • The idea that martial practice should shape the person, already present in Tokugawa discourse, continued.

Who experienced change most directly? Former samurai teachers faced loss of status and income; they were most affected. Younger students and school-based practitioners experienced the new forms as normal. Who experienced greater continuity? Small provincial schools and some classical lineages preserved older forms with less change. Why does the pattern matter? It shows that modern budō is neither unchanged survival nor wholesale invention.

Section 58.15

From Kenjutsu and Gekiken toward Modern Kendō

Swordsmanship was the most prominent martial art of the samurai age and the one most directly affected by the loss of the warrior order. Before the Restoration, schools of kenjutsu taught sword technique through forms and, increasingly from the eighteenth century, through practice with bamboo swords (shinai) and protective equipment (bōgu), developments associated with names such as Naganuma Kunisato and Nakanishi Chūzō. This "gekiken" ("striking sword") practice allowed vigorous contests without fatal injury. Not all schools adopted it, and some classical lineages objected to what they regarded as sport at the expense of form, a tension that remained central to later sword culture.

After 1868 and especially after the 1876 prohibition on wearing swords, sword training lost its social anchor. Sakakibara Kenkichi's paid exhibitions in 1873 drew attention but also drew criticism. A decisive change came with the Satsuma Rebellion of 1877, in which police units drawn from experienced swordsmen fought rebels. Afterward, the Metropolitan Police Department incorporated sword training for its officers, and by around 1879 had established regular instruction led by experienced teachers. Swordsmen such as Yamaoka Tesshū, a former bakufu retainer and a figure in the early Meiji court, founded dōjō and taught a combination of technique and spiritual discipline. Police sword training provided income and prestige to teachers, created a link between martial skill and state service, and produced competitions among styles. This was a hybrid process: the practitioners were often former samurai, but the context was a modern civil institution.

Students should be careful about terminology. In the 1870s and 1880s, people spoke of kenjutsu, gekiken, and kenpō, and the word kendō, though it existed earlier, did not become the standard name until later; the Butokukai adopted it officially in 1919. The 1912 establishment of standardized sword forms (Dai Nippon Teikoku Kendō Kata) belongs to the early twentieth century and shows how long standardization took. Treating the 1870s as the age of "kendō" as it is known today projects later institutions backward. The Meiji decades were instead a period of experimentation: competitions between schools, local police tournaments, and debates between emphasizing forms and emphasizing free contest. Kendō's modern identity, with its standard equipment, rules, ranking, and role in school curricula, emerged across decades and under changing political conditions.

The social setting of sword training also changed. Practitioners included former samurai, police officers, students, and, increasingly, commoners who now had access to martial training once largely reserved for warriors. This democratization coexisted with an effort to link the sword to the moral ideal of the samurai, a connection that grew stronger in the 1890s. The practice of swordsmanship thus became both more accessible and more ideologically charged.

Section 58.16

The Dai Nippon Butokukai and the Institutionalization of Martial Traditions

The Dai Nippon Butokukai ("Greater Japan Martial Virtue Association") was founded in Kyoto in 1895, in the context of celebrations of the 1,100th anniversary of the founding of Heian-kyō and the Fourth National Industrial Exposition held there that year. Backed by government officials and imperial family members, the organization aimed to preserve and promote traditional martial arts, to gather practitioners, and to hold displays and tournaments. A Butokuden hall was built near the Heian Shrine, and the association organized annual events. Its first president was a prince of the imperial family. It grew over time into a large national organization with branches across the country and millions of members by the early twentieth century, though the figures need care because organizational claims were sometimes inflated.

The Butokukai's significance lay in standardization and legitimation. It created systems of ranks and titles, organized competitions, and sponsored schools for training instructors, including a martial arts training school established in the early 1900s. By bringing together previously separate lineages under a national umbrella, it encouraged shared standards, and it linked martial culture to schools, police, and the military. It also promoted a particular ideology: the idea of martial virtue as a part of national character, closely connected to the language of bushidō. The term "bushidō" had been used before the Meiji period but was extensively reinterpreted in the 1890s and after, when writers presented it as the ethical code of the samurai and of Japan. Nitobe Inazō's Bushido: The Soul of Japan, published in English in 1900, was an influential statement, written for foreign readers and itself a synthesis rather than a transcript of historical samurai values. Historians now stress that a single, timeless "bushidō code" is a modern construction.

The Butokukai also shows the growing role of nationalism in martial culture. In the 1890s, after the Sino-Japanese War (Chapter 60), martial arts were increasingly connected with military preparedness and loyalty. Later, in the 1930s and wartime years, the organization became closely tied to state mobilization (Chapter 77) and was dissolved under the Allied occupation (Chapter 82). For the present chapter, the important point is the early institutionalization: in the 1890s martial arts moved from scattered, often struggling lineages to a state-supported national cultural institution with ideological aims. Chapter 58 therefore treats the Butokukai as part of the reinvention of tradition, and it should not be read as evidence that the organization's later militarism was already complete in 1895.

Cause and Effect

Abolition of the Warrior Order, Institutional Adaptation, and Modern Budō

Long-term causes

Tokugawa peace had already transformed martial arts into forms of education and cultivation; bamboo-sword practice, schools, and published manuals created a base for later reorganization.

Short-term causes

Abolition of domains and warrior status, the end of stipends, conscription, the 1876 sword prohibition, and the 1877 Satsuma Rebellion deprived martial teachers of patrons and purposes.

Event or transformation

Teachers adapted by performing for audiences, teaching police and schools, founding new institutions such as the Kōdōkan, and eventually joining national organizations such as the Butokukai.

Immediate effects

Martial practice became more urban and more accessible, was organized into graded systems and competitions, and was increasingly linked to education and state institutions.

Long-term consequences

Modern jūdō and kendō became school subjects and later international or national sports; budō ideology could be mobilized for nationalism in the early twentieth century, and the postwar occupation restricted and then allowed their revival.

Historical complexity

The change cannot be reduced to one cause such as Westernization or nationalism. Domestic politics, educational ideas, police needs, commercial entertainment, individual teachers, and international interest all contributed.
Section 58.17

Naginata, Archery, Sumō, Women's Training, and the New Physical Culture

Other martial and athletic traditions underwent comparable transformations. Archery (kyūjutsu, later kyūdō), once a central warrior skill, survived as a ceremonial and educational discipline with schools of different lineages, and its modern standardization came mostly after the period covered here. The naginata, a polearm historically associated with warriors and, in some households, women of the warrior class, was practiced by some women of samurai families before the Restoration. In the later Meiji period it was introduced into girls' schools as physical and moral education, though its wider adoption belongs mainly to the early twentieth century. Women's participation in martial arts was shaped by the new ideals of womanhood described earlier: training in graceful, disciplined activity could be presented as consistent with the role of the "good wife, wise mother," even as it echoed a warrior heritage.

Sumō had been supported by daimyo and shrines and organized in the Edo and Osaka professional groups. The end of domain patronage placed wrestlers in a difficult position, and sumō faced a period of uncertainty in the early Meiji years, as professional wrestlers struggled with income, public reputation, and the 1870s hostility to some "old customs." Its fortunes improved when it received renewed elite support, including an imperial viewing of sumō in 1884, which gave the sport prestige, and it adopted a more formal organization. The modern association, the permanent hall (Kokugikan, 1909), and the idea of sumō as a "national sport" came later. As with other practices, sumō's ritual elements were maintained and elaborated in these years, and presenting it as an unchanged ancient ritual overlooks this modern shaping.

Alongside martial traditions, Western physical education and sport entered Japan. The government invited foreign teachers and set up gymnastics training; an American, George Adams Leland, directed the Gymnastics Institute (Taisō Denshūjo) from 1878 to train teachers in physical education. Military-style drill was incorporated into schools in the later 1880s under the policies of Mori Arinori (Chapter 55). Baseball was introduced in the early 1870s, with Horace Wilson often credited with teaching it in Tokyo, and clubs formed in the late 1870s; rowing, athletics, tennis, and later football and rugby followed. Annual school athletic meetings (undōkai) began in the 1870s and became a distinctive part of school life. By the end of the century "physical culture" included both Western sports and reorganized Japanese martial arts, and the two coexisted rather than replacing one another. Both served ideals of national strength and disciplined bodies that the state and many educators embraced, but access differed. Boys' and men's education received more attention than girls', rural communities had fewer facilities, and the fit and military-ready male body was the main object of national concern.

Section 58.18

Resistance to Westernization and the Search for Cultural Balance

Criticism of Westernization emerged almost immediately and intensified in the 1880s. Its sources were varied. Some critics were Confucian scholars who valued older moral teachings. Some belonged to the rural elite or religious communities who disliked the cost and cultural effects of reform. Others were younger intellectuals who had themselves been educated in Western learning but considered the Rokumeikan style superficial and politically submissive. The failure of Inoue Kaoru's treaty-revision proposals in 1887 and the widespread sense that Japan was being mocked by foreigners fed a reaction. Government leaders, too, concluded that cultural policy needed more emphasis on national cohesion, and the Imperial Rescript on Education (1890; see Chapter 55) expressed a moral program that drew on Confucian language and imperial loyalty.

The reaction took intellectual form in the 1880s and 1890s. Nishimura Shigeki, a former Meirokusha member, published a work on Japanese morality in 1886 emphasizing Confucian and indigenous ethics. A group of younger writers formed the Seikyōsha in 1888 and published the journal Nihonjin, advocating preservation of "national essence" (kokusui hozon), including figures such as Miyake Setsurei and Shiga Shigetaka. Kuga Katsunan's newspaper Nihon (1889) expressed similar views. These writers did not reject modernization; most accepted science, industry, and constitutional government. They argued instead that Japan should select what to adopt based on its own character, and that borrowing without cultural self-confidence would produce dependence. Tokutomi Sohō, by contrast, began as an advocate of a commoner-centered, peaceful modernization and later shifted toward nationalism and imperial expansion. The debate was therefore not a simple contest between pro-Western and anti-Western camps.

Resistance also occurred outside elite discussion. Villagers resisted calendar changes, meat eating, new school fees, and the hygiene campaigns described earlier; religious communities resisted some state religious policy (Chapter 55). Ainu and Ryukyuan communities faced assimilation policies they could not fully resist but did not simply accept; in Hokkaido and Okinawa people maintained languages, customs, and identities in the face of pressure, subjects taken up in later chapters. Popular culture, including satirical prints and fiction, mocked the new fashions. Selective adoption was a form of agency: people took what was useful or attractive and ignored what was not.

Historical Debate

Was Meiji Westernization Imitation, Pragmatic Adaptation, or a Transformation of Japanese Culture?

The question: How should historians characterize the early Meiji adoption of foreign practices, and what does the characterization imply about Japanese agency and cultural change?

Interpretation A — Imitation under pressure: Some scholars, and many contemporaries, emphasized that foreign threat and unequal treaties pushed leaders to copy the West in order to be taken seriously. On this view much of bunmei kaika, especially the Rokumeikan, was display aimed at foreign powers and elite rivals, and its superficiality explains the backlash of the 1880s.
Interpretation B — Pragmatic adaptation and transformation: Other scholars stress selective borrowing, hybridity, and Japanese agency. Giyōfū architecture, yōshoku, school songs, the reorganization of martial arts, and the creation of nihonga show people adapting foreign forms and redefining Japanese ones, producing something new. On this view the language of "Westernization" understates the creativity and the importance of domestic motives.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on government decrees, diplomatic records, prints and photographs, intellectual writings, surviving buildings, and martial and artistic institutions. A may rely on diplomatic motives and criticism; B on the evidence of hybrid practices and everyday adaptation. Many historians combine the views, noting that motives and outcomes differed by domain, class, and period.

Why it matters: If Westernization is understood only as imitation, Japanese agency and the continuity of earlier traditions are minimized. If it is understood only as adaptation, the coercive pressure of the treaty system, the unequal costs, and the exclusion of groups such as the Ainu may be minimized.

Student task: Choose one example from this chapter, such as the Rokumeikan, giyōfū architecture, Kōdōkan jūdō, or nihonga, and explain which interpretation it supports most strongly and what evidence supports your judgment.

Section 58.19

Inventing Modern Japanese Tradition: Continuity, Nationalism, and Cultural Identity

The historians Eric Hobsbawm and Terence Ranger argued in The Invention of Tradition (1983) that many practices presented as ancient were actually created or formalized in the recent past, often in connection with the needs of modern nation-states. Scholars of Japan, including the contributors to Stephen Vlastos's edited volume Mirror of Modernity: Invented Traditions of Modern Japan (1998) and Carol Gluck's work on Meiji ideology, applied the idea to Japanese history. It is a useful lens, with caution. "Invented" does not mean fabricated from nothing. Meiji people drew on existing materials, but they selected, reorganized, standardized, and gave them new meanings, often claiming age and continuity that the particular form did not have.

Several examples from this chapter and elsewhere illustrate the process. In 1873 the government created a national holiday, Kigensetsu, to mark the legendary accession of Emperor Jimmu, calculated from the chronology of the Nihon Shoki, a mythological source that students should treat as a later textual tradition (Chapter 8). The emperor's public image, with its uniforms, portraits, and ceremonies, was constructed through photographs, engravings, and tours. The preservation of antiquities and the creation of nihonga produced a modern idea of national art. The Shinto-style wedding, as noted, acquired its widely recognized form in the early twentieth century. Sumō's ritual presentation, the kata-based structure of jūdō, the ranking and ideological framework of the Butokukai, and the rhetoric of bushidō all show similar combinations of older elements with new organization. State Shinto (Chapter 55) was another major case. None of this makes these practices inauthentic. It means that "authenticity" is a historical product, and that traditions live by being adapted.

The process also had exclusions. National tradition was defined largely from the perspective of the central state, the male elite, and the Wajin majority. Ainu customs were suppressed as "uncivilized," and Hokkaido development proceeded on land taken from Ainu communities. The Ryukyu Kingdom, annexed in 1879 as Okinawa Prefecture (Chapter 54 and later chapters), was brought into the national narrative while its distinct language, court culture, and political history were subordinated. Women were often addressed as guardians of tradition within the household while having little voice in defining it. Burakumin and other stigmatized groups were nominally emancipated but were left out of the dominant image of the nation. A comprehensive account of cultural identity must therefore note who wrote the definition and who was defined.

The central lesson is that tradition and modernization were intertwined. The Meiji state and society adopted new institutions and technologies, and in the same decades created or reorganized cultural forms that were presented as expressions of an unchanging Japan. The relationship between borrowing and identity-making, so evident in this period, recurs in later chapters on empire, mass culture, and postwar reconstruction.

Section 58.20

Chapter Review and Historical Thinking Checkpoint

Before proceeding to Chapter 59, which turns to the constitutional order and the Imperial Diet, students should be able to explain how cultural change in the early Meiji decades fits together with the political and economic changes studied in earlier chapters.

The core insights of this chapter are as follows. Bunmei kaika was a movement of ideas, fashion, and policy that aimed to acquire Western knowledge and practice, motivated partly by treaty-revision diplomacy and national independence. Its effects were uneven across city and countryside, class, gender, and region. Everyday life changed through selective additions, not wholesale replacement. Urban and architectural projects such as the Ginza and the Rokumeikan displayed ambition and revealed limits, while giyōfū buildings reveal Japanese craftsmen's creativity. Intellectual debates, represented by Fukuzawa and the Meirokusha, treated civilization as a means to independence. Literature, music, theater, and painting were transformed through translation, institutions, and redefinition, including the sharpening of the nihonga/yōga distinction. The collapse of the warrior order forced martial traditions to reorganize; Kōdōkan jūdō, police-linked sword training, and the Butokukai illustrate both continuity and innovation, and later terminology and institutions should not be projected backward. Finally, criticism of Westernization and the invention of tradition show that the Meiji state and society were creating a national culture that included some people and marginalized others.

Students should be able to answer: What did people gain and lose through cultural change? Who decided what was "Japanese"? How did martial arts, art, and ritual acquire their modern forms? And why does it matter that traditions can be both old and new at once?

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the cultural dimension of the Meiji transformation: how Japanese people experienced, adapted, resisted, and reinterpreted foreign influence between 1868 and about 1900. Its central argument is that bunmei kaika was neither a simple replacement of Japanese customs by Western ones nor a mere surface imitation, but a selective, unequal, and contested process through which new habits, institutions, and definitions of Japanese identity were created.

In everyday life, hairstyles, uniforms, food, hygiene, the calendar, and clocks changed, often first among officials, students, and urban residents. Government decrees established some changes, but commerce, fashion, and curiosity drove others. Many rural people and most women continued to wear kimono and eat largely traditional diets, and mixed forms were common. Gender norms were renegotiated through new etiquette, elite women's public roles, and a domestic ideal that combined Western and Confucian influences.

In architecture and urban space, the Ginza Bricktown and the Rokumeikan expressed state ambition and diplomatic motives. The Ginza was only partly realized, and the Rokumeikan faced criticism and had a short life, but both shaped imagery and elite practice. Giyōfū buildings and the training of Japanese architects under Josiah Conder show Japanese builders' agency. Prints, photographs, and newspapers made change visible while also shaping and sometimes exaggerating it.

Intellectually, Fukuzawa Yukichi and the Meirokusha treated civilization as a relative, staged process whose purpose was national independence, and they debated how to modernize without surrendering autonomy. Translation created new vocabulary, language reform debates produced experiments such as genbun itchi, and literature, music, theater, and painting were reorganized through new institutions. The distinction between nihonga and yōga, and the idea of a national art heritage, were products of these decades.

The crisis of samurai martial traditions after the Restoration led to adaptation. Kanō Jigorō reorganized jūjutsu into Kōdōkan jūdō in 1882, with an educational emphasis and new methods of free practice. Sword training moved from private lineages and public exhibitions into police and school settings, and modern kendō developed over decades, with terms and forms standardized mainly after 1900. The Dai Nippon Butokukai, founded in 1895, institutionalized and promoted martial culture with growing nationalist emphasis. Other practices, including naginata, archery, and sumō, also changed, as did physical education.

Resistance and debate accompanied these changes. The failure of Rokumeikan diplomacy and the cultural reaction of the late 1880s gave rise to arguments for preserving national essence, but most critics accepted modernization and sought selective adoption. Villagers, religious communities, and minority populations also resisted or negotiated change. The experience was not uniform: policies directed at the Ainu and later at Ryukyuans show that "civilization" could also be coercive.

The idea of invented tradition helps explain the paradox that Japan borrowed heavily from abroad while also elaborating images of an unchanging national culture. The Kigensetsu holiday, the imperial image, national art, martial ideology, and ritualized sports were assembled from older materials but given modern forms. This does not make them false, but it shows that traditions are historical products.

The historical debate over imitation versus adaptation shows that Meiji cultural change involved both pressure and creativity. Foreign threat and treaty diplomacy shaped choices, but Japanese actors determined what to adopt, transform, or reject. A fair account recognizes both.

These developments connect to wider themes. They link the political revolution to everyday experience, foreshadow the constitutional and imperial developments of Chapters 59 through 62, and prepare for later chapters on mass culture, modern cities, martial ideology in wartime, and postwar debates about identity.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter shows why the Meiji transformation cannot be understood solely through constitutions, ministries, and factories. Cultural change was where people lived the revolution: in how they dressed, ate, built, trained, and understood themselves. It also shows that culture was a field of power, in which officials, intellectuals, craftsmen, women, villagers, and minority communities had unequal capacity to shape outcomes.

The chapter's central lesson, that tradition and modernization were intertwined, has consequences far beyond the Meiji period. The processes by which martial arts, art, ritual, and symbols were reorganized and presented as the heritage of the nation would recur in later debates over empire, wartime ideology, postwar democracy, and contemporary culture. Students who understand how traditions are made are better prepared to read claims of unbroken continuity critically, without dismissing the real inheritance on which such traditions drew.

The chapter also connects Japan to the wider world. Bunmei kaika was shaped by Western powers, treaty diplomacy, and foreign advisers, but also by Chinese-character translations, Confucian ethics, and domestic traditions, and it fed into Japan's own assertion of equality and, increasingly, empire. At the same time, the exclusion and assimilation of the Ainu and Ryukyuans shows how a national culture was defined within the archipelago.

Chapter 59 turns to the constitutional framework that accompanied these cultural developments. The ideas about national identity, civilization, and tradition examined here formed part of the intellectual setting in which the Meiji Constitution and the Imperial Diet were created.
