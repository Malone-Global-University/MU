Part VII — Tokugawa Japan

Shogun, Emperor, and the Bakuhan Political System

Japanese History: From Jōmon Japan to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

Within about two months of the fall of Osaka Castle in the summer of 1615, the lords gathered at Fushimi were, by the standard account, read a set of thirteen articles. The articles said little about how to fight. They addressed how a warrior house should behave in peacetime: what to study, whom to marry, how to treat a castle wall, and what to do about a neighbor's suspicious activity. At nearly the same moment, a separate set of rules was issued for the imperial court and the court nobility. Together these texts reveal the central insight of this chapter: the Tokugawa did not secure peace simply by winning battles. They built a system of rules, ranks, obligations, and recognitions that led other powerful actors to accept a place within it.

Chapter 41 traced the road from the death of Toyotomi Hideyoshi through the crisis of succession, the battle of Sekigahara in 1600, Tokugawa Ieyasu's appointment as shogun in 1603, and the destruction of the Toyotomi house at Osaka in 1615. By the end of that story the Tokugawa were militarily dominant and no credible rival remained. But military dominance is not government. A victorious house still has to decide how hundreds of lords are to be ranked, how their loyalty is to be secured without directly administering their lands, how a dynasty is to be perpetuated across generations, and what is to be done with a court in Kyoto whose rank, titles, and ritual authority could not simply be ignored. This chapter examines how those questions were answered.

The answer historians call the bakuhan system, a compound of bakufu (the shogunal government) and han (the territories of the daimyō), was neither a modern centralized state nor a loose federation of sovereign territories. It was a layered political order. The shogunate claimed supremacy over the warrior elite, controlled a large block of directly held land and strategic cities, and set rules for other lords. The domains retained substantial authority to tax, adjudicate, and govern within their territories. The imperial court, stripped of practical governing power, remained a distinct source of rank, ritual authority, and legitimacy that the Tokugawa both regulated and relied upon. Stability depended less on abolishing regional power than on regulating the relationships among these centers of authority.

This chapter is therefore an institutional chapter, and it must be read with a division of historical labor in mind. It explains the structure of power: the shogun and the Tokugawa house, the offices of the bakufu, the direct shogunal holdings, the domains and their lords, the classifications of daimyō, the legal instruments that bound the warrior houses, the instruments of reassignment and punishment, and the relationship between shogun and emperor. It does not attempt to describe the lives of the people who lived inside this structure. Samurai, peasants, artisans, merchants, households, outcaste communities, and gender relations belong to Chapter 43. Village administration, agriculture, famine, and protest belong to Chapter 44. Roads, sankin-kōtai in its social and economic dimensions, Edo, Osaka, and merchant life belong to Chapter 45. Religion and the persecution of Christians belong to Chapter 47, and the controlled international world, including Ryukyu, Ezo, and the Ainu, to Chapter 48.

The evidence for this system is unusually rich and unusually official. Historians rely on the texts of the 1615 laws and their later revisions; on shogunal documents conferring territory and confirming succession; on the genealogical compilations commissioned by the bakufu in the 1640s; on directives issued by senior officials to domain lords; on the records of individual domains; on later chronicles of the Tokugawa house compiled in the early nineteenth century; and on diaries and records kept by court nobles in Kyoto. This evidence has strengths and a built-in bias. Much of it records what the bakufu wished to be the case, or how its officials justified their actions, and it speaks far less about how subordinates, local communities, or the imperial court perceived the same arrangements. Where possible this chapter tries to ask whose voice each source carries and whose is absent.

A caution about vocabulary is also in order. Words such as "feudal," "centralized," and "federal" are modern analytical labels borrowed from other histories. They are useful as comparisons but misleading if treated as descriptions. The same is true of "han," which is a convenient and conventional term but was not the ordinary label that contemporaries used for a lord's territory. And "emperor" translates a variety of terms, and a variety of roles, that changed across Japanese history. The aim of the chapter is not to fit the Tokugawa order into a prior category but to describe how it worked, and then to consider why historians disagree about what to call it.

By the chapter's end, students should understand how the Tokugawa converted victory into a durable political settlement by about the middle of the seventeenth century. They should be able to see why a system that left domains with so much governing capacity nonetheless proved remarkably stable, and why that stability did not mean uniformity. The chapter also sets the stage for the remainder of Part VII, which turns from the architecture of rule to the societies, economies, cultures, and regional worlds that operated inside it.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why military victory in 1600–1615 did not by itself create a functioning political system, and what problems the Tokugawa still had to solve.
  2. Define the bakuhan system and distinguish it from a modern centralized state, a feudal federation, and a collection of sovereign territories.
  3. Describe the principal offices and councils of the bakufu and explain how they developed over time rather than appearing fully formed in 1603.
  4. Distinguish direct shogunal holdings (tenryō) from the broader property of the Tokugawa house and from the domains of other lords.
  5. Analyze how domains functioned as governments and why daimyō retained real authority within Tokugawa supremacy.
  6. Explain the role of kokudaka in defining who counted as a daimyō and in ranking territorial lords.
  7. Compare shinpan, fudai, and tozama daimyō, and correct the common assumption that tozama lords were weak.
  8. Interpret the 1615 Laws for the Military Houses and the instruments of confiscation, transfer, reduction, hostage-taking, and castle regulation as mechanisms of governance.
  9. Assess the relationship between shogun and emperor, avoiding both "the emperor ruled" and "the emperor was irrelevant" simplifications.
  10. Evaluate competing historical interpretations of whether Tokugawa Japan was centralized or decentralized.
  11. Contextualize the Tokugawa settlement as one that produced political stability without social or regional uniformity.

Historical Context

Three legacies shaped what the Tokugawa could build. The first was the territorial state of the Sengoku era (Chapter 35). Warlords had learned to govern compact domains through castle towns, retainer bands, law codes, and systematic taxation, and the daimyō of the early seventeenth century were largely heirs to that tradition. The second was the unification program of Oda Nobunaga and Toyotomi Hideyoshi (Chapters 39–40), which had separated warriors from direct control of agricultural land, disarmed much of the peasantry, surveyed productivity, and assigned domains by assessed rice yield. The third was the Toyotomi fall. Hideyoshi's regime had failed to convert personal supremacy into a stable dynasty, and the Tokugawa had watched both the success and the failure at close range.

The Tokugawa also inherited the problem of the court. Since the twelfth century, warrior governments had existed alongside an imperial court in Kyoto that conferred rank and title, retained ritual functions, and possessed a prestige that no warrior could simply claim for himself. Ashikaga shoguns had dominated the court, and Oda Nobunaga and Hideyoshi had used court rank for their own purposes, Hideyoshi taking the title of regent (kanpaku). Ieyasu's own legitimacy rested on being named shogun (sei-i taishōgun) by the emperor in 1603. The Tokugawa thus needed the court even as they sought to prevent it from becoming an alternative political center.

Finally, the Tokugawa had to settle the ranks of men who had been their allies, rivals, or former equals. The redistribution of domains after Sekigahara, treated in Chapter 41, had divided territory among Tokugawa relatives, trusted retainers, and lords who had submitted only after the battle. But a redistribution is a moment, whereas a political order needs procedures: how a lord's succession is recognized, whether he may repair his castle, whom his children may marry, and what happens when he or his heir fails. Those procedures were still being created in 1615, and the first three shoguns (Ieyasu, Hidetada, and Iemitsu) spent most of the next four decades creating and testing them.

A final point about chronology: the period covered here runs from about 1615 to about 1651, from the end of the Toyotomi threat to the death of the third shogun, Iemitsu. It would be a mistake to project the mature administrative system of the eighteenth century back onto these decades. Many offices were improvised, renamed, or formalized only in the 1630s; many practices described in later sources as ancient custom were in fact the product of this formative generation.

Section 42.1

From Victory to Government: The Problem After 1615

When Osaka Castle fell in 1615 and Toyotomi Hideyori died, the Tokugawa faced a situation that victorious warlords had often failed to manage: how to turn supremacy into an institution that would outlast the winner. Ieyasu had, in effect, answered part of the question before the final campaign. He had resigned the office of shogun in 1605 in favor of his son Hidetada while continuing to direct affairs from his retirement, a signal that the position would pass within the Tokugawa house and not be bestowed anew on whichever general proved strongest. Chapter 41 treated this as a statement about dynasty. This chapter treats it as the first move in a longer process of building a government that did not depend on a single person.

The problems that remained were practical and urgent. Several hundred lords held domains of very different sizes, and many had been Ieyasu's equals a generation earlier. Some had fought for the Tokugawa at Sekigahara; others had fought against them or joined late. Large numbers of warriors had lost their employers in the wars and the reassignments of land, and a population of masterless samurai (rōnin) represented a continuing source of potential disorder. The imperial court had its own claims on prestige. And Ieyasu was in his seventies; he died in 1616. In this setting the Tokugawa needed rules more than they needed further victories.

The three generations that follow, Ieyasu, Hidetada, and Iemitsu, were not three versions of the same ruler. Ieyasu, whose posthumous cult at Nikkō is discussed in Chapter 47, set the initial terms. Hidetada, who held the title from 1605 to 1623 and remained influential until his death in 1632, enforced them with notable severity against several major houses. Iemitsu, shogun from 1623 to 1651, completed much of the machinery of government, formalized obligations that had been informal, and, by the end of his life, presided over a system in which the shogun's personal commands were increasingly supported by routine administrative procedure. The mid-seventeenth-century test of the system's maturity was the succession of Iemitsu's young son Ietsuna in 1651. A child shogun could hold the office, and the machinery continued, because by then the offices and rules did not depend on an adult commander's personal authority.

This chapter does not retell the campaigns of Sekigahara or Osaka; those belong to Chapter 41. It begins at the point where the question shifts from "who won?" to "how will this be governed?" and it proceeds by taking the institutional pieces in turn: first the concept of the bakuhan system itself, then the shogun and the Tokugawa house, then the machinery of the bakufu, then the territories, the lords, the laws that bound them, and finally the court.

Section 42.2

What Was the Bakuhan System?

Historians use the term bakuhan (or bakuhan taisei, "bakuhan system") for the political order that combined the shogunal government, the bakufu, with the territorial domains, han, of the daimyō. The word is a modern scholarly construction, a compound of the first syllables of the two terms, and it describes a relationship, not a single institution. The bakufu was the central government of the warrior class and the administrator of the lands the Tokugawa controlled directly. The domains were the territories governed by other lords. The bakuhan system was the set of arrangements that linked the two: who recognized whom, who could command whom, who owed what to whom, and who controlled which resources.

It is easier to say what this system was not than to give a short positive definition. It was not a modern centralized state. There was no uniform national tax administered by a central bureaucracy, no national army, no standard system of local administration, and no concept of citizens equally subject to a single legal code. It was not an absolutist monarchy in which a ruler directly governed subjects through appointed officials. Nor was it a collection of hundreds of sovereign states: domain lords could not wage war on one another, make independent foreign policy, build castles at will, or ignore shogunal summonses. Their authority existed within a framework of obligations and permissions the shogunate defined, and the bakufu could and did confiscate or reassign territories. Authority was layered and unequal, and the key to understanding Tokugawa politics is to trace how the layers were connected.

The vocabulary used in the period itself reflected this layering. The shogunal government commonly referred to itself with terms conveying public authority, such as kōgi, while a lord's territory was often described in ordinary usage as a country (kuni) or by reference to the lord's house. The word han was borrowed from Chinese political vocabulary and was used by some scholars and writers in the period, but it became an official designation of the domains only at the very end of the Tokugawa era, when the new Meiji government adopted it before abolishing the domains altogether (Chapter 54). For convenience this textbook uses "domain" and han for the lords' territories, but students should not imagine that each lord governed a standardized unit called a han in the modern textbook sense.

Historical Vocabulary
Bakufu, Bakuhan, and Han

Bakufu ("tent government") originally referred to the headquarters of a general in the field and came to mean the government of a shogun. In the Tokugawa period the bakufu was based in Edo and administered the shogun's direct holdings while supervising the warrior class as a whole. Han is the conventional English-language label for a daimyō's domain. Bakuhan, a modern scholarly coinage, names the combined system of bakufu and domains.

These labels help students describe the system, but they risk suggesting a tidy two-level structure that never quite existed. Domains differed in size from the minimum of 10,000 koku to more than a million, in the degree of bakufu supervision, and in their relationships with the center. The word "han" also did not carry its later official meaning for most of the period. The labels are shorthand for a relationship that was negotiated, enforced, and sometimes resisted in practice.

Section 42.3

The Shogun and the Tokugawa House

At the apex of the order stood the shogun, formally the "barbarian-quelling generalissimo" (sei-i taishōgun), an office conferred by the emperor and held by the head of the Tokugawa house. The office carried the authority to command the warrior class, to confer and confirm territorial holdings, and to speak for the government in its dealings with the court and with the outside world. Yet the shogun was never merely an individual. He was the head of a family whose survival was itself a political project, and the Tokugawa went to considerable lengths to ensure that the office did not leave the house.

The Tokugawa succession illustrates how a dynasty was built. After Ieyasu's retirement, the office passed to Hidetada, and then, in 1623, to Hidetada's son Iemitsu. Iemitsu's succession over a younger brother, Tadanaga, was contested within the family and the retainers' circle. Tadanaga was later ordered into confinement in 1631, his holdings were confiscated in 1632, and he died by compelled suicide in 1633; sources attribute his fall to misconduct and political conflict, and it should not be read simply as punishment for rivalry over the succession. The episode nonetheless shows that even the shogun's closest relatives could be disciplined and dispossessed, and Iemitsu's succession asserted the principle that the designated heir should succeed. To provide for the possibility that the main line might fail, Ieyasu established branch houses headed by his sons, those of Owari, Kii, and Mito, later called the "Three Houses" (gosanke), from which a successor could be drawn. Other branches, often bearing the Matsudaira name that the Tokugawa had used before Ieyasu's rise, held domains as well and provided a reservoir of kinsmen.

It is important to distinguish the individual shogun from the bakufu as an administrative structure. Ieyasu and Hidetada governed in a relatively personal manner, often through trusted advisors, while Iemitsu's later years saw the consolidation of a council-based system in which the shogun approved or confirmed decisions prepared by senior officials. The point should not be overstated. The shogun remained the ultimate source of authority, and strong personalities could still dominate policy. But the long-term trend was toward an institutionalized government in which offices, procedures, and precedents mattered more and in which the shogun, even when weak or very young, remained indispensable as a symbol and as the formal source of legitimacy. This distinction between the man and the office is part of why the system could survive a succession crisis and why later shoguns of very different capacity could occupy the same position.

The shogun also had a body of direct personal retainers. Those of higher rank who had the right to an audience with the shogun were known as hatamoto; those without that right, as gokenin. Both held stipends or small landholdings below the 10,000-koku threshold that defined a daimyō, and together they provided officials, guards, and military personnel for the bakufu. Their lives and status belong to Chapter 43; here it is sufficient to note that they formed the human base of the shogunal government, and that their loyalty was personal to the Tokugawa in a way the loyalty of the domains' retainers was not.

Section 42.4

The Bakufu as Government: Councils, Magistrates, and Officials

The bakufu's administrative structure was not designed at once. In the early seventeenth century it grew from the household and military organization of the Tokugawa house, and the offices that later appear in tidy charts emerged over several decades, often in response to specific problems. By the 1630s, under Iemitsu, the main outlines had become clearer, and later generations would elaborate them. Students should therefore avoid taking the eighteenth-century organization as a description of 1603 or even of 1615.

At the top of the administration stood the senior councillors, the rōjū, a small group, commonly four or five, drawn from fudai lords. The rōjū handled matters of general policy, relations with the daimyō, and relations with the court. On rare occasions, a single official of still higher stature, the tairō ("great elder"), was appointed; this was an exceptional rather than regular office. Below the senior councillors were the junior councillors, the wakadoshiyori, who dealt especially with the shogun's direct retainers, an office that took shape in the 1630s. Beside these councillors stood officials who performed a function of oversight. The ōmetsuke (grand inspectors) and the metsuke (inspectors) monitored the conduct of daimyō and of bakufu officials respectively, reporting irregularities to the council. These offices turned supervision into a routine function of government.

Below the councils were officials responsible for particular domains of administration. Magistrates (bugyō) handled the shogun's cities, such as the city magistrates of Edo (machi-bugyō), temple and shrine matters, finance, and other specialized fields. Intendants (daikan) and larger-district officials (gundai) administered the shogun's agricultural lands. Deputies stationed in Kyoto and other key points represented the bakufu in strategic places. First, access to the central council was restricted by classification: the senior councillor posts were reserved for fudai lords, a point examined in Section 42.8. Second, the system relied on a considerable degree of collective decision-making and of monitoring. Officials were supervised by inspectors; councils reviewed proposals; and shoguns confirmed decisions. This did not make the bakufu a bureaucracy in the modern sense, since offices were tied to hereditary houses and rank, but it did make it something more regularized than a warlord's household. The emergence of this structure in the 1630s, as the first generation of founders passed away, was one of the principal institutional achievements of the early Tokugawa period.

Section 42.5

Shogunal Lands, Cities, Mines, and Strategic Resources

A government needs resources, and the bakufu's most important resource was its direct territory. Historians commonly call the lands administered directly by the bakufu tenryō ("heavenly domain"); in the early seventeenth century, documents more commonly used terms such as goryō or bakuryō, and tenryō became the standard term later. As Chapter 41 explained, it is important to distinguish these lands, which supported the bakufu as an institution, from the broader holdings and property of the Tokugawa house, including the lands of retainers and of the related branch houses. The tenryō were the bakufu's own resource base, though the bakufu, the shogun, and the house were so closely intertwined in practice that the distinction was sometimes blurred.

Around the end of the Keichō era (1596–1615), direct shogunal holdings amounted to roughly two million koku of assessed productivity. They expanded through confiscations and transfers during the seventeenth century and exceeded four million by the late Genroku era (the early 1700s). Lands held by hatamoto and other direct retainers formed an additional substantial block. These figures are approximate, but they establish a crucial point: the shogunate was richer than any single domain, though it did not control the whole country's wealth. Even the largest domain, at about a million koku, was considerably smaller than the shogunate's direct holdings. This economic advantage was one foundation of the bakufu's supremacy.

The tenryō were scattered strategically and not blocked into a single territory. Large concentrations lay in the Kantō region around Edo, in central Japan, and in the Kinai region around Kyoto and Osaka, with smaller holdings elsewhere. The bakufu also held the great cities: Edo, Kyoto, Osaka, and, among others, the port of Nagasaki and the commercial city of Sakai. It held major mining districts, including important silver and gold producers such as Iwami and Sado, a significant source of precious metal. It also exercised control over coinage through authorized mints. Control of mines, ports, and currency was a form of political power because it supplied revenue and affected the supply of money across the whole country; the economic detail belongs to Chapter 45.

Rule over the tenryō was carried out by appointed officials, the intendants and magistrates introduced in the previous section, in contrast to domains, where lords governed through their own retainers. In the tenryō, the bakufu approached the situation of a direct territorial government, and the contrast between direct and indirect rule is part of what made the system layered. The shogunate also constructed and maintained great castles, notably Edo Castle, which was rebuilt and expanded in the early seventeenth century using labor and materials supplied by other lords, a point returned to in Section 42.12. Edo Castle itself became a statement of Tokugawa authority, the stage on which the order was ritually enacted.

Section 42.6

Domains: Government Within the Tokugawa Order

Outside the bakufu's direct holdings, much of the country was governed through daimyō domains, alongside hatamoto, court, temple, shrine, and other holdings. A domain was a functioning government, with a castle at its center, a retinue of warrior retainers, a body of laws, a system of finance, and officials who administered the territory. Within the limits set by the shogunate, a lord possessed considerable authority: he appointed and rewarded his retainers, issued domain laws, determined how revenues were collected and spent, judged most disputes and crimes in his territory, and represented the domain in dealings with the center. The daimyō's retainers, in turn, owed their loyalty primarily to their lord. This is the heart of the bakuhan relationship: the daimyō was simultaneously the shogun's subordinate and the ruler of his own territory.

The internal administration of domains varied, but there were common patterns. Senior retainers, often called karō (elders), served as the principal officials and advisors to the lord. Other officials oversaw finances, the castle town, the countryside, and law and order. Many domains gradually converted their retainers from holders of land to recipients of stipends paid out of the lord's revenues, which strengthened the lord's control over them, a process that built on the Sengoku and Toyotomi separation of warriors from the land. Domain governments collected taxes from villages, maintained local order, and enforced the lord's rules. Their detailed workings, including the experience of villages and the burdens of taxation, belong to Chapter 44; for now the relevant point is that domains possessed the capacity to govern, and that this capacity was an essential component of the whole system and not a flaw in it.

That capacity had limits. A domain could not alter its basic status without shogunal approval. Its succession required confirmation. Its castles were subject to regulation. Its lord was required to attend the shogun's court, to supply military forces when requisitioned, and to perform services as ordered. Gradually, the bakufu also developed a practice of intervening in serious domain disorders. When domain conflicts, such as disputes among retainers or between a lord and his chief officials, grew severe enough to threaten the domain's order, the shogunate could and sometimes did step in, as in the troubles within the large domain of Sendai in the 1660s. The threat of intervention gave lords a strong incentive to resolve their internal problems quietly, a practice discussed in Section 42.15.

Domains also differed radically in character. Some were vast, comprising what had been multiple provinces, and others consisted of little more than the minimum necessary to qualify. Some were compact; others were scattered. Some were governed from magnificent castles; others had no castle at all. Some lords inherited long-established territorial roots, while others had been transferred repeatedly and had to rebuild administration in an unfamiliar land. This diversity, rather than a standard model, is the reality that the term "domain" masks, and it recurs in later sections.

Section 42.7

Who Counted as a Daimyō? Koku and Territorial Rank

The term daimyō had once referred loosely to powerful regional lords. In the Tokugawa period it acquired a more precise meaning: a warrior lord with a territorial holding assessed at 10,000 koku or more of rice yield who held that territory directly from the shogun. Warriors holding less, even if they were direct retainers of the shogun, were hatamoto or gokenin and not daimyō. The threshold was a political line as well as an economic one, because crossing it brought a distinct status, distinct obligations, and distinct privileges.

The yardstick was kokudaka, the assessed productivity of land expressed in koku, a unit of volume of rice conventionally regarded as enough to feed one person for a year. Hideyoshi's land surveys had established the principle of assessing land by assumed rice yield and assigning territory by that measure, and the Tokugawa adopted and extended it. When a lord's domain was confirmed, he received a document specifying the assessed value of his holding, and the number thereby recorded came to serve as the basis for ranking lords, for calculating military service, and for assigning obligations such as construction duties. National totals, though debated, are often estimated at about 18 million koku in the late sixteenth century, rising to near 30 million by the early eighteenth century.

Historical Vocabulary
Kokudaka

Kokudaka was a lord's assessed rice-equivalent productivity, measured in koku (roughly 180 liters of rice). It was a bureaucratic and political fiction as much as a measurement. It was originally derived from land surveys, but the nominal figure often diverged from actual yields and from the lord's real income, and it could include productivity that was not strictly rice. Once fixed in the documents confirming a lord's holdings, it served as a standard of status and obligation even as the real economy changed.

Students should not equate kokudaka with income or with a precise measure of wealth. It is better understood as a common language of rank that allowed the shogunate to compare very different territories. That standardization of measurement was an important tool of governance: it made it possible to rank, to assign burdens proportionately, and to reward or punish by adding to or subtracting from a recorded number.

Within the daimyō class, size mattered. Holdings ranged from the minimum of 10,000 koku to the roughly one million koku of the Maeda house of Kaga, the largest. Some lords were called kokushu (province-holders) when their territory was very large, others were castle-holders (jōshu), and others had no castle at all. Another system of rank, distinct from kokudaka, was that of court titles and honors, which the bakufu managed on lords' behalf, and the Edo Castle seating arrangements, in which lords were assigned to particular waiting rooms according to rank and house, expressed these gradations ritually. Domains Not Measured in Rice

Not every lord's authority rested on rice. In the far north, the Matsumae house governed southern Ezo (the later Hokkaido) with a formal standing as a daimyō-level house, but its position rested on trade privileges, including a shogunal grant of exclusive rights in dealings with Ainu communities, and not on agricultural yield. Ainu communities were not Matsumae's subjects in the sense that villagers in a rice domain were subject to their lords; they had their own political organization and trading networks. In the southwest, the Satsuma domain's control over the Ryukyu Kingdom after the 1609 invasion added an assessed value to Satsuma's records while the kingdom retained a distinct political and ceremonial existence, and the Sō house of Tsushima conducted diplomacy and trade with Korea on the shogunate's behalf. These cases show that the kokudaka system was a convenient standard that was bent to fit frontier realities. Chapter 48 examines them in detail as part of Japan's controlled international world.

Section 42.8

Shinpan, Fudai, and Tozama

The Tokugawa divided daimyō into three conventional categories. Shinpan ("related domains") were held by lords of Tokugawa descent, including the Three Houses of Owari, Kii, and Mito and a range of branches often bearing the Matsudaira name. Fudai ("hereditary") lords were those whose houses had served the Tokugawa as vassals before Sekigahara or had otherwise been brought into the Tokugawa retainer network, and many of them were rewarded with domains in the redistributions after 1600. Tozama ("outside") lords were those who had not been Tokugawa vassals before Sekigahara and who submitted to or allied with the Tokugawa on different terms, sometimes after fighting against them or standing neutral.

The categories mattered because they determined how the shogunate used each kind of lord. Fudai lords were the pool from which the senior councillors, the Kyoto deputy, and other major offices were drawn. A fudai lord with a modest domain could therefore hold much more influence over national affairs than a tozama lord with ten times his assessed wealth. Fudai houses were also often placed in strategically sensitive locations and were transferred more frequently than others as the bakufu adjusted its map. Shinpan lords held some of the largest domains, but the best-known of them, the Three Houses, were not primarily office-holders; their function was dynastic, to provide a reserve of heirs. Tozama lords, by contrast, were generally excluded from the central offices of the bakufu, although they were required to perform ceremonial duties, to supply labor and resources, and to follow the same laws.

It is a mistake to conclude that fudai were "good" lords and tozama "suspect" ones, or that tozama were weak. The wealthiest domains in Japan were tozama. Maeda of Kaga, Shimazu of Satsuma, Date of Sendai, and Hosokawa of Kumamoto commanded vastly more assessed wealth than most fudai houses, and the Tokugawa managed these houses through marriage alliances, favors such as granting the Matsudaira surname to some houses, and strategic placement, as well as through suspicion and supervision. The classification expressed a distinction of political access and of trust more than one of power, and the categories were not perfectly rigid; individual houses could be treated as especially favored or especially distrusted regardless of the label.

The system also encouraged different outlooks. Fudai lords had a reason to cultivate the bakufu, since service in its offices was the principal path to advancement. Tozama lords, excluded from that path, could concentrate on their domains, on maintaining autonomy, and on the cultivation of their own prestige. Over time these structural differences shaped political life, and in the nineteenth century (Chapters 51–53) it would matter that the domains most distant from the shogunate's offices were among those with the greatest resources and the longest memories of their relationship to the Tokugawa. That later development should not be read back into the seventeenth century, but the classification system made it possible.

Section 42.9

Geography as Political Control

The assignment of domains created a political geography. Chapter 41 explained how the redistribution after Sekigahara moved lords from one territory to another; the question here is what the resulting map accomplished. At the core, the Kantō plain around Edo was held by the Tokugawa and their retainers, with the shogun's direct lands and the lands of hatamoto and fudai surrounding the capital. The Tokugawa heartland was thus ringed by those most closely tied to the house. Along the major routes between Edo and Kyoto, particularly the Tōkaidō and Nakasendō highways, the bakufu placed fudai and shinpan lords, so that the main corridor of travel and military movement passed through territories held by trusted men. The Owari domain at Nagoya, held by a branch of the Tokugawa, guarded the central corridor, and the Ii house at Hikone held a major fudai position near the approaches to Kyoto and the Kinai region.

In the west and north, the pattern was more complex. The shogunate retained direct control of Osaka and Kyoto and installed deputies there. Large tozama domains were commonly located at a distance from Edo, in Kyushu, western Honshu, the northeast, and along the Sea of Japan coast, and they were often separated or flanked by fudai or shinpan territories. Some rearrangements, such as the transfer of the Asano house to Hiroshima in 1619 after the reduction of Fukushima Masanori (Section 42.11), allowed the bakufu to adjust the balance of friends and rivals. Other measures operated at the level of movement and observation: barrier stations on major routes allowed officials to monitor travel, with a special emphasis, according to later accounts, on the movement of weapons toward Edo and of lords' family members away from it.

Historians should treat this map with some caution. It would be tempting to see it as a master plan designed in advance to isolate every possible enemy. In reality it grew from the circumstances of 1600–1615: from the positions of existing lords, from wartime alliances, from rewards and punishments, and from ad hoc adjustments over succeeding decades. Moreover, geography was not destiny. A lord distant from Edo might be loyal, and a nearby lord might not be; the same fudai house might be moved. The accurate claim is a more modest one: that the Tokugawa, through repeated placement decisions, produced a pattern in which the heartland and the main corridors were held by trusted houses, the most powerful lords were mostly at a distance, and the center could observe and respond to events in a way that an earlier, unsettled map would not have allowed.

Section 42.10

Law for the Warrior Houses: Buke Shohatto

One of the Tokugawa's most distinctive instruments was law addressed to other rulers. The Buke shohatto ("Laws for the Military Houses," also rendered "Regulations for the Warrior Houses") was issued in 1615, shortly after the fall of Osaka. According to the standard account, it was drafted with the assistance of the Zen monk Konchiin Sūden, an advisor in the Tokugawa circle, and read out to the assembled lords at Fushimi. It consisted of thirteen articles, and it was revised and expanded by later shoguns: notably in 1635 under Iemitsu, and again in later reigns.

The content of the original articles is revealing. The first emphasized that warriors should cultivate both literary and martial arts, the ideal of "bun and bu," an expression of the expectation that warrior lords should be governors and scholars as well as soldiers. Other articles forbade drunkenness and revelry, prohibited harboring lawbreakers or rebels, regulated the repair and construction of castles, required lords to report suspicious combinations forming in neighboring domains, prohibited marriages made without shogunal approval, and set norms for ceremonial conduct, dress, and the choice of capable officials. The articles were short and general, and they reflected a preoccupation with preventing the formation of independent power centers. Castles, alliances, and marriages were all potential vehicles for it, and the law addressed each.

Note what the law did not do. It did not tell lords how to tax their villages, how to organize their retainers, or how to conduct day-to-day government. It did not create a uniform administration. It set boundaries and expectations. Tokugawa supremacy was exercised in part through setting the rules by which other rulers operated, and by making those rulers responsible to the shogun for observing them. This is a form of governance quite different from direct administration, and it explains how the bakufu could maintain supremacy over hundreds of lords without staffing hundreds of local governments.

The revisions are as significant as the original. The 1635 version, issued under Iemitsu, was longer and more detailed and formalized the requirement that lords attend the shogun in Edo on a recurring schedule, the arrangement known as sankin-kōtai. It was formally codified in the 1635 revision, and attendance arrangements for fudai lords were further regularized in 1642. Subsequent revisions in the later seventeenth and early eighteenth centuries adjusted the emphasis, softening or modifying some provisions in light of changed conditions. The existence of repeated revision shows that the law was not a fixed code handed down at the founding but an instrument of governance that adapted. Sankin-kōtai is introduced here as a political institution; its effects on roads, cities, finances, and social life are the subject of Chapter 45.

Section 42.11

Confiscation, Transfer, Reduction, and Succession

Law without sanctions is a request, and the Tokugawa possessed three principal sanctions over territorial lords. Kaieki was the confiscation of a domain and the extinction of the lord's house as a territorial power. Genpō was the reduction of a lord's holdings. Kunigae (or tenpō) was transfer to a different territory, which might be a punishment, a reward, or a strategic adjustment. In the first decades of Tokugawa rule, dozens of houses were subjected to one of these measures. Chapter 41 described the redistribution after Sekigahara as a postwar settlement. In the period treated here, the same tools became standing instruments of governance.

Several cases illustrate how they worked. In 1619 the large domain of Fukushima Masanori at Hiroshima was sharply reduced and he was moved to a far smaller holding in the interior after repairs to his castle that the bakufu considered unauthorized. The case showed that the castle rules of 1615 applied even to a major lord with a long record of service to the Tokugawa side. In 1632, the large domain of the Katō house at Kumamoto was confiscated, and the Hosokawa house was moved there; the case shows how the bakufu could reconfigure the map of Kyushu in a single stroke. Even the shogun's own relatives were not immune: Iemitsu's brother Tadanaga lost his domain in 1632. These cases varied in cause, from rule violations to succession failures to political suspicion, and historians debate the extent to which any given case reflected an actual offense, a pretext, or a structural opportunity for the bakufu to reshape the map.

Succession was a second major lever. A lord's heir required shogunal confirmation, and if a lord died without a recognized heir the house could be confiscated. In the early period, lords who were gravely ill and childless sometimes tried to adopt an heir at the last moment, and the bakufu often refused to recognize such deathbed adoptions. The consequence was a steady supply of extinguished houses and, with them, a growing population of masterless warriors. In 1651, soon after Iemitsu's death, a plot associated with a military instructor named Yui Shōsetsu was uncovered, and the authorities judged that the pool of rōnin had become a danger. The bakufu then relaxed the rules governing deathbed adoption, which reduced the number of confiscations and shifted the system toward stability. This episode is a good example of how the order was adjusted in response to its own consequences.

The human cost of these instruments was considerable. When a domain was confiscated, its retainers lost their positions and income, and many families became rōnin or sought new employment. Their experience, along with the broader social place of the samurai, belongs to Chapter 43. Here the political point is that the threat of territorial reassignment gave the shogunate leverage that did not depend on the continuing presence of its armies. A lord who knew that his castle, his marriage alliances, his heir, and his house's continued existence required shogunal approval had every reason to behave as a loyal participant in the order.

Section 42.12

Castles, Hostages, and Obligations

Military infrastructure was itself regulated. In 1615, the bakufu issued the one-castle order (ikkoku ichijō rei, literally "one castle per province"), which generally required daimyō to retain their principal castle while dismantling subsidiary fortifications within their territories. Application was not perfectly uniform. Several hundred castles were demolished or reduced. The rule applied to the physical foundations of military power, and its rationale was similar to that of the 1615 laws: a lord with many fortresses could resist, and a lord with one, subject to inspection and permission for repair, was easier to supervise. The castle, in other words, became both a symbol of a lord's status and a sign of his dependence.

Hostage-taking developed in parallel. Even before formal rules existed, some lords sent family members to reside in Edo as pledges of loyalty; the Maeda house of Kaga, for example, sent a family member to Edo in the 1610s. Over time the practice was regularized so that lords' wives and heirs resided in Edo, effectively as hostages under the bakufu's eye. The attendance obligation, sankin-kōtai, required the lord himself to spend alternate periods in Edo and in his domain. Formally codified in the 1635 Laws for the Military Houses, with fudai schedules further regularized in 1642, it combined ceremonial service to the shogun, a surveillance mechanism, and a continuing demonstration of hierarchy. This chapter treats it as a device of political control; its extraordinary effects on highways, urban growth, domain finances, and everyday life will be examined in Chapter 45.

Beyond attendance, the shogunate imposed a variety of burdens on lords. These included military service, with the number of troops owed set in proportion to a domain's kokudaka in regulations of the 1630s, and large construction projects: the building and rebuilding of Edo Castle, of Osaka Castle after its destruction, and of other works were often assigned to domains, whose lords supplied labor, materials, and funds. Such obligations were not a regular national tax; instead the Tokugawa governed to a considerable degree through occasional heavy demands on lords. They accomplished several aims at once. They drew on the wealth of the domains, they gave the bakufu a practical means of limiting the surplus that any lord might accumulate, and they gave lords an incentive to maintain good relations with the center. The mobilization of multiple domains' forces during the Shimabara Rebellion of 1637–1638 (Chapter 47) demonstrated that these obligations were not merely theoretical.

Everyday Life and Environment

Rank, Ceremony, and Residence: Living Inside the Political Order

A political order of this kind was experienced as much through ritual and daily routine as through law. At Edo Castle, lords attended ceremonies at which they were assigned to particular waiting rooms and seating places according to their rank and house, so that who sat where, who greeted whom first, and who was permitted to approach the shogun made the order visible. For the lords, these ceremonies were occasions of obligation and of competition; for the officials who arranged them, they were instruments of governance. The ceremonial calendar and precise etiquette were part of the Tokugawa method of making hierarchy habitual.

The women and children of daimyō families who lived as residents in Edo experienced the system differently. Required to live in the shogunal capital, away from the domain where the lord's authority was strongest, they lived under a form of supervision even as they maintained households of considerable size. Their experience, like those of the retainers who accompanied or served them, anticipates themes that will recur in Chapters 43 and 45. Meanwhile, the families of retainers whose domains were confiscated faced uncertainty and often poverty, and court nobles in Kyoto, subject to regulation and supported by modest revenues, lived in a world of rank rich in ceremony and constrained in resources. The political order was thus lived in many registers: ceremonial for the elite, precarious for those who fell, and constraining for those at its margins.

Section 42.13

Shogun and Emperor: Two Kinds of Authority

The title of this chapter names two figures, and the relationship between them is one of the most frequently misunderstood features of Tokugawa Japan. Two popular simplifications compete. One holds that the emperor was the true sovereign and that the shogun merely administered on his behalf. The other holds that the emperor was an irrelevant puppet, kept in a palace and ignored. Neither is adequate. The emperor did not govern the domains, command the warrior hierarchy, collect revenue on any significant scale, or direct policy. But the imperial institution did not disappear, and the Tokugawa did not treat it as unimportant. It continued to provide things the shogunate could not supply on its own.

Consider what the court supplied. It was the source of the shogun's own office, since the shogun's appointment came from the emperor. It was the source of court rank and honorific titles, which lords valued and which the bakufu managed on their behalf; a lord's court rank and office remained a marker of status distinct from his kokudaka. It maintained the imperial ritual calendar, including ceremonies of long standing, and the era names (nengō) by which years were designated. It was the center of a living tradition of poetry, scholarship, and ceremony, and it carried the accumulated prestige of a lineage that the warrior houses, whatever their power, could not replicate. Authority in this sense was not governing capacity but legitimacy, ceremonial primacy, and the right to confer recognition.

The words used in the period reflect the situation. In everyday Tokugawa documents the emperor was often referred to by terms such as kinri or kinchū (the "forbidden" imperial precincts) or tenshi (the "Son of Heaven"), and the word tennō was not the usual way to refer to a living sovereign, though modern textbooks conventionally use "emperor." The shift in terminology is a reminder that the role of the imperial institution varied across history, as noted in the book's framework: the emperor of the Tokugawa period was not the emperor of the eighth-century ritsuryō state nor the emperor of the Meiji constitution.

Historical Vocabulary
Buke and Kuge

Buke ("military houses") designated the warrior families, from the shogun's house to daimyō and retainers, who held power through military and administrative capacity. Kuge ("court houses") designated the aristocratic families of Kyoto who held court ranks and offices and who were associated with the emperor and the imperial court. The Tokugawa order depended on keeping these two worlds distinct: the buke governed, while the kuge ritually and culturally remained the court.

The distinction is not perfectly sharp. The shogun himself held court rank and titles, daimyō held court ranks conferred through the bakufu, and the buke regulated the kuge. But the binary captures a central feature of the system: two elites with different sources of authority, one possessing governing power and the other legitimating and ceremonial capacity, linked by regulation, marriage, and mutual need.

Section 42.14

Regulating the Imperial Court

At about the same time as the Laws for the Military Houses, the Tokugawa issued regulations for the court, the Kinchū narabi ni kuge shohatto (roughly "Regulations for the Imperial Court and the Court Nobility"), in 1615. They were issued with the participation of the shogun Hidetada, Ieyasu, and the senior court official, the regent Nijō Akizane, which gave them a joint character. Their seventeen articles addressed the emperor's duties, which were described primarily in terms of learning, scholarship, and cultivation, the arrangement of court ranks, qualifications for offices, appointments of the regent, the choice of era names, dress, and conduct. They defined the court as a body with specified roles and restricted its political activity.

The bakufu supervised the court through a deputy, the Kyoto shoshidai, a fudai lord stationed in Kyoto who also oversaw the city and exercised a watching brief over the western domains. The first holder of the post was Itakura Katsushige, who was succeeded by his son Shigemune, who served for several decades. The shoshidai worked with court-noble liaison officials known as buke tensō (also read buke densō), who transmitted communications between the court and the bakufu. The court's revenues, derived from modest imperial lands and from support provided by the bakufu, were limited, and the shogunate's control over finances reinforced its influence. At the same time, the Tokugawa made lavish gestures of respect: Nijō Castle in Kyoto, built at the beginning of the century as the shogun's residence in the capital, hosted a famous visit by Emperor Go-Mizunoo in 1626, a display of mutual deference that also demonstrated the shogunate's ability to host the sovereign.

Marriage tied the houses together. In 1620 Hidetada's daughter Masako (known by her later title Tōfukumon'in) entered Go-Mizunoo's court as consort, and their daughter later reigned as Empress Meishō (r. 1629–1643), the first woman to reign in more than eight centuries. In this way the Tokugawa sought to bind the imperial house to themselves. But the relationship was not frictionless, and the Purple Robe Incident reveals where the lines were drawn. In 1627, the bakufu declared void a series of honors, notably the conferring of purple robes on Buddhist monks, that Go-Mizunoo had granted without obtaining its approval, on the grounds that the grants violated the regulations. Monks who protested, including the well-known Zen priest Takuan Sōhō, were punished with exile. Two years later, in 1629, Go-Mizunoo abdicated abruptly in favor of his young daughter. Historians continue to discuss how much the abdication reflected anger at the bakufu's assertion of authority, but the episode shows that the shogunate regarded control over honors, ranks, and imperial appointments as a matter of political substance.

The Tokugawa strategy toward the court may be described as one of containment combined with use. They contained the court by regulating its activities, supervising its personnel, limiting its resources, and preventing lords from independently courting the emperor. They used it by deriving the shogun's office from the emperor, by presenting the order as consistent with the imperial tradition, and by keeping the court alive as a source of rank and ritual. This dual approach had long-term consequences that no one in the 1610s could have foreseen. Because the court remained a distinct institution with a continuous tradition, it retained the capacity to become a focus for political legitimacy when the Tokugawa order itself came into crisis in the nineteenth century (Chapters 51–53). That outcome was not inevitable; it was made possible by the way the seventeenth-century settlement preserved the imperial institution while limiting it.

Section 42.15

A Political Order Built on Negotiated Autonomy

The pieces described so far can now be brought together. The Tokugawa order functioned because the shogunate did not try to replace every local government. Domains governed their territories because they had the retainers, administrative traditions, and local knowledge to do so, and because the bakufu lacked the personnel to administer the entire country. In exchange, lords accepted a framework in which their holdings, successions, castles, marriages, and attendance depended on shogunal recognition. The bakufu retained supremacy because it controlled the decisive levers: the confirmation of lords, the allocation of territory, the rules for military and marital conduct, the legitimacy derived from the imperial court, and the power to punish. It was a reciprocal structure, though not an equal one.

The word "negotiated" should be used with care. Lords did not bargain with the shogun as equals, and the bakufu could impose severe penalties. But the system also contained numerous informal practices through which lords and officials adjusted their relations. Lords cultivated connections with senior councillors, offered gifts, and used marriage and adoption strategies to build ties. They also avoided drawing attention to internal problems. A domain that could settle a succession dispute or a retainer conflict internally avoided inviting shogunal intervention, and lords often had strong reasons to keep such troubles quiet, a concern connected to the concept of naibun, a house or domain's recognized internal sphere of authority, which lords had strong incentives to protect by resolving disputes within it before they escalated into matters requiring bakufu intervention. This preference for quiet resolution is part of why domain governments could appear remarkably autonomous in practice even though their formal position was subordinate.

This is the point at which it is useful to challenge the phrase "centralized feudal government" when it is used loosely. The system did have strong central features: shogunal supremacy, a large direct resource base, and effective sanctions. But it also left domains with substantial governing capacity, and the shogunate did not levy a national tax or administer most of the population directly. It combined central supremacy with regional governmental capacity. Whether that combination is best described as centralized, decentralized, or something else is a question on which historians differ, as the following feature shows.

Historical Debate

Was Tokugawa Japan Centralized or Decentralized?

The question: Historians have long argued over how to characterize the Tokugawa political order. Did the shogunate's supremacy make Tokugawa Japan fundamentally a centralized state, or did the domains' governing capacity make it better understood as a decentralized or composite polity? The debate bears on how to interpret the order's stability and on how to understand the transition to the modern state in the nineteenth century.

Interpretation A — Centralized supremacy within a feudal structure: Some historians, drawing on the evidence of confiscations, transfers, regulatory law, and the shogun's ability to command lords, emphasize the powers of the center. Scholars in this tradition have used terms such as "centralized feudalism" to capture the combination of a powerful overlord and territorial vassals. On this view the bakufu's controls were real and effective, and domains functioned within a framework that the center largely defined.
Interpretation B — A composite polity of governing domains: Other historians, drawing especially on domain records and local studies, emphasize that domain governments possessed their own legitimacy, administrative institutions, and sometimes distinctive political cultures, and that the shogunate rarely intervened in ordinary governance. On this view the Tokugawa order is better understood as a composite state in which the center's authority coexisted with robust regional government, and some scholars argue that this arrangement should be understood on its own terms and not as an incomplete version of a modern centralized state.

Evidence: Both positions draw on the same events and documents but weigh them differently. Interpretation A emphasizes confiscations, the Laws for the Military Houses, attendance requirements, and shogunal confirmation of succession. Interpretation B emphasizes the extensive domain archives, the limited bakufu intervention in domain administration, the practice of settling disputes internally, and the way retainers and subjects identified with their domains. These interpretations are not entirely exclusive, and many historians accept elements of both.

Why it matters: If Tokugawa Japan was already substantially centralized, the Meiji state-building of Chapters 53–54 looks like an extension of existing tendencies; if it was a composite of governing domains, the abolition of the domains in 1871 looks like a more radical rupture. The question also shapes how students understand regional diversity, the relative importance of the bakufu and the domains in the nineteenth-century crisis, and the sources of the order's stability.

Section 42.16

Stability Without Uniformity: The Tokugawa Political Settlement

By the middle of the seventeenth century, much of the Tokugawa political order had become durable. The line of shoguns had passed through three generations and was about to demonstrate, in the succession of a child, that it could survive a weak holder. The council system, the supervision of lords, the regulation of castles and marriages, the attendance system, and the controls over the court were in place. The mass confiscations of the first decades gradually gave way to a different style of government, sometimes described as a shift from the military governance of the founding era toward civil administration. Hoshina Masayuki, a half-brother of Iemitsu and lord of the Aizu domain, served as an influential guardian to the young Ietsuna and is associated with measures of this kind, including the prohibition of the custom of following one's lord in death.

But what had become stable was a framework, not a single uniform society. Domains continued to differ enormously in size, wealth, administrative practices, geography, and their relationship with the center. A great tozama domain with a million koku was not governed like a castle-less lord at the edge of the threshold, a domain in the northeast was not governed like one in Kyushu, and a frontier domain such as Matsumae was not governed like a rice domain of the interior. Political unity at the top coexisted with considerable variety below. Stability did not mean social sameness: the lives of the people who lived inside the system varied by domain, region, rank, and status in ways that the following chapters will explore.

This variation is not a failure of the order but part of its design. By leaving domains to govern themselves within a regulated framework, the Tokugawa avoided the enormous administrative task of governing the country directly and gave regional elites a stake in the system's continuation. At the same time, this design carried costs and tensions. Lords bore heavy obligations; excluded lords developed distinct outlooks; the court retained latent significance; and the administrative structure would be strained when financial problems and foreign pressure arrived. These tensions belong to later chapters. For the period covered here, the central achievement was the conversion of military victory into an institutional settlement.

Chapter 43 takes up the people who lived inside this structure: samurai, peasants, artisans, merchants, households, gender relations, and the outcaste communities known as eta and hinin, whose status provides the historical background to later discrimination against Burakumin. The political order described in this chapter set the stage on which those groups lived and acted.

Living Inside the Tokugawa Order

Note: the perspectives below describe categories of position reconstructed from institutional evidence. They are not quotations from, or biographies of, specific individuals, and groups differed in power and responsibility.

The shogun and his councillors: For the bakufu, the order meant securing a dynasty, supervising a large lord class with limited administrative personnel, and preserving legitimacy by working through, and not against, the imperial institution. The priority was predictability: lords who obeyed rules and requests without compelling the bakufu to impose sanctions.
A fudai lord: A fudai lord with a comparatively modest domain could hold a major office, influence national policy, and expect favor, but he also faced frequent reassignment and the demands of service. His advancement depended on a close relationship with the bakufu, which was both an opportunity and a dependency.
A tozama lord: A tozama lord of a large domain commanded great assessed wealth and a substantial retinue yet was excluded from central offices and subject to intensive supervision, heavy obligations, and the knowledge that his house could be reduced or transferred. His strategy emphasized the stable governance of his domain, the cultivation of relationships in Edo, and careful compliance.
A court noble in Kyoto: A kuge family retained rank, ritual function, and prestige, but lived under regulation, with modest resources, in dependence on the bakufu's support and under the supervision of its deputy. For the court, the settlement meant preserving the institution at the price of political autonomy.
A retainer of a confiscated house: For the warriors of a domain that was confiscated, the political order was experienced as sudden loss of employment and status. Many sought new service or became masterless, and their situation drew the bakufu's concern in 1651. Their view reveals the human price of an order whose stability was partly maintained through the threat of dissolution.
Continuity and Change Over Time

From Toyotomi Supremacy to Mature Tokugawa Control

What Changed?

  • Personal supremacy became hereditary dynastic rule, with offices and procedures able to function under a young or weak shogun
  • Ad hoc confirmations and commands became a body of law addressed to lords, with revision and enforcement
  • Hostage-taking and attendance, which began informally, became routine and universal
  • The court was brought under a regular supervisory system rather than treated as a source of titles to be used opportunistically

What Remained Continuous?

  • The use of assessed rice productivity as the measure of rank and obligation, inherited from the Toyotomi surveys
  • The territorial domain governed by a lord through castle, retainers, and local administration, inherited from the Sengoku period
  • The separation of warriors from direct control of the land
  • The dependence of warrior rulers on court rank and the imperial office for legitimacy

Who experienced change most directly? Daimyō and their retainers, whose holdings, successions, and residence were newly regulated, and the court nobility, now subject to supervision. Who experienced greater continuity? Villagers and townspeople, for whom the day-to-day experience of local lordship changed less visibly (see Chapters 43–44). Why the pattern matters: The Tokugawa did not invent most of their tools. They inherited them and fitted them into a durable arrangement, and understanding what was borrowed and what was new prevents the mistake of attributing everything to Ieyasu's invention.

Cause and Effect

The Consolidation of a Durable Political Order, c. 1615–1651

Long-term causes

The territorial states of the Sengoku era, the Toyotomi land surveys and separation of warriors from land, the long tradition of warrior government alongside an imperial court, and the experience of the Toyotomi failure to secure dynastic continuity.

Short-term causes

Victory at Sekigahara and the destruction of the Toyotomi at Osaka, the redistribution of domains, the presence of many lords of uncertain loyalty, and the need to regulate a large population of warriors and a court whose rank and legitimacy the Tokugawa required.

Event or transformation

The conversion of military supremacy into a layered order through law, office, rank, and sanction, between about 1615 and 1651.

Immediate effects

Domains governed under shogunal supervision; the court regulated but preserved; hostage-taking and attendance routinized; a population of rōnin created by confiscations, prompting adjustments by 1651.

Long-term consequences

A long period of internal political stability, a regionally varied society, and a system whose domains and court would become central to the political crisis of the nineteenth century.

Historical complexity: No single cause explains the order. Individual leadership mattered, but so did inherited institutions, the balance of resources, the willingness of lords to accept a place in the system, and adjustments made in response to emerging problems. The stability was also produced partly by coercion and partly by the benefits the order offered to participants. It was neither inevitable nor the product of a single design.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has explained how the Tokugawa converted the military victories of 1600–1615 into a functioning and durable political system. Its central argument is that Tokugawa Japan was neither a modern centralized state nor a loose collection of independent territories. It was a layered order in which the shogunate claimed supremacy over the warrior elite, domains retained substantial governing authority, and the imperial court remained a distinct source of rank and legitimacy. Stability rested on regulating relationships among these centers of authority and not on abolishing regional power.

The chapter began with the problem that followed victory. Supremacy did not itself provide procedures for ranking lords, securing loyalty, perpetuating a dynasty, or dealing with a court whose rank and titles the Tokugawa needed. Over the generations of Ieyasu, Hidetada, and Iemitsu, practices became institutions. The succession of the child shogun Ietsuna in 1651 showed how far the system had come: offices and procedures were able to carry on even when the person at the top could not personally direct them.

The concept of the bakuhan system describes the relationship between the shogunal government and the domains. Its defining feature was layered and unequal authority, and its vocabulary, including the term han, which became an official label only at the end of the period, should be used with awareness of its limits. The shogun was head of the Tokugawa house, appointed by the emperor, and supported by branch houses, notably the Three Houses, that provided a reserve of heirs. The bakufu itself developed from the Tokugawa household into a structure of councils, inspectors, magistrates, and intendants, many of whose offices took firmer shape only in the 1630s.

The shogunate's resource base was the tenryō, directly administered lands that, together with the lands of the shogun's retainers, made the Tokugawa far richer than any single domain. The bakufu also held strategic cities, mines, ports, and mints. These holdings should be distinguished from the broader property of the Tokugawa house and from the domains of other lords, and their scattered distribution reflected strategic purpose.

Domains were governments. Daimyō, warrior lords with holdings of at least 10,000 koku, governed through castles, retainers, laws, and finances, exercising extensive authority within the limits the shogunate set. The measure of rank and obligation was kokudaka, an assessed and partly fictional rice productivity inherited from the Toyotomi surveys. Frontier cases such as Matsumae, Satsuma's relationship to Ryukyu, and Tsushima showed that the rice-based standard was adapted to realities that it did not neatly fit.

The classification of daimyō into shinpan, fudai, and tozama determined political access and the use the bakufu made of each kind of lord. Fudai lords supplied the senior officials; tozama lords were generally excluded from central offices but included some of the wealthiest domains. The distribution of these houses created a political geography in which the Tokugawa heartland and the main routes were held by trusted houses and the largest tozama domains were generally at a distance, though the pattern emerged from circumstance and adjustment and not from a single master plan.

The shogunate governed the lords largely through law and sanction. The Laws for the Military Houses of 1615 and their later revisions set rules for castles, marriages, alliances, and conduct without administering the domains themselves. Confiscation, reduction, transfer, and control over succession gave the rules force, and castle regulation, hostage-taking, sankin-kōtai, military levies, and construction duties bound lords to the center. The consequences, including a population of masterless warriors, led to adjustments, such as the easing of deathbed-adoption rules in 1651.

The relationship between shogun and emperor was neither one of imperial rule through the shogun nor one of imperial irrelevance. The court supplied the source of the shogun's office, court rank, era names, ritual, and prestige; the bakufu regulated it through the 1615 court regulations, the Kyoto deputy, limits on resources, marriage ties, and, in episodes such as the Purple Robe Incident, direct assertion of authority. The Tokugawa contained and used the court at once, and in doing so preserved an institution that would later acquire renewed political significance.

Historians disagree about whether to call the resulting order centralized or decentralized. Those who emphasize confiscations and regulation see central supremacy, while those who emphasize domain governance and local archives see a composite polity. The two views are not mutually exclusive, and the evidence suggests that the order combined central supremacy with regional governmental capacity.

Finally, the settlement produced stability without uniformity. Domains differed greatly in size, wealth, administration, and relationship to the center. The following chapters examine the people, economies, and cultures that lived within this structure. This chapter has shown the framework, built incrementally across a generation, that made them possible.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it explains how a political order that looks, from a distance, like a simple regime of one ruling house actually worked as a system of relationships. The Tokugawa did not conquer the country into administrative uniformity. They built a framework in which lords, court, and shogunate each had a recognized place and in which the rules, sanctions, and rituals of the framework made it costly to step outside. Understanding that design is essential for understanding why Japan experienced a long period of internal peace and why that peace coexisted with substantial regional diversity.

The chapter also reveals continuities across periods. Assessed productivity from the Toyotomi surveys, the territorial domain from the Sengoku era, the dual existence of warrior government and imperial court from the twelfth century onward: each was inherited and reshaped. The Tokugawa order was a new arrangement of old materials. At the same time, it began new things, including the routinization of attendance, a body of law addressed to rulers, and a regular supervisory system for the court, that would shape the following two centuries.

The chapter connects different regions of the archipelago by showing how a central house related to peripheries as different as the Kantō heartland, the great domains of Kyushu and the northeast, and frontier houses such as Matsumae, Tsushima, and Satsuma. These frontier positions, and the societies of Ainu and Ryukyuan peoples beyond or alongside them, will return in Chapter 48. Here they are a reminder that the Tokugawa order was never uniform and that its edges were negotiated differently from its core.

Finally, the chapter prepares students for the rest of Part VII by drawing a boundary between structure and life within it. The councils, laws, ranks, and obligations described here defined the arena. But the people who lived in it, the samurai, farmers, artisans, merchants, outcastes, women, and entertainers, shaped a society and culture far richer than the political order alone could explain. Chapter 43 begins with those people, and the chapters that follow trace how a stable political settlement became the setting for one of the most dynamic social and cultural worlds in Japanese history.
