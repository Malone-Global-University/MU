The Home Front, Okinawa, Firebombing, Hiroshima, and Nagasaki

Japanese History: From Jōmon Japan to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

Between March 9 and August 9, 1945, a single night of incendiary bombing burned much of eastern Tokyo, a ground battle killed a large part of the population of Okinawa, and two bombs, each carried by a single aircraft, destroyed the centers of Hiroshima and Nagasaki. These were different events with different causes, but they happened to the same wartime society, and many of the people who endured them had already spent years short of food, fuel, medicine, and sleep.

Earlier chapters in Part XI explained how Japan came to be at war: the continental conflict (Chapter 76), the mobilization of ideology, schooling, and institutions (Chapter 77), the Pacific campaigns (Chapter 78), and the colonial exploitation and atrocities that accompanied imperial expansion (Chapter 79). This chapter turns to a different question. What did the final stages of total war mean for the people living in the Japanese home islands, and for the people of Okinawa, whose experience was both part of and distinct from that of metropolitan Japan? The answer requires attention to material life as well as to military operations: how a household obtained rice, what a twelve-year-old was told to do, how a neighborhood association tried to fight a fire that no bucket could put out.

The chapter's argument is that between 1942 and 1945 military defeat, maritime blockade, economic shortage, and Allied air attack steadily undermined the material foundations of civilian life, while the state continued to demand sacrifice from a population it could no longer adequately feed, house, or protect. Destruction did not arrive all at once or evenly. Rural households often fared better in food terms than urban ones; some national infrastructure, including much of the main railway system, kept operating even as cities burned; and the experience of Okinawan civilians, who were caught in a ground battle fought on their own land, differed from that of residents of bombed cities on the main islands. The atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki were distinct in their physical effects and their long-term consequences, but they belong to a sequence of civilian destruction that began before them and cannot be understood apart from it.

The chapter also asks students to hold two kinds of explanation together. One concerns decisions: why American commanders shifted from high-altitude precision bombing to low-altitude night incendiary attacks, why Japanese authorities told residents to stay and fight fires rather than flee, why the 32nd Army chose to prolong the battle in southern Okinawa, why American officials decided to use atomic weapons. The other concerns experience: what those decisions meant for the people they affected. Neither explanation substitutes for the other, and the chapter does not attempt to settle by assertion the moral questions that historians and the public continue to debate. It tries instead to present the evidence, define the categories carefully, and show where interpretations diverge.

The evidence is unusually rich and unusually difficult. It includes Japanese government and police records, civil-defense manuals, school and neighborhood-association documents, diaries, American operational records, and the postwar reports of the United States Strategic Bombing Survey. It also includes a large body of survivor testimony, collected by museums, municipal bodies, and individual researchers, much of it recorded decades after the events. Such testimony is indispensable and also shaped by memory, by the purposes of those who collected it, and by postwar politics. Casualty figures are estimates whose meaning depends on how the dead were defined and counted. Wherever this chapter gives a number, it is approximate unless stated otherwise, and students should attend to what each figure counts.

The chapter's boundaries matter. Chapter 80 ends with a devastated home front in August 1945. It introduces the Potsdam Declaration, the Soviet entry into the war, and the debates over the atomic bombings because they are essential context, but the full sequence of Japanese government deliberations, the imperial decision to accept surrender, and the aftermath belong to Chapter 81. Postwar hunger, homelessness, and the black market are examined in Chapter 85, and the lasting politics of Okinawa and of the hibakusha in later chapters. Here the task is to explain the destruction and the lives it ended and changed.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how blockade, shipping losses, and military demands undermined civilian food supply and living standards between 1942 and 1945, distinguishing wartime shortage from the postwar food crisis.
  2. Describe how rationing, neighborhood associations, evacuation, and labor mobilization operated in the daily lives of households, women, and children.
  3. Analyze why American air strategy shifted from high-altitude precision attacks to low-altitude incendiary raids, and why Japanese urban construction and industry influenced target selection.
  4. Reconstruct the Tokyo air raid of March 9–10, 1945, and explain why casualty estimates differ.
  5. Assess the limits of Japanese civil defense and distinguish urban devastation from the continued functioning of some national infrastructure.
  6. Explain Okinawa's distinct historical position within the empire and how it shaped militarization, evacuation, and civilian vulnerability before April 1945.
  7. Distinguish among Okinawan civilians, Okinawan auxiliaries, mainland Japanese soldiers, and American forces when analyzing responsibility and casualties in the Battle of Okinawa.
  8. Evaluate the causes of Okinawan civilian deaths, including bombardment, starvation, disease, coerced group suicide, and violence by Japanese soldiers.
  9. Compare the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki with each other and with conventional bombing, using clearly defined casualty categories.
  10. Distinguish blast, thermal, and radiation effects, and explain what is and is not known about acute and long-term health consequences.
  11. Interpret competing historical arguments about the atomic decision without treating counterfactual outcomes as established fact.
  12. Evaluate primary sources and survivor testimony for creator, purpose, provenance, and limits.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How did the gap between the state's mobilization goals and its ability to supply the population widen between 1942 and 1945, and how did households respond?
  2. Why did American planners abandon high-altitude precision bombing against Japan, and what assumptions about Japanese cities and industry informed the change?
  3. Why did Japanese civil-defense policy emphasize staying to fight fires, and what were the consequences when incendiary raids overwhelmed it?
  4. How did Okinawa's history as a formerly independent kingdom, annexed in 1879 and subjected to assimilation policy, shape its experience of the war?
  5. Why was the Battle of Okinawa so destructive for civilians, and how should historians assign responsibility among several armies and decisions?
  6. How do historians construct casualty estimates for events such as the Tokyo raid, Okinawa, Hiroshima, and Nagasaki, and why do the estimates persist in differing?
  7. In what ways did the atomic bombings differ from conventional incendiary bombing, and in what ways were they continuous with it?
  8. What kinds of evidence would be needed to judge arguments about military necessity, alternatives, and the role of Soviet entry, and what are the limits of counterfactual reasoning?

Historical Context

By 1942 Japan was fighting simultaneously in China and across the Pacific, and its economy was organized around that effort. Chapter 77 described the institutions through which the state mobilized labor, schooling, religion, and public opinion: the National Mobilization Law of 1938, the Imperial Rule Assistance Association, the neighborhood associations, and a press and education system aligned with the war. Those institutions presupposed that the empire could draw resources from Korea, Taiwan, Manchuria, and, after 1941, Southeast Asia. Between 1942 and 1944 American naval and air power progressively broke that assumption by sinking merchant shipping, taking island bases, and cutting the sea lanes on which Japan depended for oil, ore, rice, and other imports.

Japan's loss of the Marianas in mid-1944 (Chapter 78) was the pivotal strategic event for this chapter. Saipan, Tinian, and Guam gave the United States airfields within range of the main islands for the new B-29 bomber. Until then the home islands had been struck only occasionally. From late 1944 they were struck repeatedly, and from March 1945 the character of the attacks changed. Meanwhile the Philippine campaign and the capture of Iwo Jima in early 1945 brought American forces to the approaches of the main islands, and Okinawa, about 640 kilometers from Kyushu, became the next objective.

Okinawa had a history different from that of the main islands. It had been the center of the Ryukyu Kingdom, a maritime state with tributary ties to China and, after 1609, subordination to the Satsuma domain (Chapters 32, 33, and 48). The Meiji government annexed the kingdom in 1879 and created Okinawa Prefecture. In the following decades Okinawans were incorporated into the Japanese state unequally: they faced discrimination as migrants on the main islands, schooling policy suppressed the Ryukyuan languages, and political rights, including representation in the Diet, arrived later than elsewhere. This background matters for understanding why Okinawan civilians were both mobilized as loyal imperial subjects and regarded with suspicion by some military officers.

Finally, the Allied war aims and the Japanese leadership's strategy shaped the final year. The Allies demanded unconditional surrender, a formulation that left the status of the imperial institution uncertain. Japanese military leaders pursued plans to defend the home islands by inflicting heavy casualties on an invading force (the Ketsu-Gō plan), while some civilian officials explored mediation through the Soviet Union. These debates, treated in Chapter 81, formed the backdrop to the events in this chapter without being its subject.

Section 80.1 · Division I — The Home Front: Rationing, Labor, Families, and the Breakdown of Everyday Life

Japan's Home Front under Total War: Military Defeat, Economic Pressure, and Civilian Life

The term "home front" describes the civilian side of a nation at war: the households, farms, workshops, and neighborhoods that supplied soldiers, produced weapons, and absorbed the costs of the conflict. In Japan the home front was never a single place. A silk-reeling town in Nagano, a shipyard district in Kobe, a tenant village in Tōhoku, a fishing community in Okinawa, and a Tokyo neighborhood of wooden row houses experienced the war in different ways, and the differences grew as the war went badly. Chapter 77 explained how the state built institutions to direct this population. This section asks a different question: what happened when the state's demands exceeded its ability to supply the people it governed?

Japan depended heavily on imports. It brought in most of its oil, large shares of its iron ore, bauxite, rubber, and other industrial materials, and a considerable portion of its rice from Korea and Taiwan. The war in China and then the expansion into Southeast Asia in 1941–1942 were in part attempts to secure these resources. For a brief period the strategy appeared to work, but it depended on merchant shipping. American submarines, which attacked with increasing effectiveness from 1943, together with carrier aircraft and, from 1945, aerial mine-laying in Japanese home waters, destroyed the greater part of Japan's merchant fleet. The Strategic Bombing Survey later concluded that the loss of shipping was among the most damaging single factors in the collapse of Japan's economy. Shipments of oil, ore, and food fell sharply from 1944, and by 1945 many routes were effectively closed.

The government's priorities determined who bore the shortage. Materials and labor were allocated first to aircraft, shipbuilding, munitions, and other war production, and consumer goods, civilian transport, and agricultural inputs such as fertilizer received what remained. The Imperial Army and Navy absorbed millions of men, more than six million by the war's end, drawing workers from farms and factories. Farms lost labor and fertilizer, factories lost skilled men, and households lost their primary earners. At the same time production itself was increasingly hampered by shortages of raw materials, power, and spare parts, so that sacrifices at home did not translate into the output the planners expected.

This gap between objective and capacity is the core of the home-front story. Mobilization planning, expressed in annual materials plans and labor-allocation orders, assumed resources that did not exist. The state responded by tightening controls, by appealing to spirit and sacrifice, and by shifting burdens onto households, schoolchildren, women, and colonial and foreign laborers. It did not, for the most part, respond by reducing military demands. The result was a society in which official rhetoric spoke of endurance while ordinary people experienced hunger, fatigue, cold, and a growing sense that the outcome was beyond their control. Historians find evidence of this in Home Ministry reports on public morale, police records of rumor and complaint, diaries, and later testimony, and also in collections of household objects such as ration tickets, substitute clothing, and school materials preserved in museums such as the Shōwakan in Tokyo.

It is important not to overstate the case. Japanese civilian society did not simply collapse in 1944 or 1945. Neighborhood associations continued to distribute rations, factories continued to operate in many places until bombed, trains ran, and most people continued to do what the authorities asked of them, in part from loyalty or fear and in part because there were few alternatives. The Special Higher Police and the military police monitored dissent, and open opposition to the war was dangerous. But the material basis of daily life was steadily eroding, and by mid-1945 observers inside and outside the government recognized that it could not be sustained.

Section 80.2 · Division I

Rationing, Food Shortages, Substitutes, and the Struggle for Survival

Rationing of rice began in six major cities in April 1941, and the Food Control Law of February 1942 placed the collection and distribution of staple foods under state management nationwide. Under the system, farmers delivered fixed quotas of rice to the government at set prices (kyōshutsu, compulsory delivery), and urban households received a basic adult allowance, calculated in gō (a traditional unit, roughly 150 grams of uncooked rice), with larger allowances for heavy laborers and smaller ones for children and the elderly. The basic adult ration was around 2.3 gō per day in the early war years, a quantity that officials considered adequate when combined with other foods. Rationing extended to other goods as well: clothing was controlled through a point system introduced in 1942, and sugar, matches, soy sauce, cooking oil, fish, and many other items were distributed in small quantities through designated shops and the neighborhood associations.

As imports fell and the harvest suffered from labor and fertilizer shortages, the official allowance was repeatedly supplemented or replaced by substitutes. Barley, sweet potatoes, soybeans, and dried noodles replaced a growing share of rice. In some regions households ate pumpkin, potato leaves, and flour made from other starchy sources, and by 1945 the rice itself was often mixed with other grains. In July 1945 the basic ration in the six largest cities was reduced to about 2.1 gō. Even when the paper allowance was delivered, its nutritional value declined as substitutes replaced rice, and deliveries were frequently delayed or short. Surveys and later estimates indicate that average caloric intake in the cities fell well below what adults needed for sustained work, and malnutrition-related illness, including tuberculosis, increased, though historians caution that reliable national statistics for the last months of the war are limited.

Households responded in several ways, and their options depended on where they lived and what they had. Urban families planted vegetables in any available space, in yards, along railway embankments, and on land cleared by demolition. Many made kaidashi, or buying trips, traveling by crowded train to rural districts to exchange kimono, tools, or other possessions for rice and vegetables. These trips were a widespread and well-documented feature of wartime and early postwar life, though the authorities viewed them with ambivalence and the economic police sometimes confiscated what travelers carried. Illegal markets, in which prices rose to many times the official level, operated in nearly every city, and their existence reflected both shortage and the limits of enforcement. A household's access to these alternatives depended on cash, on goods to trade, and on connections with farming relatives, so the burden fell most heavily on the urban poor, on families without rural ties, and on those who had lost their homes.

Rural–urban differences were real but not simple. Farm households generally had greater access to food than city dwellers, and some prospered by selling surplus produce at market rates. But farmers faced compulsory delivery quotas, labor shortages as young men were conscripted, shortages of fertilizer and tools, and, for tenants, rent obligations to landlords. In poorer districts and in regions that supplied labor to the mills and mines, farm families also experienced severe hardship. The countryside was not a refuge for everyone, and relations between hungry urban visitors and farm families were sometimes strained.

Two distinctions deserve emphasis. First, wartime deprivation was severe and growing, but the historical record does not support describing it as a national famine with mass starvation deaths before August 1945; the worst of the food crisis came after the surrender, when a poor 1945 harvest, the collapse of imports from the former empire, the return of millions of soldiers and civilians from overseas, and the disruption of transport and distribution combined (see Chapters 81 and 85). Second, Okinawa and some besieged or bombed communities experienced acute hunger during the war itself. The chapter's later divisions return to these cases.

Historical Vocabulary
Historical Vocabulary: Home Front, Rationing, Evacuation, and Incendiary Bombing

Home front refers to the civilian population and economy of a nation at war, as distinct from its armed forces. In Japan the term is a modern scholarly one; contemporaries spoke of the "rear" (jūgo, literally "behind the gun") and expected civilians to support the front through labor and sacrifice. Rationing was state allocation of scarce goods by fixed quantity, through registration, tickets, and designated outlets. It differed from market purchase in that entitlement, not price alone, determined access, and in practice rations were supplemented by gardens, barter, and illegal purchase. Evacuation (sokai) described several different policies: the dispersal of schoolchildren to the countryside, the movement of elderly and young residents out of cities, and the demolition of buildings to create firebreaks. The Japanese word covered both moving people and clearing space.

Incendiary bombing is the use of bombs designed to start fires rather than to destroy structures by blast. Area bombing is the attack of a whole district rather than a specific installation, in contrast to precision bombing, which in theory targets particular factories or facilities. In Japan incendiary bombs were especially destructive because of dense wooden construction, a point developed in Sections 80.6 to 80.8. Students should not assume that "precision" bombing was accurate in practice, nor that "area" bombing was random: it was a deliberate strategy with specific assumptions about industry and urban form.

Section 80.3 · Division I

Factories, Women, Students, and the Burdens of Wartime Production

As men were drafted, the labor force had to be replaced, and the state turned to women, students, and colonial and foreign workers. The National Labor Service Corps of 1941 had required young men and some women to perform periodic labor service, and as the war deepened the policy expanded. In 1943 and 1944 the government mobilized students on a rising scale, and an order in August 1944 formalized the system of student labor mobilization (gakuto kinrō dōin). Middle-school and girls' school students, and eventually many upper elementary pupils, were assigned to factories, farms, and construction sites for a large share of the school year. In the same month the Women's Volunteer Labor Corps order required unmarried women within a specified age range to register for labor service, although in practice many were assigned through schools and workplaces. By 1945 most formal schooling above the lower grades had been suspended in favor of work.

Conditions in wartime factories were demanding. Working days of ten to twelve hours were common, often with night shifts, and holidays were curtailed. Shortages of raw materials, electricity, and spare parts disrupted production and forced improvisation. Food at workplace canteens declined in quantity and quality, heating was scarce, and clothing and footwear wore out and could not be easily replaced. Accidents were frequent, in part because inexperienced workers, including adolescents, operated machinery or handled materials with little training. Wages were controlled and failed to keep up with the cost of living in the black market, and absenteeism, driven by illness, fatigue, family obligations, and the search for food, rose as the war went on. Employers and officials complained of declining discipline, and the authorities responded with campaigns of exhortation and with pressure on workers and families.

The mobilization of women was extensive but uneven, and it reflected ideas about gender as well as needs. Japanese policy was more reluctant than that of Britain or the United States to draft married women into industry, in part because the official ideology emphasized women's roles in the household as mothers and caretakers. Women nevertheless filled a growing share of factory and agricultural jobs, and the many women who had always worked, in textile mills, on farms, and in family businesses, carried heavier burdens as husbands, sons, and brothers were conscripted. Women in cities also performed the tasks of the home front outside employment: queuing for rations, participating in air-raid drills, collecting scrap metal, and sewing for soldiers. Memoirs and oral histories convey the fatigue and the pressure, and also pride and a sense of purpose that many participants later recalled.

Students occupied an especially exposed position. Mobilized adolescents worked in aircraft plants, munitions factories, shipyards, and arsenals, many of which became targets of bombing. In some cases schoolchildren were killed at their workplaces during raids. Student laborers also bore the immediate effects of the later atomic bombings, as Sections 80.16 and 80.17 explain, since many were working in demolition crews or factories in Hiroshima and Nagasaki at the moments of the explosions.

Colonial and foreign laborers worked alongside Japanese in many of these workplaces, and often in worse conditions. Koreans, many of them brought under wartime labor recruitment and conscription systems, worked in mines, factories, construction, and ports; Chinese laborers and Allied prisoners of war were also assigned to mines and industrial sites. Chapter 79 analyzes the systems of forced labor and their coercive character. For this chapter it is enough to note that these workers shared the dangers of bombing, were frequently denied access to shelters or adequate relief, and had no ability to leave their assignments. Their experiences should be kept in view whenever the chapter refers to "workers" or "civilians" in bombed cities.

Section 80.4 · Division I

Neighborhood Associations, Civil Defense, Evacuation, and Household Mobilization

The neighborhood associations (tonarigumi) were the principal point of contact between the state and the household. Formalized by a Home Ministry directive in 1940, they grouped households, typically about ten, into units whose heads relayed official announcements, collected contributions, organized salvage drives for metal and other materials, and distributed rations. Their functions had roots in older forms of local mutual responsibility, and many participants understood them as a continuation of neighborly cooperation. But under wartime conditions they also made it difficult to avoid obligations and easy for neighbors to monitor one another. A household that did not turn out for a drill, or that expressed doubts about the war, risked social pressure and, in some cases, police attention. Historians continue to discuss how far the associations should be understood as instruments of state control and how far as vehicles for community assistance. The evidence suggests both.

Air-raid defense relied on the neighborhood associations. The Air Defense Law of 1937, amended in 1941, required residents to remain in place and fight fires caused by bombing rather than flee. Drills taught households to form bucket brigades, to use stirrup pumps and fire swatters, and to cover windows and extinguish lights. Families dug small shelters in yards or under floors, and local authorities designated public shelters, though these were limited in number and capacity. The policy assumed that fires from small incendiary bombs could be contained by prompt local action, an assumption that reflected earlier experience, including that of the 1923 earthquake fires and early German and British incendiary raids, but that proved wrong against large raids using low-altitude clusters of incendiaries on dense wooden districts.

Authorities also tried to reduce vulnerability through demolition and evacuation. From late 1943 the government ordered the destruction of buildings in selected districts to create firebreaks, a program known as tatemono sokai. Residents were given short notice and limited compensation, and in many cases whole neighborhoods were cleared by neighborhood labor and by mobilized students. Hundreds of thousands of people lost their homes in this way before any bomb fell. Meanwhile evacuation of people from cities was discouraged for able-bodied adults, who were expected to stay and defend, but encouraged for children, the elderly, and pregnant women. By 1945 the populations of Tokyo and other major cities had fallen substantially, in Tokyo's case by more than half from its peak, as residents left for the countryside through official channels or on their own.

As the situation worsened the government extended mobilization to the entire population. In 1945 it prepared for the decisive battle of the home islands under the Ketsu-Gō plan, and a law of June 1945 established the National Volunteer Combat Corps (Kokumin Giyūtai), organizing men, and women in specified age ranges, for eventual armed resistance to invasion. Civilians trained with bamboo spears and other improvised weapons. Historians generally interpret these measures as evidence of the leadership's determination to continue the war and of the state's inability to equip the population, not as realistic military preparation. The measures also show how the boundary between civilian and combatant was being erased, a development with consequences in Okinawa, where civilians were drawn directly into combat.

The limits of civil defense became clear as bombing intensified. The fire services were small, short of equipment and fuel, and in many cities depended on volunteer brigades. Shelters built for blast protection could become deadly in firestorms, and the instruction to remain and fight fires contradicted the reality of conflagrations that spread faster than people could respond. Section 80.7 shows what this meant in Tokyo.

Section 80.5 · Division I

Children, Education, Family Separation, Health, and Social Anxiety

Children experienced the war largely through decisions made by adults and by the state. By 1944 the school curriculum had been reshaped around military and patriotic themes (see Chapter 77), and students were being mobilized for labor. In June 1944 the cabinet adopted a policy of evacuating schoolchildren from major cities to rural areas, concentrating initially on children in the third through sixth grades of the national (elementary) schools in thirteen cities, among them Tokyo, Yokohama, Kawasaki, Yokosuka, Nagoya, Osaka, and Kobe (Kyoto was added to the designated program in 1945, not included among the original thirteen). Japan’s Ministry of Education records about 360,000 children in organized evacuation centers during the first phase, apart from children who stayed with relatives. Several hundred thousand children were sent away, often as whole classes accompanied by teachers, and housed in temples, inns, and private homes in the countryside. The policy was officially described as a measure to preserve the nation's future, and it was voluntary in form but strongly encouraged, and in practice many families felt they had little choice.

The evacuation separated families for the first time on a mass scale. Children's memoirs describe homesickness, hunger, lice and skin disease, cold, bullying, and the tedium of life in crowded quarters, along with kindness from some host families and teachers. Hosts were themselves short of food and could resent the additional mouths to feed. Parents in the cities, meanwhile, remained exposed to air raids, and the bombing of 1945 left many evacuated children orphaned or without a home to return to. The later problem of war orphans, discussed in Chapter 85, had its roots in this period. Children who stayed in cities attended school irregularly, worked in factories or farms, and participated in drills, and in March 1945 the government decided to suspend most classes for the following year, ending ordinary schooling for many.

Health conditions deteriorated for the population as a whole. Doctors and nurses were conscripted into military service, leaving civilian hospitals and clinics short of staff. Medicines such as sulfa drugs were prioritized for the military, and supplies of soap, fuel, and clean water diminished. Tuberculosis and other infectious diseases spread among malnourished and overcrowded populations, and infant and child mortality rose, though precise national figures for the final year are uncertain. Clothing shortages and the lack of laundering and bathing added to discomfort and illness, especially in winter. These everyday privations mattered because they reduced people's resilience in the face of the bombing that followed.

Public morale during the final year cannot be reduced to a single attitude. Many people continued to support the war effort, out of loyalty to the emperor and the nation, grief for the dead, a belief that sacrifice must have meaning, or lack of alternatives. Others became weary, resentful of shortages and inequalities, or privately convinced that the war was lost. Home Ministry and police reports recorded complaints, rumors, and expressions of defeatism, and the authorities treated rumor-mongering as a punishable offense. People turned to religion and local ritual for consolation: they visited shrines and temples, carried charms for soldiers, such as the thousand-stitch belts sewn by many hands, and held funerals for the dead whose remains often did not return. Historians use diaries, letters, school records, and later oral histories to reconstruct these attitudes, with attention to the fact that diaries and letters were written under censorship and that memories recorded decades later are shaped by postwar knowledge.

Differences between urban and rural survival grew more pronounced as the war went on. Rural communities often had more food but faced labor shortages and, for those in the path of the later bombing of small cities, the same dangers as townspeople. City dwellers who had relatives in the countryside could escape or obtain food, while those without ties were more exposed. Class, gender, and region therefore shaped who suffered most, and these differences carried into the experience of the bombing described in Division II.

Section 80.6 · Division II — The Air War against Japan: Strategic Bombing and Urban Destruction

From the Doolittle Raid to the B-29 Campaign: The Expansion of the Air War

The air war against Japan began as a gesture. On April 18, 1942, sixteen B-25 medium bombers, launched from the carrier USS Hornet under Lt. Col. James Doolittle, struck Tokyo and targets in other cities including Yokohama, Nagoya, and Kobe. The physical damage was small, but the raid showed that the home islands were vulnerable and embarrassed a military that had promised their security. It had consequences beyond Japan's cities: the Navy's determination to eliminate American carriers contributed to the decision to attack Midway, and the Army's campaign in the Zhejiang and Jiangxi provinces of China, undertaken partly to deny bases to such raiders, caused heavy Chinese civilian casualties, estimates of which vary widely among scholars (see Chapter 76). The raid is a reminder that air power was an instrument of the war from the start, and that Japan had itself bombed Chinese cities, including Chongqing, for years before 1945.

Sustained strategic bombing required a long-range aircraft. The Boeing B-29 Superfortress, a pressurized four-engine bomber able to fly at high altitude with a heavy load, was the instrument. The first B-29 raids against Japan began in June 1944 from bases around Chengdu in China, supplied by an expensive airlift over the Himalayas. The campaign from China achieved little, and its logistics were prohibitive. The turning point was the American capture of Saipan, Tinian, and Guam in the summer of 1944, which gave the United States large airfields about 2,400 kilometers from Tokyo. The first raid on Tokyo from the Marianas took place on November 24, 1944, and the B-29 force built up through the winter.

The initial American doctrine called for high-altitude daylight precision attacks on aircraft engine plants and other key industries. The results were disappointing. At altitudes of nine thousand meters or more, bombers encountered powerful jet-stream winds that disrupted bombing runs, and frequent cloud cover over Japan obscured targets. Mechanical failures, long overwater flights, and Japanese fighter attacks added to losses. The XXI Bomber Command, under Brig. Gen. Haywood Hansell, did not achieve the destruction planners had hoped for. In January 1945 the Twentieth Air Force replaced Hansell with Maj. Gen. Curtis LeMay, who had commanded bombers in Europe and in China.

LeMay changed the method. Analysts of the Army Air Forces had long considered Japanese cities vulnerable to fire. They noted the dense construction of wood and paper, the closely packed neighborhoods, and the fact that much Japanese industry was dispersed among small workshops and home factories that supplied larger plants, so that attacking a district could damage production even where no large factory stood. The Air Forces had tested incendiary munitions on replica Japanese buildings at a proving ground in Utah in 1943. After limited incendiary raids in February 1945, LeMay ordered attacks at low altitude, between roughly 1,500 and 3,000 meters, at night, with aircraft carrying incendiary clusters in place of high explosives and, in many cases, stripped of defensive guns to increase payload. Night attack at low altitude reduced the effect of jet-stream winds and, because Japanese night-fighter and radar-directed antiaircraft defenses were weak, was expected to be less costly in American aircraft.

The change was a decision about method and, implicitly, about who would bear the consequences. High-altitude precision bombing had been justified in American doctrine as a way to destroy industry while limiting civilian casualties, though in practice it too killed civilians. Area incendiary attack deliberately targeted urban districts, where workers and their families lived and where small-scale production was embedded. Historians debate how far military planners saw civilian deaths as an objective, as an accepted consequence, or as irrelevant to the aim of disrupting production and morale. The legal context was also unsettled: no binding international rule specifically governed aerial bombardment of cities in the Second World War, and all major belligerents, including Germany, Britain, and Japan, had attacked urban areas. These facts do not settle the moral questions; they explain the setting in which decisions were made, and Section 80.19 returns to them.

Section 80.7 · Division II

The Great Tokyo Air Raid, March 9–10, 1945

On the night of March 9–10, 1945, the XXI Bomber Command carried out the operation known as Meetinghouse. Roughly 330 B-29s took off from the Marianas and about 280 reached Tokyo, dropping on the order of 1,600 to 1,700 tons of bombs, most of them incendiary clusters filled with a gelled petroleum mixture. Lead aircraft began marking the target zone shortly after midnight on March 10, and the following aircraft saturated it. The target was a rectangular area of eastern Tokyo, including the districts of Asakusa, Fukagawa, Honjō, and parts of Nihonbashi, lying along and east of the Sumida River. It was one of the most densely populated urban districts in the world, filled with wooden houses, small workshops, and narrow lanes, and inhabited largely by working families.

Weather made the attack more destructive. A strong, gusty surface wind, reported at about 25 to 30 miles per hour or more, spread the fires from the first bombs into continuous conflagration. The fires merged, generating extreme heat and rising columns of air that drew in more wind and oxygen, producing what survivors and later analysts described as firestorm conditions with burning embers carried across firebreaks and streets. The fire services, small and equipped for isolated blazes, were quickly overwhelmed, and the instruction to residents to stay and fight fires was impossible to follow. Many people fled, but the district's geography limited escape. Streets were narrow and blocked by burning buildings and debris; rivers and canals offered refuge but not safety, as people packed bridges and riverbanks and were overcome by smoke, heat, and flames, and as some drowned or were crushed. Public shelters and earthen trenches, intended as protection from blast, could trap occupants in fire.

By dawn, approximately 40 square kilometers (about 16 square miles) of Tokyo had been burned, according to American assessments, and roughly a quarter of a million buildings were destroyed. Police and fire authorities estimated that about a million people had lost their homes. The wind subsided and the bombers returned, with the loss of some fourteen aircraft. In the following days survivors searched for relatives and the authorities collected the dead. Bodies lay in streets, canals, and parks, and in many cases whole families, including those without surviving relatives to report them, were never identified. Temporary mass burials were made in parks and temple grounds, and remains were later exhumed and cremated. Emperor Hirohito toured the burned districts on March 18, an act that survivors and historians have interpreted in different ways.

The number of dead remains uncertain, and the reason illuminates the problem of historical evidence. The figure most commonly cited in early postwar sources derives from Tokyo Metropolitan Police and Fire Department reports and was reproduced by the Strategic Bombing Survey: about 84,000 killed (the survey's figure is 83,793) and some 41,000 injured. The Tokyo Raids and War Damage Center and some researchers argue that the true number exceeded 100,000. Their reasoning is that official counts were based on bodies recovered and identified or reported and could not capture people who were incinerated, washed away, or whose entire households perished; that the district included many transient residents, evacuees, and laborers, including Koreans, who may not have been registered; and that the accounting itself was done amid chaos. Neither estimate is an exact enumeration. A responsible account states both and explains what each counts, rather than choosing the larger figure for emphasis or the smaller for caution. By any estimate, the raid was among the most destructive single air attacks of the Second World War.

Press and official reports at the time understated the severity. Japanese authorities described the raid in terms that minimized the damage, and civilian morale reports show that survivors' accounts spread by word of mouth, contradicting official language. Survivors' later testimony, collected from the 1970s onward by groups of survivors and researchers, forms the main source for the experience of those who lived through the night. The next feature takes up one such account.

In his recollection, the initial warning and immediate need to leave turned into the problem confronting thousands of other families: routes rapidly became impassable as wooden buildings burned, air-raid preparations proved inadequate, and survival depended on movements made under extreme uncertainty. His words, as republished and translated long after 1945, should be attributed to their specific edition and translation when quoted. Compare his experience with police records, the U.S. Strategic Bombing Survey, and testimony from other parts of Tokyo, including people whose relatives did not survive. Later writing is both personal memory and deliberate public history; it should be evaluated on both terms.

Section 80.8 · Division II

The Firebombing of Japan's Cities: Osaka, Nagoya, Kobe, Yokohama, and Regional Centers

Tokyo was the first of a series. In the ten days following March 9, the XXI Bomber Command carried out major incendiary raids on Nagoya (March 11–12), Osaka (March 13–14), Kobe (March 16–17), and Nagoya again (March 18–19), burning, by American estimates, many square miles of built-up area in three cities that had not previously been struck on this scale. The supply of incendiary bombs then ran short, and for a time the command shifted attention to airfields in Kyushu to support the invasion of Okinawa. Incendiary attacks resumed in May and June on Nagoya, Tokyo again (including an attack on May 24–25 on districts not burned in March), Yokohama on May 29, Kawasaki, Osaka, and Kobe. By then the capture of Iwo Jima in March 1945 had provided an emergency landing field for damaged bombers and a base for long-range P-51 fighters that could escort them, further reducing American losses.

The effects differed from city to city. Osaka's canals and open spaces, Nagoya's wider streets and more dispersed industrial layout, and Kobe's setting between mountains and sea affected the spread of fires and the number of casualties. Not every raid achieved the effect of Tokyo's. Casualties in the March raids on other cities were generally lower than in Tokyo, in part because the fire-spreading conditions of March 9–10 were not matched, and in part because many residents had already left or reacted more quickly. Civil defense differed as well: some neighborhoods managed to organize escape routes and limited fire containment, others did not. Yokohama in May suffered severe damage to its central districts, and the port, a major site of the foreign community before the war and of the earthquake destruction of 1923 (Chapter 71), was again devastated.

From June 1945 the command turned to medium and small cities, where it conducted raids on a large scale against regional centers. These included, among many others, Fukui, Toyama, Hachiōji, Kumamoto, and Ōmuta. Toyama, struck on the night of August 1–2, was reported by American assessments to have been destroyed to an extraordinary degree, with the great majority of its built-up area burned. Planners selected these cities because they contained factories, rail junctions, or concentrations of the dispersed small industry, and because their wooden construction made them susceptible. American aircraft sometimes dropped leaflets, beginning in July 1945, naming cities that would be attacked and urging civilians to leave. The Japanese authorities sought to suppress the leaflets, and their effect on those who read them was uneven, since leaving a city required means, a destination, and a willingness to defy the instruction to stay and defend.

A small number of cities, among them Kyoto, Hiroshima, Kokura, and Niigata, were deliberately withheld from conventional incendiary attack for part of this period. The reasons, discussed in Section 80.15, involved their reservation as possible atomic targets and, in Kyoto's case, a decision by American officials to spare a city of great cultural significance. Residents of these cities had no way of knowing. Hiroshima's inhabitants in particular noticed that the city had been spared and some speculated about the reason; the explanation proved to be the opposite of reassurance.

By August 1945, according to the postwar Strategic Bombing Survey, American bombers had attacked about sixty-six Japanese cities with incendiaries, destroying on average roughly forty percent of their built-up areas, and the full count of cities struck was higher when smaller targets are included. The survey's figures are estimates produced by a victor's agency, and the Japanese government's own postwar compilation produced different numbers, but both agree on the scale of devastation. The destruction was therefore not confined to a few great raids: it was a sustained campaign that reached down to provincial towns and altered the lives of millions of people who had never seen an enemy soldier.

Section 80.9 · Division II

Urban Displacement, Civil Defense Failure, and the Destruction of Community Life

The incendiary campaign destroyed housing on a scale that exceeded any previous event in modern Japanese history except the 1923 earthquake. The Strategic Bombing Survey estimated that 2,094,374 buildings were destroyed across the sixty-six cities in its urban-economy statistical table, with 8,324,000 persons recorded as “dehoused” and 178 square miles of built-up area destroyed (roughly 43 percent of the built-up area of the sixty-six cities). The table separately estimates 2,250,000 buildings destroyed and 8,740,000 people dehoused for all Japan. Those different geographic denominators, and the distinction between all buildings and dwellings, must not be blurred. Those who lost their homes moved in with relatives, built shelters from burned corrugated metal and scrap wood, or left for the countryside. In the ruins of the cities, residents lived in huts and trenches, and by mid-1945 the populations of many major cities were a fraction of their prewar size.

Bombing destroyed more than buildings. It killed or scattered the neighbors who made up the associations that administered rations and civil defense, and it destroyed the records on which administration depended, including household registers and ration lists. Family workshops, the basis of much of the economy of the cities, were burned along with their tools and stocks. Schools, clinics, and hospitals were destroyed or crowded with the injured, medical supplies were exhausted, and doctors and nurses who survived were overwhelmed. Distribution networks for food and fuel, already strained, broke down in many urban districts. Bereavement was pervasive: many families lost members, and many people did not know what had happened to relatives and searched among the dead and the living.

The failure of civil defense should be understood as structural, not simply as a lack of effort. Policy had told residents to stay and fight fires, and had relied on volunteer neighborhood associations and short-handed fire brigades. These measures could not stop mass incendiary attack. Shelters offered protection from blast and splinters but not from firestorms, and evacuation, encouraged late and incompletely, was possible mainly for those with resources and connections. Postwar assessments by both American and Japanese analysts concluded that the system was inadequate to the scale of attack. Individual acts of courage and improvisation were common, and many lives were saved by neighbors' actions, but the framework within which they acted had been designed for a different war.

Precision about the term "collapse" matters. The devastation of cities did not mean that Japan's national systems failed everywhere or at once. Much of the main railway network, according to the Strategic Bombing Survey, continued to operate through the summer of 1945, carrying coal, food, and refugees, and the American planners turned to attacks on rail targets and the mining of coastal waters only in the final months. Government ministries, the police, and the military continued to function, and rural administration was largely intact. The destruction was concentrated in cities, where local administration, housing, medical care, and distribution were shattered, while the national structure remained in place. This unevenness helps explain both the severity of the civilian suffering and the fact that the government continued to operate and, for a time, to resist surrender.

The consequences extended beyond the war. Civilians killed or injured in air raids received no compensation comparable to that provided for former servicemen and their families in the postwar period, a disparity that survivors' groups, including those who gathered Tokyo testimony, have contested for decades. Those who lost homes and livelihoods entered the postwar period without resources, a condition examined in Chapter 85.

Cause and Effect

Blockade, Mobilization, Bombing, and the Weakening of Everyday Survival

Long-term causes

Dependence on imported food, fuel, and raw materials; an economy organized around heavy industry and empire; a civil-defense doctrine built on local mutual aid; dense wooden urban construction; a political system that gave the military priority over civilian supply.

Short-term causes

The loss of merchant shipping from 1943–1945; the capture of the Marianas in 1944; conscription of farm and factory labor; the decision to use low-altitude incendiary attack in 1945; the continued refusal of Japan's leadership to end the war.

Event or transformation

Between 1944 and 1945, shortages deepened, cities burned, and the state's capacity to feed, house, and protect urban populations broke down, while national institutions continued to function.

Immediate effects

Millions homeless; reduced rations and malnutrition; family separation; destroyed workshops and records; shattered local administration; mass bereavement.

Long-term consequences and historical complexity

The destruction shaped the postwar food crisis, the war-orphan problem, the rebuilding of Japanese cities, and the struggle of civilian victims for recognition. No single factor explains the outcome. Blockade without bombing would have strained supply; bombing without prior shortage would have met a better-provisioned population; and mobilization policy determined who bore the costs. These processes reinforced one another, and their effects fell unevenly on city and countryside, rich and poor, Japanese and colonial subjects.

Section 80.10 · Division III — Okinawa: The Battle, Civilian Mobilization, and the Destruction of a Society

Okinawa before the Invasion: Ryukyuan History, Militarization, and Civilian Evacuation

The Battle of Okinawa cannot be understood without the history of the people on whom it fell. The Ryukyu Kingdom, centered at Shuri on Okinawa Island, had been a maritime trading state linking China, Japan, Korea, and Southeast Asia, with its own court, language family, religious practices, and arts (Chapters 32 and 33). After the Satsuma domain's invasion in 1609 the kingdom remained a tributary of China while also being subordinated to Satsuma and, through it, to the Tokugawa order (Chapter 48). In 1879 the Meiji government abolished the kingdom, removed its king, and organized the islands as Okinawa Prefecture. For the next decades Okinawa was governed differently from the other prefectures: its institutions, land system, and representation were adjusted slowly, and conscription and electoral rights arrived later than on the main islands.

Okinawans were incorporated into the Japanese state through schooling, language policy, and ideology of imperial loyalty (kōminka). Schools taught standard Japanese and discouraged the use of Ryukyuan languages, and in some settings students who spoke local speech were punished or made to wear tags. Okinawan migrants to Osaka and other industrial centers encountered discrimination in housing and employment. In the 1920s a collapse in sugar prices produced severe rural poverty, and many Okinawans emigrated to Hawaii, South America, and the Japanese mainland. These conditions fostered, in different people, both a desire to prove their standing as Japanese and a sense of being treated as inferior. By 1944 the prefecture had a population of roughly half a million, most of them farmers, fishermen, and townspeople whose daily life remained shaped by local custom, family tombs, and religious practice distinct from the mainland.

The military buildup began in earnest in 1944. The Army established the 32nd Army in March 1944, and from the summer, reinforcements arrived, including units transferred from Manchuria and China. The army's commander from August 1944 was Lt. Gen. Ushijima Mitsuru, with Lt. Gen. Chō Isamu as chief of staff and Col. Yahara Hiromichi as senior operations officer. The army built airfields and defensive positions, and in doing so mobilized thousands of Okinawan civilians for labor, requisitioned buildings, schools, and food, and imposed burdens on a population that now had to support many tens of thousands of additional troops on an island of limited resources. In March 1945 the army conscripted tens of thousands of Okinawan men, approximately between seventeen and forty-five, into local defense units (bōeitai), and mobilized secondary-school students, male and female, into auxiliary units.

Evacuation of civilians was attempted but partial. In the summer of 1944 the government decided to move elderly people, women, and children from Okinawa to Kyushu and Taiwan, and some tens of thousands left. The effort suffered a major setback on August 22, 1944, when an American submarine sank the evacuee ship Tsushima Maru near the Ryukyus, killing well over a thousand people, including several hundred schoolchildren. Evacuation by sea became dangerous, and many families were reluctant to leave land, tombs, and relatives. Authorities also moved some residents from the south to the northern part of Okinawa, but facilities were inadequate. On October 10, 1944, a large American carrier raid destroyed most of Naha, the prefectural capital, burning much of its historic urban fabric and revealing how little air defense the island had. Hundreds of thousands of civilians remained on the island when the invasion came.

Okinawan civilians were therefore vulnerable by design: they were integrated into the defense effort as laborers, auxiliaries, and ideological subjects, but denied adequate means of escape. The 32nd Army's plan, as Section 80.11 explains, was to bleed American forces in a prolonged defense, and its leaders did not make the protection of civilians a priority. Some officers and soldiers also viewed Okinawans with suspicion, a legacy of the prefecture's status and of the differences in language and custom, and this suspicion shaped events during the battle.

Section 80.11 · Division III

Operation Iceberg and the Battle of Okinawa, April–June 1945

The American operation to seize Okinawa, code-named Iceberg, was the largest amphibious assault of the Pacific War. It was intended to provide airfields and harbors for the planned invasion of Kyushu, and the invasion force included the U.S. Tenth Army under Lt. Gen. Simon Bolivar Buckner Jr., made up of Army and Marine divisions, supported by the Fifth Fleet and by a British carrier force. After the capture of the Kerama Islands to the west in late March, American troops landed on April 1, 1945, on the Hagushi beaches on the island's west coast. They met almost no resistance at the beaches and quickly seized the Yontan and Kadena airfields.

The absence of opposition at the beach reflected deliberate Japanese strategy. The 32nd Army, with its senior officers divided on tactics, had decided against contesting the landing and instead prepared a defense in depth in the southern part of the island. The central position was the Shuri Line, a belt of fortified ridges north of Shuri and Naha, where soldiers fought from caves, tunnels, and the island's distinctive tortoiseshell-shaped tombs, using the terrain of coral limestone ridges and ravines to shelter from American naval and aerial bombardment. The aim was to inflict heavy casualties, delay the American advance, and gain time for the defense of the home islands. Offensives that some officers favored, notably attacks in April and early May, failed with severe losses to the Japanese forces, which had less artillery and air support than the Americans.

The fighting in the south was extraordinarily intense. American forces advanced slowly against ridges such as those near Kakazu, Maeda, and Sugar Loaf, fighting in rain and mud that turned the landscape into a quagmire littered with corpses. The bombardment, in which naval gunfire, aircraft, and artillery poured an enormous weight of explosives on a small area, came to be remembered by Okinawans as a "typhoon of steel" (tetsu no bōfū). The battle lasted far longer than American planners had anticipated, and the fighting at sea added to the toll. Between April and June, Japanese naval and army air forces mounted a series of mass attacks, called Kikusui, using hundreds of aircraft, many flown as kamikaze suicide planes, against the American fleet. They sank or disabled dozens of ships and killed roughly five thousand American sailors, making Okinawa the costliest campaign of the war for the U.S. Navy. On April 7 the battleship Yamato, sent on a one-way mission toward Okinawa, was sunk by American aircraft before it could reach the island, with the loss of the great majority of its crew.

Strategic analysis of the battle has centered on the decisions of both sides. American commanders chose to advance steadily into the Japanese fortifications rather than land behind them, a choice debated by contemporaries and historians. The Japanese leadership chose to prolong the fight, rather than conserve forces or seek an earlier end, in line with the army's broader strategy of attrition, and in late May decided to withdraw from the Shuri Line to the southern end of the island, a decision that moved the battle into the area where most civilians had taken refuge. Chapters 81 and 90 return to the strategic significance of Okinawa, but for this chapter the central fact is that a long battle fought on an inhabited island meant that its people could not stand aside from it.

Section 80.12 · Division III

Okinawan Students, the Himeyuri Corps, and Civilian Service in Battle

The 32nd Army mobilized students of Okinawa's secondary schools in the weeks before the landing. Male students, generally between fourteen and nineteen, were organized into units, including the Blood and Iron Student Corps (Tekketsu Kinnōtai), assigned to communications, supply, and combat duties, and many died in the fighting. Female students were assigned to nursing and support work in army hospitals and field units. The best known of these groups is the Himeyuri Student Corps, the name given in memory to students and teachers from two schools in Shuri and Naha: the Okinawa First Prefectural Girls' High School and the Okinawa Women's Normal School. The name, meaning "lily princess," was taken from a combination of the two schools' publications and became associated with the group after the war.

According to the Himeyuri Peace Museum, 240 students and teachers were mobilized from the two schools in late March 1945 and assigned to the Okinawa Army Hospital, whose branch positions were in caves and tunnels near Haebaru, south of Naha. Their work was exhausting and dangerous. They carried water and food, cared for wounded soldiers, disposed of amputated limbs and the bodies of the dead, and assisted in rudimentary surgery in caves with little light, ventilation, or medicine. The dead and wounded were numerous as the Shuri Line fighting intensified, and the cave hospitals were repeatedly under bombardment. As the front line collapsed in late May, the hospital withdrew southward, and the students moved with it amid the retreat.

On June 18, 1945, the army disbanded the student corps, releasing the students from duty in the midst of the final fighting, with American forces closing in and little protection for those who left. According to survivors' accounts preserved by the museum, the students were sent out of the caves with little guidance, into areas under fire, and many died in the following days, some in a cave at Ihara that was attacked on June 19. The Himeyuri Peace Museum's records state that 136 of the 240 mobilized students and teachers died. A separate figure, 227, refers to the students and teachers of the two schools who died, a larger group that includes people who were not part of the mobilized hospital group but served in other units or died elsewhere. These numbers describe different categories and must not be confused.

The Himeyuri became one of the most prominent symbols of Okinawan wartime suffering, and the interpretation of their story has been debated. In the early postwar decades a memorial was built at the site and the group's story was told in books and films, sometimes in terms that emphasized the students' loyalty and sacrifice. Survivors, who established the museum in 1989, have emphasized instead the fear, the waste of young lives, and the responsibility of those who mobilized them, and have connected their testimony to a commitment against war. The case illustrates how memory can be shaped by those who tell it, and why testimony recorded at different times may emphasize different things. It also shows the point that the mobilization of schoolchildren was not unique to Okinawa but was more deadly there than in most places, because the children were in the battle zone.

Section 80.13 · Division III

Civilians under Fire: Refugees, Hunger, Coerced Suicides, and Military Violence

When the battle came, civilians moved in several directions at once. Some left Shuri and Naha for the north; many in the south sheltered in natural caves (gama) and family tombs, or in shelters dug in the hillsides. As the front moved south, the civilian population, joined by those retreating from the Shuri area, was pressed into the narrow southern end of the island, where the last fighting took place. They lived in caves without sanitation or sufficient food, frequently in darkness, with the sounds of bombardment overhead. Disease, especially malaria and dysentery, spread, and starvation killed the weakest. The burden of hunger was increased because the army had requisitioned food and because the fighting disrupted the harvest.

The causes of civilian death were varied, and responsible history must distinguish them. American naval gunfire, artillery, aircraft, and ground assault killed many civilians directly and destroyed the villages and farms on which they depended. Flamethrowers and explosives were used against caves, often without knowing who was inside. Disease and starvation killed many others, in the caves and later in the refugee camps. The Japanese military also killed Okinawan civilians, and the battle's civilian toll cannot be explained without this fact. Soldiers took food from civilians, evicted them from caves they wished to use, and in some cases killed those who were accused of spying or who seemed to threaten secrecy, including mothers whose infants cried. The use of Okinawan speech was sometimes treated as suspect, since the army distrusted people who spoke in ways soldiers could not understand. Survivors' testimony, collected by Okinawan researchers and municipal histories, documents such incidents, and Japanese historians have analyzed them as part of the pattern of violence in the battle.

The most debated category is group suicide (shūdan jiketsu), which some scholars and Okinawan survivors describe as coerced or compelled group death (kyōsei shūdan shi). In late March 1945, on the Kerama Islands, where American forces landed first, hundreds of civilians died in mass suicides and killings on islands such as Tokashiki and Zamami. Similar events took place on the main island, including at a cave called Chibichiri Gama in Yomitan in early April. Participants used grenades distributed by soldiers, razors, sticks, and other means, and in many cases family members killed one another. The causes were multiple. Years of schooling and propaganda had taught civilians that American troops would torture and kill them and that surrender was shameful. The presence of soldiers who distributed grenades and conveyed expectations of honorable death, and the circumstances of isolation and terror in which civilians found themselves, applied powerful pressure. Historians argue that these deaths cannot be described as simply voluntary, nor can the decisions of individuals under such conditions be reduced to orders; the evidence points to the interaction of ideology, military instruction, and fear.

The interpretation of group suicide has been a matter of political controversy in postwar Japan. In the 2000s disputes over how school textbooks should describe the role of the military in these deaths led to protests in Okinawa and to revisions of official screening decisions. For historians the central evidence is the testimony of survivors and the documentary record of military practice, and the scholarly consensus emphasizes military involvement and coercion, while acknowledging that circumstances differed from one location to another. The reader should note the difference between explaining the context in which people died and assigning legal blame to individuals, a distinction this chapter maintains.

Other groups were affected as well. Korean laborers brought to Okinawa for construction and military service, and women forced into military brothels, were among those who suffered and died, and their experiences, treated in the context of Chapter 79, are part of the human cost of the battle. Okinawan women faced the danger of sexual violence from soldiers, and research by Okinawan historians indicates that American troops committed rapes during and after the battle, with the scale a matter of continuing research; Japanese soldiers also committed violence against civilians. When American forces collected civilians into camps in the central and northern parts of the island, survivors recalled both relief at receiving food and medical care and continuing hardship from hunger, malaria, and crowded conditions. Memories of American treatment ranged widely, and generalizing from any single account risks distortion.

Section 80.14 · Division III

The Final Battle, Okinawan Losses, and the Meaning of the Island's Destruction

The withdrawal from the Shuri Line began in late May 1945, as American forces closed in on the Japanese headquarters beneath Shuri Castle. The castle, the symbol of the former Ryukyu Kingdom, had been shelled heavily and was destroyed, along with much of the surrounding historic district, and its loss was both a military fact and a cultural catastrophe. The army retreated to the southern end of the island, around Kiyan and Mabuni. There its remaining units fought a final series of battles amid crowded refugees. Because the retreat moved combat into the area where tens of thousands of civilians were sheltering, many historians have judged that it increased civilian casualties, even though some officers argued that the position was needed to prolong resistance.

On June 18 General Buckner was killed by Japanese artillery fire while observing the front. A few days later, on or about June 22, General Ushijima and General Chō took their own lives in a cave headquarters at Mabuni. Organized resistance largely ended in the following days, although fighting by scattered units and the hunting of survivors continued. The campaign had lasted about eighty days, and Okinawa Memorial Day, observed on June 23, commemorates the end of organized Japanese resistance. The island was then in American hands, and its civilians were under American military control, a situation that continued, with modifications, until 1972 (Chapters 90 and 99).

The scale of loss was enormous, and the figures require care. The U.S. Naval History and Heritage Command records 12,520 American killed or missing in the campaign, including 4,907 Navy personnel killed; these are not the same denominator as total U.S. casualties, which also include the wounded. An Okinawa Prefecture-based breakdown used in the Okinawa historical literature gives 188,136 Japanese-side dead: 65,908 Japanese soldiers from prefectures outside Okinawa and 122,228 people from Okinawa Prefecture, the latter including approximately 28,228 Okinawan-born military personnel and auxiliaries and an estimated 94,000 other local residents. The lines between civilian, conscripted worker, student auxiliary and uniformed service member were not always clear. The prefectural museum notes that some estimates of Okinawan civilian deaths exceed 100,000. These figures represent historically reconstructed categories, not a complete individual-level census.

The destruction extended beyond lives. Villages were erased, farmland was left unusable, cultural treasures of the kingdom and the prefecture, including archives, objects, and buildings, were destroyed, and families and community institutions that had maintained local traditions lost members. The loss of records complicated the postwar reconstruction of family and land registers. The island's survivors were, at the war's end, a population of survivors in camps whose homes and land were often occupied by military installations, a source of lasting grievance that Chapter 90 and later chapters trace.

Okinawan historical memory of the battle has been shaped by these facts. Memorials such as the Himeyuri Tower, the Cornerstone of Peace, which since 1995 has inscribed the names of the dead regardless of nationality or status, including Americans, Koreans, and Taiwanese, and the Okinawa Prefectural Peace Memorial Museum articulate a memory centered on civilian suffering and on opposition to war. This memory exists alongside, and sometimes in tension with, national narratives that emphasize the sacrifice of the nation's soldiers. The proverb often cited in Okinawan peace education, that life itself is the treasure (nuchi du takara), reflects a response to the experience. For the purposes of this chapter, the most important point is that Okinawa's history in 1945 cannot be folded into a general story of Japanese home-front suffering. It was a distinct society, with a distinct past, whose civilians were exposed to the full force of a ground war.

Section 80.15 · Division IV — Hiroshima, Nagasaki, Radiation, and the Human Consequences of Atomic Warfare

The Manhattan Project, Atomic Weapon Development, and the Decision to Use the Bomb

The atomic bomb grew out of scientific discoveries of the late 1930s, including the recognition that the nucleus of uranium could be split, releasing great energy. Fear that Germany might develop such a weapon led émigré physicists to urge the United States government to act, and in 1942 the Army took charge of a vast program, the Manhattan Engineer District, directed by Brig. Gen. Leslie Groves, with the physicist J. Robert Oppenheimer leading the weapons laboratory at Los Alamos. Production sites at Oak Ridge and Hanford produced fissile material, and the first test of a plutonium implosion device, code-named Trinity, took place in New Mexico on July 16, 1945. Japan also conducted research on nuclear weapons, but it was far from producing one and the effort played no role in the events of this chapter.

Decisions about use were made in 1945 within a small circle. President Harry Truman, who took office after Franklin Roosevelt's death in April, was briefed on the project soon after. A Target Committee met in May and considered cities that had not been heavily bombed, so that the weapon's effects could be clearly assessed; its list included Kyoto, Hiroshima, Yokohama, Kokura, and Niigata. Secretary of War Henry Stimson removed Kyoto from the list because of its cultural importance, and Nagasaki was added later. An Interim Committee chaired by Stimson, advised by scientists, met in late May and early June and recommended that the bomb be used against Japan as soon as possible, without prior warning, on a target that combined a military installation or war plant with surrounding workers' housing. A group of scientists associated with the Chicago laboratory submitted a report urging a demonstration, and some petitioned against use; these views did not prevail. The decision to deploy was embodied in an order of late July authorizing the first bomb to be dropped after about August 3, weather permitting.

Contemporary arguments for using the bomb centered on ending the war quickly and avoiding an invasion. American planning contemplated landings on Kyushu in November 1945 (Operation Olympic) and on the Kantō plain in the spring of 1946 (Operation Coronet), and the intensity of resistance at Iwo Jima and Okinawa shaped expectations of the costs. Estimates of potential casualties in the planning documents varied widely, and the much larger figures sometimes cited after the war are disputed by historians who have examined the records. Other considerations also appear in the documents: the desire to justify the enormous expenditure on the project, the wish to end the war before the Soviet Union, which had agreed to enter it, could gain a significant position in Asia, and the role of the weapon in postwar diplomacy. Historians disagree about the weight of these factors, and the debate is taken up in Section 80.19.

The Potsdam Declaration, issued on July 26 by the United States, Britain, and China, called on Japan to surrender unconditionally and warned of "prompt and utter destruction," while promising that the Japanese would not be enslaved or destroyed as a people. It did not mention the atomic bomb or explicitly address the emperor's status. The Japanese government's response, announced by Prime Minister Suzuki Kantarō in the press, used the word mokusatsu, which can be translated as "to treat with silent contempt" or "to withhold comment," and which the Allies took as a rejection. The meaning and effect of the response, and the Japanese leadership's simultaneous efforts to obtain Soviet mediation, belong to the surrender story in Chapter 81. What matters here is that no Japanese acceptance followed, and the order to use the bomb remained in force.

Students should distinguish what the documents show from later justification. The records of the Interim Committee, the Target Committee, and the wartime correspondence of the participants show what officials considered and decided in 1945. Later accounts, including Stimson's article of 1947 and memoirs written by participants, shaped public understanding and in some respects reframed the decision. Neither category can be ignored, but the documents from the time are the principal evidence for the reasoning at the time.

Section 80.16 · Division IV

Hiroshima, August 6, 1945: The First Atomic Bombing

Hiroshima, a city on the delta of the Ōta River facing the Inland Sea, was a military and administrative center. It had been the site of an army garrison since the Meiji period, a major port of embarkation at Ujina, and the headquarters of the Second General Army, responsible for the defense of southwestern Japan. Its population on the morning of the attack is usually estimated at somewhat over 300,000, including tens of thousands of soldiers, as well as students and laborers mobilized for war work, Korean residents and laborers, and a small number of Chinese, Southeast Asian students, and Allied prisoners of war. The city had experienced relatively little conventional bombing. Many residents had been mobilized for building demolition to create firebreaks, a task that placed thousands of young students outdoors in the city center early on August 6.

The B-29 Enola Gay, commanded by Col. Paul Tibbets, took off from Tinian shortly before three o'clock in the morning, accompanied by instrument and photographic aircraft. Weather scouts reported good conditions over Hiroshima. At about 8:15 a.m. local time the bomb, a uranium gun-type weapon known as Little Boy with an explosive yield estimated at about fifteen kilotons of TNT equivalent, was released and detonated at an altitude of roughly 600 meters above the city, near the Aioi Bridge, which had been the aiming point. An air-raid alert had sounded and been lifted earlier in the morning, and many people were outdoors or beginning their work.

The bomb produced three principal kinds of effect, which survivors and investigators experienced together. A flash of thermal radiation burned exposed skin and ignited materials at considerable distances, and surface temperatures near the hypocenter were extremely high. A blast wave destroyed or damaged nearly all buildings across a large area, collapsing wooden structures and hurling debris and glass. Ionizing radiation, mostly gamma rays and neutrons released in the first moments, penetrated bodies and shelters, causing injury whose consequences emerged over days, weeks, and years. Fires broke out across the city and merged, and a rain of large, dark droplets, later called black rain, fell on some districts later in the morning, carrying soot and radioactive material. By the end of the day roughly four to five square miles of the city were in ruins, and the great majority of the buildings in the area had been destroyed or burned.

The human toll is uncertain in its details and large in any estimate. A figure of roughly 70,000 to 80,000 deaths within the first day or so is often cited. The city of Hiroshima and Hiroshima Prefecture report an estimate of about 140,000 deaths by the end of December 1945, with an uncertainty of about 10,000, including those who died of burns, injuries, and radiation illness in the following months. These estimates include military personnel as well as civilians, whose proportions cannot be precisely determined, and rely on reconstructions from partial records, since many of the city's records, and many of its families, were destroyed. Among the dead were large numbers of mobilized schoolchildren working outdoors; Korean residents and laborers, with different Korean estimates: the Korean Atomic Bomb Victims Association’s 1972 reconstruction cited approximately 30,000 deaths among roughly 50,000 exposed Koreans in Hiroshima, while a separate memorial estimate has been reported around 20,000. These are retrospective estimates with incomplete denominators, not exact confirmed counts; Chinese and Southeast Asian students; and a number of American prisoners of war held in the city.

The medical and relief response was improvised in the midst of ruin. Most of the city's physicians and nurses were killed or incapacitated, and most hospitals were destroyed or damaged. Survivors walked toward the edge of the city, to the hills and the rivers, and were treated in makeshift facilities at schools, shrines, and the surviving hospitals. Army units from Ujina and elsewhere, and boats on the rivers, assisted in rescue and transport. Rail service into and out of the city resumed in part within a day or two, and relief trains carried the injured to neighboring towns. The authorities and the public had no ready understanding of what had occurred: the first reports spoke of a new kind of bomb, and the nature of the weapon was announced by President Truman on August 6 (Washington time). A Japanese scientific team, including physicist Nishina Yoshio, visited Hiroshima within days and confirmed the character of the attack.

Section 80.17 · Division IV

Nagasaki, August 9, 1945: The Second Atomic Bombing

Nagasaki, on Kyushu's west coast, was a port with a long history of foreign contact (Chapter 48) and, after the Meiji period, a center of shipbuilding and arms manufacture by Mitsubishi. It was also home to the largest Catholic community in Japan, concentrated in the Urakami district, whose members descended from Christians who had survived centuries of persecution (Chapter 47). Its population was roughly 240,000, including mobilized students, Korean and Chinese laborers, and a camp of Allied prisoners of war. Unlike Hiroshima's flat delta, the city lay in a series of valleys separated by hills, with its harbor and older central districts in one valley and the Urakami valley, containing the arms works and many homes, to the north.

The mission of August 9 was nearly abandoned. The B-29 Bockscar, piloted by Maj. Charles Sweeney and carrying a plutonium implosion bomb known as Fat Man, took off from Tinian before dawn. The primary target was the arsenal city of Kokura, but haze and smoke, partly from fires set during a conventional raid on nearby Yawata the day before, obscured the aiming point over several passes. With fuel running low, the crew proceeded to the secondary target, Nagasaki, where a gap in the clouds allowed a visual drop. The bomb exploded at about 11:02 a.m. at an altitude of roughly 500 meters over the Urakami valley, several kilometers from the intended aim point in the city center. Its yield is estimated at about twenty-one kilotons.

The hills determined the pattern of damage. The blast and fires devastated the Urakami valley, destroying the Mitsubishi arms and steel works, the Nagasaki Medical College and its hospital, schools, and the residential districts, including the Urakami Cathedral and the Catholic community around it, thousands of whose members died. The central district, shielded by intervening hills, suffered much less. This meant that a bomb larger than the one used at Hiroshima caused a smaller area of destruction, and also that the extent of damage depended on topography as well as on the weapon. Fires spread through residential areas, and survivors, many severely burned, sought refuge in the hills and on the harbor's shores.

The dead included Japanese civilians, mobilized students and workers, soldiers, Korean forced laborers and residents, Chinese laborers, and Allied prisoners of war; the numbers of Koreans and other non-Japanese are less well established than those for Japanese residents, though estimates run to the thousands. The Nagasaki Atomic Bomb Museum and municipal authorities give a retrospective estimate of about 74,000 deaths by the end of December 1945 (73,884 in one calculation), with considerable uncertainty. Medical personnel who survived, among them physicians at the destroyed medical college, treated the injured under makeshift conditions. One of them, the Catholic radiologist Nagai Takashi, who was already suffering from leukemia, became known for his writings about the city and his care for victims. Relief efforts were hampered by destroyed facilities and by the sheer number of the injured, and the city was left without functioning hospitals in the affected district.

The two bombings should not be treated as repetitions. The weapons were of different design, the cities had different geographies and populations, and the effects on residential areas, industry, and religious communities differed. Each city also has its own memory: Hiroshima, often associated with the call for abolition of nuclear weapons, and Nagasaki, whose Catholic and port history shaped its own commemoration. Section 80.19 compares the casualty categories carefully.

Section 80.18 · Division IV

Radiation Exposure, Acute Illness, Medical Response, and the Experiences of Hibakusha

Medical analysis distinguishes among the injuries the bombs caused. Blast injuries included crush injuries, lacerations from flying glass, and trauma from being thrown or struck by debris. Thermal injuries included flash burns on exposed skin, sometimes in patterns that matched the clothing worn at the instant of the flash, and burns from the fires that followed. Many people died of these injuries on the first day or within a few days. Radiation exposure caused a third set of effects, whose severity depended on dose, in turn determined by distance from the hypocenter and by shielding. People near the hypocenter received lethal doses; those farther away received lower doses that produced illness without death or few effects at all. Not all survivors were exposed to the same degree, and it would be wrong to suppose that radiation affected every survivor equally.

Acute radiation syndrome, the illness caused by high doses, appeared in the first hours to weeks. Typical symptoms included nausea and vomiting, diarrhea, fever, loss of hair, bleeding under the skin and from the gums, and susceptibility to infection as radiation damaged the bone marrow's ability to produce blood cells. Many victims died in the second to eighth weeks after the bombing. The cause was at first mysterious to doctors and patients. Some physicians suspected poison gas or infection, and public rumors circulated that the cities would be uninhabitable for decades. Scientific investigation soon showed otherwise: residual radiation, from fallout and from radioactivity induced in soil and materials, was highest near the hypocenter and in areas of black rain, but declined rapidly, and residents returned and rebuilt in both cities. The claim that Hiroshima and Nagasaki were permanently uninhabitable is incorrect, though the claim that residual radiation caused no harm to those who entered the cities or lived under the fallout is also not supportable, and research on exposure from these sources continues.

The medical response was constrained by the destruction of the cities' medical systems and by the state of knowledge. Surviving staff treated burns and wounds with what they had, and early understanding of radiation sickness was limited. In the following weeks Japanese scientists and, from September, American survey teams studied the cities. After the Allied occupation began, the press and publishing code imposed by the occupation authorities restricted public discussion of the bombings' effects until 1952 (Chapter 82), and an American-run organization, the Atomic Bomb Casualty Commission, established in 1947, studied the survivors' health, a program that provided important scientific data but offered survivors little treatment and was criticized by some of them. Its successor, the Radiation Effects Research Foundation, continues to study the cohort.

The longer-term health effects are better documented than their early understanding. Studies have found elevated rates of leukemia among survivors, beginning within a few years and peaking in the early 1950s, and increased rates of various solid cancers appearing over subsequent decades. Cataracts and, among children exposed in the womb, developmental effects including small head size and intellectual disability were also observed. The risk depended on dose and age at exposure, and most survivors did not develop these conditions. These findings have informed international radiation-protection standards. Their statistical character means that they describe populations, and that for an individual survivor the cause of a particular illness cannot generally be determined with certainty.

The people affected came to be called hibakusha, a term that originally referred to those exposed to the bombs and was later extended to cover several categories recognized by law: those who were in the cities at the time, those who entered the cities soon afterward, those who aided victims, and those exposed in the womb. Hibakusha faced social difficulties as well as medical ones. Scarred or ill survivors encountered discrimination in employment and marriage, fueled by fear that radiation was contagious or hereditary, a fear that scientific evidence did not support. Some concealed their status. Korean survivors, many of whom returned to Korea or remained in Japan, faced additional obstacles in obtaining recognition and assistance. The development of legal relief for survivors, which began in the 1950s, and the growth of the survivors' movement, are subjects for later chapters, and this section is limited to the immediate experience and the introduction of the long-term consequences.

Historical Vocabulary
Historical Vocabulary: Radiation, Hibakusha, and Military Targeting

Ionizing radiation is energy, in the form of particles or high-energy waves such as gamma rays, that can strip electrons from atoms and damage living cells. In an atomic explosion most of the exposure that caused acute illness came from initial radiation, released within roughly the first minute, while residual radiation from fallout and induced radioactivity was a smaller and more localized source. The hypocenter is the point on the ground directly beneath the explosion. Acute radiation syndrome is the illness caused by a large dose delivered in a short time, as distinct from the later, statistical increase in cancer risk caused by lower doses.

Hibakusha (literally "explosion-affected people") is the Japanese term for survivors of the atomic bombings; its legal scope was defined in postwar relief legislation. The term should not be used as though it described a single uniform experience. Military targeting refers to the choice of what to attack in war. The distinction between military and civilian targets, central to ethical and legal discussion, was blurred in area bombing, where the target was a district containing both industry and homes. Students should be alert to the fact that "military target" in 1945 documents could mean a factory, a headquarters, or an urban area defined by its industrial function, and should read each source's usage carefully.

Section 80.19 · Division IV

Conventional and Atomic Destruction: Military Strategy, Humanitarian Consequences, and Historical Debate

Comparing the four catastrophes in this chapter requires discipline about definitions. The Tokyo raid of March 9–10 killed on the order of 84,000 to over 100,000 people, depending on the estimate, in a single night, using conventional incendiary weapons. The Battle of Okinawa, lasting about eighty days, killed an estimated 188,000 Japanese and Okinawan military personnel and civilians together, plus some 12,000 Americans, with Okinawan civilian deaths estimated at about 94,000 to over 100,000. Hiroshima's deaths through the end of 1945 are estimated at about 140,000, and Nagasaki's at about 74,000. These numbers count different things, over different time periods, with different mixtures of civilian and military dead and different degrees of certainty. The data study below sets them side by side with those distinctions stated.

A second comparison concerns not only the number killed but the relationship between the form of attack and the warning or escape available to civilians. A bomber formation approaching Tokyo could trigger alarms, yet nighttime incendiaries transformed narrow lanes and rivers into hazards that neither routine drills nor improvised shelters could control. Okinawa was different: many civilians endured weeks of artillery fire, military requisitioning, forced movement and confinement among combatants, and there was often no safe route out of the battle zone. Hiroshima and Nagasaki experienced a concentrated release of energy from single weapons; most people near their hypocenters could take no meaningful defensive action, and some apparently uninjured survivors later developed radiation illness. These distinctions help explain why casualty counts alone do not measure the character or duration of suffering.

At the strategic level, American leaders presented the air campaign as a way to undermine Japan’s war production and force an end to hostilities. The Japanese state, meanwhile, maintained military objectives that imposed enormous additional burdens on its own population, including an unwillingness to reduce military demands as transport and food distribution failed. Neither side’s official statement of intent should be confused with an independently measured outcome. The U.S. Strategic Bombing Survey documented physical damage and interviews with Japanese officials, but its claims about when Japan might have surrendered without particular attacks are counterfactual judgments rather than testable counts. The enduring argument over alternatives therefore cannot be settled by citing a bombing tonnage or projected invasion casualty estimate in isolation.

Historically responsible comparison also requires keeping social position visible. An urban worker without rural relatives faced different choices from a farmer with access to food; a Korean laborer in Hiroshima faced colonial restrictions alongside the bomb; a mobilized Okinawan student might be recorded in a military category even though brought into the war from a school. The same caution applies to public memory: Americans, metropolitan Japanese, Okinawans and Korean survivors developed different commemorative institutions and priorities. A chapter on civilian devastation must acknowledge the documented crimes committed elsewhere by Japan’s empire, as Chapter 79 does, without making victims of later attacks responsible for those acts merely because they were Japanese. It must also distinguish responsibility for the decisions to bomb, to continue resistance, and to mobilize civilians. Those are separate questions that require different kinds of evidence.

Historical Debate

Strategic Bombing, Atomic Weapons, Alternatives, and Responsibility

The question: Why did the United States use atomic weapons against Japan in August 1945, and were there realistic alternatives for ending the war? How should historians weigh the atomic bombings against the conventional bombing campaign and against the role of Soviet entry?

Interpretation A — Military necessity and the avoidance of invasion: Many historians, following the account given by American officials after the war, argue that the bombs were used to end a war that Japan's leadership showed no sign of ending, and that they saved American and Japanese lives that an invasion or prolonged blockade would have cost. They point to the fierce resistance at Iwo Jima and Okinawa, to the Japanese military's plans for a decisive defense of Kyushu, to the continued refusal to accept the Potsdam terms, and to the divisions within the Japanese government that made a negotiated end uncertain. They note that American leaders did not know whether alternatives would succeed.
Interpretation B — Alternatives, diplomacy, and the Soviet factor: Other historians argue that Japan was already near defeat, that modification of the surrender terms to guarantee the imperial institution, continued blockade and bombing, or the expected Soviet entry might have ended the war without atomic weapons, and that some American leaders also hoped the bomb would influence the Soviet Union. Some scholars, such as Tsuyoshi Hasegawa, emphasize the Soviet declaration of war as an event that shaped the Japanese leadership's decisions at least as much as the bombs. The Strategic Bombing Survey's judgment that Japan would likely have surrendered before the end of 1945 is cited by this group, though others dispute it.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on American records of the Interim Committee and military planning, intercepted Japanese diplomatic messages, the records of Japanese government and military deliberations, postwar memoirs, and estimates of invasion casualties. They differ in how they weigh these sources, how far they credit postwar justifications, and how they assess counterfactuals. Many recent works emphasize that several factors operated together and that a single explanation is unlikely to be adequate. The question of whether the atomic bombs or Soviet entry was more decisive for the Japanese leadership is treated further in Chapter 81.

Why it matters: The answer shapes how historians understand responsibility, the beginning of the nuclear age and the Cold War, the memory of the war in Japan and the United States, and the standards by which attacks on civilians are judged. Counterfactual claims, that an invasion would have cost a certain number of lives or that a particular diplomatic step would have worked, cannot be verified, and students should treat them as arguments, not as established facts.

Student task: Select one piece of documentary evidence from this chapter that supports each interpretation, and explain what additional evidence would help to decide between them. Then explain why the moral evaluation of civilian bombing requires more than an assessment of military effectiveness.

The Soviet Union declared war on Japan on August 8, effective the next day, and began a large offensive in Manchuria and elsewhere on August 9, the same day as the Nagasaki bombing. Both the atomic bombings and the Soviet entry belong to the transition into Chapter 81, where the sequence of Japanese deliberations, the imperial decision, and the surrender are traced in full. This chapter has shown the condition of the country at that moment: its cities burned, its islands in the south devastated, its people hungry and exhausted, and its government still unwilling or unable to end the war.

Section 80.20 · Division V — Historical Synthesis and Chapter Review

The Destruction of Japan's Home Front: Historical Synthesis and Review

How did the progressive destruction of Japan's ability to sustain war become a humanitarian catastrophe for civilians, and why did that catastrophe unfold differently across Japan's cities and Okinawa? The answer begins with the relationship between military strategy and civilian vulnerability. Japan's war economy depended on overseas resources and on a population that was expected to absorb whatever the state demanded. When American naval and air power severed the sea lanes, the state could not supply that population, but it did not reduce its demands. When American air power reached the home islands, it met a civil-defense system built on the assumption that fires could be fought by neighbors with buckets, in cities built of wood. The American decision to turn to incendiary area bombing, taken in pursuit of military aims and in a legal and moral climate in which all major belligerents had attacked cities, translated that vulnerability into mass death.

The differences among the chapter's cases are as important as the connections. In the bombed cities, the principal experiences were fire, displacement, and the loss of neighborhoods, while railways and ministries continued to function. On Okinawa, a ground battle fought across an inhabited island, combined with the militarization of a population treated with suspicion by the army that mobilized it, produced a proportion of civilian deaths far exceeding that in any bombed city, and involved violence by all armies, including the Japanese. At Hiroshima and Nagasaki, single weapons destroyed city centers within moments and added radiation to the effects of blast and fire, with consequences that lasted for generations. Each case requires its own explanation, and none should be allowed to stand for the others.

Responsibility is distributed across several actors and levels. The Japanese leadership's decisions to continue the war, to mobilize students and civilians, to prolong the battle on Okinawa, and to refuse the Allied ultimatum are part of the explanation. American decisions on bombing methods and on the use of atomic weapons are part of it as well, and the ethical evaluation of those decisions remains contested. Historians distinguish between explaining decisions in their context and justifying them, and this chapter has tried to do the first without presupposing the second. It has also tried to attend to the evidence: casualty figures with their definitions, documents with their authors' interests, and testimony with its strengths and limits.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Japanese Civilian Life from the Early War Years to August 1945

What Changed

  • Rations shrank and were increasingly replaced by substitutes; the black market and buying trips became essential to survival.
  • Schooling gave way to labor mobilization, and children were separated from their parents by evacuation.
  • Home islands once beyond the enemy's reach became the target of sustained bombing, and about two million dwellings were destroyed.
  • Civilians in Okinawa were drawn directly into combat, and two cities were destroyed by atomic weapons.
  • The expectation that the state could protect and feed the population collapsed in the cities.

What Remained Continuous

  • The neighborhood associations, police surveillance, and the basic framework of state control continued to operate.
  • The government's refusal to end the war and its reliance on appeals to sacrifice persisted.
  • National railways, ministries, and rural administration largely continued to function.
  • Colonial subjects and foreign laborers remained at the bottom of the labor hierarchy and were excluded from protection.
  • Inequalities of region, class, and gender shaped who suffered most.

Who experienced change most directly? Urban residents, schoolchildren, and Okinawans experienced the most abrupt changes. Who experienced greater continuity? Rural households often continued familiar routines of farming, though under mounting shortages, until the war's end, and officials continued to function. Why the pattern matters: It shows that the destruction was uneven and that the "collapse" of 1945 was a collapse of particular systems in particular places, and not a uniform breakdown of the entire society.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the destruction of Japan's wartime society between 1942 and August 1945 through the experiences of those who endured shortages, evacuation, incendiary bombing, the Battle of Okinawa, and the atomic bombings. Its central argument is that military defeat, blockade, economic shortage, and Allied air attack steadily undermined the material foundations of civilian life, while a state unable to supply or protect its population continued to demand sacrifice.

On the home front, Japan's dependence on imports and the loss of merchant shipping reduced the supply of food, fuel, and materials, while the military's priority claims and the conscription of millions of men drew resources and labor from the civilian economy. Rationing, established in 1941 and 1942, delivered shrinking and increasingly substituted allowances, and households survived by gardens, buying trips, and the black market. Wartime deprivation was severe and growing but was distinct from the postwar food crisis that followed the surrender.

Women, students, and colonial and foreign laborers filled the places of men in factories and farms, working long hours in difficult and dangerous conditions. The neighborhood associations administered rationing, drills, and salvage, and the state's civil-defense policy told residents to stay and fight fires. Evacuation of schoolchildren separated families, schooling was suspended, and health and morale deteriorated, with differences between urban and rural survival growing as the war continued.

The air war began with the Doolittle Raid but became a sustained campaign only after the capture of the Marianas gave the United States bases for the B-29. High-altitude precision bombing disappointed, and in 1945 General LeMay shifted to low-altitude night incendiary attack on urban areas, motivated by the belief that Japanese cities and their dispersed industry were vulnerable to fire. The great Tokyo raid of March 9–10 burned some forty square kilometers and killed, by different estimates, between about 84,000 and over 100,000 people, and the campaign extended to dozens of cities.

The bombing left millions homeless and destroyed neighborhoods, workshops, records, and medical services. Civil defense, designed for small fires, was overwhelmed. Yet some national systems, including much of the railway network and government administration, continued to function, so that the destruction was concentrated in cities and the state remained able to continue the war. Civilian victims of the raids received far less postwar recognition than military dead.

Okinawa's experience was distinct. A former kingdom annexed in 1879 and subjected to assimilation policy, it was militarized in 1944, with civilians mobilized as laborers and auxiliaries while evacuation was incomplete. The American invasion of April 1945 led to an eighty-day battle in which the Japanese 32nd Army fought a prolonged defense, kamikaze attacks inflicted heavy damage on the American fleet, and the island's civilians were caught between armies. Students such as those of the Himeyuri Corps served in the battle, and many died.

Okinawan civilians died from bombardment, starvation, disease, coerced group suicide, and violence by Japanese soldiers. Estimates of civilian deaths, about 94,000 in the prefectural count and above 100,000 in other estimates, depend on how civilians and auxiliaries are defined and on the loss of records. The destruction of Shuri Castle and of villages, archives, and families marked a cultural as well as a human catastrophe, and its memory continues to shape Okinawan politics.

The atomic bombings of Hiroshima on August 6 and Nagasaki on August 9 followed a program developed by the Manhattan Project and a decision made by a small circle of American officials in 1945. They destroyed city centers by blast, heat, fire, and radiation, and killed by the end of 1945 an estimated 140,000 and 74,000 people respectively, including Koreans and other non-Japanese. Survivors, the hibakusha, suffered acute radiation illness, long-term health effects, and social discrimination, though the cities were not permanently uninhabitable.

Historians continue to debate the atomic decision, weighing arguments from military necessity and the avoidance of invasion against arguments about alternatives, diplomacy, and the Soviet declaration of war. Counterfactual claims cannot be verified, and the moral evaluation of civilian bombing requires more than assessment of effectiveness. This chapter has ended with a devastated home front in August 1945; Chapter 81 explains the surrender, the collapse of the empire, and the immediate consequences of defeat.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows what total war meant for those who did not fight it as soldiers. The destruction of Japan's cities, the Battle of Okinawa, and the atomic bombings were not separate catastrophes but parts of a single process in which the capacity of a society to protect its members was progressively exhausted, and in which decisions by governments on both sides translated military aims into civilian deaths. Understanding that process requires both strategy and experience, and the chapter has tried to hold them together.

The chapter also shows the importance of precision. Casualty figures, source types, and categories of victims must be defined before they are compared. Okinawa must not be absorbed into a general story of Japanese suffering; Korean and other colonial subjects must not disappear from accounts of bombed cities; conventional bombing must not be minimized because atomic weapons were more terrible in their effects. Historical interpretation of the atomic decision remains contested, and the evidence does not allow certainty about what would have happened had choices been different.

The events of this chapter shaped what followed. They produced the homelessness and hunger of the first postwar years, the problem of war orphans, the struggle of civilian victims for recognition, the long politics of Okinawa under American administration and its bases, and the movement of hibakusha against nuclear weapons. They also shaped how Japanese and others remember the war, with memory in Hiroshima, Nagasaki, Tokyo, and Okinawa each emphasizing different experiences and different lessons.

Chapter 81 turns to the surrender itself: the deliberations among Japan's leaders, the intervention of the emperor, the collapse of the empire, and the return of millions of soldiers and settlers. Students who understand the condition of the home front in August 1945 will be better placed to see why those events took the form they did, and why the work of reconstruction that follows began from so devastated a starting point.
