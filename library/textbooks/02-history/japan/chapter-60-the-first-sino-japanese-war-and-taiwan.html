Part IX — Constitution, Industry, and Empire

The First Sino-Japanese War and Taiwan

Japanese History: From Jōmon Japan to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

Before dawn on July 23, 1894, Japanese troops fought their way into the royal palace in Seoul. Within a year, Japan had defeated Qing China, signed a treaty at Shimonoseki, and was fighting to occupy an island that no Taiwanese had agreed to give it.

This chapter studies the First Sino-Japanese War of 1894–1895 and the acquisition of Taiwan. Its subject is more than a military contest. It examines a political struggle over Korea, the campaigns on land and sea, the international settlement, the Triple Intervention, and the conquest and early colonial government of Taiwan. Together they changed Japan's position in East Asia and its relationship with the Great Powers.

Three achievements must be kept apart. Japan won a military victory in 1894–1895. It obtained a diplomatic settlement at Shimonoseki, which it could not fully protect against Russia, Germany, and France. And it acquired Taiwan on paper, though effective control required months of fighting and years of suppression. The chapter treats each as a separate historical problem.

Korea appears here as a society with its own rulers, reformers, and peasant movements, and not only as ground contested by Japan and China. The Donghak revolution, the Gabo Reforms, and the resistance to Japanese power were Korean developments with Korean causes. Taiwan likewise appears as a society of Hoklo and Hakka settlers, Indigenous peoples, local elites, and militias, whose responses to cession differed.

The evidence is varied and uneven. Japanese official reports, army and navy records, treaty texts, and diplomatic correspondence survive in abundance, as do newspapers and woodblock prints. Foreign correspondents and officials, Qing documents, and Korean and Taiwanese local histories give other views, but fewer ordinary people left records. Historians must therefore ask whose voices each source represents and whose are missing.

The chapter draws on the military reforms of Chapter 54, the industrial base of Chapter 56, and the constitutional politics of Chapter 59, and it leads to the Russo-Japanese War (Chapter 61) and the establishment of Korean colonial rule (Chapter 62). It does not tell the war as inevitable, and it does not treat Japan's victory as proof that Japan was modern and China was not.

Historical Context

By the 1890s Japan had a constitution (Chapter 59), conscription (Chapter 54), and growing industry (Chapter 56). Qing China, strained by internal rebellion and foreign pressure, had also pursued military reform. Korea was formally independent but pressed by both neighbors, Russia, and Western treaty powers.

Division I — Korea, Qing China, and the Origins of War · Section 60.1

Korea and the East Asian Political Order before 1894

Before 1894 Korea belonged to no single international order. After the Manchu invasions of the 1630s, the Joseon court accepted Qing seniority: it sent regular envoys to Beijing, used Qing calendars, and received investiture of its kings. In practice this tributary relationship left Korea in charge of its own taxation, law, and officials. Korea also kept a separate, limited relationship with Tokugawa Japan through the Tsushima domain, the trading post at Busan, and periodic embassies to Edo. Historians therefore warn against reading the Qing–Joseon tie as either colonial rule or as an empty ceremony. It was a flexible set of obligations and customs that both courts interpreted to suit their needs.

Meiji Japan challenged this arrangement. In the late 1860s and 1870s the new government sought direct relations with Korea, and the Joseon court refused letters whose wording claimed Japanese imperial superiority. Japanese leaders debated punitive action in 1873, and in 1875 a Japanese naval vessel's clash with Korean forts off Ganghwa Island supplied a justification for gunboat diplomacy of the kind Japan had itself experienced in the 1850s. The resulting Treaty of Ganghwa (1876) declared Korea an independent state with equal rights. Its first article is often read as an attack on Qing claims, but it also opened Korean ports to Japanese trade and gave Japanese subjects extraterritorial privileges, making it an unequal treaty imposed on Korea.

Korean rulers and officials responded in divided ways. The Min family and the court sought outside support to preserve their authority, some reformers admired Japan's institutions and wanted rapid change, and conservative scholars opposed foreign influence altogether. The Qing government, under Li Hongzhang, reacted by encouraging Korea to sign treaties with other powers, beginning with the United States in 1882, in order to balance Japan, and by tightening supervision through advisers, trade regulations, and, from 1885, Yuan Shikai as its representative in Seoul. By 1894 Korea's "independence" meant different things to Seoul, Beijing, and Tokyo, and the contest was over who defined it.

Historical Vocabulary

Tributary relations, suzerainty, and sovereignty

Tributary relations were ritual exchanges of envoys and gifts that recognized Chinese seniority; suzerainty is a Western term for overlordship; sovereignty in treaty law means an equal state. Qing efforts after 1880 to convert ritual ties into closer control, and Japan's insistence on Korean "independence," were opposed claims, not a clash of backwardness and modernity.

Section 60.2

Competing Interventions: Imo, Gapsin, and Tianjin

Two crises in the 1880s turned that rivalry into a military one. In July 1882 soldiers of the old-style units in Seoul, who had gone unpaid and been given poor rations while favored new units trained with Japanese instructors, rioted. They attacked officials associated with the Min family and the Japanese legation, whose staff escaped to Incheon. The former regent, the Daewongun, briefly returned to power. The Qing government sent several thousand troops, restored the court, and took the Daewongun to China. Korea paid an indemnity to Japan and allowed Japanese guards in Seoul under the Treaty of Jemulpo, so both China and Japan now had soldiers on Korean soil.

In December 1884 a small group of reformers, the Gapsin Coup leaders around Kim Ok-gyun, Pak Yeong-hyo, and others, seized the king with the backing of Japan's minister and a few Japanese troops, and announced a program of radical change. Qing forces under Yuan Shikai intervened within days and the coup collapsed; the Japanese legation was burned, and the leaders fled to Japan. The coup's failure discredited the idea that reform could be imposed through a Japanese-backed seizure of power and showed that the king's own officials could not by themselves resist Qing supervision.

The Convention of Tianjin, concluded by Itō Hirobumi and Li Hongzhang in April 1885, was meant to stabilize matters. Both countries agreed to withdraw their troops, to send no military instructors, and to notify the other before sending troops again. The convention did not mention Korean sovereignty or Qing suzerainty, so each side could later claim it as justification. When a Korean assassinated Kim Ok-gyun in Shanghai in March 1894 and his body was sent back to Korea, Japanese opinion was inflamed, and the stage was set for the next crisis. The convention's notice clause would shape the opening moves of June 1894.

Section 60.3

The Donghak Peasant Revolution and the Korean Crisis of 1894

The crisis of 1894 began inside Korea. Donghak, founded by Choe Je-u in 1860, combined Confucian, Buddhist, and folk themes in a teaching of human dignity, and opposed Western Learning. Choe was executed in 1864, but the church survived underground and spread among poor farmers and local scholars in the southern provinces. By the early 1890s its leaders were petitioning for the rehabilitation of Choe and for relief from official extortion. The grievances were concrete: corrupt magistrates levied illegal surcharges on the land tax and grain loans, rice exports to Japan contributed to high prices and shortages, and foreign merchants were gaining ground in the ports.

In early 1894 the abuses of the magistrate of Gobu in Jeolla Province set off a revolt led by Jeon Bong-jun, a local Donghak leader. After government efforts to calm matters failed, Donghak forces mobilized again in spring with far larger numbers, defeated provincial troops, and took Jeonju, the provincial capital and a dynastic ancestral city, at the end of May. The court, frightened, asked the Qing government for troops. The rebels meanwhile negotiated a truce in early June, sometimes called the Jeonju armistice, in which they dispersed in return for promises of reform. Accounts of their demands vary, but they generally call for punishing corrupt officials, ending illegal taxes, and improving the treatment of the poor and of lower-status groups. Reform offices called jipgangso were set up in many Jeolla counties and became centers of local self-rule.

Historians interpret the revolution in several ways: as a religious movement, a peasant uprising, an early nationalist and anti-foreign protest, or a combination. What is clear is that Korean participants acted for Korean reasons. The rebels did not call on Japan, and the Japanese government did not cause the movement. But the request for Qing troops gave Japan a legal opening under the Tianjin Convention, and that is how a domestic conflict became an international one.

Historical Vocabulary

Donghak

Donghak ("Eastern Learning") was a Korean religion founded by Choe Je-u in the 1860s that opposed Western Learning and promoted equality. It became the base of a peasant movement with social and political aims.

Section 60.4

Troop Deployments, Failed Diplomacy, and the Decision for War

In early June 1894 China notified Japan under the Tianjin Convention that it was sending troops to assist the Korean government, describing the move as a response to a tributary state's request. Qing soldiers landed at Asan. Japan, citing the same convention and the protection of its legation and subjects, sent a much larger force, including a mixed brigade under Ōshima Yoshimasa, to Incheon and Seoul. By the time the main forces had arrived, the Jeonju truce had ended the fighting. Korea asked both powers to withdraw, and the Qing was willing in principle to do so.

Japan did not. The Japanese cabinet, led by Itō Hirobumi with Mutsu Munemitsu as foreign minister, proposed that China and Japan jointly undertake the reform of Korea's government. Li Hongzhang rejected the proposal as interference in a tributary's affairs and proposed simultaneous withdrawal. Japan then stated that its troops would stay until Korea had been reformed and later demanded that the Korean court carry out changes under Japanese guidance. Britain tried to mediate in July but its efforts did not succeed, while Japan's treaty revision talks with Britain, signed on July 16, reduced the chance that London would oppose Tokyo. Japan sent a strongly worded demand to the Korean court in mid-July.

Why Japan chose confrontation is debated. Mutsu's memoir, written afterward, describes a deliberate policy of seizing the initiative, and some scholars point to domestic politics: Itō's government had clashed with the Diet and had dissolved it in early June, and military leaders pressed for action. Others stress the instability of the situation, the Qing's reluctance to give up its position, and the risks of a Russian role if Japan withdrew. Memoirs, diplomatic correspondence, and military orders each support different parts of the story. The decision for war was a series of choices made by officials in Tokyo, Beijing, and Seoul, not an inevitable result.

Division II — The Outbreak of War and the Korean Campaign · Section 60.5

The Japanese and Qing Armed Forces on the Eve of War

Both armies had been reshaped by two decades of reform, and neither was a uniform or predetermined winner. Japan's army rested on conscription (Chapter 54), a divisional structure, and a general staff that drew on German advice in the 1880s. Its leaders created an Imperial Headquarters in June 1894, which coordinated army and navy operations and kept the emperor and cabinet informed. Its navy, directed at sea by Admiral Itō Sukeyuki, relied heavily on foreign-built cruisers with quick-firing guns, such as the Yoshino, and on a fleet trained for speed and coordinated maneuver.

The Qing military was larger on paper but divided. It was built up from regional armies, notably the Huai Army raised by Li Hongzhang, which had fought the Taiping and had been modernized with Western rifles and artillery and foreign instructors, while Manchu banner units had little modern training. The Beiyang Fleet, based at Weihaiwei and Port Arthur, included two German-built armored ships, the Dingyuan and Zhenyuan, with heavy guns, but it had few quick-firing guns and sometimes poor ammunition. Qing commanders answered to different governors and viceroys, and the southern fleets did not join the Beiyang Fleet. Later critics charged that funds meant for the navy were diverted to palace building; the extent of this is debated, and shortages of money, standardization, and central coordination are better-attested causes of weakness.

These differences should not be exaggerated into a contrast between a modern Japan and a backward China. Both states used new weapons and techniques within very different political and financial systems. Japan's advantages lay in centralized command, transport by sea, and staff planning; the Qing's weaknesses lay in divided authority and in Li Hongzhang's reluctance to risk his own fleet. Victory depended on how these conditions interacted with decisions made in the field.

Section 60.6

The Seizure of Gyeongbokgung and the Coercion of Korea

On the morning of July 23, 1894, Japanese troops under the command of Ōshima Yoshimasa attacked Korean guards and entered Gyeongbokgung, the main royal palace in Seoul, and took control of King Gojong and the court. This happened two days before the first naval clash and over a week before declarations of war, and it makes the war's beginning a matter not only of Japanese and Qing forces but also of Japanese coercion of Korea. The Min faction was removed, the Daewongun was installed as nominal head of government, and a new cabinet led by Kim Hong-jip announced that Korea had ended its tributary relationship with the Qing and asked Japan to expel Qing troops from Korea. A reform council was established a few days later.

Japanese accounts at the time said that the Korean government had asked for Japanese help and that Korean soldiers had fired first. Later work has challenged this account. The Japanese historian Nakatsuka Akira, working in army archives, found an earlier draft of the General Staff's war history that described the palace operation as a prepared plan, whereas the published history presented it as an accidental clash. Korean historians have also shown that Gojong was a prisoner in his own palace and that the officials who signed the demands did so under pressure. Documents produced under occupation are poor evidence of consent.

The seizure matters independently of later battles. It put a Korean government that Japan controlled in a position to authorize Japanese military operations, it gave Japan a basis for claiming that it was fighting at Korea's invitation, and it set a pattern, repeated in 1895 and 1905, in which Korean sovereignty was reduced under the name of reform. For Koreans, who remembered the event as an invasion, it also contributed to the opposition that led to a second Donghak uprising.

Section 60.7

Pungdo, the Kowshing, and the Formal Outbreak of War

On July 25, 1894, off Pungdo near Asan, Japanese cruisers under Tsuboi Kōzō met Qing ships returning from escorting transports. The Qing cruiser Guangyi was wrecked, the Jiyuan escaped, and the Japanese captured the gunboat Caojiang. This attack came before any declaration of war. The same day, the cruiser Naniwa under Captain Tōgō Heihachirō stopped the Kowshing, a steamer chartered from a British company and flying the British flag, which was carrying roughly 1,100 Qing soldiers to Korea. After a standoff in which the Qing troops would not allow the British officers to follow Japanese orders, the Naniwa sank the ship. Many of the troops drowned, and only a limited number of Chinese and the European officers were rescued.

Britain's public was at first angry, and the incident raised the question of whether Japan had violated the rights of a neutral vessel. Legal opinion, including that of prominent British jurists, soon held that Japan's action was lawful because hostilities had begun and the ship was carrying enemy troops, and the British government did not break with Tokyo. That outcome strengthened Japan's diplomatic position at a crucial moment, just after the commercial treaty signed with Britain on July 16. On August 1 both governments issued formal declarations of war, the emperor's rescript presenting the war as one for Korean independence and Japanese security, and the Qing denouncing Japan's attack on its vassal.

Section 60.8

Seonghwan, Asan, and Pyongyang: The War for Korea

Once the palace was secured, the Japanese advanced against Qing troops in southern Korea. At Seonghwan on July 29 and then at Asan, the Qing force under Ye Zhichao was defeated or withdrew and marched north to join other Qing armies at Pyongyang, where around fifteen thousand troops had gathered. A Japanese First Army under Yamagata Aritomo had to cross poor roads in the summer rain with limited transport, and relied heavily on local porters, oxen, and food requisitioned from Korean villages, which caused hardship among civilians and strained relations.

The Japanese plan was to attack Pyongyang from several directions at once. On September 15 columns approached from the south, the east, and the west, and attacked fortified positions, while Qing commanders, divided about whether to hold or retreat, lost an important officer, Zuo Baogui. That night Ye Zhichao's troops tried to slip out of the city in heavy rain and suffered many losses on the way. The Japanese took the city and large stores of supplies and moved the front north to the Yalu. Japanese losses were comparatively light and Qing losses heavy, though figures vary by source. With Pyongyang lost, Qing land power in Korea was broken, and the decisive question became control of the sea.

Section 60.9

The Second Donghak Uprising and the Gabo Reforms

Japanese military success did not end resistance in Korea. In the autumn of 1894 Donghak leaders revived their movement, this time against Japanese troops and the government they had installed. Jeon Bong-jun remobilized southern forces, and the northern leader Choe Si-hyeong brought troops from Chungcheong, so that tens of thousands marched toward Gongju and Seoul. Japanese detachments, supported by Korean government troops and equipped with modern rifles, defeated them in a series of battles, most importantly at Ugumchi near Gongju in November and December. Estimates of the dead run into the tens of thousands and are uncertain, since the evidence consists largely of Japanese and Korean official reports and local memory. Jeon was captured and executed in 1895, and Japanese commanders in the south were ordered to suppress the rebels harshly.

The same Korean government was carrying out the Gabo Reforms, which began in July 1894 and continued into 1895. They ended the legal distinctions of social rank, abolished the old civil-service examinations, banned slavery and certain punishments, allowed widows to remarry, and reorganized the state's ministries and fiscal arrangements. Many Korean reformers wanted such changes, and some of the reforms echoed Donghak demands for equality. But they were carried out under occupation, with Japanese advisers and in the absence of broad consent, so Koreans experienced them as both reform and domination. After the Triple Intervention weakened Japan's position, the court turned against the Japanese-backed ministers, a conflict that led to the murder of Queen Min in October 1895 and belongs to Chapter 62.

Multiple Perspectives

The War in Korea and Taiwan

Japanese officials: framed intervention as reform and security, while using force to control Korea's government.
Korean peasants and reformers: Donghak leaders sought relief from corruption and later fought Japanese power, while some reformers welcomed change but resented its imposition.
Qing officials: sought to defend a tributary relationship and avoid risking the northern fleet.
Taiwanese Hoklo and Hakka residents: elites divided between resistance and accommodation, while militias fought.
Indigenous communities: largely outside both regimes' control and affected by later campaigns. Groups did not have equal power or responsibility.
Division III — Naval Warfare, Manchuria, and Qing Defeat · Section 60.10

The Battle of the Yalu River

On September 17, 1894, the Japanese Combined Fleet under Admiral Itō Sukeyuki met the Beiyang Fleet under Admiral Ding Ruchang off the mouth of the Yalu River, as the Qing fleet was returning from escorting troop transports. The battle lasted several hours and was a major clash of modern steam-powered warships, watched closely by naval observers following the earlier ironclad fleet battle at Lissa in 1866. Itō sent a fast flying squadron in line ahead against the Qing formation, which was deployed in a line abreast and unable to maneuver easily, and used its heavily armed cruisers to concentrate fire on the Qing's weaker ships. The Qing flagship Dingyuan was hit early, Ding Ruchang was injured, and the signal gear was damaged, so command was difficult to exercise.

The Qing lost five ships and several others were badly damaged, while the Japanese lost none but saw several heavily damaged. The large guns of the German-built Dingyuan and Zhenyuan scored hits but were slow to fire, whereas Japanese quick-firing guns produced large volumes of shells and fires. Contemporary and later accounts also point to deficient ammunition, differences in training, and tactical error, though the weight of each factor remains debated because the main records are Japanese reports, foreign observers' accounts, and later Chinese testimony. The Beiyang Fleet withdrew to its bases. Japan then had command of the Yellow Sea, which allowed it to carry armies to Liaodong and protect its supply routes.

Section 60.11

Across the Yalu: Manchuria and Liaodong

With Korea secured and the sea open, Japanese leaders decided to carry the war into Qing territory. In late October the First Army crossed the Yalu near Jiuliancheng and drove the defenders back, while the Second Army under Ōyama Iwao landed on the Liaodong Peninsula and moved against Jinzhou and Dalian, which fell in early November. The emperor had moved to the Imperial Headquarters at Hiroshima in September, and the government now debated aims that went beyond Korean independence, including territory in Manchuria and possibly Taiwan.

The campaign took place on the homeland of the Qing dynasty, with local villagers caught between armies. Winter closed in, and problems of supply and command grew. Yamagata Aritomo was recalled in December after disputes and illness, and later operations around Haicheng and Niuzhuang in the winter and early spring of 1895 showed that the Qing still fielded forces capable of counterattacks. Japan's rapid advance changed what its leaders thought they could ask at the peace table.

Section 60.12

Port Arthur and the Massacre of November 1894

The Second Army attacked Port Arthur, the principal Qing naval base and fortress, on November 21, 1894, and took its forts within a day. Many Qing soldiers fled or were killed in the fighting. What followed was a different matter: over several days Japanese troops killed large numbers of people in the town, including soldiers who had surrendered or dropped their weapons and civilians. Japanese accounts say that earlier in the campaign soldiers found the mutilated remains of Japanese captives, and that the troops were enraged and that some Qing soldiers had changed into civilian clothes. These claims, even if partly true, do not explain the scale of the killing of unarmed people.

Foreign observers and journalists, among them correspondents for the New York World and the London Times, reported what they saw, and their accounts caused alarm in Europe and the United States. The Japanese government at first denied or minimized the reports, then argued that those killed were soldiers, and made efforts to influence foreign opinion. Numbers are disputed: estimates run from a few thousand to far higher figures, and the sources, which include press dispatches, diplomatic reports, and Japanese military records, were produced for different purposes. Very few prisoners reportedly survived. The massacre is nevertheless well documented, and it should be placed in the main narrative, as it was one of the events by which contemporaries judged whether Japan's claim to be a civilized power was credible.

In Japan, official histories and newspapers stressed the victory, and for years the killings were overshadowed or denied. In China, Port Arthur became a lasting symbol of the war's cruelty. Historians today treat the episode as a failure of command discipline and an example of how the ideology of war shaped the conduct of Japanese troops, rather than as a sudden aberration.

Section 60.13

Weihaiwei and the Destruction of the Beiyang Fleet

The last campaign of the war was aimed at Weihaiwei, the base of the Beiyang Fleet's surviving ships on the Shandong coast. In late January 1895 Japanese troops landed east of the port, and within days took the land forts on both sides of the harbor, turning the Qing's own guns on the fleet. The fleet, anchored near Liugong Island, was protected by a boom and the fortified island, but was now under bombardment. In early February Japanese torpedo boats broke in at night and torpedoed several ships, including the Dingyuan.

Ding Ruchang faced a hopeless situation, with his men's morale low and foreign advisers and some officers urging surrender. On February 12 he sent a proposal to surrender to the Japanese command and took his own life, as did other senior officers. The formal capitulation was signed on February 17. These were three different events: the proposal, Ding's death, and the surrender terms, and later accounts sometimes blur them. Japan took the remaining ships, including the Zhenyuan, and returned Ding's body with honors, a gesture that Japanese authorities used to project an image of chivalry. The destruction of the Beiyang Fleet ended Qing naval power in the north, left Beijing exposed, and increased the pressure on Li Hongzhang to seek peace.

Section 60.14

The Human and Material Costs of War

The war's costs fell unevenly. For Japan's army, disease was far deadlier than combat. Cholera, dysentery, and beriberi, the latter linked to a diet of polished rice, killed many times more soldiers than enemy fire, and the toll rose in the Liaodong winter and in the occupation of Taiwan in 1895. Totals vary by source, and official reports, produced by the army and Red Cross, are better for soldiers than for civilians. Hospitals and hospital ships treated the wounded, and the Japanese Red Cross, which had grown rapidly in the 1880s, became a symbol of the war's modern and humane side, which was also used for propaganda.

Armies depended on civilians. The Japanese army hired large numbers of military laborers to carry supplies and baggage, and in Korea and Manchuria used oxen, requisitioned grain, and forced labor. Villages in the path of the armies lost food, animals, shelter, and men, and many Chinese residents fled. Qing records on casualties and civilian suffering are sparse, and Korean accounts come largely from later memory and local histories, so the human cost of the war can be reconstructed only partially. Official military reports tell us less about these experiences than the history of ordinary people requires.

Everyday Life and Environment

Soldiers, Porters, and Civilians

Conscripts faced cold, poor roads, and disease, with cholera, dysentery, and beriberi the greatest dangers. Korean and Chinese villagers supplied food, oxen, and labor, sometimes under compulsion, and families in Japan followed the war through newspapers, prints, and letters. Wounded soldiers and war dead were honored publicly.

Division IV — Shimonoseki and the International Settlement · Section 60.15

Li Hongzhang and the Negotiations at Shimonoseki

Qing leaders began seeking an end to the war in the autumn of 1894 and turned to Britain and other powers for mediation without result. In early 1895 Beijing sent envoys to Hiroshima, but the Japanese government refused to negotiate with them, saying their credentials were inadequate. This left Li Hongzhang, the official most associated with the fleets that had been destroyed, to represent the Qing. He arrived at Shimonoseki in March 1895 and began talks with Itō Hirobumi and Mutsu Munemitsu at an inn in the port city.

On March 24, after a negotiating session, a Japanese man, Koyama Toyotarō, shot Li in the face. The wound was not fatal, but the attack embarrassed the Japanese government, which feared that foreign powers would intervene, and Mutsu and Itō agreed to an armistice. Notably, the armistice covered fighting in Manchuria and northern China but excluded Taiwan and Penghu. Japan had taken Penghu in the days just before and thereby kept the freedom to occupy the main island, a point of great importance to Chapter 60's later sections. Li's adopted son Li Jingfang joined the negotiations, and Li himself recovered enough to bargain. Japan's initial demands included a larger indemnity than the final figure, and Li succeeded in reducing the amount, though the principal terms, including the cession of territory, were not seriously altered.

The talks show the Qing's weakness: Li had little leverage, the court in Beijing wanted peace, and Japan was confident of its military position. They also show Japanese diplomacy at work. Mutsu and Itō had to balance the demands of the army, public opinion, and the likely reaction of the powers, and Itō wanted to settle before the situation changed.

Section 60.16

The Treaty of Shimonoseki

The Treaty of Shimonoseki was signed on April 17, 1895. Article I recognized the full independence of Korea and ended Korea's tribute to China, which Japan presented as the war's central aim. Article II ceded to Japan the southern part of Fengtian province in the Liaodong Peninsula, Taiwan and its associated islands, and the Penghu Islands. Article IV set an indemnity of 200 million Kuping taels, to be paid in installments, several times Japan's annual government revenue. Other provisions opened new treaty ports, including Shashi, Chongqing, Suzhou, and Hangzhou, allowed Japanese ships on the Yangtze and the right to manufacture in the ports, gave Japan most-favored-nation treatment, and allowed Japan to occupy Weihaiwei until the indemnity was paid. Article V allowed residents of ceded territories two years to sell property and relocate; those who remained could then, at Japan's option, be deemed Japanese subjects. The inhabitants had no voice in negotiating these terms.

The treaty was thus a document of the Qing's defeat but not of the wishes of the people it concerned. Neither Korea nor the Taiwanese were parties. A cession on paper was also not the same as control of the territory: Japan still had to occupy Taiwan, and the transfer in early June 1895 was followed by months of fighting (§§60.20–60.22). Historians read the text for Japan's goals, but need Korean, Taiwanese, and Qing sources to understand what the clauses meant in practice. The commercial clauses also opened the door for other powers, who could claim the same rights through the most-favored-nation principle, and this contributed to the increasing foreign presence in China in the later 1890s.

Historical Vocabulary

Indemnity, Cession, and Treaty Port

An indemnity is a payment by the loser; cession is a transfer of territory by treaty; a treaty port is a city opened by treaty to foreign trade and residence. Shimonoseki used all three.

Section 60.17

The Triple Intervention

Six days after the treaty was signed, on April 23, the Russian, German, and French ministers in Tokyo delivered a recommendation, described as friendly advice, that Japan give up the Liaodong Peninsula. They argued that Japanese possession of it would threaten Beijing, make Korean independence unreal, and disturb peace in the Far East. Russia was the prime mover, as it had its own plans for Manchuria and a Pacific port, and France supported its ally, while Germany sought favor with Russia and its own opportunities in China.

The Japanese government debated its options at Hiroshima. The army and navy were stretched, its forces were committed in China and, soon, Taiwan, and Britain declined to give support. Japan decided in early May to give up Liaodong, and in November 1895 agreed to return it in exchange for an additional 30 million taels from Qing China. Public reaction was furious, and the slogan of perseverance in hardship, gashin shōtan, became popular as a call to prepare for the future. The sense of humiliation was strengthened when Russia, in 1898, obtained a lease of Port Arthur and the Liaodong tip for itself.

The intervention taught Japanese leaders that a victory in war could be reversed by the Great Powers, and it made them focus on military expansion and alliances. It did not make the war with Russia in 1904 unavoidable, as the later relationship between the two states included negotiations as well as conflict, but it was part of the context in which Japanese strategy was formed. Qing leaders, for their part, drew closer to Russia, a relationship that Chapter 61 will follow.

Section 60.18

Treaty Revision and Japan's International Standing

The war changed how Japan was seen. A few days before it began, Britain had signed with Japan, on July 16, 1894, a treaty of commerce and navigation that ended consular jurisdiction in Japan, to take effect in 1899, a major step in the revision of the unequal treaties begun by earlier diplomats. Tariff autonomy was not fully regained until 1911. During the first months, Western governments and many newspapers praised Japan's military efficiency, the Red Cross hospitals, and the care of prisoners, and Japanese legal scholars wrote on the law of war to show that Japan observed it.

Port Arthur, the palace seizure, and the pressure on Korea complicated that image. Reports of the massacre led some Western papers to question whether Japan was civilized or merely armed in a modern way, though the powers did not impose sanctions, and the Triple Intervention showed that Japan's new rank had limits. At the same time, Qing weakness invited a rush by the powers for leases, loans, and railway concessions in the late 1890s, with the indemnity itself financed by loans from European banks. Japan's own status rose, since the victory showed that a non-Western state could mobilize modern armies and navies, and its leaders sought to use that standing to be treated as an equal of the powers.

Division V — Taiwan, Cession, Resistance, and Colonial Rule · Section 60.19

Taiwan under Qing Rule

When the Qing defeated the Zheng regime in 1683 it incorporated Taiwan into Fujian Province as a prefecture, and for two centuries ruled it lightly. Migrants from southern Fujian, the Hoklo or Hokkien speakers, and from eastern Guangdong, the Hakka, cleared land along the western plains and built irrigation, towns, and port markets. The settlers organized by lineage, native place, and temple, and fought frequent armed feuds between ethnic and surname groups. Qing authority depended on local magnates, gentry, and militias as much as on the few officials sent from the mainland.

Indigenous peoples were not a single group. Plains peoples, many of whom lived in villages that traded and intermarried with Han settlers, were increasingly assimilated or pushed off their land, while the mountain peoples, including the Atayal, Bunun, Paiwan, and others, held their territories and were mostly outside Qing control. Qing officials sorted them into categories, often called cooked and raw, according to their degree of acceptance of Qing rule, which reflected the state's perspective, not Indigenous identities. The 1874 Japanese expedition to southern Taiwan after the killing of shipwrecked Ryukyuans, and the French attacks on the north during the Sino-French War of 1884–85, showed the island's strategic exposure.

The Qing responded with a program of development. The decision to make Taiwan a separate province was made in 1885, and the reorganization was completed in 1887, with Liu Mingchuan as its first governor. He built telegraph lines and a railway from Keelung toward Hsinchu, surveyed land for taxation, and pursued a policy of opening the mountains that included military campaigns against Indigenous groups. Exports of tea, camphor, and sugar through ports such as Tamsui and Anping linked Taiwan to world markets. By 1895 the island had around two and a half million people. Qing sovereignty was real but uneven: it was accepted in many places, resisted in others, and defined differently by the island's different communities.

Section 60.20

Penghu, Cession, and the Republic of Formosa

Japan moved on Taiwan before the treaty was signed. In late March 1895 its forces took the Penghu Islands with little resistance, though disease soon struck the troops there, and the armistice (§60.15) left Taiwan open to attack. When the terms of the treaty were known, Taiwan's officials and gentry sent protests to Beijing, and many in the island's elite refused the transfer. Some had been loyal Qing officials, and others had land, trade, and local power to lose.

On May 23, 1895, Governor Tang Jingsong and local leaders announced the Republic of Formosa, with Tang as president, and a new era name that expressed loyalty to the Qing. Liu Yongfu, the commander of the Black Flag troops who had fought the French in Vietnam, was given command in the south, and the Hakka scholar Qiu Fengjia was active in organizing militia. The leaders sought help from Britain and France and received none. The republic was therefore not a modern representative Taiwanese national government but a short-lived device by Qing officials and local elites to block cession, and a few days of existence cannot be confused with later forms of Taiwanese nationalism. Historians who see it as a precedent for later Taiwanese identity must also account for its pro-Qing wording.

The republic's leaders did not stay. As Japanese forces landed, Tang Jingsong and others left for the mainland in early June, and the capital fell. The proclamation of the republic is a document of elite politics. It cannot tell us what farmers, Hakka villagers, or Indigenous communities thought, and those groups' actions in the months that followed are the best evidence for them.

Section 60.21

The Japanese Landing and the Conquest of Northern Taiwan

The Japanese Imperial Guards Division, led by Prince Kitashirakawa Yoshihisa, landed on May 29, 1895, on the northeast coast at Aoti, away from the Keelung forts, and advanced overland. Keelung fell in early June. In Taipei, the Qing officials had left and disorder broke out among unpaid soldiers, and some local gentry, notably the merchant Koo Hsien-jung, went to the Japanese to ask them to enter and restore order. Japanese forces had entered Taipei by June 7. Kabayama Sukenori, the first governor-general, arrived and held ceremonies in the city in mid-June to mark the start of Japanese rule. This early success reflected both the force of the Japanese army and the willingness of some elites to negotiate.

The rest of the north did not submit. Local militias, many of them Hakka, fought the Japanese in the hills around Taoyuan, Sanxia, and Hsinchu, and the Japanese, who found the terrain, rain, and heat difficult, burned villages as they advanced. Japanese troops entered Hsinchu on June 22, but militia attacks continued around the city; control of the surrounding district was not consolidated until late July. Japanese casualties from illness were heavy, and more soldiers fell to disease than to fighting. Taipei's welcome was thus one of several responses to the invasion, and in much of the north the conquest was contested village by village.

Section 60.22

Resistance in Central and Southern Taiwan

In the summer and autumn of 1895 the army moved south. Local forces, among them militia raised by gentry and remnants of Qing units, organized resistance around Miaoli and Changhua, and in late August the Japanese took Changhua after a battle on the Baguashan ridge. Liu Yongfu, who ran the defense from Tainan with the Black Flag forces, had little money, few supplies, and a divided command, and he had been unable to obtain either foreign aid or reinforcements from the mainland. Chiayi fell in early October after heavy fighting.

The Japanese then landed a second force in the far south and prepared to attack Tainan. Liu left for the mainland on a British ship in mid-October. On October 21 Japanese troops entered Tainan without a battle after foreign missionaries and local leaders helped negotiate the city's surrender. The campaign had cost the Japanese heavily, mostly through disease: Prince Kitashirakawa himself died of illness in Tainan a week later. Taiwanese casualties are not well recorded, with estimates in the thousands. Kabayama announced the pacification of the island in November, but the declaration reflected the army's control of cities and main roads, not of the countryside.

Section 60.23

From Military Conquest to Colonial Government

The Governor-General's Office in Taipei, headed by the admiral Kabayama, began as a military administration. Its first goals were security, supplies, and taxation. Resistance continued after the fall of Tainan: attacks on Japanese posts occurred in late 1895 and early 1896, and guerrilla bands in the north and south were not suppressed for several years. Indigenous mountain communities, who had not recognized Qing or Japanese rule, remained largely outside Japanese control, and Japanese military campaigns against them continued into the 1910s. The defeat of the Republic of Formosa was therefore a different thing from the establishment of secure rule.

Legal and political arrangements set the colony apart from Japan proper. Unlike mainland prefectures, Taiwan lacked elected representation in the Imperial Diet and was administered through special colonial institutions rather than ordinary mainland arrangements. The Imperial Diet nevertheless enacted Law No. 63 in March 1896, delegating to the governor-general authority to issue ordinances with the force of law; this exceptional arrangement itself provoked constitutional objections. Taiwanese were treated as subjects of a lower legal status, and the Treaty's two-year option meant that those who did not leave by 1897 were treated as Japanese subjects. Many Taiwanese elites adapted, cooperated, or resisted according to their circumstances. The following decades of colonial administration, economic change, and Taiwanese politics belong to later chapters; for now, the key point is that Japan gained a colony by treaty and then fought for it.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Taiwan from Qing to Japanese Rule

What changed: sovereignty, legal status, policing, and the governing apparatus. What remained continuous: village and lineage society, local elites, Hoklo, Hakka, and Indigenous communities, and the export economy. Who experienced change most directly: militia members, officials, and Indigenous communities targeted by campaigns. Who experienced continuity: many farmers and merchants, at least at first. Why it matters: conquest and rule were different processes.

Division VI — War, Empire, and the Remaking of East Asia · Section 60.24

War Reporting, Woodblock Prints, and Propaganda

For most Japanese the war was a mass experience, mediated by print. Newspapers sent correspondents to the front, published extras, and competed for readers, and their circulation grew rapidly. Fukuzawa Yukichi's paper Jiji shinpō presented the conflict as a struggle of civilization against barbarism, and he and other prominent figures raised money for the war. The government censored military information, though not always consistently, and limited the movements of foreign correspondents. Public celebrations, including lantern parades after victories, donations, and war songs, tied communities to the war effort.

The most distinctive evidence is the woodblock print. Artists produced thousands of multicolor nishiki-e prints of battles and heroes, among them Kobayashi Kiyochika, Mizuno Toshikata, and Toyohara Chikanobu, often working from newspaper reports and official accounts, not from experience at the front. Heroic stories circulated widely, such as that of a soldier said to have died with his bugle at his lips, and their details were embellished or disputed. Prints and newspapers represented Chinese soldiers as weak and cowardly and often showed Koreans as backward objects of Japanese reform, which fed a sense of superiority. Chinese popular prints and Korean memory told different stories, but they have survived in far smaller numbers. Historians use these images as evidence of how the war was sold to the public, and not as accurate pictures of combat.

Section 60.25

Indemnities and the Political Economy of Victory

The financial results of the war were substantial. Japan received the 200 million taels in the treaty, plus 30 million for the return of Liaodong, a total of roughly 360 million yen, many times annual government revenue. Much of the payment was made in London in pounds sterling, which meant that Japan held large sterling balances. In 1897 the government used these reserves to adopt the gold standard, linking the yen to the international monetary system and making it easier to borrow in foreign markets.

The money did not turn Japan into an industrial economy, which had been under construction since the 1870s (Chapter 56). Rather it accelerated certain choices. More than half went to military and naval expansion, including new battleships ordered from Britain, and funds were also devoted to the founding of the government's Yawata Iron and Steel Works, to education, and to the imperial household. War orders and the new colonial markets encouraged cotton spinning and shipping. At the same time, taxes rose, and the cost of governing Taiwan required subsidies from the central government in the early years. Victory brought both financial resources and larger commitments.

Section 60.26

A New East Asian Balance

The war changed the situation of each of the societies involved. For Japan it brought a colony, a larger military, international recognition, and a sense of resentment toward the powers that had forced the return of Liaodong. For the Qing, the defeat discredited the self-strengthening program in the eyes of many officials and intellectuals, and encouraged proposals for deeper institutional reform, culminating in the 1898 reform attempt and its suppression. The weakness it revealed also encouraged a rush by the powers: Germany took Jiaozhou Bay in 1897, Russia leased Port Arthur and Dalian in 1898, and Britain took Weihaiwei.

In Korea, the Japanese-backed reforms lost influence after the Triple Intervention, and the court turned to Russia. Queen Min was murdered in October 1895 by a group that included Japanese agents, and in 1896 King Gojong took refuge in the Russian legation. In 1897 he proclaimed the Korean Empire as a way of asserting independence from all neighbors. Japan and Russia reached agreements on Korea in 1896 and 1898 that did not settle the underlying rivalry. These developments belong to Chapters 61 and 62, which will examine the confrontation with Russia and the later colonial rule of Korea. For Taiwan, the war meant the loss of its Qing connection and the start of a new colonial order. Whether the war was mainly a conflict over Korea, a clash of modernizing states, or a turning point in empire (see the Historical Debate) depends on which of these outcomes is emphasized.

Section 60.27

Chapter Review and Historical Thinking Checkpoint

The war had different meanings for different communities. Japanese policymakers saw a successful assertion of strategic and diplomatic interests, and many citizens celebrated it as proof of national strength, until the Triple Intervention showed the limits of that strength. Qing officials and subjects experienced military defeat, financial burden, and a shift in the regional order, and for many Chinese the war became a symbol of national crisis. Korean reformers and peasants faced occupation, and the war's first and last violence in Korea fell on Korean society. In Taiwan, a treaty signed in another country led to months of fighting and to a colonial regime that its inhabitants had not chosen.

Students should keep three achievements apart: military victory, which Japan won in 1894–1895; the diplomatic settlement, which Japan obtained at Shimonoseki but could not fully protect against the powers; and effective territorial control, which in Taiwan required additional months of conquest and years of suppression. Keeping these distinctions in mind explains why the war can be seen as both an enormous success and a source of lasting problems for Japan and its neighbors.

Chapter Summary

The First Sino-Japanese War began as a Korean crisis. Korea stood in a flexible tributary relationship with Qing China, while Japan treated it as an independent state and used the 1876 Ganghwa Treaty to open it to Japanese influence. Interventions in 1882 and 1884 left both Qing and Japanese troops in Seoul and produced the 1885 Convention of Tianjin, with its clause requiring notice before new deployments.

In 1894 the Donghak revolution, a Korean peasant and religious movement against corruption and heavy taxes, led the government to ask for Qing help. China and Japan both sent troops, and Japan, which refused to withdraw, pressed Korea to reform. The seizure of Gyeongbokgung on July 23 gave Japan a Korean government it could direct, and the clashes at Pungdo and the sinking of the Kowshing preceded the declarations of war on August 1.

Japan won on land at Pyongyang and at sea off the Yalu, because of command, transport, and quick-firing naval guns as well as Qing divisions. It carried the war into Liaodong and took Port Arthur on November 21, where the killing of unarmed people damaged its claim to be civilized. The campaign ended with the destruction of the Beiyang Fleet at Weihaiwei in February 1895.

In Korea the war continued through a second Donghak uprising, crushed at Ugumchi with great loss of life, and through Gabo reforms carried out under Japanese military pressure. Korean society was therefore an actor in the war as well as its location.

At Shimonoseki, signed April 17, 1895, the Qing recognized Korean independence, ceded Taiwan, Penghu, and Liaodong, and agreed to a 200-million-tael indemnity and new commercial concessions. Six days later Russia, Germany, and France forced Japan to give up Liaodong in return for further payment, showing that military victory could be limited by the Great Powers.

Taiwan's cession did not give Japan control. The short-lived Republic of Formosa, a Qing-loyal effort to block transfer, was followed by months of Japanese conquest from the north to Tainan, by resistance from militias and Qing units, and by disease that killed many Japanese soldiers. A military governor-general's administration was established, but resistance continued, and many Indigenous communities remained outside Japanese control for years.

At home, newspapers and woodblock prints turned the war into a mass experience, and the indemnity financed military expansion and the 1897 gold standard. The war strengthened Japan's international standing while increasing its military commitments, encouraged reform and then a rush for concessions in China, and left Korea open to Russian and Japanese rivalry.

The chapter's central lesson is that the war should be understood from several vantage points, and that military victory, diplomatic settlement, and effective control were different things. It prepares for Chapter 61, on the Russo-Japanese War, and Chapter 62, on Korea and the colonial empire.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how the East Asian order changed in 1894–1895. A tributary-based order, in which Korea and Qing China were closely connected, gave way to one defined by treaties, indemnities, colonies, and the competition of powers. Japan was a principal beneficiary, but the settlement was neither simple nor stable, and its costs fell on Korea, Taiwan, and the Qing.

The chapter also shows the importance of seeing the war through several societies. Korean peasants and reformers, Qing officials, Taiwanese militias and elites, and Japanese soldiers and citizens understood the war differently, and the sources they left differ in quantity and bias. Treaty texts and official reports cannot take the place of the voices that are missing from them.

Many consequences extended beyond the chapter: the Russo-Japanese rivalry (Chapter 61), the annexation of Korea and colonial empire (Chapter 62), the history of Taiwan under Japanese rule, and the Chinese reform and revolutionary movements. Japan's first colony was acquired in the same moment as it learned the limits of its power, a combination that shaped later strategy.
