War Responsibility, Hirohito, and the Tokyo Trials

Japanese History: From Jōmon Japan to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

In 1946 the former headquarters of Japan’s army at Ichigaya became the courtroom in which Allied judges tried twenty-eight accused leaders. Across Asia, other courts confronted violence committed in prisons, camps, occupied cities and battle zones. The question was not whether war had ended, but how law could judge those who had directed it.

Chapter 82 examined the governing system of the Allied occupation, including the deliberate survival of Japan’s ministries and the imperial institution. Chapter 83 examines a different but related exercise of power: the effort to prosecute crimes committed in Japan’s wars. It traces how courts were constituted, what judges were asked to decide, how defendants and survivors appeared in evidence, and why the prosecution of some powerful officials proceeded while the trial of others did not. The central subject is not a retelling of the battlefield or a gallery of condemned men; it is the construction and limits of responsibility after imperial defeat.

Justice after 1945 involved several overlapping projects. The International Military Tribunal for the Far East, commonly the Tokyo Trial, emphasized leaders accused of planning and waging aggressive war. National military courts across Asia and the Pacific heard thousands of cases involving unlawful killings, abuse of prisoners, forced labor, torture and related crimes. The two systems were not identical in jurisdiction or procedure. A judgment about the responsibility of Tōjō Hideki therefore differed in evidence and law from a hearing concerning a camp guard or an officer accused of failing to control troops.

The court also operated within a politically constrained environment. General Douglas MacArthur established the tribunal while governing occupied Japan. The occupation retained Emperor Hirohito as a central institution but did not bring him to trial, despite disagreement among Allied governments. Later investigations revealed additional boundaries of prosecution, including U.S. intelligence arrangements involving Japanese biological-warfare specialists. These omissions do not automatically invalidate convictions; they require analysis of how law and political choices structured what the court could investigate.

Victims and former colonial subjects did not experience the proceedings equally. Chinese civilians, Filipino survivors, Allied prisoners, Korean and Taiwanese personnel formerly mobilized within Japanese forces, and women subjected to military sexual slavery had different access to testimony and different postwar legal statuses. Some obtained recognition through regional courts, others remained largely outside the judicial record. The chapter therefore treats the experiences of colonial populations as central to the history of imperial responsibility rather than as marginal illustrations.

Three problems recur throughout the chapter. A historical crime can be established independently of whether a particular accused person is convicted. The law used in the 1940s was still developing, especially concerning aggressive war and command responsibility. And the judicial record is a product of human selection—of defendants, charges, witnesses, translations and evidence—as well as a source through which later historians can reconstruct events. Students must separate a prosecutor’s allegation, a court’s finding and a historian’s interpretation.

The surviving evidence is unusually extensive. The tribunal charter, indictment, judgment, separate opinions, diplomatic telegrams, regional court files and archival finding aids can be examined alongside scholarship by Yuma Totani, Neil Boister and Robert Cryer, Herbert Bix, Kayoko Takeda and others. The record nonetheless remains incomplete: not all testimony was collected, not every crime was prosecuted, and some publications and memoirs were shaped by postwar political circumstances. Where the chapter includes statistics, it identifies whether they concern Tokyo defendants or separate regional proceedings.

The narrative ends where a legal history becomes a history of memory. In 1948 the tribunal delivered its judgments and seven defendants were executed. In 1951–1952 Japan accepted obligations concerning Allied war-crimes sentences under the peace treaty, while public responses and scholarly controversies continued to evolve. Chapter 84 will address the constitutional and democratic reforms through which occupied Japan reorganized government. Chapter 83 first asks what it meant for the state’s wartime leaders to be judged—and why judging some people never settled responsibility for the empire as a whole.

Historical Context

Japanese military expansion began well before the Pacific War of December 1941. The seizure of Manchuria in 1931, full-scale conflict with China after 1937, wartime mobilization and occupation across East and Southeast Asia all created different records of state decisions and civilian suffering. Previous chapters explain those events as wartime history. This chapter revisits them only to show how investigators and judges converted them into evidence about particular defendants. The distinction is important because a trial’s chronology may be broad while its legal finding is necessarily case-specific.

By 1945 international law included rules governing prisoners, occupation and the conduct of war, as well as treaties such as the 1928 Pact of Paris condemning aggressive war as national policy. Whether these norms had already established individual criminal responsibility for waging aggression was contested. The Nuremberg proceedings in Europe provided a closely related but not identical precedent. Allied governments in the Pacific sought both to punish unlawful wartime conduct and to establish legal principles that would apply beyond national borders.

Chapter 81 described the surrender of August–September 1945. The Potsdam Declaration demanded punishment of war criminals, and Japan accepted that condition. Chapter 82 explained how MacArthur and SCAP exercised overriding authority through Japanese governmental institutions. The Tokyo Tribunal was established within that occupation order in January 1946; regional courts were constituted separately by Allied states according to their own military commands and legal regulations. The court did not replace the Japanese government, nor was it simply one of the Japanese domestic courts.

The postwar landscape shaped judicial practice. Government records had been dispersed or destroyed; witnesses were returning from captivity or fleeing shattered territories; political power in China and Southeast Asia remained contested. Allied governments differed about defendants and the emperor’s wartime role. The legal process therefore unfolded within the same shortages, migrations and Cold War pressures studied elsewhere in Part XII. Its decisions must be assessed in their context without assuming that the victors’ political interests made every finding false or that the existence of a formal tribunal removed the influence of politics.

Section 83.1 · Division I — The Demand for Justice and the Establishment of the Tokyo Tribunal

Defeat, Evidence, and the Question of War Responsibility

When Japanese officials and officers entered Allied custody in September 1945, their arrest was the beginning of an investigation rather than the completion of a historical judgment. The capitulation described in Chapter 81 had ended organized resistance, but the governments that defeated Japan still had to decide what acts were crimes, which individuals might be answerable for them, where a court could sit, and what evidence could fairly establish guilt. In Tokyo, a military occupation was taking shape; across Asia, survivors of prison camps, occupation armies, forced labor, bombing and massacres were describing profoundly different experiences of the war. The gap between demanding justice and proving a case in court is the opening problem of this chapter.

The Potsdam Declaration promised severe punishment for war criminals, explicitly including those responsible for cruel treatment of prisoners. But it did not identify every suspect or create a single tribunal for every wartime act. American, Chinese, British, Australian, Dutch, Philippine and other authorities gathered evidence through separate channels. Their jurisdictions reflected wartime theaters, recovered territories, military commands and political interests. At the same time the Soviet Union occupied parts of Northeast Asia and pursued its own investigations. What became popularly known as the Tokyo Trial was one institution within a much wider, uneven program of prosecutions rather than the exclusive court of Japanese wartime wrongdoing.

Responsibility itself had several meanings. Political responsibility concerned the decisions that led to expansion and war, whether or not a particular minister could be convicted under a specific criminal statute. Military responsibility implicated command, planning, orders and the supervision of subordinates. Legal responsibility demanded a charge, jurisdiction, evidence satisfying a tribunal's rules and an explanation of why the accused personally met the applicable standard. Historical responsibility could be investigated beyond the boundaries of an indictment by examining government archives, operational records, oral testimony and the longer institutional processes through which violence became possible. Moral judgments and the suffering of victims mattered, but neither removed the need to distinguish different levels of accountability.

Japan's modern state had complicated this task by dividing authority among the imperial court, cabinet, armed services, general staffs and regional commanders. Army officers sometimes acted beyond civilian direction, yet civilian institutions financed, authorized or accepted policies that made those actions possible. A commander might have ordered an attack, knowingly tolerated atrocities, failed to intervene when warning arrived, or simply lacked effective control at the critical moment. Those circumstances could produce different legal results. The trial of a foreign minister was not interchangeable with the trial of an officer who supervised a prison camp; neither could be assessed solely because each held an important title.

The available evidence was also shaped by the end of war. Some Japanese records had been destroyed, some remained in dispersed ministries or private hands, and some became accessible only after Allied searches. Prisoners of war could describe coercion and treatment that were absent from military paperwork. Chinese civilians could testify to the consequences of occupation operations, but distance, language, political insecurity and the rules of a particular court could keep them from appearing. Memoirs written after surrender might contain important observations while simultaneously serving a defendant's effort to separate himself from colleagues. A court's written judgment is therefore a valuable but purpose-built historical source, not a substitute for the entire historical record.

The demands of justice had a political setting. SCAP sought to demilitarize Japan and secure compliance with surrender without provoking administrative collapse. Allied governments faced domestic publics eager for punishment, especially after news of brutal prisoner-of-war treatment became widely known. Their governments also disagreed over the emperor, the timing of trials, the breadth of accusations and the relative priority of aggressive war and atrocities. A defendant's absence from an indictment could therefore reflect political calculations, limits of jurisdiction, inadequate evidence or the available prosecution strategy. These explanations must be investigated, not assumed.

The distinction between crimes against peace and conventional war crimes is central. The former addressed responsibility for planning, preparing or waging aggressive war, a far-reaching theory that confronted national leaders. The latter addressed conduct during war: unlawful killings, mistreatment of captives, forced labor and other violations of wartime rules. A trial could address both, but different defendants were usually selected to illustrate different forms of conduct. Later shorthand describing Class A and Class B/C proceedings should not be mistaken for a ranking of the value of victims' experiences: the killing of civilians was not made less serious because it was prosecuted in a regional military court.

Students should accordingly ask four questions of every prosecution discussed in this chapter: What act was alleged? What authority or duty connected the accused to it? What evidence did the court accept, and what did it reject? Which other individuals or injuries fell outside the case? These questions allow examination of the tribunals as attempts at accountability without treating the proceedings as either pure impartial justice or empty theater. They also explain why the subject belongs after Chapter 82's analysis of occupation government and before Chapter 84's account of new constitutional institutions: justice was part of state reconstruction, but it did not settle the meaning of Japan's wartime past.

Section 83.2 · Division I — The Demand for Justice and the Establishment of the Tokyo Tribunal

Allied Commitments, Arrests, and the Selection of Defendants, 1945–1946

Allied prosecutions grew out of declarations made during the war and administrative decisions after it. The Potsdam Declaration of July 26, 1945 stated that war criminals would face justice, but the mechanism was still unsettled when Japan surrendered. In September occupation authorities began identifying leading political and military suspects. The arrests that attracted the most attention included former prime minister Tōjō Hideki, whose wartime cabinet had directed Japan during the opening of the Pacific conflict. His failed suicide attempt when American personnel came to arrest him became a conspicuous image of defeat, but it tells little by itself about which criminal counts prosecutors could prove against him.

Suspects were detained at Sugamo Prison in Tokyo, a facility that acquired a new role as the center of confinement and later executions connected with occupation justice. Detention did not settle guilt. Investigators had to assemble career histories, cabinet decisions, diaries, records of conferences and evidence relating to prisoners and civilians. Some suspects were questioned and released; others remained under investigation without eventually appearing before the International Military Tribunal for the Far East. The selection of defendants was a legal and political process under Allied supervision rather than an automatic conversion of former officeholders into convicted criminals.

The roster ultimately charged at Tokyo reflected multiple pathways into authority. Tōjō had served as army minister and prime minister. Kido Kōichi was closely connected to the emperor as lord keeper of the privy seal. Former foreign minister Hirota Kōki had held both cabinet and diplomatic responsibilities; another former foreign minister, Tōgō Shigenori, had resisted some decisions leading to wider war but still faced charges grounded in the years of Japanese expansion. Matsui Iwane was associated with Japanese command during the 1937 assault on Nanjing. Other defendants had served as generals, admirals, intelligence organizers, financial administrators or diplomats. Explaining these roles individually prevents an indictment from flattening the state into one undifferentiated military conspiracy.

Prosecution priorities developed as evidence was collected. Investigators relied on surrendered documents, interrogation reports, Allied military records, civilian witness statements and files supplied by governments that had fought Japan. The United States led the work in Tokyo, but the Soviet Union, China, Britain, Australia and other participating governments advocated for their own concerns. The United Nations War Crimes Commission had already facilitated the exchange of allegations, yet it was not itself the tribunal conducting the Tokyo proceedings. Competing Allied views about which officials should stand trial could persist even after a formal prosecution staff existed.

The emperor's position made selection particularly contentious. Australia placed Hirohito on a proposed list of major suspects and pressed for consideration of his role. American occupation leaders saw possible prosecution as a threat to the use of the imperial institution for maintaining public compliance. Legal inquiries into his wartime decisions took place, but exclusion from the indictment preceded any adversarial judicial determination of guilt. It would be misleading to say that a court absolved Hirohito. He was never a defendant. The documentary and political history of that omission belongs to Sections 83.10 and 83.11, not to a short note appended to the trial's verdicts.

The investigators simultaneously distinguished between two sets of cases. The Tokyo court was designed principally for figures accused of planning or waging aggressive war, whereas regional courts investigated particular acts against prisoners, civilians and Allied personnel. These investigations could overlap. Documents describing atrocities might help establish what civilian and military leaders had known, while a regional tribunal might focus on the officer or camp commandant nearest the acts. Because the venues operated under different national legal systems and administrative rules, the same wartime hierarchy could appear differently depending upon the jurisdiction.

Time and political geography affected what could be proved. Evidence from China had to be gathered in the midst of renewed civil war. Records from Southeast Asia were collected while former European colonial powers, emerging nationalist states and returning Allied militaries struggled over authority. Witnesses who had survived captivity were scattered across continents. An occupied person might have vivid evidence about a camp guard but no basis for describing what ministers in Tokyo knew; a cabinet diary might reveal high policy while omitting what military units did to villagers. Successful prosecution required connecting different kinds of material rather than privileging rank or proximity alone.

The indictment was submitted on April 29, 1946, and the proceedings opened the following month. Twenty-eight original defendants entered the record, but that number should not be confused with the twenty-five men later convicted: two died during proceedings, and one was judged unfit to stand trial. The distinction is not a clerical detail; it shows that the trial was a process unfolding over more than two years, during which persons, evidence and the institutional setting changed. The arrests of 1945 created the possibility of judgment, but they did not predetermine every finding in November 1948.

Section 83.3 · Division I — The Demand for Justice and the Establishment of the Tokyo Tribunal

The IMTFE Charter: Crimes against Peace, War Crimes, and Crimes against Humanity

On January 19, 1946, MacArthur proclaimed the establishment of the International Military Tribunal for the Far East, commonly abbreviated IMTFE. Its charter was amended on April 26, just before the indictment and opening proceedings. The formal choice to create an international military tribunal under SCAP's authority reflected the Allied surrender settlement and subsequent policy agreements. It also embodied a fundamental asymmetry: the victorious governments established the court, defined its jurisdiction, selected its judges and ultimately exercised authority over the sentences. This fact shaped the debate over the tribunal's legitimacy, although it does not by itself answer whether the conduct charged was criminal or whether particular defendants were guilty.

Article 5 of the amended charter is decisive for understanding the court's jurisdiction. It authorized the tribunal to try individuals whose offenses included crimes against peace. It then set out three categories: crimes against peace, conventional war crimes, and crimes against humanity. The first included planning, preparing, initiating or waging aggressive war, or participating in a common plan or conspiracy to do so. The second encompassed violations of the laws or customs of war. The third addressed murder, extermination, enslavement, deportation and related inhumane acts or specified persecutions connected to crimes within the tribunal's jurisdiction. The categories overlapped in possible evidence but were not identical descriptions of the same offense.

The conventional shorthand 'Class A,' 'Class B' and 'Class C' became common after the war. It roughly tracked the charter's three categories, but can mislead if treated as three levels of moral seriousness. A guard convicted for torturing prisoners was not convicted of a trivial offense because the charge belonged to the conventional-war-crimes category. Moreover, Class B/C courts were generally separate national military tribunals, not subordinate chambers that automatically sent appeals to the Tokyo court. Some accused at Tokyo faced charges involving conventional war crimes as well as crimes against peace; the legal architecture must not be reduced to a tidy hierarchy of persons.

Article 6 addressed responsibility for persons who acted in official positions or followed orders. Neither rank nor government orders automatically exempted an accused from punishment, although superior orders might enter a sentencing decision. Article 9 established rights concerning notice of charges, representation by counsel, translation and adequate opportunity to prepare a defense. These provisions matter because the tribunal was not framed as summary punishment. Yet rights written into a charter are not proof that every accused had equal practical access to resources or that interpreters could resolve all disputes over meaning. Students must compare formal procedural guarantees with the surviving proceedings.

Article 13 allowed a flexible evidentiary process. Rather than binding the tribunal to the restrictive evidence rules familiar in some domestic courts, it authorized consideration of material deemed to have probative value and permitted certain official and documentary forms of proof. This made it possible to admit extensive diplomatic correspondence, reports and affidavits documenting distant wartime theaters. It also created an obvious question: if a document was accepted without the author appearing for cross-examination, what limitations did the defense face? Flexibility could make a vast international prosecution workable, but it could also complicate testing reliability. Not every exhibit received the same scrutiny or carried equal weight.

The crimes-against-peace jurisdiction generated the sharpest legal objections. Japan and other states had agreed to instruments such as the 1928 Pact of Paris renouncing war as an instrument of national policy. The majority later argued that aggressive war violated existing international law and that individuals who led it could be punished. Defense lawyers and dissenting judges questioned whether the treaties had clearly established individual criminal penalties when the charged acts occurred. The dispute is often called the problem of ex post facto or retroactive criminal law: can a person be convicted under a penal principle not sufficiently defined beforehand? Students need not decide the issue from a slogan; they should examine the treaty language, the charter, the majority's reasoning and the separate opinions.

Comparison with Nuremberg clarifies both precedent and difference. The European tribunal influenced the Tokyo charter and the categories of responsibility, while the Pacific case extended across distinct conflicts, governments and years from the 1930s through 1945. The Tokyo judges confronted Japanese constitutional institutions, civil-military relations and an empire whose subjects included Koreans, Taiwanese and many other populations. The occupation decision to exclude Hirohito also distinguished the proceedings from the abstract legal assertion that official status was no shield. This tension between legal principle and political selection did not invalidate all findings automatically, but it demanded historical investigation.

The court's jurisdiction was not universal over all injuries of war. The charter made the prosecutor's selection and the legal characterization of crimes critical. The Tokyo indictment emphasized aggressive war and leadership responsibility; regional courts dealt with a larger universe of atrocities through their own laws and procedures. Consequently, a student reading a judicial decision should begin with its legal question rather than assume that the document gives a complete account of wartime life. A tribunal might make detailed findings about Nanjing but leave other coercive systems underexamined. Legal documentation can be rigorous evidence and still reflect its institutional purpose.

The amended charter, preserved in the United Nations treaty collection, is a primary source rather than merely background terminology. Its phrases define the rules that made some defendants prosecutable and placed other conduct beyond that specific court's practical reach. Its Articles 5, 6, 9 and 13 should be read alongside the court's eventual judgment, because the meaning of a legal text is partly revealed through its application. Throughout this chapter the distinction remains constant: the historical occurrence of an atrocity, the legal classification of an offense and the individual guilt of an accused are three propositions requiring related but not interchangeable evidence.

Historical Vocabulary

Crimes against peace, conventional war crimes, and crimes against humanity

Crimes against peace encompassed planning, preparing, initiating or waging an aggressive war or taking part in a common plan to do so. Conventional war crimes concerned unlawful conduct in warfare, such as mistreatment of prisoners. Crimes against humanity covered defined inhumane acts and persecutions connected to the tribunal's jurisdiction. The labels Class A, B and C are common shorthand, not moral rankings, and the regional B/C courts were independent national proceedings.

Historical context: The charge of aggressive war raised a genuine legality debate about individual punishment under preexisting international law; prohibitions on mistreating prisoners and civilians had a longer-established legal pedigree. Do not assume every act charged in one category was tried in only one institutional venue.

Section 83.4 · Division I — The Demand for Justice and the Establishment of the Tokyo Tribunal

Judges, Prosecutors, Defense Lawyers, and the Politics of an Allied Court

The Tokyo Tribunal brought together judges from eleven Allied states. Its president, Sir William Webb of Australia, presided over a bench that included jurists from the United States, Britain, the Soviet Union, China, France, the Netherlands, Canada, New Zealand, India and the Philippines. The eleven judges did not speak with one legal or political voice. They brought divergent experiences of war, colonial rule and domestic judicial practice. Some had personal experience of the Japanese occupation, while others approached the court principally through questions of international legal development. The composition was intended to display the multinational authority of the victors, but it did not make the proceedings free of power differences among them.

Joseph B. Keenan, an American lawyer, served as chief prosecutor and directed an international prosecution section assisted by representatives of participating governments. Prosecutors had to organize enormous numbers of documents, establish a chronology and decide which forms of individual responsibility to charge. Their cooperation was never frictionless. A country that had endured occupation could prioritize civilian suffering and atrocities; another could prioritize the legal novelty of aggressive war; the United States also had a separate interest in the occupation's stability. The court's international character therefore created both a broader evidence network and disagreements over strategy.

Defense counsel included Japanese lawyers familiar with the country's prewar legal and administrative order and American attorneys accustomed to adversarial courtroom argument. For defendants accused in relation to foreign-language treaties, cabinet memoranda and military records, these teams faced the task of reconstructing decisions made across several decades. They challenged admissibility, questioned witnesses, proposed alternative accounts of policy and argued that individual defendants had less knowledge or control than prosecutors claimed. Defense participation should not be dismissed simply because all remaining defendants were eventually convicted. An investigation into whether justice was fair must examine counsel's actual opportunities, motions and objections, not only the result.

The venue itself affected procedure. The court met in the former Japanese Army Ministry building at Ichigaya, a site associated with the military institutions now under judgment. Japanese, English and other languages circulated through proceedings, documents and translation teams. Court transcripts could create an impression of exactness, but producing them required interpretation, correction, editorial decisions and technical resources. The legal record preserved spoken testimony filtered through these procedures. Even an apparently straightforward phrase about an order, command or report might be disputed when its Japanese bureaucratic context did not match a convenient English equivalent.

Formal rules gave the accused counsel, access to charges and the ability to challenge evidence. Practical equality was harder to secure. Prosecutors worked with Allied investigative authority, access to captured records and the power to obtain materials through occupation networks. Defendants were confined and often relied on their lawyers to locate former colleagues or surviving files. Some wartime records had been destroyed; others were unavailable through ordinary defense channels. Language differences affected witness preparation and the handling of exhibits. These inequalities should be identified concretely without assuming that any procedural imperfection automatically disproved otherwise well-supported findings.

SCAP's role was equally complex. MacArthur established the tribunal under the surrender settlement and had authority affecting the implementation of sentences. He was not one of the judges deciding every count; legal reasoning and dissent were produced within the tribunal. Yet the occupation commander was not simply outside the story. His policy toward Hirohito shaped who was absent, and his headquarters provided the physical and administrative setting in which the court worked. Distinguishing the bench's adjudicative function from SCAP's policy and executive functions makes it possible to identify precisely where political influence might enter the process.

Differences among the judges became visible in the opinions written at the end. Justice Radhabinod Pal of India challenged the court's legal foundations and argued for acquittal on all charges. Justice B. V. A. Röling of the Netherlands and Justice Henri Bernard of France also dissented in important respects, though for reasons not identical to Pal's. Webb and Justice Delfin Jaranilla of the Philippines wrote separate views without joining Pal's sweeping conclusion. The final plurality of legal arguments was not an afterthought: it reflected disputes embedded from the beginning in the court's constitution, evidence and understanding of justice.

A historian should approach the courtroom as a place of work. Clerks numbered exhibits, interpreters resolved terminology, witnesses waited for questioning, defense lawyers sifted records and journalists transmitted selected portions of a sprawling proceeding to the public. The passage from an incident in 1937 to a finding delivered in 1948 required successive acts of selection and interpretation. That labor can produce accountable historical knowledge, but it can also hide omissions behind the appearance of a formal transcript. Understanding who performed each task helps students evaluate the trial as a legal institution rather than a dramatic confrontation between a single prosecutor and the defeated government.

Section 83.5 · Division I — The Demand for Justice and the Establishment of the Tokyo Tribunal

The Indictment and the Problem of a Continuous War Conspiracy

The indictment filed on April 29, 1946 contained fifty-five counts. Its principal narrative alleged participation in a common plan or conspiracy to secure Japanese domination through aggressive war, stretching across a broad period of policy development and military expansion. It also charged particular wars, murders and conventional war crimes. The prosecution's central challenge was not proving that armed conflict had occurred; it was connecting specific accused leaders to unlawful decisions and showing that their participation met the charter's standards. Because the indictment covered many years, it invited the court to transform the history of Japanese diplomacy and military policy into a coherent sequence of criminal intent.

The 1931 Manchurian Incident revealed the difficulties of that project. Elements of the Kwantung Army acted without straightforward civilian authorization and quickly altered the political situation in Northeast China. Later Japanese governments accepted or defended arrangements that had originated in military initiative. A historian can describe a cumulative expansion of empire, but a criminal conspiracy count requires more: evidence that named people joined a common plan, understood its character and contributed in legally significant ways. Subsequent cabinet policies and military coordination might support such an inference, yet they cannot simply be projected backward into every earlier decision as though one secret committee had directed everything from the start.

The war against China from 1937 created additional layers. Civilian ministers negotiated, military commanders issued orders, and regional units acted amid increasingly violent occupation. Reports of atrocities reached officials through diplomatic and military channels, while competing strategic arguments circulated in Tokyo. Evidence of the scale of the war did not automatically answer whether a particular defendant had ordered unlawful conduct, knew of it in time to intervene or possessed effective capacity to prevent it. The prosecution thus had to present policy history and individual conduct together, and its success could differ from one accused to another.

The movement toward war with the United States and European colonial powers added still more complex decision-making. Oil restrictions, negotiations in Washington, army operations in China, imperial conferences, naval planning and cabinet changes were all relevant. The attack on Pearl Harbor became an essential part of the legal narrative of aggressive war, but the trial's treatment of Japanese decision-making was not a complete diplomatic history of 1941. Documents selected as exhibits could illuminate what leaders approved while omitting the domestic disagreements, misunderstandings and contingent choices that historians reconstruct from a wider archive. Law often seeks a conclusion about liability where history also seeks an explanation of process.

A broad conspiracy theory offered prosecutors practical advantages. It could link many defendants to wars that no single person had directed continuously and frame Japanese expansion as a connected enterprise rather than unrelated campaigns. Yet it also risked flattening shifts in goals, institutions and personnel. The difference between a long-standing ideological preference for continental influence and a legally established agreement to launch specified aggressive wars mattered. The defense challenged the indictment's scope and the meaning of conspiracy in international law, while the majority relied on evidence of coordinated actions and continuing policy commitments. These arguments form the analytical core of the trial, not a technical digression.

The indictment also shaped whose suffering became visible. The aggressive-war charges emphasized the illegality of initiating or expanding conflict, while atrocity evidence entered partly to demonstrate what those wars produced and what leaders had failed to restrain. Prisoners, Chinese civilians and victims of occupation crimes could become witnesses to a larger legal thesis about leadership. This brought their experiences into an international record, but it could also subordinate their individual histories to a prosecution organized around state decision-makers. Regional trials were therefore indispensable companions to Tokyo, since many focused more directly on the conduct of particular units, camps and personnel.

The problem of scale affected evidence. Cabinet minutes were often ambiguous about intention; diary entries might be incomplete or written for posterity; intelligence reports could contain errors or political assumptions. When the prosecution offered evidence of a larger pattern, the defense sought to isolate a defendant's particular moment of service. Both positions could contain genuine historical insights, and neither should be accepted without examination. A defendant who served briefly in a foreign ministry might differ from a military planner in office for a decade, though participation in a decisive cabinet vote could still be legally consequential.

The court's eventual findings did not mirror the indictment count for count. Legal charges were assessed, narrowed and interpreted, with some defendants convicted on a different combination of counts than others. That distinction prevents the common mistake of treating the accusation of a continuous conspiracy as itself the tribunal's settled historical conclusion in every detail. Students should compare the indictment's expansive allegations to the judgment's particular findings rather than convert prosecutorial rhetoric directly into fact. The case illustrates how a war can be narrated as an interconnected sequence while the evidence for personal criminal liability remains uneven.

For the wider textbook, the lasting question is how institutions, ideology and individual agency interact. Japan's empire was not the work of one minister, nor did it expand without the decisions of responsible officials. The indictment tried to connect those scales: an international order violated by aggressive war, state structures mobilized for conquest and human beings answerable for their contributions. Whether it successfully did so in each instance is a question for evidence and judgment. The next division examines how that demanding task unfolded through testimony, translation, documentary disputes and competing legal interpretations.

Section 83.6 · Division II — The Tokyo Trial in Operation: Evidence, Accusation, and Defense

Inside the Ichigaya Courtroom: Hearings, Translation, and Evidence

The Tokyo proceedings opened on May 3, 1946 in Ichigaya, a former Japanese military headquarters converted into an Allied courtroom. This setting carried symbolic force: institutions that had helped manage Japan's military state now housed proceedings examining its leaders. But the trial's day-to-day character was less theatrical than its photographs suggest. It consisted of long arguments over jurisdiction, bundles of documentary exhibits, examination of witnesses, translations, procedural rulings and attempts to organize decades of history into a legally intelligible sequence. The hearings continued for more than two years, and the surviving transcripts preserve not one continuous conversation but an elaborate, collaboratively produced legal record.

Prosecutors presented ministerial and military records, captured documents, public statements, diplomatic communications, operational reports and testimony. Some materials were contemporary to the acts in question; others were affidavits or recollections prepared after defeat. Documents could be translated several times before a judge encountered them. A Chinese witness's experience of occupation, a Japanese officer's testimony concerning command and an English-language Allied intelligence report were not equivalent sources simply because each was numbered as an exhibit. They differed in proximity to events, purpose, institutional setting and ability to identify individual accused. The tribunal's relaxed evidence rules facilitated admitting varied materials, but historical interpretation still required distinguishing them.

The defense's work was correspondingly demanding. Attorneys questioned witnesses, challenged translations and asked that documents be read in their original contexts. Some accused sought to explain official procedures that foreign observers viewed as inexplicable or evasive. Others presented testimony emphasizing service in offices not directly controlling units accused of atrocities. The court could hear these arguments, but the practical challenge of obtaining documents under occupation and reconstructing decisions made years earlier remained severe. A procedural right to cross-examine was meaningful without being a guarantee that every relevant person could be compelled to appear.

Kayoko Takeda's scholarship draws attention to the people who mediated the court's languages. The trial employed interpretation arrangements involving Japanese and English, with additional language problems when testimony or documents arrived from other jurisdictions. Interpreters and language monitors negotiated terms such as authority, order, command, responsibility and sovereign prerogative, each of which carried historical and legal implications. Fluency alone did not resolve specialized military vocabulary or differences between Japanese official formulas and their English renderings. The court's multinational judges required a shared working record, yet a transcript may conceal interpretive adjustments behind a polished English sentence.

Interpretation could affect the speed, tone and apparent precision of testimony. A witness interrupted for clarification might appear uncertain in a printed transcript even when the source of confusion was a translation choice. An officer might use a conventional expression of bureaucratic submission that sounded like an admission of personal control when put into literal English. Conversely, technical words about operational authority could obscure responsibility if treated as untranslatable Japanese customs. Translators were not the judges of guilt; their responsibility was to convey meaning. Historians nevertheless must study how their labor influenced the evidence available to the court and the accounts later readers received.

Public reporting added another layer. Newspapers summarized days of testimony into articles, and audiences rarely followed the entire proceeding. Journalists worked in a country where SCAP exercised extensive control over publishing, described in Chapter 82. A press account's emphasis on a famous defendant, a confession or an atrocity exhibit may reflect editorial decisions and censorship constraints as well as the distribution of evidence. Japanese readers might encounter the trial through translated excerpts or radio summaries, while victims in other Asian countries often had different avenues for obtaining information. The public meaning of a trial is not identical to the full legal argument recorded in its files.

The physical court also reproduced asymmetries. The prisoners sat under guard, Allied prosecutors had substantial institutional support, and judges were selected by victorious powers. At the same time the trial afforded defense counsel opportunities to dispute charges and produced dissenting opinions far more complex than a purely ceremonial proceeding would have required. These two observations must be held together. Describing the trial as political does not demonstrate that all exhibits were false; describing it as a court does not establish procedural equality. The proper question is how institutional conditions shaped particular rulings and whether a challenged finding has independent evidentiary support.

The record that survives offers an exceptional entry into Japanese twentieth-century history. It contains competing explanations of military command, extracts from government records and testimony about violence across enormous distances. Its abundance can be misleading: a transcript is not comprehensive merely because it is lengthy. Judges determined relevance, prosecutors determined charging priorities and witnesses answered the questions put to them. Many survivors never appeared, and not every aspect of empire was legally at issue. A serious student should therefore compare courtroom sources with archives produced before and after the trial, asking not only what a witness said but why that witness was called and what the court was attempting to prove.

Section 83.7 · Division II — The Tokyo Trial in Operation: Evidence, Accusation, and Defense

The Prosecution's Case: From Manchuria and China to the Pacific War

The prosecution assembled a sweeping narrative of Japanese expansion across Northeast Asia, China and the Pacific. It began with the growing autonomy and ambition of Japanese military institutions, moved through the seizure of Manchuria in 1931, traced warfare in China after 1937 and culminated in decisions to attack the United States and European colonial possessions in 1941. The central legal argument was that leaders who planned and waged aggressive war could be held individually criminally responsible. This should not be confused with a claim that every policy memorandum was criminal or that every episode of fighting had been directed through one unchanging command center.

Manchuria presented the prosecution with both opportunity and difficulty. The Kwantung Army's actions had disrupted an international settlement and produced the puppet state Manchukuo, but military initiative and civilian acquiescence were not the same kind of decision. Prosecutors examined statements made by army leaders, diplomatic protests, official responses and the emerging institutions of occupation. The court could use this material to assess whether defendants took part in continuing illegal expansion. Historians must still distinguish the legal argument from the larger history of Japanese economic investment, security anxieties, settler colonization, Chinese political struggles and imperial ideology that made Manchuria important to the state.

The assault on China from 1937 expanded the record of military planning and atrocity. Documents related to the Marco Polo Bridge crisis, subsequent campaigns and the creation of occupation authorities entered the prosecution's case. Evidence concerning Nanjing was relevant not only to the acts of troops but to what senior commanders and ministers knew or had reason to know. Chinese survivors' testimony and contemporaneous foreign observers' reports were indispensable for reconstructing violence. Yet the tribunal had to decide whether the evidence connected named officials to particular offenses, an inquiry separate from the broader historical conclusion that mass atrocities occurred.

Diplomatic files from the late 1930s and early 1940s offered another kind of evidence. Japanese officials discussed treaties with Germany and Italy, negotiations with Britain and the United States, economic pressure, the war in China and the strategic implications of European war. The prosecution tried to show patterns of purpose and agreement. The defense countered that policy choices could have multiple motives and that the existence of a diplomatic coalition did not establish criminal agreement in every military decision. Both sides treated cabinet records as important, but neither could make the bureaucratic structure disappear. Different ministries and services pursued policies that sometimes conflicted sharply.

The 1941 crisis brought the question of aggressive war into its most recognizable form. Negotiations with the United States proceeded while Japanese commanders prepared possible military operations. Decisions made in imperial conferences and at cabinet level were particularly important because they linked high civilian office, military planning and the final turn toward war. The attack on Pearl Harbor and simultaneous operations in Southeast Asia were not isolated from the Chinese war; Japanese leaders themselves understood them in relation to resource access and refusal to abandon continental commitments. But explaining those calculations does not make the decision inevitable or settle the legal question of initiating aggression.

Prosecutors also had to establish individual roles across changing governments. An official might have left office before December 1941 but contributed to earlier territorial expansion; another might have joined a cabinet while seeking to limit conflict; an officer might have exercised operational authority without shaping national diplomacy. The tribunal's jurisdiction permitted examining their different contributions, but a meaningful verdict depended on evidence about each person's decisions. The indictment's breadth made this difficult. Historical explanation can show structural pressures and institutions, whereas criminal judgment must still articulate acts or omissions that made a defendant liable.

One risk of the prosecution's approach was that it treated Japan as a singular actor. Imperial Japan was an organized state, but the institutions operating inside it did not always have common interests. The army and navy disagreed over strategy, civilian foreign ministers attempted negotiations, and field commanders sometimes compelled policy shifts. None of these divisions proves a general absence of responsibility. They do require an account of how conflict among officials could coexist with the acceptance of expansionist outcomes. The most persuasive sections of the trial record are those where documentary decisions are connected to identifiable persons rather than attributed to an abstract national character.

Another risk was uneven international perspective. Aggressive-war charges permitted Allied lawyers to place Japan's wars into a legal narrative that foregrounded relations among recognized states. Colonized Asian populations were not equally represented as independent states at the time. Many experienced Japanese conquest while also opposing European imperial regimes. A prosecution centered on the violation of interstate peace could capture major aspects of aggression without fully explaining political movements in Indonesia, Vietnam or Korea. Historians should therefore use the Tokyo court in conjunction with regional evidence and accounts from subject peoples.

The prosecution ultimately succeeded in obtaining convictions, but success in court should not mean that every interpretive claim becomes beyond dispute. Its documentary reconstruction offered an enduring record of governmental decisions and the aggressive uses of armed force. Later historians have revisited chronologies, clarified individual motives and challenged the portrayal of uninterrupted conspiracy. The proper lesson is not that the trial's historical narrative should be either memorized uncritically or discarded. It should be analyzed as an unusually consequential argument built from many genuine sources under the constraints and objectives of a criminal proceeding.

Atrocities, Prisoners of War, Nanjing, and Command Responsibility

War crimes evidence at Tokyo extended beyond the unlawful initiation of war. The court considered treatment of prisoners of war, abuse of civilians, coercive labor and violence during occupation. The Nanjing atrocities of 1937–1938 became one of the trial's most significant episodes, both because of the scale of violence and because the accused included military and diplomatic leaders connected with the Chinese campaign. This chapter does not repeat the detailed history of massacres described in Chapter 79. It asks how evidence about atrocities entered a judicial record and how a court tried to assign personal responsibility to officials whose relationship to the events differed.

Contemporary evidence of Nanjing included reports from foreigners in the city, Japanese records, witness testimony and later investigative material. These sources had different strengths and limitations. A witness could describe the killing of civilians or assault against women but might not identify which commander issued a relevant order. Diplomatic correspondence could show that news of atrocities reached Tokyo while failing to establish what a particular minister did after receiving it. Military command documents might reveal a chain of authority, but their silence about unlawful conduct could result from omission or destruction rather than innocence. The tribunal had to connect occurrence, knowledge and responsibility with care.

Matsui Iwane, a senior commander associated with the campaign, was convicted for failures connected with atrocities. Hirota Kōki, as foreign minister during the period, was judged responsible in part for inadequate action after reports of violence reached diplomatic channels. These cases illustrate distinct theories of responsibility. One concerned the duty of a commander in relation to forces under authority; the other concerned an official's knowledge and response within a civilian ministry. The legal judgment against Hirota has remained controversial, particularly regarding the extent of his power to compel the army. Recognizing the controversy is not a denial of the atrocities but a question about the legal link between ministerial office and criminal omission.

Prisoners of war generated another immense body of evidence. Captured Allied personnel had been subjected in various theaters to executions, beatings, starvation, disease and extremely coercive labor. Affidavits and witness testimony allowed investigators to reconstruct the administration of camps and transport routes. Yet an account of suffering in one camp did not prove that every defendant in Tokyo had directed it. Prosecutors needed records of regulations, command arrangements, complaints and responses to establish responsibility of superiors. Regional trials often examined these camp-level facts more closely than the central tribunal, where broad leadership decisions occupied much of the court's attention.

The case of General Yamashita Tomoyuki in the Philippines illustrates why command responsibility was legally disputed. He was tried by a U.S. military commission over atrocities committed by troops in his command during the final campaign, with the central issue being his failure to control their actions under desperate wartime conditions. The U.S. Supreme Court upheld the authority of the commission in In re Yamashita in 1946, while dissenting justices criticized aspects of the procedure and the standard applied. The decision did not establish that commanders were automatically liable whenever subordinates committed crimes; it became a landmark in debate over knowledge, control and duty to prevent or punish. Students should examine both majority and dissents.

Patterns of atrocity also raised questions about institutional responsibility. Military training, treatment of captives, transport shortages, racial hierarchies, official propaganda and the coercive demands of imperial occupation could encourage or normalize illegal conduct. These structural conditions help explain widespread crimes, but they cannot substitute for evidence connecting an individual accused to an actionable duty or order. The challenge for courts was to avoid allowing dispersed command systems to become a refuge from accountability while also avoiding indiscriminate punishment based on rank. Historical scholarship can consider broader institutional causation alongside the necessarily more specific findings of legal tribunals.

The evidentiary record has limits of visibility. Foreign prisoners could sometimes testify in Allied languages and receive institutional assistance. Many Chinese, Korean, Indonesian, Filipino and other Asian civilians faced obstacles in making their experiences heard in Tokyo. Sexual violence suffered by women across the occupied territories did not become a comprehensive subject of prosecution, even though particular cases were tried elsewhere. Students must not infer that a harm was absent merely because it occupied little space in a Tokyo judgment. The volume and nature of courtroom evidence tell us something about prosecutorial priorities and access to justice as well as about the acts being investigated.

A careful analysis of atrocity charges therefore moves among three scales: the event, the chain of command and the institution that enabled or ignored it. The same evidence may support conclusions at more than one scale, but not automatically. Witness descriptions can establish violence; orders can connect it to command; repeated reports and inaction may bear on knowledge and omission. The legal standards applied in 1945–1948 were themselves under development, and judges could disagree about what they required. The trials contributed to later understandings of command responsibility precisely because they exposed the difficulty of assigning liability in large military organizations.

The historical significance lies in the attempt to make wartime violence answerable to law rather than leave it as a regrettable by-product of victory and defeat. The judgments recognized atrocities and created records that survivors and historians could later revisit. But the achievement was partial. Not every perpetrator was apprehended, not every victim testified and not every doctrine of responsibility was applied consistently. The limits are reasons to read the court's documents more closely, not reasons to erase either the crimes or the evidence painstakingly assembled against particular accused.

Historical Vocabulary

Command responsibility and criminal omission

Command responsibility: The legal problem of whether a military superior can be punished for crimes committed by personnel under his effective authority when the superior knew or had reason to know of offenses and failed to take legally required action. The precise standard differed across tribunals and periods; rank alone is not a sufficient explanation of liability.

Criminal omission: An alleged failure to act where a legal duty and capacity to act existed. In the Tokyo judgment, arguments about Matsui and Hirota involved different institutional capacities. In re Yamashita (1946) illustrates the importance—and controversy—of a commander's obligation to control troops (Source Register 8).

Defense Arguments, Legal Objections, and the Limits of the Record

The defense contested the legal foundation of the Tokyo Tribunal as well as the facts alleged against individual accused. One central argument was that international law before 1945 had prohibited aggressive war in treaties without clearly providing for criminal punishment of the individuals who planned it. Lawyers invoked principles against retroactive criminal prosecution and questioned the tribunal's authority to define new offenses after Japan's defeat. The majority rejected these objections, relying heavily on Nuremberg's reasoning that aggressive war was already illegal and that the individuals directing states could be held responsible. The disagreement concerned a genuine problem in the development of international criminal law, not simply a refusal to confront the suffering caused by Japan's wars.

Another defense argument challenged jurisdiction derived from the occupying powers. The tribunal was constituted under SCAP rather than a prewar standing international court. Judges came from states that had defeated Japan; the victorious Allies were not being tried there for their conduct. These facts invited the label 'victors' justice.' That label can identify a structural asymmetry, but it does not determine whether particular charges were supported. A student should test the claim at several levels: selection of perpetrators, applicable law, fairness of procedure, evidence accepted and sentences imposed. The problems are connected but analytically distinct.

Defense counsel also advanced explanations based on Japanese governmental institutions. Under the Meiji constitutional order the emperor occupied the apex of formal sovereignty, while cabinets, service chiefs and independent commands possessed different practical roles. Some defendants argued that they were not directing policy, that they acted under superior orders or that service obligations constrained their choices. Article 6 of the charter did not provide a blanket immunity for high office or obedience. Yet institutional distinctions still mattered: the difference between approving a military plan, recording an imperial decision and carrying out an order could affect the evidence of individual intention and control.

The tribunal received claims that Japanese expansion responded to international insecurity and Western colonialism in Asia. These arguments could illuminate how leaders understood their actions, but explanation of motives is not equivalent to legal justification. The fact that European empires had conquered territories and that the United States and other states had used force did not itself legalize Japanese attacks or excuse violations against prisoners. At the same time, the court's selective focus on a defeated power did raise questions about the legitimacy of applying general principles only to the losers. These issues were especially important to judges whose national histories included both colonial domination and anti-colonial movements.

Evidence rules prompted still more specific objections. Affidavits, hearsay and translated extracts could be admitted more readily than in some domestic criminal courts. Prosecutors defended flexibility as essential to proceedings covering a vast theater and numerous languages. Defenders of more restrictive evidentiary rules worried that the accused could not confront every author or test all statements. The practical consequence of a disputed document depended on how central it was to a conviction and whether independent evidence corroborated it. Blanket statements that the trial admitted hearsay, therefore the judgment was unreliable, fail to distinguish strong records from weaker ones.

The defense had opportunities to present its own witnesses and documents, but these were constrained by detention, access to witnesses and the work of translating material. Japanese records could be missing, scattered or politically sensitive. Some participants wrote memoirs during or after prosecution to cast earlier choices in a favorable light. Historians have to treat defense testimony as evidence that requires criticism, just as prosecution testimony does. The fact that a defendant was resisting conviction does not make every assertion false; the fact that a document was created by an Allied investigator does not make it automatically impartial.

The exclusion of Hirohito complicated defense strategy. Japanese officials had long framed decisions around imperial authority, yet the court did not have the emperor as a defendant or subject to cross-examination. Some defendants might shift blame upward; others might shield the institution. Prosecutors likewise had reasons to construct narratives of leadership that avoided the emperor's direct legal responsibility. The result was a trial in which references to imperial decision-making could carry political consequences beyond the defendant's case. Sections 83.10–83.12 address why that selective boundary mattered historically and how it shaped the allocation of blame.

The record also reflected the nature of criminal prosecution as a genre. A court asks whether specified accused are guilty on stated counts; it does not attempt to write an even-handed encyclopedia of every political choice and every person's wartime experience. Its judgments may contain rich historical description but are organized around the disposition of charges. Historians who quote a striking sentence about a policy should consult the surrounding reasoning, the relevant evidence, and any opposing opinion. The difference between an allegation, a finding of fact and a legal conclusion must be maintained in textbooks because it is fundamental to understanding the trial.

Ultimately, the defense arguments are essential to evaluating rather than dismissing the proceedings. Some objections challenged the evolving principles of international criminal law; others contested whether an accused had knowledge, authority or intent. Some claims deserve sympathy as procedural safeguards, while others fail when compared with contemporary documents. The task for students is neither to equate all positions nor to treat disagreement as proof that truth is unreachable. It is to identify the strongest evidence for each legal proposition and to distinguish persistent scholarly debate from facts that are securely established beyond the trial itself.

International accountability or selective victors’ justice?

The problem: Was the Tokyo Tribunal a credible application of emerging international criminal law, or were its legal and political asymmetries too substantial?

Interpretation A — An important legal accomplishment. Scholarship including Yuma Totani's work emphasizes the prosecutorial documentation of wartime violence and the development of individual liability for aggressive war and atrocities. Courts did more than announce victory: they received witnesses and evidence and produced detailed judgments.
Interpretation B — Deep structural limits. Section 83.10 · Division III — The Emperor, Selective Accountability, and Trials Beyond Tokyo

Hirohito's Wartime Office and the Historical Problem of Imperial Responsibility

Emperor Hirohito, known after death as the Shōwa Emperor, occupied the formal summit of the Japanese state during the wars examined by the Tokyo Tribunal. The 1889 Constitution located sovereignty in the emperor and associated supreme command of the armed forces with the imperial institution. In practice, generals, admirals, ministers and advisers controlled different channels of information and shaped the decisions presented for approval. The central historical problem is neither whether Hirohito was merely a ceremonial figure nor whether he personally ordered every campaign. It is how authority worked in particular moments and what evidence shows about his knowledge, intervention and choices. The tribunal never resolved that question in a verdict because he was never indicted.

The emperor received briefings and participated in imperial conferences at crucial political moments. Records of audience meetings, diaries of advisers and military documents show that he could ask questions, express approval or concern and influence the handling of cabinet crises. At the same time official procedure often masked disagreement behind formulas of imperial sanction. To determine responsibility, historians must reconstruct how decisions were prepared, whose recommendations the emperor received and whether he could have intervened differently. A signature or ritual approval cannot always be equated with detailed operational control, but neither should formal participation be dismissed as meaningless simply because ministers performed much of the administrative work.

The path to the war in China and the escalation of the Pacific conflict involved institutions that derived legitimacy from the throne. Military commanders often invoked imperial service, and dissent within the armed forces could be constrained by assertions of loyalty. This pattern allowed the imperial institution to unify political language even when the army, navy and civilian ministries were divided about strategy. It created the possibility that senior officials could justify decisions as service to the emperor while the emperor could be presented as separate from the consequences of those decisions. Historians studying responsibility must not accept either explanation merely because it appears in testimony created after defeat.

Hirohito's recorded interventions at the end of war are particularly important. In August 1945 he supported acceptance of surrender terms during leadership deadlock, as Chapter 81 explained. That intervention demonstrates that imperial political agency could be consequential in certain circumstances. It does not prove that the emperor had been equally capable of directing all earlier operations or that he lacked choices when decisions were presented over preceding years. Historical arguments must specify the time, institutional constraints and evidence relevant to each event. The ability to resolve one crisis is evidence about authority, but it is not a universal answer to the problem of wartime governance.

Herbert P. Bix and other historians have argued that the emperor exercised greater informed agency in wartime policy than postwar official narratives admitted. Other interpretations give more weight to the cabinet, senior military officers and the institutional pressures that framed imperial decisions. These positions are not simply questions of personal character. They differ in their reading of diaries, staff papers, conference notes and recollections, and in how they infer practical power from formal authority. Students should compare source dates, provenance and institutional context before weighing their conclusions. A participant's statement that the emperor was passive may have been shaped by a postwar effort to preserve the throne.

The evidence itself poses formidable problems. Private diaries may record conversations in abbreviated form; documents connected with the imperial household were not always accessible; advisers might interpret silence as consent; and recollections composed under occupation could have a self-protective purpose. Historians distinguish between contemporaneous notes and retrospective statements because the surrender and impending trials created strong incentives to rearrange responsibility. In the courtroom, defendants might avoid implicating the throne out of loyalty, calculation or genuine belief. Prosecutors had their own reasons to respect occupation policy. The resulting courtroom silence cannot be taken as definitive historical evidence of the emperor's lack of knowledge.

A further distinction concerns the difference between institutional and personal responsibility. The emperor's position gave legitimacy to policies enacted by state agencies, and the institution remained central to national mobilization. But personal criminal guilt would have required a specified offense, legal jurisdiction and evidence connecting Hirohito to it. International responsibility for state aggression, political accountability for the government's actions and moral judgments about an imperial role do not collapse into one proposition. Nor should these questions be extended indiscriminately to the Japanese population as a whole, many of whose members lived under conscription, censorship and material coercion.

That complexity explains why Hirohito's non-prosecution is central to the chapter rather than peripheral to a biography. The Allied court sought to judge leaders of aggressive war while the occupation deliberately retained the institution from which much prewar authority formally flowed. The legal case against individual defendants thus took place inside a political settlement defining who would be exempt from investigation as a suspect. The next section asks how that settlement emerged, what the surviving American and Australian records demonstrate, and why the absence of an indictment can never be treated as a judicial acquittal.

Why Hirohito Was Not Indicted: SCAP, Japanese Officials, and Occupation Calculations

The decision not to prosecute Hirohito emerged during the creation of the occupation and the Tokyo Tribunal, not from a trial finding that the emperor was innocent. American officials feared that removing him might jeopardize the compliance of armed forces and administrative institutions, undermine the government's ability to implement occupation directives and increase the manpower required for military rule. They also understood that many Japanese still associated national continuity with the imperial institution. Chapter 82 explained the broader policy of governing indirectly through surviving ministries. In war-crimes policy, that governing choice created a powerful incentive to separate the emperor from defendants whose convictions would demonstrate the consequences of militarism.

In late 1945 American authorities considered whether to investigate evidence concerning the emperor. Records show that Washington requested information while Australian officials advocated a more aggressive approach. The United States and its partners did not arrive at the tribunal with unanimous agreement about the throne. Australia assembled allegations against Hirohito and urged that his responsibility be considered. The resulting debate was an Allied political issue as well as a prospective legal question. It would be historically inaccurate to describe MacArthur's preference as the automatic will of every participating government.

MacArthur's January 25, 1946 telegram to the American army chief of staff is crucial. He reported that investigation had not found specific tangible evidence sufficiently linking the emperor to political decisions and emphasized the likely disorder and administrative burden of prosecution. The telegram is an authoritative primary source for MacArthur's assessment and policy preference, not proof that no incriminating evidence existed or would later emerge. The nature, scope and timing of the investigation must be studied. MacArthur's military government had practical reasons to minimize the risk of destabilization, and his conclusions were written during the period when Allied governments were determining the tribunal's roster.

The prosecutors were also influenced by the settlement. Some Japanese officials gave testimony that concentrated responsibility on military figures, especially former prime minister Tōjō. Defendants' explanations of the emperor's role were prepared in an environment where preserving the throne had become a central occupation objective. Historians have examined how such presentations shaped the record available to prosecutors and judges. It is difficult to reduce these effects to one secret instruction or one orchestrated script, but the interaction of Japanese institutional self-defense and American policy choices is documented. The issue is not only whether evidence about Hirohito existed; it is how evidence was sought, framed and considered.

The Far Eastern Commission's April 1946 handling of war-criminal policy added another layer. Australia continued pressing the question, but the eventual arrangement required special authorization before action against the emperor. Such authorization did not come. The Australian National Archives preserves correspondence showing that Australian prosecutors believed the emperor should not be presumed immune merely because of status and that the matter was contested before the trial opened. These records provide a useful contrast to American accounts emphasizing the operational risks of prosecution. International consensus cannot be inferred simply from the fact that no indictment resulted.

Legal theory and occupation policy therefore diverged. The charter held that official position did not automatically release an accused from responsibility. In practice one of the highest offices in the Japanese state was withheld from the court's examination. This was selective justice, though the term requires precision: political selection occurred before judicial proof, and it does not follow that all charges against those who were prosecuted were unfounded. A tribunal can impose responsibility on defendants selected for trial while failing to investigate another potentially responsible person. Students should distinguish these issues rather than treat the controversy over Hirohito as a simple judgment on the credibility of all evidence.

The decision also shaped Japanese postwar memory. The emperor publicly remained on the throne and became part of the occupation's effort to reorganize the state. A narrative emphasizing militarist advisers and a peace-minded emperor could help legitimize political continuity. Defendants who insisted that their actions had been undertaken within an imperial system faced an acute tension: explaining how policy was made might challenge an institution the occupation was protecting. This tension did not prevent the tribunal from hearing references to the emperor, but it influenced which questions could be pursued through an indictment and tested against an accused head of state.

A historically responsible chapter should conclude neither that Hirohito was found not guilty nor that a court conclusively proved his criminal guilt. Neither happened. The proper conclusion is narrower and more important: a policy decision prevented judicial examination of the emperor's potential individual liability, and historians have continued evaluating his wartime record through sources outside the tribunal. The case exposes the difference between the limits of occupation justice and the continuing responsibilities of historical research. It also prepares students to analyze the actual distribution of blame among Tōjō, Kido, Hirota and other figures who did stand trial.

Section 83.12 · Division III — The Emperor, Selective Accountability, and Trials Beyond Tokyo

Tōjō, Kido, the Military Leadership, and the Allocation of Blame

Tōjō Hideki became the most recognizable defendant at Tokyo partly because he had been prime minister when Japan attacked the United States and British possessions in December 1941. His responsibility was substantial, but his prominence should not be mistaken for personal control over every policy of the Japanese state. He had served as army minister, supported the war effort and participated in high-level decisions. His testimony also placed his actions within Japan's prewar constitutional order and, at times, emphasized loyalty to the imperial command. The trial thus required the judges to evaluate both the identifiable decisions he made and the institutional language through which he explained them.

Kido Kōichi represented a different pathway to power. As an imperial adviser and lord keeper of the privy seal, he was close to the emperor and involved in consultations over governments and policy. His diary became an important documentary source, but its existence did not make interpretation simple. Historians must ask which decisions Kido recorded, which conversations he omitted and whether later use of the diary gave certain entries a significance they did not originally possess. The tribunal assessed his responsibility through the broader charge of aggressive-war participation, not by treating proximity to Hirohito as the equivalent of military command.

Hirota Kōki likewise illustrates the challenge of civilian accountability. He served as foreign minister during crucial years, including the period of mass violence in Nanjing, and had earlier served as prime minister. The tribunal judged him criminally responsible for participation in aspects of aggressive policy and for failure to take sufficiently effective action in relation to reported atrocities. Critics have disputed whether his ministerial authority over the army justified the conclusion about omission, making his conviction a continuing subject of legal debate. The existence of disagreement about Hirota's personal liability should not obscure the independent documentation of the killings and assaults at Nanjing.

Other defendants occupied military or diplomatic offices whose histories were less visible to the public. Doihara Kenji operated in intelligence and continental politics; Itagaki Seishirō and other senior officers were connected with the expansion of Japanese military power. Admirals and army staff chiefs participated in different aspects of policy, while financial and bureaucratic officials enabled military mobilization through administration rather than battlefield command. The tribunal's sentencing distinctions reflected a combination of legal findings and judicial judgments about responsibility. Students should not assume that the severity of sentence perfectly measured every defendant's historical importance or the full suffering connected to his office.

The emperor's exclusion sharpened the distribution of blame. Tōjō could be depicted as the principal organizer of the war without requiring trial of the sovereign in whose name official actions had taken place. That arrangement was politically useful for the occupation, yet it risks encouraging a simplified account in which a handful of militarists somehow seized an otherwise innocent state. The broader system included cooperating bureaucracies, industrial organizations, political parties, public mobilization and complex forms of participation and resistance. A criminal proceeding properly sought specific defendants; a textbook must ensure that the focus on defendants does not erase the structures that made aggressive war possible.

At the same time, analysis should resist the opposite error of spreading blame so widely that individual choices disappear. Senior officials had access to information unavailable to ordinary citizens and could participate in decisions that affected millions. Responsibility for approving an aggressive operation differed from the circumstances of a conscripted soldier or an occupied civilian. Tōjō's authority and documentary record were not identical to Kido's or Hirota's. An evidence-based account specifies office, duration, knowledge, action and omission. Differences matter both for legal fairness and for explaining how an imperial state actually functioned.

The judges' verdicts therefore furnish a structured comparison. Some defendants were convicted of conspiracy and particular aggressive-war counts; others were additionally convicted in relation to conventional war crimes. The nature of the charges could vary, and not every conclusion rested on the same evidentiary path. The November 23, 1948 diplomatic report preserved in the U.S. Foreign Relations series distinguishes the sentencing groups precisely. It should be used alongside the tribunal's own judgment when assessing individual outcomes. A list of names and sentences, without the underlying reasons, is an insufficient substitute for historical analysis.

The most important lesson is that accountability should connect a person with an institution and a demonstrable decision. Courts operating after a war understandably seek manageable cases and public symbols. Historians examine those cases while asking what a courtroom's focus left out. The trials of Tōjō, Kido, Hirota and others made leaders answerable for actions previously wrapped in imperial legitimacy; their selection simultaneously displaced attention from an emperor who remained in office. Understanding both effects reveals the strengths and the limits of judgment after a defeated empire.

Section 83.13 · Division III — The Emperor, Selective Accountability, and Trials Beyond Tokyo

Class B/C Trials in Asia and the Pacific: A Wider Judicial Geography

The Tokyo Tribunal was the best-known proceeding, but it was not the site of most Allied prosecutions of Japanese war crimes. Across the Asia-Pacific region, national military courts convened hearings concerning executions, abuse of prisoners of war, massacres, torture, forced labor and other violations of the laws and customs of war. Yuma Totani's synthesis, drawing on Japanese government data, describes more than 2,240 trials involving roughly 5,700 accused persons at fifty-one locations. Those figures concern a particular counted universe of regional trials and should not simply be added to incompatible totals for Tokyo defendants. Their scale is nevertheless indispensable: postwar justice was a widely dispersed administrative and judicial operation.

The United States conducted proceedings at locations including Manila and Yokohama; British authorities tried suspects in such centers as Singapore and Hong Kong; Australian courts operated in territories connected with Pacific campaigns; Dutch proceedings took place across the former Netherlands East Indies. Chinese and Philippine authorities pursued cases arising from occupation in their territories, while French authorities investigated offenses associated with their former colonial possessions. Each government had its own military court regulations, procedural rules and approaches to evidence and sentencing. The courts were linked by common Allied objectives but not organized as branch offices of the IMTFE.

Geography shaped which victims and defendants appeared. In Manila, evidence about the destructive final campaign and atrocities against civilians became central to the case against General Yamashita. At Yokohama, American proceedings often focused on mistreatment of Allied prisoners. In Singapore or Hong Kong, British investigators drew on the experiences of captives and local populations under Japanese administration. Australian courts considered offenses in New Guinea and other Pacific areas in which military jurisdiction followed wartime deployments. Chinese courts could examine occupation violence involving Chinese communities, while Dutch tribunals prosecuted offenses against internees in the East Indies. No one venue could represent the entire wartime geography.

Trial practice also varied. Some courts used lawyers and extensive review procedures; others operated more quickly under military regulations. Defendants' access to evidence, translation, appeal or review differed, as did the willingness of an occupying authority to distinguish individual conduct from command responsibility. Historians should evaluate proceedings case by case rather than assume that all regional courts were either scrupulously fair or uniformly improvised. Philip Piccigallo's comparative work and Totani's later archival study have illuminated the organization of these prosecutions and the surviving documentary wealth available beyond the Tokyo files.

The Yamashita proceedings show why this wider context is vital. They tested a senior commander's legal responsibility for atrocities committed by forces in a disintegrating military situation. The American court's approach, and the U.S. Supreme Court's response, affected later debates over command responsibility. The case preceded many of Tokyo's final conclusions and involved a different legal tribunal. Treating Yamashita as a 'Tokyo defendant' would confuse institutions and mask how war-crimes doctrines developed across several courts. A student should locate a case in its actual jurisdiction before comparing its legal reasoning with the IMTFE.

These regional courts also brought witnesses into the record whose experiences might otherwise receive less space in a narrative centered on diplomacy and high command. Camp survivors, forced laborers and civilians testified to local conditions and specific acts. Their testimony could allow a tribunal to examine a guard, officer or local administrator more closely than an international trial focused on leaders across several decades. Yet access remained unequal. Victims without institutional support, those in unstable political circumstances and people whose stories did not fit a prosecutor's charge could still remain unheard.

Political changes sometimes complicated jurisdiction. Indonesia's declaration of independence, renewed Dutch efforts to impose colonial authority and Britain's return to Southeast Asia created contested postwar settings. Trials could be conducted by a European power whose own legitimacy was being challenged by local nationalist movements. Chinese civil war shaped the movement of officials, evidence and prisoners. The fact that an Allied court prosecuted Japanese crimes did not mean that all populations accepted the court's government as legitimate. The legal history thus intersects with decolonization as well as the defeat of Japanese imperialism.

Sentencing outcomes require the same caution as defendant counts. Some courts issued death sentences, others imprisonment, and reviewing authorities sometimes commuted or reduced penalties. Records can distinguish charges filed, people tried, people convicted, executions and later releases. These are different measures. A numerical comparison that ignores denominators and national procedures is likely to mislead. The chapter's Historical Data Study should therefore distinguish the twenty-five Tokyo convictions from aggregate B/C estimates, and identify the source and scope of each statistic before drawing conclusions about severity or fairness.

The regional trials fundamentally change the scale of the historical question. Tokyo foregrounded elite decisions about aggressive war; the broader courts investigated the behavior of units and individuals in occupied towns, camp compounds and battle zones. Neither type of court can replace the other as a source. Taken together, they offer a deeper record of wartime violence and a clearer demonstration of postwar law's uneven reach. They also expose the many territories and communities that were part of Japan's imperial history rather than peripheral spaces outside the textbook's central story.

Section 83.14 · Division III — The Emperor, Selective Accountability, and Trials Beyond Tokyo

Survivors, Colonial Subjects, and Unequal Visibility before the Courts

Postwar justice affected people who had not occupied the same position within the Japanese empire. A prisoner of war from Australia, a Chinese civilian who survived occupation, a Korean recruited into imperial service and a Japanese commander could all appear in legal records, but their relation to courts and to power differed sharply. The label 'Japanese war crimes' could describe acts committed by imperial institutions whose personnel included colonial subjects. It could also obscure people who had themselves suffered coercion under Japanese rule. This section examines those distinctions without replacing individual legal inquiry with group stereotypes.

Koreans and Taiwanese served in Japan's military and related labor organizations under conditions shaped by imperial rule, wartime mobilization and unequal citizenship. After surrender, changing nationality and residency arrangements left former colonial personnel in uncertain political positions. Some were treated by Allied courts as members of Japanese forces when charged with wartime offenses, even as postwar Japan could deny them equal recognition and benefits. Others survived forced mobilization or imprisonment and became witnesses or claimants. There was no single colonial experience; legal analysis must distinguish a person's recruitment, duties, conduct and circumstances rather than classify all colonial subjects together.

Lee Hak-rae, a Korean former guard associated with the Burma–Thailand Railway, offers a documented case of those complexities. Recruited as a teenager into a Japanese military system, he later faced Australian war-crimes proceedings concerning mistreatment of Allied prisoners. His case involved an initial dismissal, renewed charges and a death sentence ultimately commuted after legal challenge. Scholarship by Aiko Utsumi and collaborators reconstructs the court chronology and the long aftermath in which former colonial defendants sought recognition and redress. The case does not make guard violence permissible because of colonial subordination; it demonstrates why individual conduct and the coercive institutions within which it occurred must be analyzed together.

The survival of prisoners on the Burma–Thailand Railway created detailed evidence about punishments, disease, forced work and particular guards. Allied prisoners sometimes remembered those who directly supervised them more vividly than the officers designing the system. A court could prosecute the person whom witnesses identified, even when imperial policy and senior command had created the labor regime. This tendency was not unique to colonial defendants, but it could produce especially unequal outcomes when recruited Koreans or Taiwanese had limited authority yet remained accessible for prosecution. Historians need to compare the visibility of a guard with the visibility of the administrators above him.

Chinese civilians, Filipino survivors, Indonesian laborers and other Asian populations also faced unequal access to justice. In many cases their experiences were central to the underlying crimes but entered court through translated testimony, Allied investigators' summaries or limited witness appearances. Geographical disruption, intimidation, poverty and postwar political conflict affected whether survivors could travel, locate documents or obtain legal assistance. These barriers must be recognized without assuming that every testimony was inaccurate. Eyewitness accounts can establish local events with remarkable precision while remaining unable to identify distant planners and cabinet officials.

Women subjected to sexual violence faced additional obstacles. The shame imposed by surrounding societies, threats of retaliation, linguistic barriers and the prosecution's limited categories of interest could prevent testimony. Sexual slavery within the Japanese military system affected women across different occupied territories, but the major Tokyo prosecution did not systematically litigate that system. Particular regional cases did address forced prostitution, including Dutch trials arising from the Semarang affair in the Netherlands East Indies. This distinction is essential: it would be wrong to say that no Allied court ever prosecuted wartime sexual coercion, just as it would be wrong to imply that those limited proceedings provided comprehensive justice.

Survivors' testimony can be read as more than support for a legal verdict. A statement about a prisoner's route, labor assignment, food, punishment or burial may offer information unavailable in formal military orders. It also may be shaped by the conditions in which an investigator asked questions. Courts sometimes required victims to recount traumatic experiences under skeptical examination, and a successful conviction did not necessarily produce restitution or social security. The history of justice therefore includes the experience of giving evidence, the use made of that evidence and the gap between criminal punishment and survivors' broader demands.

The comparative perspective makes collective labels inadequate. A Korean former military guard could be accused in an Allied court while another Korean was seeking recognition as a victim of Japanese forced labor. A Japanese civilian might have opposed the war while a Japanese officer organized brutal occupation practices. A European prisoner could receive a hearing in a colonial military court whose government was simultaneously repressing Indonesian independence. These differences do not erase the primary responsibility of institutions that initiated aggression and coercion. They show why the law's categories and the historian's categories must be connected carefully rather than made interchangeable.

For the textbook's larger historical framework, this section restores a dimension often missing from elite-centered accounts. Japan's empire was socially and ethnically heterogeneous, with different degrees of citizenship, force and privilege. Its legal collapse did not simply convert all those who had served it into equivalent actors. Postwar courts could recognize some injuries and criminalize particular actions while leaving structural inequality largely unexamined. Evaluating these trials requires attention both to the proven acts of accused individuals and to the political conditions that determined whose suffering became visible to the law.

Section 83.15 · Division III — The Emperor, Selective Accountability, and Trials Beyond Tokyo

The Untried, the Underinvestigated, and the Political Limits of Justice

No system of postwar trials could prosecute every wartime offense or everyone implicated in it. Investigative resources were finite, many records had been destroyed, witnesses were dispersed and political priorities changed rapidly after 1945. But some omissions resulted from affirmative decisions not to pursue particular suspects. The difference matters. A court that lacks sufficient evidence acts under one constraint; an occupation government that suppresses or redirects evidence to secure intelligence gains acts under another. The historical assessment of the Tokyo system must include both kinds of limitation while avoiding invented claims about crimes never substantiated.

The most extensively studied example is the Japanese biological-warfare program associated with Unit 731 and Ishii Shirō. Japanese military researchers conducted experiments on human subjects and pursued biological weapons in occupied China. American investigators learned about the program during the occupation. Declassified documents and subsequent research show that U.S. intelligence authorities sought information about weapons and human experimentation and extended protective treatment to certain participants in return for their cooperation. The resulting non-prosecution of leading figures cannot be explained simply as a court finding that the conduct was lawful; the Tokyo Tribunal did not adjudicate their guilt. Intelligence priorities materially narrowed the prospect of prosecution.

The evidence developed over time. Early U.S. inquiries obtained information about the program but initially failed to expose its full human experimentation history. Later reports, including inquiries in 1947, recorded material that investigators considered valuable for biological-warfare research. Historian Tsuneishi Keiichi has traced successive American reports and the decision to keep the evidence in intelligence channels. The sources do not justify claiming that every scientist received identical protection through one formally published immunity order. They do support the conclusion that American policy shielded important participants and that the knowledge gathered through abuses was treated as strategic information rather than as evidence for criminal prosecution.

The Soviet Union conducted a separate trial concerning biological warfare at Khabarovsk in December 1949. Its proceedings and publications had to be evaluated in the context of Soviet political objectives and the early Cold War, but the existence of the trial demonstrates that the subject did not disappear from every postwar jurisdiction. Historians compare Soviet records, American intelligence files and Japanese testimony while distinguishing corroboration from propaganda. The fact that two governments used evidence for different political purposes does not mean the underlying historical program is beyond investigation.

Sexual violence was another field of uneven justice. The widespread military sexual slavery system affecting Asian and other women did not receive comprehensive treatment at the IMTFE. The Dutch military prosecution arising from the forced prostitution of interned women at Semarang showed that particular acts could be prosecuted as war crimes, but that proceeding covered a defined group of victims and accused persons. Fred Borch's study of the Netherlands East Indies courts illustrates both the legal significance of the Semarang case and the narrowness of its reach. The absence of fuller prosecution reflected evidentiary barriers, the identities of recognized victims, legal choices and the limited priorities of postwar investigators.

Selection also affected civilian and economic elites. No Japanese industrialist was charged at the Tokyo Tribunal, as a U.S. National Archives historical report notes. That does not establish that every industrial leader committed a prosecutable crime, but it raises questions about how the prosecution defined the relationship between economic mobilization and aggressive war. Politicians, bureaucrats and corporate organizations participated in resource extraction and wartime production under varying degrees of control. Some of these histories would be addressed through occupation economic reform rather than individual criminal trials. The distinction between institutional restructuring and personal prosecution helped shape what postwar justice could and could not establish.

Another limit concerned Allied conduct. The victorious states judged crimes committed by the defeated power without submitting their own wartime decisions to the Tokyo Tribunal. The bombings of Japanese cities, colonial policies of other empires and Soviet operations were outside the institution's prosecutorial design. Recognizing that asymmetry is part of evaluating legitimacy. It does not change the evidentiary character of a documented massacre or make an accused person's own responsibility dependent on the prosecution of every state that used force. An argument about selective jurisdiction must be kept distinct from a factual argument about the charged act.

The Cold War gradually reshaped occupation priorities, creating additional pressures to stabilize Japanese institutions and treat some former officials as useful in anticommunist policy. These developments were not fully determined at the opening of the Tokyo Trial; the sequence matters. A prosecutor operating in 1946 faced a different international setting from occupation officials assessing strategic security in 1949 or 1950. The chapter therefore identifies omissions arising during the trials and introduces, without narrating the later occupation in full, how new priorities affected the execution and political meaning of justice.

These limitations require precision in historical judgment. It is not sufficient to say that the tribunals were selective, because every prosecution must select cases. Historians need to establish which decisions were made, which evidence was available, who exercised authority and what consequences followed. Unit 731 illustrates a deliberate diversion toward intelligence; the Semarang trial illustrates a meaningful but narrow prosecution of sexual violence; the emperor's exclusion illustrates a policy choice shaping major-war-criminal charges. Taken together they demonstrate that the boundaries of justice were historical choices, not merely neutral consequences of law's inability to encompass a whole war.

Section 83.16 · Division IV — Judgment, Dissent, Punishment, and Contested Memory

The Majority Judgment: Legal Findings, Individual Verdicts, and Historical Narrative

The Tokyo Tribunal began delivering its majority judgment on November 4, 1948 and completed the reading, including verdicts and sentences, on November 12. The proceedings had lasted more than two years and produced an extensive written record. Of twenty-eight original defendants, twenty-five remained for judgment: Matsuoka Yōsuke and Nagano Osami had died, while Ōkawa Shūmei had been found unfit to stand trial. All twenty-five remaining accused were convicted of at least one charge. The numbers should be stated exactly, because shorthand descriptions of twenty-eight convicted leaders erase the difference between indictment, trial and adjudication.

The majority upheld the legal foundations of crimes-against-peace prosecution. It concluded that aggressive war violated international law and that individuals responsible for planning and waging it could be criminally accountable. In responding to defense challenges, the judgment drew heavily on legal reasoning developed at Nuremberg, though the histories of the two theaters differed. The tribunal treated the charter as binding and rejected arguments that official rank or superior orders could automatically provide immunity. These conclusions contributed to the postwar development of international criminal law, but the separate judicial opinions reveal that significant legal objections remained unresolved among the judges themselves.

The judgment also presented a substantial history of Japanese expansion. It organized evidence under themes including military domination of the state, aggression against China, relations with the Soviet Union, the Pacific War and conventional war crimes. This narrative was not simply chronological: it was designed to demonstrate that particular defendants had engaged in unlawful policy and that the charged acts occurred within an intelligible sequence. The resulting text is a major source for historians, but its purpose was to support judgments on specified charges. It is therefore important to compare its conclusions with surviving Japanese documents and later scholarship rather than treat the judgment as the definitive account of every cause of war.

The individual verdicts matter more than an aggregate number. Tōjō, Itagaki, Doihara and others received judgments connected with their political or military roles. Matsui's conviction related centrally to atrocities associated with the Nanjing campaign. Hirota's liability involved questions about diplomatic knowledge and his failure to take adequate action. Defendants with different positions were not all convicted on identical counts, nor did the tribunal formally decide that each had personally committed the same range of crimes. A careful educational summary should give students the tools to distinguish a conviction for participating in aggressive war from a conviction based on responsibility for conventional war crimes.

The majority's account of Nanjing and prisoners of war provided formal findings concerning some of the most grievous documented violations of the conflict. These findings helped preserve evidence against attempts to deny the occurrence of mass violence. Nevertheless, the court's record was selective in its victims, witnesses and legal emphases, as earlier sections explained. A finding against one commander did not constitute a complete institutional accounting of every unit involved. And an omission from the judgment did not imply that a documented abuse never occurred. The relationship between judicial history and broader historical reconstruction is therefore complementary rather than identical.

The legal reasoning concerning conspiracy remained controversial. The prosecution had attempted to connect decisions across the 1930s and 1940s into a common plan; the majority accepted important elements of this framework. Critics argued that the concept risked imposing unity on a state characterized by competing institutions and changing policies. Supporters maintained that institutional disagreement did not negate the responsibility of leaders who repeatedly participated in or authorized aggressive expansion. Assessing the matter requires close reading of how the court described the defendants' actions. Neither the existence of bureaucratic rivalry nor the continuity of expansionist policy alone resolves every individual count.

The judgment also reflected the prosecution's politically defined limits. Hirohito was not before the tribunal, major biological-warfare suspects were not charged, and victims of military sexual slavery were not represented comprehensively in the central case. The majority could not adjudicate the criminal responsibility of persons not indicted, even when the broader wartime history implicated institutions beyond the courtroom. This limitation helps explain both the power and the incompleteness of the verdict. The court established criminal findings against particular leaders while leaving crucial questions about the emperor and other actors to later investigators.

The November 23, 1948 report by U.S. political adviser William Sebald supplies a concise official account of the verdicts and judicial opinions. The Library of Congress preserves components of the full judgment, including its discussion of law, Japanese aggression, atrocities and defendant-specific findings. Students using these sources should compare an executive summary with the longer judicial reasoning. They should also distinguish the tribunal's formal holding from an American official's description of its significance. The difference between the finding and the report about it is itself part of the method of historical inquiry.

Section 83.17 · Division IV — Judgment, Dissent, Punishment, and Contested Memory

The Judges Who Disagreed: Pal, Bernard, Röling, Webb, and Jaranilla

Five of the eleven judges produced opinions distinct from the majority judgment: Radhabinod Pal of India, Henri Bernard of France, B. V. A. Röling of the Netherlands, William Webb of Australia and Delfin Jaranilla of the Philippines. Their arguments did not constitute one unified dissent. They disagreed about jurisdiction, the fairness of the proceedings, the construction of conspiracy, the responsibility of individuals and the adequacy of punishment. The existence of these opinions is significant because it shows that serious legal disagreements were expressed within the tribunal itself, not merely by later commentators opposed to its verdicts.

Pal's opinion was the most sweeping. He concluded that the defendants should be acquitted on the charges before the tribunal, challenging especially the legality of punishing individuals for aggressive war under principles he regarded as insufficiently established at the relevant time. He situated Japanese action within a global history of imperial expansion and criticized unequal standards applied to the defeated power. Later readers have sometimes used Pal as a symbol of opposition to Western imperial dominance. But his legal argument must not be converted into the assertion that documented Japanese atrocities were fictitious. A position about jurisdiction or criminal liability does not erase historical evidence of civilian massacre, torture or forced labor.

Bernard also found fundamental defects in the proceedings, including objections associated with the handling of major responsibility and the absence of the emperor. His approach was not identical to Pal's. Where Pal emphasized broad principles of legality and selective justice, Bernard's criticisms also concerned procedural foundations and the court's ability to produce a sound judgment without addressing the position of the highest authority in the Japanese state. A comparison of their separate opinions thus reveals the importance of distinguishing a call for acquittal from criticism of an institutional process for reasons that might not entail the same result in a differently constituted trial.

Röling agreed with portions of the court's project but questioned aspects of its legal construction and some individual verdicts. His opinion demonstrates that a judge could accept the need for international accountability while rejecting particular doctrines or applications. Webb, the presiding judge, wrote separately on issues including the judgment's implications and the emperor's role, without adopting Pal's wholesale rejection. Jaranilla wrote a concurring opinion emphasizing the gravity of offenses and the view that several sentences were too lenient. Jaranilla's experience of Japanese wartime violence in the Philippines was relevant to how he understood punishment, though his reasoning still demands the same close reading as the others'.

The diversity of the opinions reflects different understandings of international law after catastrophe. Some jurists feared that courts would undermine legality by inventing criminal penalties after the event. Others believed that failure to punish leaders of aggressive war would reward conduct already condemned by treaties and custom. Still others were concerned that a trial organized by victorious powers could not credibly represent international justice unless it addressed all relevant responsibility, including that of the emperor. These arguments belong to an era when the legal institutions for trying international crimes were still being created through practice, not inherited as a settled universal system.

It is tempting to treat the opinions as national positions. Pal was Indian, Webb Australian, Jaranilla Filipino and Röling Dutch, and imperial and wartime histories undeniably informed their perspectives. But each judge also worked within a professional legal tradition and confronted specific evidence. Not every person from a colonized society would have agreed with Pal, and not every survivor of Japanese occupation would have agreed with Jaranilla about punishment. Historical analysis should avoid reducing judges to expressions of national sentiment. Their opinions must be compared on arguments, supporting sources and the remedies they actually proposed.

The debate over 'victors' justice' becomes more useful when disaggregated in this way. There were questions about the choice of defendants, the law applied, whether the bench was sufficiently independent, the quality of evidence and the political use of judgments. A critic might find the emperor's exclusion damaging while still considering many atrocity findings sound. A supporter might endorse criminal liability for aggression while acknowledging inconsistencies in sentencing. Separating these propositions prevents classroom discussion from collapsing into a vote for or against the entire historical record.

The separate opinions also show why verdicts cannot be read simply as the voice of an undifferentiated Allied world. They preserve conflicts internal to the effort to construct postwar law and provide students with unusually accessible examples of legal reasoning as historical evidence. A fruitful exercise compares one paragraph of the majority on aggressive war with one of Pal's objections and then asks how Röling or Bernard reframed the problem. The goal is not to insist that all interpretations are equally persuasive, but to distinguish arguments about the existence of a crime, the scope of a court and the guilt of a particular accused.

Section 83.18 · Division IV — Judgment, Dissent, Punishment, and Contested Memory

Sentences, the Seven Executions, Sugamo, and Clemency

The tribunal pronounced sentences on November 12, 1948. Seven men were condemned to death by hanging: Tōjō Hideki, Doihara Kenji, Itagaki Seishirō, Kimura Heitarō, Matsui Iwane, Mutō Akira and Hirota Kōki. Sixteen defendants received life sentences. Tōgō Shigenori was sentenced to twenty years, and Shigemitsu Mamoru to seven years. These figures account for the twenty-five defendants convicted; two of the original twenty-eight had died during proceedings and one was unfit to stand trial. The precise distribution is preserved in contemporary American diplomatic correspondence and in the tribunal's sentencing record.

Sentence was not a simple mathematical reflection of a defendant's rank or the number of historical deaths associated with his office. Judges weighed legal findings, degrees of culpability and the perceived seriousness of conduct, sometimes without fully transparent agreement about appropriate punishment. Hirota's death sentence, in particular, raised questions among later observers about liability based on civilian office and failure to act effectively. The separate opinions show that judges disagreed about severity. A historical textbook should neither assume that every death sentence was inevitable nor imply that disagreement necessarily nullified the findings of guilt.

MacArthur's occupation command retained a role in the execution of judgments. Before the sentences were implemented, the Allied decision-making process considered the tribunal's outcomes and petitions. The government of occupied Japan did not possess ordinary independent power to commute these sentences. The seven condemned men were executed at Sugamo Prison on December 23, 1948. That date should not be confused with the November reading of the judgment. The sequence from verdict to review and punishment reveals the interaction between a multinational judicial body and the military authority that had established it.

Sugamo was both a prison and a symbol. Japanese wartime suspects were detained there during proceedings, and later public memory associated the site with the executed leaders. But the prisoners' experience differed according to legal status, sentence and time. Some were awaiting investigation, some had been convicted at Tokyo and others were involved in regional proceedings. The prison was embedded in an occupation city that also faced food scarcity, displaced populations and political transformation. Its history belongs to the ordinary physical administration of incarceration as well as to public controversy over the war.

Imprisonment continued after the executions. Defendants sentenced to life or fixed terms entered changing legal and diplomatic circumstances as the occupation progressed and the peace settlement approached. Some died in custody, while others were subsequently released through parole, commutation or other procedures authorized under the relevant arrangements. Shigemitsu, who had served as foreign minister during the surrender period and was sentenced to seven years, later returned to prominent political life. This trajectory illustrates that wartime prosecution did not permanently exclude every convicted official from all postwar influence, although particular circumstances and legal consequences varied.

The 1951 San Francisco Peace Treaty addressed the issue in Article 11. Japan accepted the judgments of the Tokyo Tribunal and other Allied war-crimes courts and undertook to carry out the sentences of imprisoned Japanese nationals. Procedures for clemency, reduction and parole required decisions by the relevant sentencing governments, with an additional specified mechanism for Tokyo convictions. The treaty thus tied the implementation of punishment to international obligations at the moment Japan recovered sovereignty. It should not be paraphrased as a demand that historical research cease questioning the legality or fairness of the proceedings.

The relation between punishment and reconciliation was contested. Some survivors and former prisoners felt that many perpetrators had escaped justice, that sentences were too light or that later releases weakened recognition of suffering. Others believed that prolonged imprisonment after political change impeded a return to normal relations. Japanese public responses were not uniform, and the short passage from defeat to reconstruction changed the salience of legal punishment for different constituencies. The historian's role is to establish those distinct perspectives rather than present a single national reaction of either remorse or resentment.

The fact of seven executions does not close the chapter's question about responsibility. The emperor remained in office, Unit 731 prosecutions were largely averted in the American-controlled system, and many victims received no direct restitution through criminal courts. Yet the sentences also marked a genuine transformation: once-powerful leaders were held before an international tribunal and punished as individuals for conduct connected with state aggression and atrocities. The longer story of clemency and political memory demonstrates how judicial outcomes acquired new meanings as the occupation ended and Japan's postwar institutions developed.

Section 83.19 · Division IV — Judgment, Dissent, Punishment, and Contested Memory

Japanese and Asian Responses, Peace-Treaty Acceptance, and the Politics of Memory

The Tokyo proceedings did not have one meaning for everyone who followed them. Some Japanese readers regarded the defeat and prosecution of former leaders as necessary consequences of the war; some felt uneasy about legality and foreign domination; others were preoccupied with food, housing and survival rather than a lengthy courtroom process. Newspapers reported the trial, but SCAP censorship and editorial selection shaped what appeared. Public interest also shifted over time. Historian Madoka Futamura has shown that contemporary coverage and later attention declined markedly after the verdict, complicating the idea that the trial remained continuously central to everyday Japanese political life.

The public setting of the trial was itself part of occupation governance. Japanese political parties and publishers operated under regulations administered by SCAP, as Chapter 82 explained. Discussion of war guilt occurred alongside reconstruction, new elections and constitutional change. The fact that courts were trying leaders of aggression did not automatically mean that people had access to a full range of evidence about the emperor, biological warfare or the experiences of women subjected to military sexual slavery. In some cases the official narrative of a protected emperor and criminal militarists became easier to circulate than a broader inquiry into institutional responsibility.

Asian perspectives were equally varied. For Chinese survivors of Japanese occupation, a judgment about aggression and Nanjing could validate injuries too often dismissed in diplomacy. Filipino survivors of the war's destructive final campaign had reasons to focus on regional atrocities and the trials held in Manila. Korean former colonial subjects faced a different transition: liberation from Japanese rule did not guarantee a stable political home or equal treatment under postwar nationality systems. Indonesian and Vietnamese nationalists contested European attempts to restore colonial government while war-crimes courts operated under Allied authority. These histories make it misleading to speak of a unified Asian response to Tokyo.

The legal and historical meaning of the emperor's non-prosecution was also debated differently across borders. Some Allied officials treated his retention as a practical necessity for securing compliance; critics in Japan and abroad saw it as compromising the principle that no official position should excuse criminal conduct. The decision influenced how the war was narrated in schools, public writing and political discourse. It did not dictate a uniform memory, but it created an asymmetry between official accountability of ministers and the continuing symbolic legitimacy of the throne. The difference between wartime responsibility and postwar constitutional status became central to later discussions.

The San Francisco Peace Treaty, signed September 8, 1951 and effective in April 1952, incorporated Article 11's acceptance of the judgments and obligation to carry out sentences. This was an international legal commitment by Japan. It did not convert every legal opinion written by the tribunal into an uncontestable conclusion for historians, nor did it extinguish the evidentiary work of survivors. Japan's own Ministry of Foreign Affairs later acknowledged that arguments about the judgment exist while maintaining that the state accepted it under the treaty. Students should distinguish such a government's diplomatic position from the separate activities of historical research and legal scholarship.

After the occupation, some public discussion emphasized the suffering of ordinary Japanese during bombardment and deprivation, while other movements investigated the suffering imposed on Asian neighbors. Commemoration, museum exhibitions, school curricula, lawsuits and publication of new archives gradually altered the public record. The political meaning of the dead convicted leaders also changed over time, including controversy connected with commemoration at Yasukuni Shrine. Because this textbook's later chapters address memory and contemporary Japan, Section 83.19 should introduce such developments only to show the afterlife of judicial choices, not become a comprehensive post-1952 history.

The trial's legal legacy is similarly mixed. It helped develop the idea of individual accountability for aggressive war, yet its selective jurisdiction, evidentiary rules and treatment of high officials generated lasting objections. The regional B/C courts contributed to law concerning command responsibility and the prosecution of violence against prisoners and civilians. New international criminal institutions developed many decades later under different treaties and safeguards. It would be teleological to portray Tokyo as a flawless first step inevitably leading to modern courts; it is more accurate to explain the proceedings as a contested episode whose ideas were adapted, criticized and sometimes rejected.

For victims, memory extended beyond the verdict. A survivor could view a conviction as recognition of a crime while still lacking restitution, secure residence or public acknowledgement. A former colonial subject convicted as a member of imperial forces could later contest the terms on which responsibility had been assigned. The descendant of a Japanese soldier or detainee might remember the suffering of defeat without knowing the experiences of communities harmed by Japan's conquest. Historical education should place these memories into dialogue without demanding that different forms of suffering be treated as identical or interchangeable.

The largest lesson is that trials produce both decisions and archives. They punish particular persons, generate official narratives and leave documentary records that later generations reinterpret. The Tokyo judgment, separate opinions, regional case files and policy correspondence concerning omitted prosecutions have all become sources for historians. Their value lies partly in the contradictions between them. The legacy of 1945–1952 is therefore not a final verdict on all Japanese history but an enduring obligation to examine how law, political power and the unequal visibility of victims shape what societies remember about the violence of empire.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From imperial service to postwar accountability and memory

What changed? Senior officials of a defeated empire could be tried as individuals under international and national military law; evidence of violence entered public legal records; the peace treaty imposed continuing obligations concerning sentences.

What continued? The emperor's institution survived; bureaucratic and social hierarchies shaped the reception of trials; many victims remained without full recognition or material redress; national political interests continued influencing remembrance.

Whose experiences differed? Convicted officials, former Allied prisoners, Chinese survivors, Korean and Taiwanese former colonial subjects, Japanese civilians and women subjected to sexual violence occupied different positions. Legal judgment was not the end of historical memory.

Historical significance: Comparing 1945, 1948 and 1952 shows that punishment, restored sovereignty and public remembrance unfolded on distinct schedules.

Section 83.20 · Division V — Historical Synthesis and Review

Justice after Defeat: War Responsibility, Judicial Accountability, and Unfinished Memory

The Allied trials after 1945 brought an unprecedented scale of criminal scrutiny to the leaders and servants of imperial Japan. The Tokyo Tribunal addressed the planning and waging of aggressive war alongside evidence of conventional war crimes, while national military courts across Asia and the Pacific investigated atrocities against prisoners, civilians and other vulnerable populations. Together the proceedings made important records and imposed individual punishment. They also exposed how difficult it is to translate the historical reality of an expansive war into legally bounded charges against identifiable persons. A criminal court must decide specific cases; historians must also explain institutions, policies and forms of harm that never entered an indictment.

The Tokyo case reveals that a government's responsibilities cannot be understood from its constitutional chart alone. The army, navy, cabinet, imperial court and ministries formed a system in which military initiative, civilian acquiescence, imperial legitimation and competing bureaucratic interests interacted. Prosecutors attempted to connect these structures through the crime of aggressive-war conspiracy, while defense lawyers challenged both the legal concept and their clients' participation. The court's majority accepted significant elements of the prosecution's argument, but its individual verdicts and the separate judicial opinions demonstrate that law did not supply one uncontested interpretation of the past.

Hirohito's non-prosecution is the chapter's most revealing political limit. The emperor was retained because occupation leaders considered him useful in securing Japanese compliance and institutional stability. The policy choice occurred before any criminal trial of his conduct and cannot be converted into an acquittal. Historians have continued to examine the emperor's wartime decisions with materials beyond the IMTFE, while questions of personal criminal liability remain distinct from judgments about the political institution's legitimating role. The decision to exclude one major figure illustrates how the framework of a court can determine which responsibilities it may never test.

The wider trials complicate the familiar story still further. A national military court examining abuse at a prisoner-of-war camp faced evidence and legal questions different from the Tokyo prosecution's sprawling diplomatic history. A colonial subject such as Lee Hak-rae could be judged for particular conduct within an imperial service he had entered under unequal conditions. Dutch proceedings concerning forced prostitution could recognize specific women's injuries without providing comprehensive justice for all victims of military sexual slavery. The non-prosecution of important Unit 731 personnel shows that intelligence priorities could directly narrow accountability. These cases require careful differentiation, not the conclusion that every prosecution was a success or every omission an identical injustice.

The judges themselves disagreed about how international law should respond to aggressive war. The majority considered individual prosecution legally justified; Pal questioned its foundation and called for acquittal, while Bernard, Röling, Webb and Jaranilla offered differing separate views. Their disagreements were not merely national sentiments. They concerned whether law had been sufficiently established before the acts, what an international criminal court could properly decide, which evidence was trustworthy and what punishment was warranted. These debates helped shape the legal questions later generations would confront, but they did not make historical evidence of atrocities disappear.

The final lesson is an evidentiary one. Students should distinguish an atrocity supported by multiple records from a particular accused person's legal responsibility; a prosecutor's allegation from a judicial finding; an occupation policy from an independent historical judgment; and a peace treaty's requirement to implement sentences from any prohibition on later scholarship. The Tokyo proceedings made formerly powerful leaders answerable to law, but their operation reflected the unequal power of the Allied victors, selective indictment and the politics of occupied Japan. International justice was real, incomplete and contested at the same time.

Chapter 84 will examine the 1947 Constitution, Article 9, women's suffrage and the democratic reforms through which occupied Japan reorganized its institutions. The two chapters are connected without being interchangeable. Chapter 83 shows how the old order was judged and selectively preserved; Chapter 84 asks how the new order was written and contested. The question that should remain with students is not whether a single verdict can close the history of an empire. It is how an informed society can acknowledge credible evidence, insist on individual fairness and continue investigating responsibility even where a court or a government chose not to act.

Chapter Summary

The Tokyo War Crimes Trial was part of a broader Allied effort to make individuals answerable for aggressive war and violations of the laws of war after Japan surrendered. Its public importance rested on the prosecution of national leaders, but the widest numerical effort occurred in separate national courts spread across Asia and the Pacific. The two systems used different procedures and did not prosecute interchangeable classes of persons.

The IMTFE was established by MacArthur in January 1946 and organized under a charter amended in April. Crimes against peace, conventional war crimes and crimes against humanity were distinct legal categories. The charge of aggressive war provoked especially difficult questions about whether international law before 1945 had adequately established individual penal liability. The majority answered affirmatively; several judges disagreed about law and application.

The twenty-eight original Tokyo defendants were not all convicted. Two died, one was declared unfit, and twenty-five received judgments. The 55-count indictment sought to connect Japanese expansion across the 1930s and 1940s to a common plan. Proving individual participation was more difficult than establishing that a pattern of aggression had occurred. The tribunal assembled important documents without producing a comprehensive history of every aspect of empire.

The courtroom operated through translators, interpreters, witnesses, clerks, prosecutors and defense teams. Its abundant record was shaped by language choices, evidence rules, access to documents and the questions the parties asked. Contemporary evidence concerning Nanjing, prisoner abuse and military command was indispensable, yet individual guilt required demonstration of duties, knowledge, orders or participation—not merely association with a military service or ministry.

Hirohito was not indicted. MacArthur and other American officials believed preserving the emperor would assist occupation governance, while Australian authorities contested his exclusion. The absence of prosecution was a policy decision rather than a judicial acquittal. Historians continue to investigate the emperor’s wartime role and distinguish personal criminal liability from political and institutional responsibility.

Tōjō, Kido, Hirota, Matsui and other accused followed different paths through the wartime state. Their verdicts and sentences differed with the evidence and legal findings. That differentiation matters because an imperial system did not operate through one individual’s orders alone, but neither did institutional complexity remove the agency of officials able to influence policy or respond to reported crimes.

The regional Class B/C proceedings brought camp administrators, officers and guards before U.S., British, Australian, Chinese, Dutch, Philippine and other courts. Their case files preserve experiences often less visible at Tokyo. They also show unequal access to justice among populations under Japanese rule, including Koreans and Taiwanese who had served in imperial structures and later faced changing nationality arrangements.

Important crimes and persons escaped meaningful prosecution. U.S. intelligence priorities protected significant figures connected with Unit 731. Dutch courts prosecuted forced prostitution in the Semarang affair, but that limited case did not constitute comprehensive accountability for military sexual slavery. The failures cannot all be explained by the same cause: policy choices, evidentiary barriers and judicial jurisdiction had to be investigated separately.

In November 1948 the court convicted all twenty-five remaining defendants; seven were executed on December 23. Five judges wrote separate opinions, revealing major disagreement about retroactive criminal law, the court’s procedure, individual liability and sentencing. These documents prevent the historical interpretation of the tribunal from being reduced to a single united Allied view.

The San Francisco Peace Treaty required Japan to accept the Allied war-crimes judgments and implement sentences under specified arrangements. That diplomatic obligation did not close historical research or survivors’ claims for recognition. The trials produced punishment, documentary evidence and lasting controversies—an example of how law, occupation power and historical memory interact without completely coinciding.

Select one documented proceeding: the Tokyo judgment concerning a named defendant, Yamashita’s U.S. commission, the Lee Hak-rae case, or the Dutch Semarang prosecution. Produce a 5–7 page archival case study that distinguishes charges, evidence, findings, omissions and later interpretations. Do not invent proceedings or quote a source that cannot be inspected.

  1. Identify the court, date, venue, jurisdiction and applicable charter or military regulation.
  2. Locate at least one primary record or judicial document and one independent scholarly study using the linked source register.
  3. Construct an evidence table separating contemporary documents, testimony, later recollections and judicial findings.
  4. Identify the accused person’s claimed or established authority, knowledge, actions and any duty to prevent or punish violations.
  5. Explain what the court could not determine and whose testimony is missing; do not assume the absence of a claim proves innocence.
  6. Present a map locating the charged event and trial venue, cite sources, and include a paragraph explaining how the case changes the history of postwar justice.

Suggested starting points: IMTFE Charter and Judgment (Sources 1–2), U.S. Supreme Court Yamashita (8), Lee Hak-rae (20), Semarang and Dutch prosecution scholarship (21, 33), regional archives (7, 13, 32).

Chapter Reflection

Chapter 83 matters because responsibility for a defeated state does not fit within a single verdict. The Tokyo Tribunal helped transform international norms condemning aggression into individual criminal judgments and preserved extraordinary documentary evidence. The regional courts recorded violations against prisoners and civilians across a wider geographic landscape. These achievements should be studied in their own terms, with attention to the persons convicted and the evidence accepted, rather than reduced to ceremonial punishment after a military victory.

The chapter also demonstrates that the boundaries of justice are formed before verdicts are delivered. Decisions about prosecutors, accused persons and legal categories created the story each court could tell. The exclusion of Hirohito, selective treatment of Japanese biological warfare and the limited prosecution of sexual violence remained consequential even when judges produced careful decisions on the cases before them. Omissions differ in cause and must be established through documents rather than inferred from slogans.

Colonial history complicates responsibility further. The Japanese empire mobilized Koreans, Taiwanese and others under unequal conditions and caused immense suffering among populations throughout Asia. Postwar courts sometimes recognized those experiences and sometimes reproduced inequalities in who could testify, obtain assistance or bear criminal charges. A historically responsible account can distinguish proven individual actions from the state systems that compelled, authorized or concealed them. Doing so neither minimizes atrocities nor imposes collective guilt.

The legacy of the trials was legal, political and social. Article 11 of the peace treaty preserved implementation of sentences even as Japan recovered sovereignty, but historical research and survivors’ memory continued beyond the occupation. Chapters 84–90 will examine the reconstruction of political and everyday life that took place around these controversies. The question left by Chapter 83 is how a society should combine respect for reliable evidence, fair judgment of individuals and continued scrutiny of the powerful institutions that courts did not fully investigate.
