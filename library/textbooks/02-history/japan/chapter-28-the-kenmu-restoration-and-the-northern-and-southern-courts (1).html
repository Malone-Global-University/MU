Part V — Ashikaga Japan and the Maritime World

The Kenmu Restoration and the Northern and Southern Courts

Japanese History: From Jōmon Japan to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

In the summer of 1333 the Kamakura shogunate, which had governed the warrior order of eastern and western Japan for a century and a half, ceased to exist. Its Kyoto headquarters at Rokuhara fell, and its eastern capital was overrun and burned. Emperor Go-Daigo returned to Kyoto as the victor. But the men who had brought the bakufu down did not agree about what should replace it.

This chapter begins where Chapter 27 ended. That chapter followed the Mongol invasions of 1274 and 1281, the fiscal and social strains they left behind, the Genkō war of 1331–1333, and the collapse of Hōjō rule. It deliberately left one question open: after the destruction of the bakufu, what would govern the realm? Chapter 28 answers it in stages, and none of the stages was foreordained. First came an attempt by Go-Daigo to govern directly, commonly called the Kenmu Restoration (Kenmu no shinsei, or “Kenmu Chūkō”). Then came its breakdown, the rise of a new warrior leader, Ashikaga Takauji, and the division of the imperial court into two rival courts. Finally came about six decades of war and negotiation, ending with reunification in 1392.

The central problem is why victory over Kamakura did not produce a durable court-centered government. An older story is easy to tell and easy to remember: Go-Daigo foolishly ignored the samurai, Takauji rebelled, and the Ashikaga naturally replaced the Hōjō. This chapter argues for a more structural explanation. The coalition of 1333 was held together mainly by opposition to the Hōjō. Once the Hōjō were gone, the emperor, court aristocrats, great temples and shrines, established Kamakura retainers (gokenin), regional warriors, and powerful military houses wanted different things. Go-Daigo tried to reassert imperial authority in a society transformed by nearly a century and a half of institutionalized warrior rule. Warrior landholding, military service, and the expectation of reward for service could not simply be reversed.

The chapter also insists that “the Northern Court and the Southern Court” describes a shifting political reality. It was never a tidy map with two centralized armies. Rival emperors existed, and each side claimed to be the true imperial court. But most fighting was carried out by regional coalitions, families divided against themselves, and warriors who changed sides when land, office, safety, or kinship made it prudent. The Ashikaga camp itself split in the Kannō Disturbance (1350–1352), when Takauji and his brother Tadayoshi fought one another, and at points the enemies of the Kyoto regime found an ally within its own leading family.

The chapter draws attention to regions and populations beyond Kyoto. The east, especially Kamakura and the Kantō, remained essential to any claim to rule warriors. Tōhoku, Kyushu, and Shikoku each had their own military coalitions. Northern Honshu was tied to the Ezo world and to Ainu commerce across the northern seas. Kyushu became entangled with the diplomacy of the new Ming dynasty and with the maritime raiding known as wakō. Farmers, temple communities, and townspeople were not bystanders. They paid dues to competing claimants and supplied labor and provisions. They also suffered the consequences of warfare that lasted, in various forms, for two generations.

The evidence is unusually rich but unusually partisan. It includes thousands of surviving reward petitions, military service reports, land confirmations, and appointment orders. It also includes war tales such as the Taiheiki and the pro-Ashikaga Baishōron, and a work of political thought, Kitabatake Chikafusa's Jinnō Shōtōki. Documents show warriors bargaining over land, office, kinship, and survival. Narratives translate those choices into loyalty, treachery, courage, and fate. Later centuries then rewrote the whole conflict again, and especially the figure of Kusunoki Masashige, according to their own political needs. Students will learn to keep those layers of evidence separate.

The chapter's governing idea can be stated briefly: 1333 did not settle the question of who should rule Japan. It reopened it. Chapter 29 will step back from the narrative of war to examine what the Ashikaga political order actually was. This chapter explains the breakdown from which that order emerged.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why the anti-Hōjō coalition of 1333 could not agree on a successor to the Kamakura bakufu.
  2. Analyze what “restoration” meant in Go-Daigo's Kenmu government and why it did not mean a literal return to earlier imperial institutions.
  3. Assess the administrative problem of land confirmation, reward, and competing claims among warriors, aristocrats, and religious institutions.
  4. Trace the sequence from the Nakasendai Rebellion of 1335 to Ashikaga Takauji's break with Go-Daigo and the struggle for Kyoto.
  5. Explain how Takauji recovered from defeat in 1336 and why regional alliances and legitimacy mattered as much as holding the capital.
  6. Distinguish the contemporary evidence for Kusunoki Masashige from the later heroic tradition surrounding him.
  7. Describe the emergence of the Northern and Southern Courts and explain the competing claims to legitimacy, including regalia, lineage, and era names.
  8. Analyze the Kannō Disturbance as evidence that the war was not a simple two-sided conflict.
  9. Evaluate why the Southern Court survived for decades and how reunification was accomplished in 1392.
  10. Compare documentary sources, war tales, and political treatises as different kinds of evidence for the same events.
  11. Contextualize the Nanboku-chō period within regional history, including Kyushu, the Kantō, Tōhoku, Ezo, and connections to Ming China and Korea.
  12. Connect the outcomes of the civil wars to the institutional questions explored in Chapter 29.

  2. What could “restoration” mean in a society where warrior institutions had governed for generations, and whose expectations did it satisfy or disappoint?

Historical Context

Chapters 24 through 27 established the setting. After 1185 a warrior government at Kamakura coexisted with the imperial court in Kyoto. The bakufu appointed provincial constables (shugo) and land stewards (jitō) and bound warrior households as retainers (gokenin) in a relationship of service and reward. After 1221 the court could no longer contest warrior supremacy in matters of military order, and the Hōjō regents governed in the name of shoguns who were themselves figureheads. The court kept great prestige and vast landholdings through the estate system (shōen), and it continued to control rank, ritual, and imperial legitimacy. Great temples and shrines were also formidable landholders and, at times, armed powers. Authority in Japan was therefore layered rather than unified.

The Mongol invasions of 1274 and 1281 strained this arrangement. Defense was costly, and unlike conquest it produced no large new pool of enemy land for redistribution. The bakufu distributed rewards to recognized claimants, but compensation was constrained and uneven relative to the extraordinary burdens of military service and prolonged defense. Meanwhile, divided inheritance had fragmented many warrior estates, and debt and hardship affected numerous households. The Hōjō, especially the head of the main line (tokusō) and his advisers, concentrated power in ways that alienated many warriors, including some within the bakufu's own network. The imperial house was divided too. Since the thirteenth century, two lines descended from Emperor Go-Saga, the Jimyōin and the Daikakuji, had competed for the throne, and Kamakura had acted as arbiter of their claims. Go-Daigo belonged to the Daikakuji line.

Go-Daigo ascended the throne in 1318 and, from 1321, ruled without a retired emperor (insei) supervising him. He came into conflict with Kamakura over succession. The 1324 Shōchū Incident implicated members of Go-Daigo's circle, although his own responsibility remains uncertain. In 1331 he mounted a much clearer challenge to Kamakura, was captured, and was exiled to Oki, and Kamakura installed Kōgon of the Jimyōin line. But regional warriors continued to fight. Go-Daigo escaped from Oki in 1333. Ashikaga Takauji, sent by the Hōjō to suppress the uprising, changed sides and seized Rokuhara, while Nitta Yoshisada, a Kantō warrior, attacked Kamakura. Those events, covered in Chapter 27, are the starting point here.

Students should keep in mind three background conditions. First, warrior society by 1333 was not a marginal force that a restored emperor could easily discipline; it was the ordinary means by which many landholders defended their holdings and sought justice. Second, imperial claims were still spiritually and politically potent. Go-Daigo could issue commands, confer rank and office, and legitimize titles, and many warriors wanted such legitimation. Third, the anti-Hōjō alliance included men who had fought for entirely different reasons, from loyalty to Go-Daigo to fear of the Hōjō, personal grievance, or opportunism.

Section 28.1

Victory without a Settlement

The collapse of Kamakura in 1333 was rapid. Ashikaga Takauji's seizure of Rokuhara, the bakufu's headquarters in Kyoto, removed the regime's supervision of the capital region and the western provinces. Nitta Yoshisada's assault on Kamakura, in the same season, destroyed the eastern center. Hōjō Takatoki and many of his followers died at the Tōshōji temple complex. Go-Daigo returned to Kyoto in the summer, treated Kōgon's enthronement as invalid, and began to reorganize the government.

Victory left the winners with a problem they had not needed to solve while fighting: who now held authority over warriors? For a century and a half, bakufu institutions had confirmed inheritances, adjudicated disputes, distributed rewards, and mobilized armies. No court institution had performed those tasks at that scale. With the bakufu gone, warriors everywhere turned to the emperor and to the men who had led the victory. The coalition of 1333 had been assembled around a negative aim, the destruction of the Hōjō, and it included Go-Daigo's loyalists, dissatisfied Kamakura retainers, regional families with local ambitions, armed temple communities, and commanders such as Takauji and Yoshisada who were each of Minamoto descent and each possessed of a large following.

Students should note what the situation was not. It was not a vacuum in which a single claimant took power by default. It was a crowded field. Each group had contributed something to the victory, and each expected the new order to protect what it held and to compensate what it had risked.

Section 28.2

What Did “Restoration” Mean?

The term Kenmu no shinsei, “the new government of Kenmu,” is more accurate than the older phrase “Kenmu Restoration,” because Go-Daigo's program was innovative as well as retrospective. The name Kenmu, adopted as the era name in 1334, evoked a renewed order. Go-Daigo unusually chose his own posthumous name during his lifetime, deliberately linking himself to Emperor Daigo, whose early tenth-century reign was remembered at court as an age of direct imperial rule. Go-Daigo did abolish the offices of regent and chancellor (sesshō and kanpaku) that had governed for the aristocratic Fujiwara, and he did not want a retired emperor supervising the throne. He placed the emperor at the center of the administration.

But he did not, and could not, recreate the eighth-century ritsuryō state described in Chapters 13 and 14. That system had rested on a census, a tax register, and a bureaucracy that had long since been replaced by estates, private jurisdictions, and warrior service. Go-Daigo worked with what existed: aristocratic offices, medieval estate law, the memory of ancient precedent, the prestige of the throne, and the practical fact that his allies were warriors. He issued directives (rinji) in his own name, and he tried to make imperial confirmation the source of legitimate landholding. This was a novel claim in the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries. In effect, it asked landholders to treat a court document as the foundation of their rights, in a society accustomed to bakufu confirmation.

Some later historians have stressed Go-Daigo's unconventional side. He was deeply involved in esoteric Buddhist practice, and he cultivated connections with groups outside the ordinary estate structure. Amino Yoshihiko, for example, argued that his regime drew on itinerant artisans, merchants, and religious specialists whose activities did not fit neatly within agrarian landholding. Others regard this as suggestive but overstated. What is agreed is that “restoration” was a program of redefinition and that Go-Daigo was not simply a conservative.

Historical Vocabulary
Rinji (綸旨)

A rinji was a written directive issued in the emperor's name, drafted by court secretaries. Such directives had long been used to convey imperial decisions, including grants and confirmations. Under Go-Daigo they became the principal instrument through which the new regime tried to convert its authority into practical governance, and for a period the court asserted that land titles required confirmation by rinji.

Students should not equate a rinji with a modern statute or a bakufu judgment. It was an act of a particular ruler, carrying legitimacy but depending on the ability of the court and its allies to enforce it. Its overuse contributed to the sense of confusion: contemporary criticism complained about the number of orders, the delays in issuing them, and the circulation of forged directives.

Section 28.3

Go-Daigo's Government after 1333

The new government created or revived several bodies. A records office (kirokujo) handled documents and land-related decisions; a court for various claims (zasso-ketsudansho) heard disputes; an office for rewards (onshō-kata) processed claims for compensation; and a warriors' office (musha-dokoro) dealt with military affairs. Officials included court aristocrats, some warriors, and men with technical skill in law and administration, some of whom had served Kamakura. Go-Daigo appointed provincial governors (kokushi) alongside constables (shugo). This dual system tried to give the court a channel of authority in the provinces while preserving warrior military organization, and it created overlapping jurisdictions.

Regional offices reflected a further attempt to hold the country together. Go-Daigo's young son Prince Norinaga (later Emperor Go-Murakami), accompanied by Kitabatake Akiie, was sent to Mutsu, and another son, Prince Narinaga, was sent to Kamakura with Ashikaga Tadayoshi as his chief administrator. In the east this arrangement gave the court a nominal presence while leaving practical control to warriors. It shows the design behind the Kenmu government: imperial princes as symbolic heads of regional administrations, with warrior leaders doing the actual work.

Contemporaries did not think well of the results. In the autumn of 1334, an anonymous satirical poster appeared on the Nijō riverbank in Kyoto, now called the Nijō-gawara rakusho. Its author or authors criticized, among other things, the confusion of appointments and land decisions, the circulation of false directives, the rise in rank of people considered unworthy, and the disorder of the capital. A satire is not a neutral report, and its complaints reflect the views of people who felt disadvantaged. As evidence, it demonstrates that the regime's competence was widely questioned within a year of its founding. Go-Daigo also proposed an expensive new palace, financed by new levies, which added to the perception that the court was demanding resources while failing to deliver order.

Section 28.4

The Problem of Land and Reward

The central administrative problem of the Kenmu government was the volume and complexity of land claims. Kamakura had confiscated the holdings of its enemies and redistributed them. Hōjō relatives and allies held vast properties, and Hōjō stewards and deputies had placed their clients in offices across the realm. With the regime's destruction, those lands were suddenly available, but their ownership was tangled. Some had been held under bakufu grants, some under court or temple proprietors' rights, and some under conflicting claims that dated back generations. Warriors who had fought for Go-Daigo submitted reports of their service (gunchūjō), and expected recognition. Court nobles who had lost rights to warriors wanted them back. Temples and shrines pressed their own claims.

The Kenmu government could not process this volume at the pace required. It was also reluctant to reward warriors at the expense of the great religious and aristocratic proprietors whose backing it needed. Its own edict of 1333 requiring imperial confirmation for land titles put every holder, in principle, in the position of a petitioner, and the government reportedly modified the policy within months in response to the disruption. A warrior who traveled to Kyoto for a decision might wait a long time, and what he received might later be reversed or contradicted by another directive. Uncertainty about tenure was itself a political grievance.

Reward was not only material. Rank and office also conveyed status. Go-Daigo bestowed titles generously on some, and this generated resentment among those who thought their service went unrecognized. Historians who emphasize this problem do not say that the government was uniquely negligent. Rather, they emphasize that any regime emerging from the destruction of the Hōjō would have faced a flood of competing claims, and that the Kenmu government had fewer institutional resources for adjudication than Kamakura had possessed at its height.

Primary Source Window

Rewards, Confirmations, and Appointment Orders of the Kenmu Period

Creator: Warrior claimants, Kenmu government offices, imperial secretaries, and later Ashikaga headquarters · Date: 1333–1336 and after · Type: Archival documents (service reports, petitions, confirmations, directives)

Context: Thousands of medieval documents survive in family archives, temple collections, and shrine holdings. Warriors preserved them because they were proof of rights. The ones relevant here include service reports (gunchūjō) in which a warrior describes his participation in a campaign, often with names of witnesses or commanders who could attest to it; confirmations issued in the name of the emperor or a regional authority; and appointment orders. Because families kept these documents for centuries, the survival rate is uneven. Winners' families and institutions with good archives are better represented than those that fell.

Typical content (a description of the genre, not a quotation of a specific document): A warrior reports where he fought and under whose command, notes any wounds, casualties among his followers, or the loss of relatives, and asks that his service be recorded so that his holdings will be confirmed or a new grant made. An issuing office responds by confirming, altering, or deferring the request, sometimes on condition of further investigation.

Analysis: These documents show the practical mechanics of rule. Loyalty in them is expressed through service and reward. They show the same men appearing under different commanders in different months as circumstances changed. They also reveal the conflict of authority: a claimant might hold a directive from Go-Daigo's court, an order from Ashikaga Tadayoshi in Kamakura, and a later confirmation from Takauji, each pointing to a different source of legitimacy. Their limitations are significant. Warriors wrote to persuade, so descriptions of service are self-interested. The documents come mostly from warrior and institutional archives and say little about the cultivators who paid dues under whichever claimant prevailed.

Questions for students:

  1. What does a service report ask an authority to do, and what does it offer as proof?
  2. Who created these documents, and what interest did each writer have in the outcome?
  3. What can historians learn from such documents about how loyalty worked in practice?
  4. What can historians not establish from a claimant's own account of his service?
Section 28.5

Warriors in an Imperial Restoration

It would be a mistake to treat warriors as a bloc that opposed Go-Daigo. Many had fought for him and hoped that imperial government would bring justice and reward. Some had personal ties to the throne or to noble houses. Others had joined the cause because the Hōjō had exploited them or excluded them from favor. Several of the most effective commanders of the war of 1331–1333, Kusunoki Masashige and Nawa Nagatoshi among them, had been outside the top ranks of the Kamakura order, and Go-Daigo did reward some such men with office and rank.

The difficulty was integration. Kamakura had built a system in which warriors served a warrior head and received recognition through his institutions. Go-Daigo's court could offer prestige and grants but lacked a comparable military organization. Warriors might receive imperial titles and still look to a military leader, such as Takauji, for practical protection and the assurance that their claims would be defended. Older gokenin families with legal traditions took for granted that written documentation, precedent, and evidence would decide disputes. The court's approach, by contrast, could seem improvised and dependent on personal favor.

Warriors also disagreed among themselves. Those who had joined late, after the tide had turned, were rewarded alongside those who had fought from the beginning. Some who had served the Hōjō sought to preserve their positions by pledging loyalty to the new government. Men of lesser rank resented the elevation of others. The restoration therefore produced not one warrior grievance but many, and they did not necessarily point toward a single alternative.

Section 28.6

Court Aristocrats, Temples, and Competing Claims

Warriors were not the only claimants. Court families regarded the destruction of Kamakura as an opportunity to recover rights that had been lost to warrior stewards and land managers, or to bakufu decisions. Aristocratic houses had different interests. Some had supported Go-Daigo early, some had served under Kōgon, and some now sought protection by changing allegiance. The new government drew on both groups, and its personnel policy required delicate handling.

Great temples and shrines were major landowners and also political actors. Mount Hiei and other Tendai centers, the Nara temples, Shingon institutions, and important shrines held estates and maintained armed followers. Go-Daigo had cultivated ties with religious centers during the wars, and some of them had contributed to his cause. After 1333 they too wanted their rights confirmed. When the interests of a temple and a warrior claimant collided over the same estate, the government had to choose, and each choice created an enemy. This made Kenmu politics a triangle of court, warrior, and religious proprietors, not a simple contest of court against samurai.

The cultivators on these lands bore the practical consequences. Dues might be demanded by the estate proprietor, by a steward, by a new warrior claimant, and by a military commander who needed provisions. The same village might therefore face several conflicting claims in a single year, a problem that would grow as the wars continued.

Multiple Perspectives

The Kenmu Regime as Experienced by Different Groups

Note: these are composite descriptions built from the kinds of evidence discussed in this chapter, not quotations or biographies of specific individuals. The groups did not have equal power.

Emperor Go-Daigo: He sought to establish the throne as the direct source of political authority, free of regents, retired emperors, and the bakufu. His experiment depended on the ability of imperial directives to command obedience from people who had grown used to warrior institutions.
A court aristocrat seeking an estate: For such a person the fall of Kamakura promised a chance to recover income lost to warrior encroachment. A restoration meant restitution, and any policy that continued to favor warrior claimants was a betrayal of the purpose of the war.
A warrior who fought against Kamakura: He expected a confirmation of his holdings and a share of the confiscated Hōjō lands. Delays, reversals, and the rise of rivals could turn gratitude into suspicion, and a military patron who could deliver was an attractive alternative.
Ashikaga Takauji: He held rank and title from Go-Daigo yet also led a following of warriors who looked to him for reward and protection. Maintaining that following required distributing benefits and defending his men's claims, whether or not the court approved.
A temple proprietor: The temple sought confirmation of estates and immunities. It was happy to obtain an imperial directive but unwilling to accept a policy that subordinated its holdings to warrior needs, and it could call on its own armed followers if it had to.
A cultivator on a contested estate: The farmer paid dues to whichever authority could enforce collection. The regime change brought uncertainty over who that authority was, and each new claimant added to the burden. The sources rarely record this person's own account.
Section 28.7

Prince Morinaga and the Politics of the New Regime

Prince Morinaga, a son of Go-Daigo, had taken up arms early in the war, rallying warriors in the Kinai region and using his religious background (he had been a Tendai cleric) to assemble supporters. In 1333 he was given the title of seii taishōgun, the old title of the warrior commander. This alone was significant. It showed that Go-Daigo's camp took the shogunal title seriously and that a prince might hold a military command as a channel to warrior support.

Morinaga and Takauji quickly became rivals. Each had a following and each distrusted the other's ambitions. Morinaga is said to have regarded Takauji as a threat to the imperial house and to have sought to build his own forces. Takauji, for his part, had reasons to worry about a prince who held a military title and controlled warriors. Go-Daigo himself was caught between them. In the autumn of 1334 he had Morinaga arrested, and the prince was sent to Kamakura, where he was held under Ashikaga Tadayoshi's supervision.

The episode has been read in different ways. Some historians see Go-Daigo's decision as a concession to Takauji, and others as an effort by the emperor to prevent a warrior faction from consolidating around his son. Either way, it shows that the victors were divided within a year, and that the emperor was willing to sacrifice a loyal son in order to manage a rival. Morinaga's later fate, in the Nakasendai Rebellion, is described below.

Section 28.8

Ashikaga Takauji after Kamakura

Takauji's position in 1333 was extraordinary. The Ashikaga were a distinguished branch of the Minamoto (Seiwa Genji), long allied with the Hōjō through marriage, and they had held important posts under Kamakura. Takauji himself had been married into the Hōjō network. When he turned against the regime he brought a large military following and enormous prestige among warriors. Go-Daigo rewarded him with high rank and a set of titles, including that of chinjufu shōgun (commander for the defense of the north), and Takauji received a character from the emperor's personal name, altering the first character of his name (Takauji, written 高氏, became 尊氏, still read Takauji) in honor of Go-Daigo's given name, Takaharu (尊治).

His power did not depend on the court. It rested on networks of kinship, vassalage, and shared expectations among warriors who considered the Ashikaga a natural head of Minamoto-descended warrior society. Takauji's household included his brother Tadayoshi, and his stewards, including Kō no Moronao, handled administration. In the same summer of 1333, his young son, later Yoshiakira, was associated with the eastern campaign, which gave the Ashikaga a foothold in the events at Kamakura despite the fact that Nitta Yoshisada led the actual assault.

Takauji's stance toward Go-Daigo in 1333 and 1334 is a matter of interpretation. The evidence does not support a claim that he intended from the start to found a shogunate. It does show that he had no interest in surrendering the military leadership that gave him influence, and that others saw him as the natural leader of warriors. This section deliberately stops before considering Ashikaga institutions in detail. That subject belongs to Chapter 29.

Section 28.9

Kamakura after the Hōjō

The east did not become a periphery when the bakufu fell. Kamakura remained a major center of eastern warrior power, and the Kantō remained one of the principal military bases of the realm, on which any government would depend. The Kenmu court recognized this. Prince Narinaga, one of Go-Daigo's sons, was sent to Kamakura with Ashikaga Tadayoshi as his adviser and chief administrator in a body sometimes called the Kamakura headquarters (Kamakura-fu). It supervised the eastern provinces, handled land matters, and represented a form of regional government that resembled the bakufu more than the court.

Warrior families in the Kantō looked to it, and its decisions could conflict with directives from Kyoto. Tadayoshi's administration, though nominally under the prince, functioned as an Ashikaga base. Nitta Yoshisada's followers in the region were rivals, and other eastern warriors, including the Uesugi and others with ties to the Ashikaga, aligned with the Ashikaga camp. The arrangement made the east a place where Ashikaga influence could grow without formal imperial sanction.

The east also retained social memory of the Hōjō. Former Hōjō retainers and relatives, scattered after 1333, were potential supporters of a restoration of the old order. This made the region combustible, as the events of 1335 demonstrated.

Section 28.10

The Nakasendai Rebellion, 1335

In the summer of 1335, Hōjō Tokiyuki, a son of the former regent Takatoki, rose in Shinano province with support from local warriors, including the Suwa family associated with the Suwa Shrine. His force advanced and defeated the defenders of Kamakura, and Tokiyuki occupied the city. The episode is called the Nakasendai Rebellion, from nakasendai, the “middle former generation,” a name given to the brief Hōjō restoration between the Hōjō regime (the “former generation”) and the Ashikaga.

Tadayoshi withdrew from Kamakura, and before leaving he ordered the killing of Prince Morinaga, who was in custody there. Morinaga died at the hands of a guard. The killing eliminated a rival of the Ashikaga and left a lasting question about Tadayoshi's responsibility, since Go-Daigo's supporters did not forget it.

The rebellion gave Takauji the emergency he needed. He asked the court for the title of seii taishōgun and for permission to lead an army east. The court declined both requests, but Takauji left Kyoto anyway, gathering warriors as he went. He reached the east, defeated Tokiyuki's forces, and retook Kamakura within weeks. The rebellion is a reminder that the memory of the Hōjō retained real force. It ended quickly, but it demonstrated that the regime's opponents could still assemble an army, and it provided a legitimate pretext for Ashikaga action.

Section 28.11

Takauji's Break with Go-Daigo

Once in Kamakura, Takauji began to act as a warrior head. He distributed rewards and confirmed land holdings to men who had served him, without waiting for authorization from Kyoto. To a warrior in the east, such confirmation was valuable, because it came from a leader who could enforce it. To Go-Daigo, it was a challenge to the principle that authority to reward flowed from the throne. Go-Daigo responded with offers of promotion and instructions to return to the capital. Takauji did not comply.

The break was a process. It did not take place in a single moment, and both sides sent mixed signals. Takauji's later statements portrayed his conduct as directed against disloyal courtiers, Nitta Yoshisada in particular, rather than against the emperor. Go-Daigo's court, for its part, moved gradually toward declaring him a rebel. By the end of 1335 that step had been taken, and Nitta Yoshisada was sent east with an army and the backing of an imperial commission.

This is the moment when the disagreement over who could reward and confirm became open war. Historians differ on how much responsibility belongs to each side (see the Historical Debate below), but they generally agree that the conflict cannot be explained solely by either man's character. It arose because two sources of authority, court and military head, both claimed the right to grant land and status.

Section 28.12

Nitta Yoshisada against the Ashikaga

Nitta Yoshisada and Ashikaga Takauji had fought on the same side in 1333. Both descended from the Minamoto and both were prominent Kantō warriors, and the Nitta had long been overshadowed by the Ashikaga. Yoshisada had led the attack that destroyed Kamakura and became one of the Kenmu government's most important military leaders. Long-standing rivalry between the Nitta and Ashikaga, competition for military prestige, and the political crisis of 1335 now placed the two houses on opposite sides. Yoshisada's forces met the Ashikaga at Takenoshita, near the Hakone mountains, in December 1335, and were defeated. Takauji's men pursued them westward.

The confrontation shows how quickly the coalition of 1333 fragmented. Personal and familial rivalries, the competition for prestige, and disputes over reward mattered as much as loyalty to a throne. Nitta warriors were not necessarily committed to Go-Daigo's program. They fought because he offered them a chance against their rivals, and because an imperial commission gave their cause a claim to legitimacy. In return, the Ashikaga could argue that they were defending the warrior order from a court faction.

Historical Debate

Why Did the Kenmu Restoration Fail?

The question: Go-Daigo's government lasted about three years. Was its failure a consequence of the emperor's own choices, or of conditions that any regime would have struggled to overcome?

Interpretation A — A failure of policy and centralization: Some scholars emphasize Go-Daigo's attempt to concentrate authority in the throne. His reliance on imperial directives, his ambitious plans (including a costly palace), his favoring of certain courtiers, and his difficulty in satisfying warrior expectations made the regime unpopular. On this view, better balance between court and warrior interests might have produced a more durable arrangement. Andrew Goble's study of Go-Daigo's government (1996) gives careful attention to the regime's own decisions and personnel, and argues against treating it as inevitably doomed, stressing its adaptability and institutional development.
Interpretation B — Severe structural constraints: Other scholars emphasize the conditions the regime inherited: an unprecedented mass of competing land claims, overlapping court, temple, and warrior rights, a shortage of trained adjudicators, and warlords such as Takauji with independent power. On this view, any successor government would have faced severe difficulties reconciling these competing claims and institutions, and the important question is why warrior institutions remained indispensable. Thomas Conlan's study of fourteenth-century warfare (2003) stresses the ways the violence itself reorganized warrior society, which supports this structural emphasis.

Evidence: Both views rely on the same body of evidence: the administrative documents of the Kenmu government, the accounts of the Taiheiki and the Baishōron, the Nijō-gawara satire, and the surviving reward and confirmation documents. They differ in how much weight to give to individual decisions versus structural constraints.

Why it matters: If the failure was mainly personal, the story becomes one of missed opportunity. If it was structural, the Ashikaga emerge as a response to problems that any government would face. The stronger conclusion, and the one adopted in this chapter, is that the Restoration faced the structural problem of trying to restore stronger imperial government in a political society transformed by nearly 150 years of institutionalized warrior rule. It is not adequate to say that Go-Daigo did not understand samurai.

Student task: Identify one piece of evidence that supports Interpretation A and one that supports Interpretation B. Then decide whether the two positions can both be partly true, and explain how.

Section 28.13

The First Struggle for Kyoto, 1335–1336

Takauji advanced west and entered Kyoto in the first weeks of 1336. Go-Daigo withdrew to Mount Hiei, where the Tendai monastic community offered refuge. The Ashikaga occupation did not last. Kitabatake Akiie, who had marched south from Mutsu with a large force, joined Nitta Yoshisada and Kusunoki Masashige. Together they counterattacked and drove Takauji's army out of the capital after several days of intense fighting.

This was a serious defeat. Takauji's army, weakened and demoralized, withdrew southwest through Settsu. The episode illustrates the importance of the alliance between Tōhoku and the court's supporters. Akiie's march, across hundreds of kilometers of difficult terrain, showed that Go-Daigo's cause still commanded serious resources. It also showed that the fate of Kyoto depended on armies from distant regions and on logistical capacity as much as on political legitimacy.

Section 28.14

Takauji's Flight to Kyushu

Defeated and pursued, Takauji retreated by sea to Kyushu. Later accounts, including the pro-Ashikaga Baishōron, describe advice from allies such as the Akamatsu family in Harima to regroup in the west and to obtain an imperial sanction of their own from Retired Emperor Kōgon, giving the Ashikaga a claim to be defending, not defying, the throne. Whatever the details of these tellings, the outcome is documented: the Ashikaga acquired an order in the name of a retired emperor of the Jimyōin line, which they could present to warriors as legitimating their war.

Why did an apparently ruined leader go to Kyushu? The island was distant from Kyoto and contained powerful warrior houses with strong regional networks and interests of their own. Kyushu had borne the heaviest burden of Mongol defense, prolonged guard duty, and expanded Hōjō supervision, while rewards and compensation were constrained and uneven relative to those costs. Local houses such as the Shōni, the Ōtomo, and the Shimazu possessed their own ambitions. A leader who could offer confirmation of holdings and protection could hope to gather followers there. The Ashikaga cause was not finished by the loss of Kyoto because the source of its strength was a network of regional alliances, not the possession of one city.

Section 28.15

Tatarahama and the Recovery of the Ashikaga Cause

In the spring of 1336, near Hakata on the coast of northern Kyushu, Takauji's forces met an army of the Kikuchi family, which was loyal to Go-Daigo, at the battle of Tatarahama. Takauji won. The Kikuchi, prominent in Kyushu warrior society, would continue to support the Southern cause for decades, and the region would remain contested, but the defeat opened the island to the Ashikaga.

The recovery that followed is a good illustration of how medieval military coalitions worked. There was no standing national army. Followers joined because they expected reward, feared the alternatives, or already had ties to a leader. Takauji issued confirmations, promised grants, and received defectors. Within weeks he had gathered a force large enough to return east. Ashikaga Tadayoshi commanded the land route along the Inland Sea coast, while Takauji sailed with the fleet, and warriors of Shikoku and the Inland Sea, including branches of the Hosokawa, joined the campaign. Maritime networks thus proved essential to the Ashikaga return. Their success owed as much to logistics and reward as to battlefield skill.

Section 28.16

Minatogawa and the Death of Kusunoki Masashige

The Ashikaga advanced by sea and land in the early summer of 1336. Go-Daigo's forces prepared to meet them west of Kyoto. At Minatogawa, on the coast of Settsu, in the summer of 1336, Nitta Yoshisada and Kusunoki Masashige faced the Ashikaga. The imperial side was defeated. Masashige, together with his brother Masasue and a number of followers, died at the battle, according to the war tales by suicide after the fighting turned against them.

The account most familiar to later readers comes from the Taiheiki. It says that Masashige advised Go-Daigo to abandon Kyoto and let Takauji enter, planning to trap him, and that the court, not wishing to leave the capital again, rejected the advice and ordered Masashige to fight. It says he went to the battle knowing he would die, and it includes a farewell scene with his young son Masatsura. These details are dramatic, and they cannot be confirmed by contemporary documents. Documents show that Masashige was a competent regional commander from Kawachi who fought for Go-Daigo in the campaigns of 1331–1333, held office and rewards afterward, and died in 1336. The rest is largely the work of later authors.

Myth and Memory

Kusunoki Masashige: One Man, Many Memories

The medieval commander. Masashige appears in documents as a warrior of Kawachi whose strongholds at Akasaka and Chihaya resisted Kamakura's armies. His background is debated: some interpretations describe him as a local magnate and others associate him with trade and transport networks. Reliable sources show his service and death. They do not give his thoughts.

The Taiheiki hero. The war tale made him a strategist, a loyal servant, and a doomed adviser. It attributes to his brother Masasue a wish to be reborn seven times to defeat the enemies of the throne. That phrase became one of the most repeated in later loyalist literature. As with the Heike and the accounts of the Mongol invasions in earlier chapters, students should treat it as literary tradition.

The early modern and nineteenth-century icon. In the Tokugawa period, the Mito domain's historical project honored the Southern loyalists, and Tokugawa Mitsukuni had a monument to Masashige erected at Minatogawa in 1692. Loyalist thinkers of the late Tokugawa period took him as a model. After the Meiji Restoration he was enshrined at Minatogawa Shrine (established in 1872), and a bronze equestrian statue of him was unveiled in Tokyo in 1900.

The twentieth-century symbol. In the age of state ideology, schools taught Masashige as an exemplar of absolute loyalty. His crest, a chrysanthemum floating on water (kikusui), was used in wartime; the mass air attacks of 1945 near Okinawa bore the name Kikusui. After 1945 the veneration collapsed, and Masashige has since been studied more critically.

Historical method: Each of these figures answers the needs of its own time. A historian must separate the person documented in fourteenth-century records from the symbol created by later ones.

Section 28.17

Kyoto Falls Again

After Minatogawa, Takauji entered Kyoto in the summer of 1336. Go-Daigo again took refuge on Mount Hiei. In the eighth month the Ashikaga installed Kōmyō, a younger brother of the deposed Kōgon and a member of the Jimyōin line, as emperor. Kōmyō's enthronement gave the Ashikaga a court that could issue appointments, ranks, and directives in an emperor's name. It was the decisive step toward two rival courts.

Go-Daigo negotiated with Takauji for a time, and in the winter he returned to Kyoto, where he surrendered regalia to Kōmyō's court. But this conciliation was short-lived. Takauji issued, in the eleventh month of 1336, a policy statement known as the Kenmu Shikimoku (Kenmu Code), a set of seventeen articles addressed to questions of government, including the place of the capital, the conduct of warriors, and the administration of justice. Its provisions belong to Chapter 29's analysis of the Ashikaga system, but its existence shows that the new regime was already defining itself in terms of policy. Its own era name, Kenmu, was retained by both sides for a time, another sign that neither camp claimed to break with the past.

Cause and Effect

From Victory over Kamakura to Rival Courts

Long-term causes

A century and a half of warrior institutions had reshaped landholding and expectations of service and reward. The Mongol crisis strained the bakufu. Rival imperial lines (Jimyōin and Daikakuji) already competed for the throne.

Short-term causes

The coalition against the Hōjō united groups with different aims. Mass land and reward claims overwhelmed the Kenmu offices. Rivalries among Go-Daigo, Prince Morinaga, Takauji, and Nitta Yoshisada intensified.

Event or transformation

The Nakasendai Rebellion of 1335 gave Takauji an independent military role. His break with Go-Daigo led to war, defeat, recovery, and the installation of Kōmyō in 1336.

Immediate effects

Go-Daigo left Kyoto and established a rival court at Yoshino. Takauji became shogun in 1338. Regional coalitions split along both court and factional lines.

Long-term consequences

Rival courts lasted until 1392. Shugo became increasingly important regional powers. Warrior government returned in a more decentralized form.

Historical complexity

None of these transitions was inevitable. Go-Daigo might have accommodated Takauji, Takauji might have accepted his removal, or the Kyoto defenders might have held the capital in 1336.

Historical complexity: Each step involved choices made by people responding to fear, ambition, obligation, and incomplete information. Historians should resist reading the sequence as a chain that had to end with an Ashikaga shogunate.

Section 28.18

Go-Daigo at Yoshino and the Birth of the Southern Court

In the twelfth month of 1336, Go-Daigo slipped out of Kyoto and made his way south to Yoshino, in the mountains of Yamato. There he declared that the regalia he had surrendered to Kōmyō's Ashikaga-backed court were not genuine and that he remained the legitimate emperor, claiming to possess the authentic regalia. He continued to issue appointments and directives, and he called on supporters across the realm. In this way the imperial court divided in two. The Kyoto court, supported by the Ashikaga, is known as the Northern Court (Hokuchō). The court at Yoshino is the Southern Court (Nanchō). The whole period from 1336 to 1392 is called the Nanboku-chō, the age of the Northern and Southern Courts.

The choice of Yoshino was not arbitrary. The mountains offered defensible terrain, ties with religious centers, and distance from Ashikaga forces. It had a sacred and historical resonance, and it was accessible to Go-Daigo's supporters in Kii and Yamato. But it was also isolated from the wealthy central plains, which limited the Southern Court's ability to raise revenues. This weakness shaped the entire course of the war.

Section 28.19

Two Courts, One Imperial Tradition

Students should understand a conceptual point. Neither side believed it was creating a second permanent monarchy. Each claimed to be the legitimate imperial court, and each regarded the other as usurping. The terms “Northern” and “Southern” are convenient labels used by later historians, chosen for the locations of the courts. Contemporaries used other descriptions, and the labels do not imply that either court was thought to have a smaller claim.

The claims rested on several resources. The first was lineage: the Southern side traced descent through Go-Daigo's Daikakuji line, while the Northern side held to the Jimyōin line of Kōgon and Kōmyō. The second was the regalia, the emblems of imperial succession, which each side claimed to possess or to have received in valid form. The third was the machinery of court authority: rank, office, and era names. The two courts used different era names, and the difference itself asserted independence. A warrior's document might be dated by an era name that showed which court he recognized. The fourth was practical support. A legitimate court had to be able to reward those who served it, and the ability to do so, depending on military success, was part of what made a claim credible.

The Southern Court's ideology was developed most fully by Kitabatake Chikafusa, who wrote that legitimacy depended on direct line succession and the regalia, and, in his fuller account, on the virtue of the ruler. The Northern side, backed by the Ashikaga, relied on possession of the capital, on the sanction of a retired emperor, and on the argument that the transfer of regalia had been made. Both sides were selective in their use of precedent, and the Ashikaga could point to earlier periods in which the imperial house had been divided.

Historical Vocabulary
Sanshu no jingi (三種の神器) — the Three Regalia

The three regalia are a mirror, a sword, and a jewel traditionally associated with the imperial house's descent from the sun goddess Amaterasu in the mythology of the Kojiki and Nihon Shoki. They were regarded as symbols of legitimate succession. Their meaning came from tradition and ritual, not from any documentary proof, and later historians regard the mythic origin as belief and not history.

In the Nanboku-chō period, possession of the regalia (or the credible claim to it) mattered greatly. When Go-Daigo left Kyoto, he claimed that the regalia surrendered there to Kōmyō's court were not authentic, a political argument about legitimacy. The transfer of the regalia in 1392 was likewise the symbolic act that ended the division.

Primary Source Window

Kitabatake Chikafusa, Jinnō Shōtōki (“Record of the Legitimate Succession of the Divine Sovereigns”)

Creator: Kitabatake Chikafusa, a court noble and adviser to the Southern Court · Date: composed in 1339 and revised in the following years · Type: Political and historical treatise

Context: Chikafusa wrote at a moment of crisis for the Southern cause. Go-Daigo had died, the young Go-Murakami had succeeded him, and Chikafusa was in the Kantō seeking to rally support. The treatise gives an account of imperial succession from the age of the gods and argues that the line of descent from the sun goddess has continued unbroken, making Japan distinctive. It develops criteria for legitimacy, including proper lineage and possession of the regalia, and it evaluates rulers according to their virtue. It defends the Southern claim.

Paraphrase of the work's opening claim (not a verbatim quotation): The treatise begins by asserting that Japan is a land of the gods and protected by them, because the line descended from the divine ancestress has been transmitted without interruption, something the author says is not true of other countries.

Analysis: The work is an argument, not a neutral chronicle. Chikafusa selected and interpreted the past to show that the Southern line was the proper successor. His emphasis on continuity and divine descent was later taken up by imperial loyalists and nationalists, who used the text in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries. Historians studying it must therefore separate his fourteenth-century purpose from later uses, and must remember that his claims about the age of the gods are traditional narrative, not evidence of remote history. The treatise reveals how a partisan of one court thought about legitimacy; it says little about ordinary warriors and cultivators.

Historical Controversy

Which Was the Legitimate Court?

The question: Was the Northern or the Southern Court the rightful imperial court? This textbook does not choose a “rightful” emperor. The question has its own history, and students should examine how different people answered it and why.

Arguments associated with the Southern claim: Go-Daigo had been the reigning emperor, had never validly abdicated according to his supporters, and had left with what he claimed were the true regalia. The Ashikaga, in this view, had rebelled against a legitimate sovereign.
Arguments associated with the Northern claim: Kōmyō's accession followed the sanction of a retired emperor of the Jimyōin line and was recognized by the Ashikaga and many court figures. Its supporters pointed to the regalia handed over in 1336. In 1392 the transfer of regalia to the Northern line completed the reunification on that side's terms.

Later interpretations: Medieval supporters disagreed and sometimes changed sides. In the Tokugawa period the Mito domain's historical work, the Dai Nihon shi, favored the Southern Court. In the modern period, a controversy over school textbooks in 1911 led the government to declare the Southern Court legitimate, and the official list of emperors was arranged accordingly, dropping the Northern emperors from the main line. That decision reflected the ideology of the time and the needs of the modern state and not new medieval evidence. After 1945, historians have generally treated the terms as descriptive and have studied the two courts as parts of one imperial institution.

Why it matters: The issue shows how legitimacy is a political resource, and how later regimes revise the past to serve their own claims. It also foreshadows the way the Southern Court became important in later loyalist thought, where Masashige and Go-Daigo were treated as models.

Student task: Explain why a modern government might care which medieval court was legitimate, and identify what kinds of evidence, if any, could settle the question.

Section 28.20

Takauji Becomes Shogun, 1338

In 1338 the Northern Court appointed Ashikaga Takauji seii taishōgun. The appointment marked the return of formal warrior government, a bakufu headed by a shogun with the court's sanction. The same year saw heavy losses for the Southern side. Nitta Yoshisada died in a skirmish at Fujishima in Echizen, and Kitabatake Akiie, who had led a second campaign from Mutsu, fell in battle in Izumi province. These deaths removed two of Go-Daigo's ablest commanders.

A new bakufu was taking shape, but this chapter does not analyze how it worked. The division of authority between Takauji and his brother Tadayoshi, the roles of the stewards, the judicial functions, the role of provincial constables, and the fiscal foundation of the regime belong to Chapter 29. The point here is narrower: the Ashikaga were not merely military rebels. They combined the legitimacy of an imperial court, the title of shogun, and the practical support of warriors seeking confirmation of their holdings.

Go-Daigo died at Yoshino in 1339. Later tradition says he wished to be remembered as still seeking to defeat the Ashikaga, but the accounts are late. His successor, Go-Murakami, the prince who had been in Mutsu, continued the Southern cause. The court would move among several bases in Yamato and Kii, and, as fortunes shifted, to other places.

Historical Vocabulary
Shugo (守護)

A shugo was a provincial military governor, an office created under the Kamakura bakufu with duties concerning policing, military mobilization, and supervision of warriors. During the Nanboku-chō wars, shugo acquired greater powers, including the right to collect supplies, mediate land disputes, and, in some cases, hold a share of estate revenue for military needs. In 1352 the Ashikaga issued an order in parts of Ōmi, Mino, and Owari permitting shugo to keep half of certain revenues for a year (the hanzei), an example of wartime measures that expanded shugo authority.

Historians often say that shugo became regional power holders in this period, though the extent varied by province. Students should not treat the word as identical in meaning across time. It is examined in institutional terms in Chapter 29.

Section 28.21

The War Spreads across the Provinces

The Nanboku-chō conflict was not a war between two centralized armies. It was a mosaic of regional struggles in which local families used the two courts as sources of authority and legitimacy. A warrior who wished to defeat a rival in a neighboring district might join the opposite court simply to obtain a commission. Rivalries within families led brothers or cousins to take different sides. Loyalties shifted repeatedly.

In Kyushu, Prince Kaneyoshi, a son of Go-Daigo, arrived in 1336 and, with the support of the Kikuchi and other families, built a Southern base that dominated much of the island for years. In the 1350s and 1360s the Southern side controlled Dazaifu, the old administrative center. Ashikaga representatives, called tandai, struggled to reassert authority, and the Ashikaga camp itself was divided in Kyushu during the Kannō period. In 1371 the Ashikaga sent Imagawa Ryōshun, who campaigned effectively and recaptured Dazaifu in 1372.

The war also had an international dimension. In the 1360s the new Ming dynasty, having replaced Mongol rule in China in 1368, sent envoys to Japan, and in 1371 recognized Prince Kaneyoshi, then the leading Southern authority in Kyushu, as “King of Japan.” The Ming court was dealing with the authority it found on the ground. Meanwhile, from the mid-fourteenth century, raiders and traders known as wakō, drawn from communities around the Tsushima Strait and northern Kyushu, attacked Korean and Chinese coasts. Goryeo sought help from Japanese authorities against them, and Ryōshun was among those approached. The Kyushu war thus connected the courts' conflict to East Asian diplomacy and maritime society.

In Shikoku, the Hosokawa and other families supported the Ashikaga. In Tōhoku, Kitabatake Akiie's successors continued to fight for the South, though their strength was reduced. In the Kantō, the Uesugi and other families took Ashikaga positions, while the Nitta descendants remained a Southern force. Kitabatake Chikafusa traveled to Hitachi in 1338 to organize Southern support in the east, though the effort had limited results. The far north, including Tsugaru and the ports connecting Honshu to Ezo, remained linked to Ainu trade networks that carried goods across the northern seas; the political changes in Tōhoku affected the warrior families who managed those ports and the exchange with Ainu communities, though the sources for this are thin.

Everyday Life and Environment

Living in a Landscape of Competing Claims

For villagers, the effects of the war were less about the legitimacy of emperors than about who would demand dues, labor, and supplies. Armies drew provisions from the places they passed. Estate managers, stewards, and military claimants might each seek payment. Villages in the Kinai region, which developed forms of collective organization in these centuries, could negotiate with claimants and sometimes petition for relief, though the surviving evidence is uneven and local. Temples served as refuges, as storehouses, and as centers of record-keeping. Merchants and transport workers, including those engaged in coastal shipping, found both risk and opportunity in the demand for supplies and the movement of armies.

The war also shaped culture. Aristocratic poetry continued, and linked verse (renga) flourished in this era, with the compilation of a major anthology in 1357 under the guidance of the court noble Nijō Yoshimoto. Some warriors adopted a showy style, called basara, marked by extravagant display and contempt for conventional authority, which the Taiheiki associates with figures such as Sasaki Dōyo, and which the Kenmu Shikimoku criticizes. Performers such as those who would develop the Noh theater, treated in Chapter 30, were active in these decades. Everyday life did not stop for war, but war shaped its risks and rhythms.

Section 28.22

The Kannō Disturbance, 1350–1352

The Ashikaga camp was itself deeply divided. Tadayoshi handled much of the civil and judicial administration, while Takauji's steward Kō no Moronao, with his brother Moroyasu, controlled military and household matters. Tadayoshi and Moronao became bitter rivals. Underlying their quarrel were disagreements over policy, including how to treat established landholders, and personal ambition. In 1349 the conflict reached a crisis. Moronao's faction forced Tadayoshi from office, and Takauji's son Yoshiakira came to Kyoto to take a leading role. Takauji's adopted son Tadafuyu, a natural son of Takauji adopted by Tadayoshi, took a stand against Takauji in Kyushu.

In 1350, Tadayoshi withdrew from Kyoto, took up arms, and sought allies, including, eventually, the Southern Court. The war that followed is called the Kannō Disturbance (Kannō no jōran). In early 1351 Tadayoshi's supporters defeated the Kō brothers, and Moronao and Moroyasu were killed by men under the Uesugi. In late 1351 Takauji himself, pressed on two fronts, negotiated an arrangement with the Southern Court, sometimes called the union of the Shōhei era, in which the Southern Court was acknowledged and the Northern emperor and crown prince were set aside. It did not last. Takauji marched east against Tadayoshi, defeated him, and Tadayoshi died in Kamakura in early 1352. Later tradition says he was poisoned; the evidence is not decisive.

The Southern Court exploited the confusion. Its forces occupied Kyoto in 1352, taking several Northern imperial figures into custody, and they entered the capital several more times over the following decade. The Ashikaga installed a new Northern emperor, Go-Kōgon, in 1352, an act complicated by the fact that the retired emperors who would normally have authorized it were in Southern hands. In the same year the hanzei measure, mentioned above, was issued to support the war effort.

The disturbance is crucial. It shows that alliances could cross the supposed North/South divide, and that the Northern side faced divisions as serious as any between the courts. It also reminds us that “Northern” and “Southern” were not the only categories in play: factions, families, and regional interests also mattered. Students should abandon the picture of a neat two-sided war.

Section 28.23

Why the Southern Court Survived

By purely military measures, the Southern Court might have been expected to collapse after the deaths of Go-Daigo's leading commanders. In 1348 Ashikaga forces under Kō no Moronao had defeated the Southern army at Shijōnawate (where Kusunoki Masatsura, son of Masashige, died) and burned the palace complex at Yoshino. Yet the court survived for decades. Several factors help explain this.

Geography mattered. The mountains of Yamato and Kii were difficult for armies to control, and the Southern Court could move between bases. Legitimacy mattered. As long as the Southern Court could credibly claim possession of the authentic regalia and issued appointments, warriors seeking leverage against Ashikaga-aligned rivals had a reason to join. Regional support mattered, particularly in Kyushu, in the Kii region, and among families with grievances against the Ashikaga. The Ashikaga divisions of 1350–1352 gave the Southern Court new life, and later conflicts within the Ashikaga camp offered further opportunities. Finally, the Northern Court and Ashikaga were unable to eliminate the Southern Court decisively because Southern forces could disperse among difficult regional bases, while the Ashikaga repeatedly had to divide their resources among provincial conflicts and internal factional struggles.

The Southern Court's weaknesses were equally real. It lacked the fiscal resources of the central provinces, and its command structure was loose. After Takauji's death in 1358 and the accession of his son Yoshiakira, the Ashikaga gradually consolidated. Yoshiakira died in 1367, and Yoshimitsu became shogun in 1368 as a child, guided by the steward Hosokawa Yoriyuki. Southern Kyushu fell to the Ashikaga in the 1370s. By the 1380s the Southern Court was a shrinking force, but it was not extinguished by force.

Section 28.24

Reunification in 1392

Ashikaga Yoshimitsu, by the 1380s an assertive shogun with growing control over the great warrior houses, worked to end the division. He negotiated with the Southern Court, then under Emperor Go-Kameyama, who had succeeded Chōkei in 1383. In the tenth month of 1392 (an intercalary month in that year's calendar), Go-Kameyama returned to Kyoto and transferred the regalia to the Northern emperor, Go-Komatsu. The division of the courts formally ended.

The terms of the agreement, as later described, included an expectation that the imperial succession would alternate between the Daikakuji (Southern) and Jimyōin (Northern) lines, and that the Daikakuji line would receive certain properties for its support. This expectation was not realized. Go-Komatsu passed the throne in 1412 to his own son, of the Jimyōin line, and the Ashikaga did not enforce alternation. Southern loyalist descendants and supporters resisted for decades, and attempts to revive the Southern cause occurred well into the fifteenth century, including a raid on the imperial palace in 1443. The terms show that reunification involved bargaining, and that the Ashikaga, by this point, were positioned to dictate the outcome. Because the exact terms are known mainly from later accounts, historians treat some details with caution.

The year 1392 also saw the founding of the Joseon dynasty in Korea. Yoshimitsu was already developing relations with Korean authorities over trade and wakō. Reunification strengthened his ability to present the Ashikaga as Japan's principal political authority abroad; his own formal diplomatic opening to Ming China followed in 1401. Those relations and the maritime world they belonged to are explored in Chapters 31 and 32.

Section 28.25

What the Nanboku-chō Era Changed

Several transformations emerge from the sixty years of conflict. First, the imperial institution survived and, in the long run, was preserved by an arrangement with the warrior government. The court lost the prospect of governing directly, but it kept ritual, rank, and prestige. Second, warrior government returned, though in a form that would differ from Kamakura's. Third, the shugo emerged as increasingly important regional powers, having acquired administrative, judicial, and fiscal responsibilities during the wars. Fourth, allegiance became fluid. Warriors changed sides frequently, and the notion of a stable, hereditary tie to a single lord weakened in practice. Fifth, documentary evidence of reward and land claims remained fundamental. The wars were fought over land, and the bureaucratic apparatus for confirming rights expanded. Sixth, political authority became more decentralized, with regional powers holding significant autonomy.

These developments raised the question that Chapter 29 addresses: how did the Ashikaga convert the unstable military coalition created during these wars into a functioning shogunate? The answer, as we will see, involved compromise, with the great shugo houses sharing power with the shogun, and with the court retained as a source of legitimacy.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From Kamakura to the Nanboku-chō Order

What Changed?

  • The Hōjō regency disappeared and the Ashikaga assumed the shogunal office.
  • The imperial court was divided for six decades and then reunited under Ashikaga oversight.
  • Shugo gained wider powers over provinces and warriors.
  • The center of warrior government moved from Kamakura to Kyoto.

What Remained Continuous?

  • Warrior government, in the form of a shogun and a bakufu, remained the framework for governing warriors.
  • The court continued to supply legitimacy, rank, and ritual authority.
  • The estate system, though eroded, persisted alongside warrior claims.
  • Reward for service remained the currency of loyalty.

Who experienced change most directly? Warriors in contested provinces, court families who lost or regained rights, and cultivators on estates subject to competing claims experienced the disruptions most directly. Who experienced greater continuity? Many villages, temples, and provincial families experienced mainly a change in the identity of the authority to whom they paid. Why does the pattern matter? It shows that Japan's political order was reconstructed in this period on the foundations of the old, not created afresh.

Chapter Summary

Chapter 28 has examined what followed the destruction of the Kamakura bakufu in 1333. Its central argument is that 1333 did not settle the question of who should rule Japan. It reopened it. The coalition that overthrew the Hōjō contained emperors, aristocrats, temples, established retainers, regional warriors, and military leaders whose aims diverged as soon as their common enemy was gone.

Emperor Go-Daigo tried to establish a government in which imperial authority was direct, and this experiment is best understood as a reinvention rather than a literal restoration. His administration faced overwhelming demands for confirmation and reward, and it lacked the adjudicative resources that Kamakura had built over generations. Conflicts among court aristocrats, warriors, and religious proprietors over the same lands made the task harder still.

The rivalry between Go-Daigo's camp and Ashikaga Takauji developed gradually. Prince Morinaga's arrest in 1334 showed divisions within the victorious side. The Nakasendai Rebellion of 1335 gave Takauji the emergency that allowed him to act independently in the east, and his distribution of rewards without imperial authorization triggered open conflict. Former allies, including Nitta Yoshisada, became enemies.

The war of 1335–1336 demonstrated that holding Kyoto did not decide the struggle. Takauji lost the capital, retreated to Kyushu, and rebuilt his following through regional alliances, promises of reward, and an imperial sanction from a retired emperor. His victory at Tatarahama and return by land and sea led to Minatogawa, where Kusunoki Masashige died. Later tradition greatly expanded his significance, and the chapter has separated the documented commander from the medieval hero and the modern symbol.

Takauji installed Kōmyō in 1336, and Go-Daigo fled to Yoshino, creating the Northern and Southern Courts. Neither side believed it was founding a second monarchy; each claimed to be the legitimate court. Claims rested on lineage, regalia, court appointments, era names, and the ability to reward supporters. Kitabatake Chikafusa's Jinnō Shōtōki provides the most developed Southern argument, and its later use by loyalists shows how legitimacy is remade by later ages.

Takauji's appointment as shogun in 1338 marked the reappearance of warrior government, though this chapter has left its institutional analysis to Chapter 29. The war spread across the provinces as a set of regional struggles: in Kyushu, the Kantō, Tōhoku, Shikoku, and the mountains of Yamato and Kii. Local families used the two courts as resources. The conflict connected to East Asia through Ming recognition of Prince Kaneyoshi and the wakō raids.

The Kannō Disturbance of 1350–1352, in which Takauji fought his brother Tadayoshi, showed that the Northern side was itself divided and that alliances could cross the courts' boundaries. The Southern Court survived because of geography, legitimacy, regional support, and Ashikaga divisions, though it was fiscally and militarily weak.

Reunification came in 1392 under Ashikaga Yoshimitsu. Go-Kameyama returned the regalia to Kyoto, and an expected alternation of succession was not carried out. The Nanboku-chō era left the imperial institution intact but subordinate in practice, returned warrior government to a more decentralized form, increased the importance of shugo, and made allegiance more fluid.

These wars were experienced by ordinary people as competing demands for dues, labor, and provisions. They also produced literature, memory, and ideology, from the Taiheiki to modern nationalism. The chapter's lesson is that political orders are constructed through conflict and compromise, and that later memory can obscure what earlier participants actually did and wanted.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a decisive victory can fail to settle a political order. The destruction of Kamakura removed a regime that had governed warriors for a century and a half, but it did not remove the conditions that had made such a regime necessary: a landed warrior society, a demand for adjudication and reward, and regional powers with their own resources. The sixty years of conflict that followed were the process by which those conditions produced a new arrangement.

The chapter also connects regions. Kyoto, Kamakura, Kyushu, Tōhoku, and the mountains of Yamato and Kii were bound by the campaigns, by the movements of armies and courts, and by shared questions of legitimacy. Kyushu's Southern government dealt with Ming China, and the wakō raids drew in Korea. The northern ports were tied to Ainu trade. These links show that the conflict was part of a wider East Asian world.

Later developments were shaped by the outcome. The Ashikaga shogunate that emerged would be a decentralized order in which shugo held real power, and the imperial court survived as a source of legitimacy under warrior protection. The memory of the Southern Court, especially the figures of Go-Daigo and Kusunoki Masashige, became a resource for loyalist movements in the Tokugawa period and for the modern state, which shows that the past is continually reinterpreted.

Students who carry away one lesson should remember that the participants did not know how the story would end. Warriors bargained, changed sides, and tried to protect their families and holdings. Chapter 29 now turns from this narrative of breakdown to ask how the Ashikaga made a workable government from the unstable coalition it had inherited.
