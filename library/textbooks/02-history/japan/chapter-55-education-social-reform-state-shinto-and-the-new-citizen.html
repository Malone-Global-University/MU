Part VIII — Bakumatsu and the Meiji Revolution

Education, Social Reform, State Shinto, and the New Citizen

Japanese History: From Jōmon Japan to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

In the autumn of 1872 the newly created Ministry of Education circulated a preface declaring that learning should reach every village and every household, and that farmers, merchants, craftsmen, women, and girls, not only the samurai who had long dominated formal schooling, should be educated. The ambition was extraordinary. Within a year, in several prefectures, crowds angered by conscription, new taxes, and the demands of the new regime were attacking school buildings as symbols of that regime.

The gap between those two moments is the subject of this chapter. Chapter 54 examined how the Meiji government dismantled the domains, the hereditary stipends of the warrior class, and the military monopoly of the samurai. Those measures removed old political and military institutions. They did not by themselves create a population prepared to live under the new order. Between 1868 and the 1890s, the government and many private actors therefore set out on a second and in some ways more difficult project: changing how people were classified, taught, registered, ritually integrated, and morally instructed, and how they understood their relationship to the emperor and the state.

The chapter title refers to "the new citizen," and that phrase must be handled carefully. The Meiji state did not create citizens in the sense of equal bearers of political rights who could choose their government. Officials, educators, and the emperor's own pronouncements most often addressed the population as shinmin, "subjects" of the emperor, and also as kokumin, members of the nation. Subjects had duties, such as paying taxes, serving in the army, sending children to school, and showing loyalty. They received protections and opportunities that the old status order had denied to many people, but their rights were defined and limited by the state. The tension between new opportunity and prescribed obligation runs through every section that follows.

The chapter follows four connected fields of change. The first is social status: the abolition of the old four-tier order and of the legal categories of eta and hinin in 1871, together with the registration system that classified households and preserved ancestry as a social fact. The second is education: the 1872 Education System Order, the repeated revisions of the 1879 and 1880 orders and the 1886 ordinances, and the experience of teachers, parents, and children. The third is religion: the separation of kami and buddhas, the destruction visited on many Buddhist institutions, the state's attempt to teach a unified moral doctrine, the gradual emergence of what scholars call State Shinto, and the fates of Christianity and new religions. The fourth is the household, where marriage, divorce, inheritance, and the authority of the household head were reshaped by laws culminating in the Civil Code of 1898. Throughout, the chapter asks how these policies reached, or failed to reach, the Ainu of Hokkaidō, the people of the former Ryukyu Kingdom, women, rural families, and the descendants of the outcast communities.

The evidence is unusually rich but strongly weighted toward the state. Historians read proclamations of the Council of State (Dajōkan), ministry reports and school statistics, household registers, textbooks, temple and shrine records, newspapers, missionary correspondence, and the memoirs of teachers and students. These sources tell us much about what officials intended and counted. They tell us less about how a rural mother in a poor village weighed school fees against her daughter's labor, how a leatherworker in a former outcast hamlet experienced the loss of old occupational privileges, or what Ainu families thought of orders that banned customs they practiced. Where the voices of these groups survive, they often do so through the words of officials, missionaries, or later activists, and the chapter flags that mediation wherever it matters.

The central argument is that the Meiji government did not merely establish new institutions. It tried to redefine social status, education, religious authority, family obligations, and the individual's relationship to an emperor-centered state, and it sometimes succeeded in changing legal categories more quickly than lived practice. Legal emancipation did not end discrimination, compulsory schooling did not at once produce universal attendance, and the reorganization of religion did not eradicate established beliefs. The chapter therefore distinguishes consistently among ambition, legislation, implementation, and lived experience. It connects backward to Chapter 54's institutional revolution and forward to the economic transformation of Chapter 56, the movements for representative government in Chapter 57, the cultural debates of Chapter 58, the Meiji Constitution of Chapter 59, and the fuller histories of women and new religions in Chapter 64.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how the Meiji government sought to replace Tokugawa status divisions with a population of imperial subjects, and why "subject" is a more accurate term than "citizen."
  2. Analyze the 1871 Emancipation Edict, its practical limits, and the continuing discrimination faced by communities later identified as Burakumin.
  3. Describe how household registration and social classification recorded status and ancestry, and why those records mattered.
  4. Compare Tokugawa schooling with the 1872 Education System Order and assess the distance between its ambitions and its early results.
  5. Distinguish the successive phases of educational policy in 1872, 1879, 1880, and 1886 rather than treating them as one uninterrupted reform.
  6. Evaluate why girls, rural children, and poor families enrolled at lower rates and how communities resisted or negotiated schooling.
  7. Trace the separation of Shinto and Buddhism, the anti-Buddhist movement, and the Buddhist response, noting regional variation.
  8. Interpret the development of State Shinto as a gradual and contested process rather than a finished system in 1868.
  9. Assess the experiences of Christians, new religious movements, Ainu communities, and Okinawans under national policies of uniformity.
  10. Explain the family provisions of the Civil Code of 1898 as the culmination of a contested legal process.
  11. Interpret the Imperial Rescript on Education of 1890 as a document and as a school ritual, and connect it to earlier reforms.
  12. Evaluate competing historical interpretations of Meiji educational reform as empowerment, state-building, or both.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. What did Meiji leaders hope to achieve by changing how people were classified, schooled, and taught to worship, and how did those goals relate to the strategic problems of the new government?
  2. Why did the legal abolition of outcast status fail to abolish discrimination, and what does that failure reveal about the difference between law and social practice?
  3. How did the record-keeping of the new registration system both create a unified legal population and preserve distinctions of ancestry?
  4. Why did a school system announced in 1872 take decades to reach most children, and who bore the costs?
  5. How should historians weigh the empowering effects of mass schooling against its role in building loyalty to the state?
  6. Was State Shinto fully formed in 1868, and how did government religious policy change across the 1870s and 1880s?
  7. How did the experiences of Ainu communities, Okinawans, women, and rural families complicate the claim that reforms created a uniform national population?
  8. To what extent did the Rescript of 1890 introduce something new, and to what extent did it consolidate policies already under way?

Historical Context

Tokugawa society, as Chapter 43 explained, was organized by hereditary status groups with differing obligations and privileges. The conventional "four classes" of warriors, farmers, artisans, and merchants described an ideal more than a lived reality, and the system also contained people outside that scheme, including the imperial court and nobility, clergy, and the outcast groups designated eta and hinin. Status determined what work a person could do, whom one could marry, how one dressed, and which authorities held jurisdiction. It was administered through domains, villages, and status-based corporate bodies rather than through a single national population register. Chapter 54 showed how the abolition of domains and warrior privileges began to dissolve this order from above. Chapter 55 asks what replaced it in everyday social life.

Religious life was likewise entangled with the state in ways that the Meiji reformers inherited. Under Tokugawa rule, every household was required to affiliate with a Buddhist temple that certified it was not Christian, which tied the clergy to a quasi-administrative function (Chapter 47). Kami and buddhas were commonly worshipped together, with many shrines governed by Buddhist institutions and many temples containing shrines to local deities. Mountain religion, pilgrimage to Ise, and popular devotions flourished with little regard for any clear boundary between "Shinto" and "Buddhism." The nativist scholarship that had developed since the eighteenth century, especially the tradition associated with Motoori Norinaga and Hirata Atsutane, challenged this mixture by insisting on the primacy of the kami and of the imperial line, and nativist ideas influenced the first Meiji religious policies.

The Tokugawa educational legacy was also considerable. Domain schools trained samurai in the Confucian classics, private academies taught a range of subjects, and thousands of terakoya, commoner schools often taught by priests, samurai, or villagers, gave many boys and a smaller number of girls the ability to read and write. This inheritance gave the Meiji government a social foundation of demand for schooling, but it also meant that education was uneven by region, gender, and household wealth. At the same time, the Meiji state operated under intense pressure. Leaders believed that the unequal treaties and the threat of Western expansion required a unified, productive, and loyal population. The Charter Oath of 1868 had promised that knowledge would be sought throughout the world, and the Iwakura Mission of 1871 to 1873 sent senior officials abroad to observe how Western states organized schools, churches, and families. They returned with admiration for some institutions, an awareness that Christian-majority powers condemned Japan's anti-Christian laws, and a conviction that national strength depended on an educated, disciplined populace.

Finally, the archipelago's peripheries entered this story on terms very different from those of central Japan. Hokkaidō, formerly called Ezo, was renamed and placed under a Development Commission (Kaitakushi) in 1869, subjecting Ainu communities to colonization and administrative reclassification. The Ryukyu Kingdom, a tributary state of both China and Satsuma (Chapters 32 and 48), was converted into a domain in 1872 and annexed as Okinawa Prefecture in 1879. Policies of schooling, naming, language, and custom reached these regions as instruments of incorporation rather than as extensions of a shared tradition, and later sections treat them on their own terms. The chapter concentrates on the period from 1868 to about 1900, with the formative decisions of the 1870s and 1880s receiving the most attention.

Division I — Social Status, Emancipation, and the New Imperial Subject · Section 55.1

From Tokugawa Status Society to Meiji Subjects

The new government did not begin with a blueprint for a unified population. Its first priority in 1868 was survival in a civil war and a diplomatic crisis. But within months, its leaders took steps that implied a very different social order. The Charter Oath of April 1868 promised that officials and commoners alike would be able to pursue their aims, and the return of domain registers to the throne in 1869 (hanseki hōkan) was followed by the abolition of domains in 1871 and the dissolution of the warrior class's formal privileges, topics treated in Chapter 54. As political institutions fell away, the government faced a practical question: what should the population now be called, and what should it owe?

In the transitional years the answer was a simplified hierarchy. In 1869 former court nobles and daimyō were merged into a new peerage, the kazoku. Former samurai were designated shizoku, and a lower warrior group, the sotsu, was reclassified in 1872 into either shizoku or commoners. Everyone else became heimin, "commoners." This scheme preserved visible distinctions of rank and honor, and the shizoku, for a time, kept stipends and cultural prestige. Yet it also marked a decisive break, because it detached privilege from the older web of domain and corporate affiliation and tied status to a national register. In the early 1870s the government also lifted many restrictions that had defined the old order. Commoners were permitted to take family names in 1870, and in 1875 family names were made compulsory. Restrictions on cutting the topknot and wearing swords were relaxed in 1871, internal barriers to travel were removed, and the old limitations on occupation and on marriage across status lines were loosened.

The ideological purpose behind this restructuring was articulated in different ways by different leaders. Some, such as the writer Fukuzawa Yukichi, argued for individual independence, equality before the law, and the value of practical learning. Others, including many senior officials, were more interested in a unified, productive, and obedient population capable of supporting a strong army and a modern economy. Neither vision was identical with democratic citizenship. The emperor was increasingly presented as the center of the nation, as in the imperial tours of the 1870s and 1880s, and the term shinmin placed the population in a relationship of loyalty to the throne. The language of "subjects" did not necessarily imply passivity, and the Freedom and People's Rights Movement of the 1870s and 1880s (Chapter 57) would demand political voice from within that framework. But the framework itself assigned the state, acting in the emperor's name, the power to define rights and duties.

The historian should avoid two simplifications. The first is to treat the new order as an instantaneous break from Tokugawa society. Many older distinctions persisted in practice, from the prestige of former warrior families to local village hierarchies and the social standing of landlords. The second is to treat the reforms as mere cosmetic relabeling. The abolition of legal status barriers opened real opportunities, from new occupations to access to schooling and, eventually, military and civil service careers, even if those opportunities were distributed unevenly. The sections that follow examine this ambiguity in the case of the outcast communities, the household registers, and the social effects of formal freedom.

Section 55.2

The Emancipation Edict of 1871

In October 1871 the Council of State issued a proclamation that abolished the designations eta and hinin, directing that people formerly bearing those labels be treated as commoners in both status and occupation. It is often called the Emancipation Edict (Kaihōrei) or, in the language of the period, an order abolishing the "base" designations. The reform came during a year of sweeping legal change, which had already included the abolition of domains and the creation of a national household register, and it fitted the logic of a government that wished to replace status corporations with a single legal population.

The people affected were numerous. Estimates of the size of the Tokugawa outcast groups vary, but they are commonly placed at several hundred thousand, roughly one percent of the population. Chapter 43 explained that eta and hinin were distinct and internally varied groups. Eta communities, including those called kawata or chōri in some regions, were associated with work such as leather processing, the disposal of dead animals, and, in many places, policing and punishment duties under local authorities. Hinin included beggars, entertainers, and others, and in some regions the category included people who had fallen into it by circumstance and could sometimes leave it. Neither group was uniform in wealth. Some eta communities, particularly in the Kansai region, controlled valuable leather production and built substantial economic bases, while others lived in poverty on marginal land. Their status was inherited and enforced through residential separation, dress regulations, and restrictions on marriage and social contact, and it was justified by notions of ritual pollution associated with death and with animal slaughter.

The practical effects of the edict were mixed. Legally, former outcasts gained access to the same occupations, residence rights, and marriage possibilities as other commoners. Some individuals used these opportunities to move into new trades, enter schools, or migrate. But the edict also removed customary monopolies and protections. Rights over the collection of dead animals and the leather trade, which had provided some communities with income, lost official backing, and in some places new commercial competitors, including those with greater capital, entered these trades. Many former outcast households had little or no farmland and were exposed to new taxes and to the costs of schooling and household registration without corresponding resources.

Equally important was the reaction of neighboring populations. The proclamation changed the law, but it did not change attitudes, and in some communities it provoked hostility. During the large rural uprisings of 1873, which were directed mainly at conscription, taxes, and school costs, rioters in parts of western Japan attacked outcast hamlets. In the Mimasaka region of Okayama, mobs burned dwellings and reportedly killed residents of former outcast communities, a pattern showing how popular anger at the new regime could be redirected toward groups that rioters associated with the government's reforms. Elsewhere discrimination operated more quietly, through refusals of marriage, exclusion from certain jobs, and segregated settlement that persisted for generations. The term Burakumin ("hamlet people") emerged later, and the later political history of the Buraku liberation movement, beginning with organizations of the early twentieth century, is taken up in Chapters 69 and 94. At the level of this chapter, the important lesson is that formal emancipation changed legal categories faster than social relationships, and that the same state that abolished the labels also built administrative systems that preserved information about ancestry.

Section 55.3

Household Registration, Social Classification, and Legal Identity

Before 1871, population records had been maintained through a patchwork of domain-level and temple-based registers, including registers that recorded religious affiliation for the anti-Christian inspection system. The Household Registration Law (Koseki-hō) of 1871, first implemented nationwide in 1872 in the register known by its zodiac year name as the Jinshin koseki, replaced that patchwork with a standardized system. Every household was recorded at a registered domicile (honseki), with its members listed by name, relationship to the head, age, and status classification. The register served several purposes at once. It made possible conscription, taxation, and school enrollment by identifying who lived where, it gave legal substance to the new status categories of kazoku, shizoku, and heimin, and it reinforced the principle that a person belonged to a household as well as to the state.

The register also recorded origin. Local officials in many places noted the former outcast backgrounds of residents, using terms such as shinheimin, "new commoner," or phrases noting a person as formerly eta or hinin. National policy was inconsistent about whether such notations were required, and practices varied by prefecture and over time, but the effect in many localities was to attach a recognizable mark to a household's entry. Because the register traced families by place of origin, a household's ancestry could be discovered long after the law had abolished the status. The Jinshin registers later became the subject of intense controversy in the twentieth century, when it became clear that people could use them to identify descendants of former outcast communities. Their restricted circulation and later legal controls on access to household registers form part of the long modern history of Buraku discrimination.

Historical Vocabulary
Shinheimin (新平民)

Shinheimin, literally "new commoners," was a label used in the early Meiji period, and in later popular speech, for people of former eta and hinin background. It conveyed that these people had been "added" to the commoner category after 1871 rather than belonging to it from the start. The word was never a neutral description. Applied in registers and in everyday conversation, it perpetuated the distinction the Emancipation Edict claimed to remove.

Students should distinguish the word from Burakumin, a later term associated with residential communities (buraku), and from the older labels eta and hinin, which are now widely regarded as discriminatory and which this textbook uses only when referring to the historical legal categories. Shinheimin matters to this chapter because it shows how a law could abolish a status in theory while the administrative language of registration, and the popular language that followed it, preserved the memory of ancestry as a social marker.

The Meiji registers thus embody an important paradox. They created the first nationwide legal framework in which every inhabitant of the main islands was recorded within a common system, and in that sense helped produce the unified population that reformers desired. At the same time, they made family lineage a legally recorded fact and rendered ancestry visible to officials, employers, and neighbors who wished to look for it. In the household system of the later Meiji Civil Code (Section 55.15), the register would also become the legal embodiment of the household (ie), listing the head of household and the persons under his authority. Registration was therefore both an instrument of national integration and a mechanism that carried older distinctions into the modern state.

Section 55.4

Freedom, Equality, and the Limits of Meiji Social Reform

The reforms of the early 1870s removed a long list of restrictions. Freedom to choose an occupation, to move between regions, to sell land, and to marry across former status lines was recognized in a series of ordinances from the late 1860s through the early 1870s. In 1872 the government also issued an order freeing people held in bonded servitude and, in particular, indentured prostitutes. The immediate trigger was the Maria Luz incident of 1872, in which a Peruvian ship carrying Chinese laborers put in at Yokohama and a Japanese court examined the conditions of those aboard. Peru's counsel pointed to the existence of contracts binding women into prostitution within Japan itself, and the government responded with an emancipation decree. The decree declared that such contracts were not enforceable, yet licensed prostitution continued under new regulations, with the system reorganized rather than abolished, and the episode shows both the power of international criticism and the limits of reform.

Formal freedom did not translate into equal opportunity. Former samurai were better placed to enter government offices, police forces, schools, and the military, while many lost their stipends and struggled in the new economy. Former outcast households could legally enter any trade, but customers, employers, and neighbors often refused to deal with them. Tenant farmers gained freedom of movement but remained dependent on landlords for land and credit. The land tax reform of 1873, which replaced rice payments with a cash tax calculated on land value, gave landowners clear title but increased the burden on small cultivators when harvests or prices fell. Poor households, in particular, found that the removal of old restrictions was accompanied by new obligations to pay money taxes, school fees, and, for sons, military service. The government's rhetoric of "rising in the world" (risshin shusse), which became closely associated with education, described real possibilities, but it also placed responsibility for success and failure on the individual.

Historians therefore distinguish between formal legal equality, which the reforms substantially advanced, and social equality, which they did not. The distinction matters because it helps explain why the same decade could produce both liberating legislation and popular protest. Villagers who rioted in 1873 were reacting to new burdens, not to the principle of reform as such, and their targets sometimes included schools, local officials, and, as noted above, communities associated with the changes. The next division turns to the reform that most directly connected the state to ordinary households: the school.

Division II — Building a National Education System · Section 55.5

Education Before the Meiji Reforms

The Meiji school system was built on a substantial and varied Tokugawa foundation. At the top of the educational hierarchy, the shogunate's academy at Shōheizaka in Edo, domain schools (hankō) in nearly every domain, and many private academies (shijuku) trained samurai in Confucian texts, history, writing, martial arts, and, increasingly in the nineteenth century, practical subjects such as medicine and Western learning. Some academies, such as those of Ogata Kōan in Osaka and Yoshida Shōin in Hagi, became centers of intellectual and political ferment (Chapters 50 and 52). In the Ryukyu Kingdom, a royal academy at Shuri and a Confucian temple complex at Kumemura provided instruction for the elite (Chapter 33).

For commoners, the most important institution was the terakoya, literally "temple school," though many were held in private homes and taught by priests, samurai, doctors, or literate villagers. Children learned to read and write, practiced calligraphy, memorized readers known as ōrai-mono that taught practical vocabulary and correct behavior, and learned basic arithmetic on the abacus. Instruction was often individualized rather than delivered to classes, and families paid in cash or goods. Girls could attend, though in smaller numbers, and were often taught sewing and household manners alongside reading, with moral guidebooks addressed to women, such as the Onna daigaku tradition, commonly used.

Scholars estimate that by the end of the Tokugawa period a large fraction of boys and a smaller fraction of girls had basic literacy, with figures often cited in the range of roughly forty to fifty percent for boys and ten to twenty percent for girls. These numbers are approximate and vary greatly by region and class: literacy was higher in commercial districts, among villagers who managed documents, and in the neighborhoods of major cities, and lower in remote or poor communities. Tokugawa education was therefore broad but uneven, decentralized, and shaped by status and gender. It was not state-controlled, and curricula varied. The Meiji reformers consequently faced two tasks: to extend schooling to those previously excluded and to replace a highly diverse system with a standardized national one. Many of the earliest elementary schools were established in former terakoya, temples, and private houses, and many teachers were former terakoya masters, so continuity with Tokugawa practice was stronger than the rhetoric of a new beginning suggested.

Section 55.6

The Education System Order of 1872

The Ministry of Education (Mombushō) was created in 1871, bringing the earlier fragmentary educational agencies under one central body. In September 1872 it promulgated the Education System Order, known as the Gakusei, which proposed a nationwide structure of schools. The system divided the country into eight university districts, each subdivided into thirty-two middle-school districts and each of those into 210 elementary-school districts. In principle this scheme would have produced 256 middle schools and more than fifty-three thousand elementary schools. The structure echoed French centralized models, while American practices and the advice of foreign teachers and consultants also influenced specific features. Officials such as Tanaka Fujimaro, who had studied school systems abroad during the Iwakura Mission, played an important role in its design.

The Gakusei was more a statement of aims than a description of a functioning system. The central government supplied limited funds and expected local communities to establish and finance schools through local levies and tuition. In practice, communities were asked to raise money for buildings, salaries, and supplies at the same time as they faced new taxes and conscription. Nonetheless, the pace of school founding was striking. Reported elementary schools numbered roughly twelve thousand in 1873 and more than twenty thousand by 1875. Many were improvised, housed in temples or private houses, staffed by former terakoya teachers or former samurai, and offering varying quality of instruction. Enrollment figures for the early 1870s are commonly given as around a quarter to a third of school-age children, with a large gap between boys and girls. Statistics from the period measure enrollment, not daily attendance, which was often much lower, and officials had incentives to report optimistic figures.

The Gakusei set out a multi-stage ladder from elementary schools through middle schools to universities, with elementary instruction divided into lower and upper levels. It also aimed at subject matter that reflected the era's emphasis on "practical learning" (jitsugaku): reading, writing, arithmetic, geography, natural science, and basic moral lessons, drawing on translated Western textbooks. Foreign influence was visible but selective. Japanese officials adopted the structures they found useful, translated what they could, and often adapted methods to local conditions. The ambition behind the Gakusei is best understood through its preamble, the prefatory notice that explained why the state wanted universal schooling.

It frames education as personal opportunity open across status lines and across gender, and it rejects the older prestige of purely literary scholarship. In that sense it expresses the same spirit as the reforms that opened occupations and ended status privileges. The document is also a statement by officials rather than evidence of what parents and children wanted or experienced. Because it is a policy statement, it cannot tell historians how many children attended school, how teachers taught, or how rural communities received the order. Section 55.7

Teachers, Classrooms, Textbooks, and the New Curriculum

A national system required teachers trained to deliver a common curriculum. The Tokyo Normal School, opened in 1872 with the guidance of an American instructor, Marion M. Scott, introduced class-based teaching, the use of blackboards and graded lessons, and "object lessons" influenced by Western pedagogy. Other normal schools followed in the 1870s, including a women's normal school in Tokyo opened in 1875. Graduates fanned out to local schools as models, but they were few compared with the demand. Most early elementary teachers had been trained in the Tokugawa system, including former samurai who turned to teaching after the loss of their stipends, former terakoya masters, and priests, with wide variation in quality. Salaries were low and often irregular, and communities that struggled to finance schools sometimes failed to pay them.

Instruction changed from individual lessons to simultaneous teaching of a class of children of similar age, with the school year divided into graded stages. The curriculum emphasized reading, writing, arithmetic, and moral lessons, supplemented by geography and elementary science. Textbooks in the early 1870s included translations and adaptations of Anglo-American readers and primers as well as works by Japanese authors, and the popular writings of Fukuzawa Yukichi circulated widely. Older ōrai-mono texts did not disappear overnight, and many schools continued to use them because they were familiar and cheap. In the 1880s the Ministry gradually moved toward screening and approving textbooks, a process that increased state control over content and reached a new stage after the 1886 ordinances. Moral education (shūshin), which in the 1870s had been one subject among others, rose in importance in the 1880s, a shift explained in Section 55.9.

Everyday Life and Environment

The Early Meiji School

For many children, the arrival of a Meiji school meant a change in the rhythm of daily life. Lessons were organized by the clock and the calendar rather than by the agricultural season or the individual pace of a terakoya student. The adoption of the Gregorian calendar in 1873, with its seven-day week and Sunday rest, altered the structure of the school week, though rural communities continued to follow agricultural and festival calendars for much of everyday life. Schools were often located in a converted temple hall or farmhouse, with limited furniture, and children frequently sat on the floor. Costs included tuition and, in some places, additional expenses for supplies, which could be heavy for poor families.

For families, sending a child to school meant giving up household labor, particularly at planting and harvest, and the burden fell most heavily on poor households and, in many places, on daughters, who often cared for younger siblings or worked in the household. Historians reconstruct these experiences from school registers, local petitions, memoirs, and later oral accounts, all of which have limitations: official registers measure what administrators recorded, and memoirs tend to be written by people who succeeded in school.

Section 55.8

Girls, Rural Children, Poverty, and Resistance to Schooling

The numbers show a sharp disparity. In the early 1870s, boys' enrollment was roughly two and a half times that of girls, with approximate national figures of around forty percent for boys and fifteen percent for girls. Over the following decades both rates rose, with boys reaching a majority of the age group by the 1880s and girls lagging far behind, so that girls' enrollment reached about a third of the age group by 1890 and approached that of boys only after the turn of the century. Overall enrollment in the age group is commonly estimated at around half by 1890 and above 80 percent by 1900, and near universal by the first decade of the twentieth century, though these figures reflect enrollment rather than regular attendance and are approximate.

Several factors produced these disparities. Tuition and associated costs were a real burden, particularly in rural areas where cash was scarce. Families depended on children's labor in farming, fishing, textile work, and the care of younger siblings. Gender expectations also mattered: many parents regarded a daughter's schooling as less valuable because she would marry into another household, a view reinforced by the curriculum in girls' schooling, which emphasized sewing and domestic skills. The government's rhetoric supported girls' education in principle, and in 1871 it had sent five young girls abroad with the Iwakura Mission, including Tsuda Umeko and Yamakawa Sutematsu, who later became prominent educators and advocates. But the system as a whole did not remove the practical obstacles facing most girls. Regional differences were large: urban areas and prosperous regions generally had higher enrollment than mountain villages, remote islands, and poor districts.

Resistance took several forms. Some was passive, expressed in non-attendance, delayed payment of fees, or petitions for relief. Some was active. During the large rural disturbances of 1873 in western Japan, rioters attacked school buildings among other symbols of the new regime, and local protest against school levies recurred in later decades. Such actions should not be dismissed as simple hostility to learning. Many communities valued literacy highly, as Tokugawa terakoya enrollment shows, but they objected to the cost, to central control, to the loss of their children's labor, and to the perception that schools served the government rather than local needs. Some communities responded by building their own schools and selecting their own teachers, an assertion of local agency that officials sometimes welcomed and sometimes tried to restrict. The outcome was a negotiation between state ambition and local circumstances, not a simple imposition.

Section 55.9

Education Reform from 1879 to 1886

By the late 1870s the Gakusei was widely regarded as unrealistic, expensive, and poorly suited to local conditions. In 1879 the government replaced it with the Education Order (Kyōikurei), drafted under the influence of American models. The new order devolved authority to local communities, allowed for more flexible school attendance requirements, set a minimum of sixteen months of attendance over several years, and created locally elected school committees. Its critics, including conservative officials and the Confucian tutor Motoda Nagazane, argued that it gave too much freedom, encouraged a shallow, individualistic education, and neglected moral training. Motoda's statement of the "Great Principles of Education" (Kyōgaku taishi, 1879), written in the emperor's name, urged that benevolence, righteousness, loyalty, and filial piety stand at the foundation of schooling. Itō Hirobumi and others responded with a defense of the openness of the new policy, and the exchange reveals a genuine disagreement within the government.

The result was a swift revision. The Revised Education Order of 1880 strengthened central supervision, tightened attendance requirements, and placed moral training (shūshin) first among school subjects. Further regulations in 1881 set guidelines for elementary school curricula and teacher conduct. These measures were not a return to the 1872 plan but a different approach, one that gave a larger role to prefectural authorities and to moral and ideological aims. The pattern shows why the educational sequence must be understood as distinct phases, with the 1872 order emphasizing expansion and practical learning, the 1879 order emphasizing local flexibility, and the 1880 revision and later measures emphasizing discipline and moral instruction.

The most important restructuring came under Mori Arinori, who became the first Minister of Education in the cabinet system created in 1885. In 1886 a series of ordinances established separate frameworks for imperial universities, normal schools, middle schools, and elementary schools. The elementary school ordinance created a four-year ordinary elementary school and a higher elementary school, with attendance in the lower level made obligatory, though tuition was still charged. The ordinances also began a system of government screening for textbooks. Mori placed particular emphasis on the training of teachers, whose normal schools were to cultivate qualities of obedience, mutual friendship, and dignity, and introduced military-style drill into the curriculum. Mori was also a man of complex views: he had advocated reforms in marriage practice, favored English education, and was later accused by opponents of disrespect toward tradition. He was assassinated on 11 February 1889, the day the Meiji Constitution was promulgated. Accounts of the assassin's motives commonly link the attack to rumors of an act of disrespect at Ise Shrine, though the details of the rumors are debated. The incident illustrates how closely educational policy, religion, and imperial reverence had become intertwined.

Taken together, the changes from 1879 to 1886 represent a shift from the language of individual advancement to the language of national purpose. The state retained the aim of expanding access, but it increasingly specified what children should learn, how teachers should conduct themselves, and which texts would be used. The 1890 Elementary School Order further emphasized moral education, and the Rescript on Education of the same year (Section 55.19) crowned the process. Before turning to that conclusion, the chapter examines the parallel transformation of religion, which proceeded through a different set of institutions but with similar underlying aims.

Division III — Religious Reform and the Emergence of State Shinto · Section 55.10

Shinbutsu Bunri: Separating Kami and Buddhism

The new government's first religious measures reflected the influence of nativist and imperial-restorationist thought. In 1868 the government revived an ancient-style Council of Rites (Jingikan) and, in a series of orders issued in the spring, directed that Buddhist priests who served at shrines return to lay life, that Buddhist titles such as gongen be removed from kami, and that Buddhist images no longer be treated as the embodiments of kami. The measures are collectively called shinbutsu bunri (or shinbutsu hanzen), the "separation of kami and buddhas." The government's stated goal was to purify shrine worship of Buddhist elements and to restore what its advisers regarded as the original character of Shinto, closely linked to the imperial line.

Historical Vocabulary
Shinbutsu bunri (神仏分離)

Shinbutsu bunri refers to the Meiji government's orders of 1868 and after to separate shrines and Buddhist temples, which had been closely intertwined for roughly a thousand years under the logic of shinbutsu shūgō, the combination of kami and buddhas. Shrines had often been administered by Buddhist clerics, temples contained shrines, and deities were frequently understood as manifestations of buddhas.

The term does not mean the abolition of Buddhism. The central orders targeted shrines and the relationship between priests and deities, and the government at times warned against violence. But the orders created a legal and social environment in which local actors could attack Buddhist institutions, as the next section explains. Students should avoid the common mistake of equating shinbutsu bunri with the later movement of destruction, haibutsu kishaku, which grew out of it but was not identical to it.

The effects on religious practice were far-reaching. Buddhist clerics who had served shrines were forced to choose between lay life and temple life, and many became shrine priests. Statues and ritual implements were removed from shrines, and in many places the shrine and temple compounds, once seamlessly joined, were physically divided. Sacred sites such as Kasuga Shrine in Nara, long associated with the Kōfukuji temple, saw their institutional connections severed. The government also abolished, in 1871 and thereafter, many of the hereditary privileges and administrative structures associated with the old system, including the hereditary succession of shrine priestly lines, a topic taken up below. The separation was effective in redefining official categories, but popular religion continued to blend kami and buddha worship in many households, festivals, and local practices, and the clean boundary imagined by officials was never fully realized.

The government also tried to replace the old temple-based registration system. In 1871 it ordered that residents register as parishioners (ujiko) of local shrines, an attempt to give shrines a role in civil record-keeping once held by temples. The experiment proved unworkable and was abandoned within a couple of years, as the civil household register took over the function. The episode shows how experimental and improvised early religious policy was: the government was making religious institutions into administrative instruments and discovering that existing practice resisted simple reorganization.

Section 55.11

Haibutsu Kishaku and the Buddhist Response

The decrees of 1868 provoked a wave of anti-Buddhist action known as haibutsu kishaku, literally "abolish the buddhas and destroy Shakyamuni." Its character varied enormously by region. In some places the response was limited to the removal of Buddhist objects from shrines. In others, local officials, nativist activists, and crowds destroyed temples, melted bells, burned images, and forced clergy to return to lay life. One of the most notable early incidents occurred at the Hie shrine at Sakamoto near Lake Biwa, where armed groups of shrine priests and supporters removed and destroyed Buddhist objects. The most extreme campaigns were conducted at the domain level. Satsuma, for example, closed on the order of its authorities an enormous number of temples, commonly cited as more than a thousand, and returned thousands of priests to lay life, while other domains such as Toyama drastically reduced temple numbers by consolidation. Regional variation was therefore great: some areas saw little destruction at all, while in others almost the entire Buddhist infrastructure was dismantled.

Government policy contributed to the damage in indirect ways. In early 1871 the government confiscated most of the lands held by temples and shrines, which had formerly been exempt from taxation and had provided stable income, and this measure impoverished many temples regardless of local attitudes. In 1872 the government lifted legal restrictions that prevented clergy from eating meat, marrying, and growing hair, a change that reflected the realities of some sects, such as Jōdo Shinshū, which already permitted marriage, but that also altered the social identity of Buddhist priests and removed a distinction between clergy and laity. In the same year it banned the independent practice of Shugendō, the mountain asceticism tradition, requiring its practitioners to affiliate with Tendai or Shingon. Together, these measures weakened Buddhist institutions that had formed the backbone of local religious and social life, from funerals and memorial services to education and charity.

The Buddhist response combined adaptation, petitioning, and reform. Leaders such as Shimaji Mokurai of the Nishi Hongwanji argued that religion and state should be distinct and that the state should not impose a single set of teachings, and he traveled to Europe in 1872 to observe religious institutions. Buddhists argued that their traditions supported the emperor and the nation and offered contributions to social order, education, and welfare. Sectarian leaders also pursued institutional reform, scholarly modernization, and overseas study; Nanjō Bunyū, for example, studied Sanskrit at Oxford, and later thinkers such as Inoue Enryō developed Buddhist philosophy in dialogue with Western thought. Buddhist institutions also participated in the colonization of Hokkaidō: the Higashi Hongwanji financed road building and other projects there in the early 1870s, tying a persecuted tradition to the state's expansion.

The destruction also had cultural consequences. Temple treasures were sold, dispersed, or exported, and many works of Buddhist art were threatened. The government began, in 1871, to issue regulations for the preservation of antiquities, and in the early 1870s officials surveyed temples for important objects. Later in the century, scholars such as Ernest Fenollosa and Okakura Tenshin, who surveyed Buddhist treasures in the Kansai region in the 1880s, helped to give Buddhist art new prestige as national heritage, and in 1897 the government passed a law for the preservation of old temples and shrines. By the 1890s Buddhism had survived and, in many respects, been redefined: it was no longer the quasi-administrative arm of the state, but it remained deeply embedded in funerals, ancestor rites, and community life.

Section 55.12

Shrines, Imperial Ritual, and the Religious Institutions of the New State

While dismantling the old arrangements, the government built a new relationship between the state and shrines. In May 1871 it declared that shrines were institutions of the state rather than private property, abolished hereditary succession among shrine priestly lines, and created a ranking system that placed shrines in a hierarchy, with Ise Shrine standing apart as the shrine of the imperial ancestral deity Amaterasu. The Meiji Emperor visited Ise in 1869, an event commonly described as the first imperial visit in many centuries. Ise also became the source of nationwide distribution of talismans (jingū taima), spread through the Great Promulgation Campaign. The shrine hierarchy tied local shrines into a national structure oriented toward the imperial house, and the court itself reorganized its ritual life in the early 1870s, reducing Buddhist elements in the palace and developing new ceremonies, including a national calendar of imperial rites and holidays such as the celebration of the legendary founding of the state by Emperor Jimmu (Kigensetsu) and the emperor's birthday, both instituted in 1873.

The relationship between shrines and the state was in flux. The Council of Rites was downgraded to a ministry in 1871 and absorbed into the Ministry of Doctrine (Kyōbushō) in 1872, a body that included both Shinto and Buddhist personnel, reflecting the government's pragmatic need to cooperate with Buddhist institutions. After the Ministry of Doctrine was abolished in 1877, shrine and temple affairs came under a bureau of the Home Ministry. Meanwhile shrines for the war dead, beginning with the Tokyo Shōkonsha established in 1869 and renamed Yasukuni Shrine in 1879, honored those who died fighting for the imperial side, a practice that tied ritual commemoration to the military and the throne. The institutional arrangements were thus experimental and changed repeatedly, and historians caution against assuming that a coherent system existed from the beginning.

Historical Vocabulary
State Shinto (国家神道, kokka Shintō)

State Shinto is a scholarly term for the system of government-sponsored shrine rites, imperial ritual, and associated ideology that developed from the Meiji period and was dismantled by the Allied occupation's Shinto Directive of December 1945 (Chapter 82). It is not the name of a single religion with a founding date. Historians generally distinguish between shrine ritual, treated by the government as a civic and national duty, and sectarian or denominational Shinto, which consisted of religious groups with their own founders, doctrines, and followers.

The term is debated. Some historians emphasize the continuity and coherence of an emperor-centered ritual and educational system that shaped national identity. Others stress that policies shifted repeatedly, that the system was assembled gradually, and that the label can obscure local variety. In this chapter, State Shinto refers to a developing arrangement, visible especially from the 1880s, in which shrine rites were separated from "religion" in official definitions so that participation could be treated as the duty of all subjects, regardless of personal belief.

The decisive conceptual move came in the early 1880s. In 1882 the government barred shrine priests from serving as religious instructors and from conducting funerals, and in 1884 it abolished the system of national doctrinal instructors that had been created in the 1870s. Shrine priests became officials responsible for ritual, while religious teaching, funerals, and organized proselytizing were left to sectarian groups. This distinction allowed the government to claim that shrine rites were not religion and therefore not subject to the principle of religious freedom, an argument that was developed more fully after the Meiji Constitution of 1889 guaranteed freedom of belief within limits. For Shinto groups that operated as organized denominations, a separate administrative framework developed, and by the end of the century a number of groups, including Kurozumikyō and others, were recognized as Shinto sects. The result was a dual structure: shrines understood as national institutions and denominational Shinto understood as private religion. How completely this arrangement was accepted by ordinary worshippers, who often continued to visit local shrines for festivals, blessings, and community events without regard to the official classification, remains a subject of historical debate.

Section 55.13

The Great Promulgation Campaign and Imperial Moral Instruction

In 1870 the government issued a proclamation, often called the Great Promulgation Edict, that launched a campaign to spread teachings centered on reverence for the kami and respect for the emperor. The effort became formally organized in 1872, when the Ministry of Doctrine adopted three principles of instruction: to revere the gods and love the country, to make clear the principles of heaven and the way of humanity, and to revere the emperor and obey the court. A corps of national instructors (kyōdōshoku) was recruited, drawing on Shinto priests, Buddhist clergy, and, according to scholarly accounts, even some popular entertainers and storytellers, and was organized in a ranked hierarchy. In 1873 the government established the Great Teaching Institute (Daikyōin) in Tokyo as a central training and preaching center, with branch institutes in the provinces.

The campaign met serious obstacles. Its doctrine was vague, its instructors were of widely varying background and training, and the effort to unify teaching under shared principles ran against the doctrinal commitments of the participating groups. Buddhists resented being asked to teach a doctrine centered on Shinto themes and being required to enshrine kami in their own training halls. In 1875, led by Shimaji Mokurai and the Jōdo Shinshū, the Buddhist sects withdrew from the Great Teaching Institute, and the institute was dissolved. A reorganized system of instruction continued for several more years, but the original design had failed. Historians read this failure as evidence that the government could not simply impose a unified religious doctrine from above, and as a stimulus to the later strategy of separating shrine ritual from religion and pursuing moral instruction through the school system instead.

The shift from preaching to schooling was gradual but unmistakable. The same concern for moral cohesion that animated the campaign shaped the revised education policies of 1880, the textbooks and ethics courses of the 1880s, and, ultimately, the Rescript of 1890. The campaign also left a practical legacy: it established networks of instructors, it normalized the idea that loyalty to the emperor was a subject for public teaching, and it demonstrated the difficulty of building a shared national ethic from competing traditions. The ideological work that religious preaching could not accomplish was now assigned to the classroom.

Section 55.14

Christianity, New Religions, and Religious Diversity

Christianity had been prohibited under the Tokugawa and, initially, under the Meiji government, which maintained public notice boards proscribing it. The most dramatic early episode occurred at Urakami near Nagasaki, where descendants of earlier Christians, who had maintained their faith in secret, revealed themselves to a French priest in 1865 and were arrested in subsequent years. After 1868 the government deported several thousand Urakami Christians, commonly given as roughly 3,400, to domains across western Japan, where they were subjected to forced labor and pressure to renounce their faith, and several hundred died. Western diplomats protested vigorously, and the issue came up during the Iwakura Mission's travels, where officials learned that the prohibition was a serious obstacle to treaty revision. In February 1873 the government removed the public notice boards, and the exiles were allowed to return. The change did not amount to formal religious toleration, and legal restrictions persisted in various forms, but it permitted missionary activity to expand, especially in the treaty ports and through schools.

Protestant, Catholic, and Orthodox communities grew in the following decades, drawing converts disproportionately from former samurai families and urban educated groups. Christian groups became associated with education and social reform. Examples include the Kumamoto and Sapporo "bands" of students in the 1870s, Niijima Jō's founding of Dōshisha in Kyoto in 1875, mission schools for girls, and the construction of the Russian Orthodox cathedral in Tokyo, completed in 1891. The Meiji Constitution of 1889 guaranteed freedom of religious belief to the extent consistent with peace and order and with the duties of subjects, a qualified guarantee that in practice left room for state pressure. Christians were sometimes accused of divided loyalties, as in the controversy of 1891 when the Christian teacher Uchimura Kanzō hesitated to bow before the signature on the Rescript on Education and was widely denounced, and in the philosopher Inoue Tetsujirō's argument of 1893 that Christianity conflicted with the national polity. In 1899 the Ministry of Education prohibited religious instruction in schools under its regulations, constraining mission schools that wished to receive official recognition.

The nineteenth century also saw the growth of new religious movements that arose outside established temple and shrine institutions. Tenrikyō, founded by Nakayama Miki in Yamato Province in 1838, attracted followers among farm households and women with its teachings of salvation and communal joy, and its founder was arrested several times by authorities who suspected it of unlicensed healing and unorthodox ritual. Konkōkyō, based on the revelations of Kawate Bunjirō beginning around 1859 in Bitchū Province, similarly drew a following among ordinary families. Both movements found it necessary to affiliate with recognized organizations in order to operate, and both later received official recognition as independent bodies, Konkōkyō in 1900 and Tenrikyō in 1908 (Chapter 64). Other groups, including Kurozumikyō, Fusōkyō, and Ontake-kyō, were recognized in the system of sectarian Shinto. The state's position toward these movements was ambivalent: it permitted some, harassed others, and expected all to accept the primacy of the emperor and the national ritual structure.

Religious life as practiced was more diverse than any of these official categories. Households continued to maintain Buddhist altars for ancestors, to visit shrines at festivals and life events, to make pilgrimages, and to consult healers and diviners, often without sensing contradiction. Official policy reshaped some practices, as when women's access to holy mountains from which they had been barred was legally opened in 1872, but it did not produce a single, tidy religious identity. The government's categories, which divided religion into approved doctrine, national ritual, and suspect superstition, shaped the terms in which religion was publicly discussed, while popular practice continued to cross those boundaries.

Division IV — Household Reform, Gender, and Regional Experiences · Section 55.15

Marriage, Household Authority, and the Modern Family System

The household (ie) was the basic unit of the Tokugawa social order, understood as a continuing corporate entity that held property, occupation, and status across generations and was headed by a male household head. Among samurai, the ie was typically patrilineal and organized around the transmission of rank and stipend to a single heir. Among commoners, practice varied considerably by region, class, and local custom, with some areas favoring inheritance by the eldest son, others by the youngest, and others accepting adopted heirs, including sons-in-law (mukoyōshi), as a normal strategy for continuity. Meiji law gradually moved toward a more uniform system modeled in large part on the samurai household, in which the household head held legal authority over members.

The Household Registration Law of 1871 gave the household a legal form by listing each household's head and members. Early Meiji legislation also changed some features of family life. Marriage across former status lines was permitted, and a measure of 1873 allowed wives to petition for divorce, a significant departure from the Tokugawa norm in which divorce was generally initiated by the husband. Women's names were treated differently from men's: an instruction of 1876 stated that wives should retain their natal family name in the register, a rule that sat uneasily with later ideas about unified household names. The Penal Code of 1870 had recognized concubines as a category of relatives, and later codes removed that recognition, with the Old Penal Code that took effect in 1882 abolishing it. Yet these changes were piecemeal, produced by directives and court rulings rather than by a comprehensive code, and they left considerable room for local custom.

The effort to produce a comprehensive civil code was long and contentious. The government engaged French legal adviser Gustave Boissonade, whose draft code, completed in 1890, was heavily influenced by French law. It provoked what became known as the Civil Code controversy (Minpō ronsō). Critics, including the jurist Hozumi Yatsuka, argued that the draft would undermine loyalty and filial piety and the authority of the household, and the Diet postponed its implementation in 1892. A new commission, which included Hozumi Nobushige, Tomii Masaakira, and Ume Kenjirō, drafted a code that retained elements of European legal structure but gave greater weight to household authority and Japanese custom as the drafters understood it. The property sections were promulgated in 1896, and the family and succession sections took effect in July 1898.

The Civil Code of 1898 formalized a household system in which each household had a head (koshu), usually male, who held authority over household members. The head's consent was required for marriage of members, he determined residence, and headship passed, in most cases, to the eldest son. A wife took her husband's household name and was subject to legal limitations on her capacity to manage property and enter contracts without his approval. Divorce by mutual agreement remained possible, but the code treated a wife's adultery as grounds for divorce while a husband's adultery counted only if he had been convicted. The code did not create the household system from nothing: it drew on samurai norms, earlier registration law, and regional custom, and it standardized and strengthened practices that had varied. It also reflected deliberate ideological choices, including the effort to link family authority to the emperor-centered state, a conception often described as the "family-state" ideology. Its provisions would remain in force, with later modifications, until the postwar Constitution of 1947 and the revised Civil Code of 1947 and thereafter (Chapter 84).

Section 55.16

Women, Family Life, and the Uneven Consequences of Reform

For women, the Meiji period brought both new opportunities and new restrictions. The expansion of schooling gave girls access to literacy that was previously available to a minority, and a few missionary and government schools offered advanced education. The government's decision in 1871 to send five girls abroad with the Iwakura Mission signaled a state interest in educated women, though its purpose was largely to prepare future mothers and teachers. Women entered new kinds of work, including teaching, nursing, and factory employment in textile mills (Chapters 56 and 63). The abolition of bonded servitude in 1872 offered some legal relief to women in the licensed quarters, though the quarters themselves persisted. The legal opening of divorce petitions to wives in 1873 and the opening of sacred mountains to women in 1872 reflected the willingness of reformers to adjust some inherited restrictions.

At the same time, state policy promoted a particular ideal of womanhood, expressed in the phrase ryōsai kenbo, "good wife, wise mother." The slogan, drawing on earlier ideas and on reformers' conceptions of the role of women in the nation, became prominent in educational policy by the late 1890s and was embodied in the 1899 order governing higher girls' schools. It defined women's primary role as household management and child-rearing, in service of the family and the nation. Education for girls emphasized domestic skills, sewing, and moral instruction, and curricula for girls and boys diverged beyond the early grades. Political rights were excluded: the Law on Assembly and Political Associations of 1890 barred women from joining political associations and attending political meetings, a restriction that targeted the growing involvement of women in the people's rights movement, where figures such as Kishida Toshiko had spoken publicly in the early 1880s. Such restrictions were extended in 1900 (Chapters 57 and 64).

Everyday experience diverged widely from the legal ideal. In rural households women's labor was essential to farming, silk production, and the maintenance of the household economy, and women exercised considerable practical authority within the household, particularly as they aged. Divorce rates in the early Meiji period were relatively high by later standards and declined as the household system and its social expectations strengthened. Samurai households tended to adopt strict patriarchal norms, while commoner practice, especially in fishing and farming communities, continued to reflect local customs. Differences by class, region, and generation meant that the "modern family system" was an ideal promoted by the state and elite reformers more than a uniform reality. Historians therefore speak of uneven consequences: legal change opened some doors, ideology and law closed others, and ordinary women negotiated between them.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Household Authority and Social Inequality Before and After the Reforms

What Changed

  • Legal status categories were simplified, and eta and hinin were abolished as legal designations in 1871
  • A national household register and, in 1898, a unified national family law replaced diverse local practice
  • Schooling became a state-organized institution, with the aim of reaching girls and rural children as well as boys of the elite
  • Shrines were reorganized as state institutions and Buddhist temples lost their administrative and landholding privileges

What Remained Continuous

  • Patriarchal authority within the household, now codified rather than diminished
  • Social prejudice against former outcast communities, preserved in registers and in marriage and employment practices
  • Inequalities of wealth and region, with landlords, urban merchants, and prosperous districts retaining advantages
  • Popular religious practice that mixed kami and buddha worship and resisted official categories

Who experienced change most directly: Former samurai, who lost formal privileges; children entering schools; former outcast households, who gained legal equality but not social acceptance; and Ainu and Okinawan communities, who encountered assimilation policy. Who experienced greater continuity: Rural women and poor families, for whom household authority and economic constraint changed little. Why the pattern matters: The same reforms that opened some opportunities also reinforced hierarchies of gender, ancestry, and wealth, which is why historians avoid describing the period as simple liberation or simple repression.

Section 55.17

Ainu Communities, Hokkaidō, and Assimilation Policies

The Ainu had long maintained their own political and economic life in Hokkaidō, Sakhalin, and the Kuril Islands, engaged in trade with Wajin (ethnic Japanese) merchants and the Matsumae domain, and in some periods in conflict with them (Chapter 48). Under the Tokugawa, the fishing-site system tied many Ainu to forced and often exploitative labor in Wajin-run fisheries. In 1869 the Meiji government renamed Ezo as Hokkaidō and established the Development Commission (Kaitakushi) to oversee settlement, resource extraction, and administration. Settlers, including former samurai, farmers, and military colonist-soldiers, arrived in increasing numbers, and the Wajin population of Hokkaidō grew from a few tens of thousands to over a million by around 1900, while the Ainu population, commonly estimated in the range of sixteen to eighteen thousand, became a small minority in their own homeland.

The state's policy toward the Ainu combined formal inclusion with systematic assimilation. In the household registers of 1871 and thereafter, the Ainu were incorporated as commoners but often recorded as "former natives" (kyūdojin), a label that marked them as distinct. Authorities assigned Japanese-style names, required use of the Japanese language, and banned customs such as the tattooing of women, men's earrings, and the traditional practice of burning a house after a death. Hunting and fishing practices on which Ainu communities depended were restricted by regulations, such as limits on deer hunting and on salmon fishing, in the name of resource management and development. Land policy proved especially consequential: regulations of 1872 and 1877 allowed the state to treat land without recognized private ownership as state land and to sell or grant it to settlers, in effect overriding Ainu use rights. The Treaty of Saint Petersburg of 1875, which gave Japan the Kuril Islands in exchange for Sakhalin, was followed by the removal of Ainu communities from Sakhalin and the Kurils to Hokkaidō. Roughly 840 Sakhalin Ainu were relocated to a site near Ishikari and later suffered heavily from epidemic disease, and small numbers of Kuril Ainu were moved to Shikotan.

Education was part of this assimilation. From the 1870s, authorities and missionaries set up schools for Ainu children, often separate from those for Wajin children, that emphasized Japanese language, ethics, and agricultural skills, in line with the government's view that education would "civilize" the Ainu. Individuals such as the Anglican missionary John Batchelor worked among Ainu communities and recorded their language and customs, though their aims and attitudes were shaped by their own religious and cultural assumptions. The Hokkaidō Former Aborigines Protection Act of 1899 summed up this approach. It allocated limited farmland to Ainu households who took up agriculture, subject to restrictions on its sale, and provided for schooling and relief, while formalizing a paternalistic and assimilationist framework. Ainu responses varied: some adapted to farming and schooling, some resisted, and some, in later generations, developed new forms of cultural activism that later chapters trace (Chapter 94). The Ainu should be understood as historical actors who negotiated, endured, and resisted policies that took their land and curtailed their practices, not simply as recipients of "development."

Section 55.18

Ryukyu, Okinawa, and the Limits of National Uniformity

The Ryukyu Kingdom had been a distinct state with its own court, language or languages, tributary relations with Ming and Qing China, and, after 1609, a subordinate and dual relationship with the Satsuma domain (Chapters 32 and 48). In 1872 the Meiji government declared the kingdom a domain and its king, Shō Tai, a peer, a unilateral change that Ryukyuan leaders did not accept. In 1879 government officials and soldiers removed the king from Shuri Castle and established Okinawa Prefecture, an act commonly called the Ryukyu Disposition (Ryūkyū shobun). Ryukyuan supporters of the kingdom petitioned and sent envoys to Qing China seeking assistance, and the status of the islands remained the subject of Sino-Japanese diplomacy through the 1880s. Within Okinawa, elites divided between those who resisted incorporation and those who accepted it, sometimes labeled the "obstinate" and "enlightened" factions.

The Meiji government's approach to Okinawa differed from its policy elsewhere. Rather than rapidly replacing local institutions, it adopted a policy often called the "preservation of old customs" (kyūkan onzon), retaining the old land and tax systems and traditional elites in the administration. This approach reflected concern about unrest and about the political costs of rapid change, and it meant that Okinawans did not receive the full set of institutions and rights extended to other prefectures; the old tax arrangements persisted into the early twentieth century, and Okinawa gained representation in the Diet only in 1912. Education became the primary instrument of integration. The prefecture established a conversation school for teaching standard Japanese in 1879 and a normal school in 1880, and elementary schools appeared in the following years. The Ryukyuan languages, which are distinct from Japanese though related to it, were treated by officials as dialects to be replaced by standard Japanese, and schooling in a language many children did not speak at home was a difficult experience.

Enrollment in Okinawa lagged behind the national average through the 1880s and 1890s, partly because of poverty, partly because of the distance and unfamiliarity of the new schools, and partly because many families and elites distrusted the new regime. Policies of cultural uniformity also reached customs: in 1899 prefectural authorities prohibited the traditional hand tattooing of women (hajichi), paralleling measures against Ainu tattooing in Hokkaidō. The experiences of the two regions were not identical. The Ainu were reclassified and colonized as an Indigenous population on land the state claimed, while the Ryukyu Kingdom was a recognized state whose absorption was contested diplomatically. Both, however, illustrate that the Meiji ideal of a uniform national population coexisted with differentiated treatment. Later chapters follow Okinawa's history through annexation's later effects, the Battle of Okinawa, American administration, and contemporary politics (Chapters 80, 90, and 99).

Division V — Education, Imperial Loyalty, and the New National Identity · Section 55.19

The Imperial Rescript on Education of 1890 and the Making of Meiji Subjects

On 30 October 1890 the Meiji Emperor issued the Imperial Rescript on Education (Kyōiku ni kansuru chokugo), a short statement of moral principles that became the central text of Japanese schooling until 1945. It was drafted with the participation of Inoue Kowashi and Motoda Nagazane, after an earlier draft by Nakamura Masanao, a Confucian scholar who had converted to Christianity, was set aside. Inoue sought a text that would avoid sectarian religious language and open political argument, and the Rescript was issued as the emperor's own statement rather than as a law countersigned by a minister. The cabinet at the time was led by Yamagata Aritomo, and the document preceded the opening of the first Imperial Diet by about a month (Chapter 59).

In content, the Rescript presented the imperial line and the loyalty and filial devotion of the people as the foundation of the national polity (kokutai) and of education. It then listed virtues for subjects: filial piety toward parents, affection among siblings, harmony between husband and wife, trust among friends, modesty and moderation, benevolence toward others, the pursuit of learning and the cultivation of skills, the development of intellect and moral character, the advancement of public good and service, and respect for the constitution and the laws. It concluded that, should an emergency arise, subjects should offer themselves courageously to the state and thereby support the imperial throne. The text was brief, formal, and drawn largely from Confucian vocabulary, but it joined that vocabulary to the framework of the modern state and the constitution. The word it used for the people was shinmin, subjects.

The Rescript's significance lay as much in its use as in its words. Copies were distributed to schools, and in 1891 the Ministry of Education issued regulations for ceremonies on national holidays at which teachers and students gathered, bowed before the emperor's portrait, heard the Rescript read aloud, and sang designated songs; by 1893 the Ministry had designated songs, including "Kimigayo," for these occasions. Teachers were expected to model the virtues it named, and moral education textbooks were organized around its themes. The ritual elements made school a site of national participation, in which the experience of standing, bowing, and listening to the emperor's words formed part of every child's education. Commentaries by scholars such as Inoue Tetsujirō explained its meaning and ties to the national polity, and the controversy over Uchimura Kanzō's hesitation in 1891 showed that the text could be used to test loyalty. The Rescript can thus be read as the culmination of the shift visible since the late 1870s, in which moral and national purpose came to occupy the center of education.

The Rescript did not erase the tensions in the system. Education continued to offer practical skills and avenues of advancement, and students and teachers interpreted and used the text in different ways. Some teachers, parents, and local communities embraced its moral language, some treated the ceremonies as formalities, and some Christian and other religious groups worried about the conflict between the reverence demanded by the ceremonies and their consciences. Nor did it create a uniform population, as the experiences of the Ainu, Okinawans, and former outcast communities show. What the Rescript did provide was a compact statement that tied schooling, family morality, loyalty to the emperor, and readiness for sacrifice into a single frame, available for use by later governments.

Cause and Effect

How Educational and Religious Reforms Helped Consolidate the Modern State

Long-term causes

Tokugawa literacy and school networks; nativist and Confucian traditions that tied loyalty and ritual to rulership; the weakening of the status order; and the pressure of Western expansion and the unequal treaties.

Short-term causes

The abolition of domains and warrior privilege in 1871; the need for taxpayers and conscripts; observations by the Iwakura Mission; the failure of the Great Promulgation Campaign; and fears about social unrest and the people's rights movement.

Event or transformation

The creation of a national school system, the reorganization of shrines and Buddhism, the household registers and Civil Code, and the Rescript on Education, together producing an emperor-centered framework of loyalty.

Immediate effects

Growing enrollment, local protest, the weakening of Buddhist institutions, new shrine hierarchies, legal equality alongside persistent discrimination, and increased state supervision of moral instruction.

Long-term consequences

A school system able to reach most children by 1900, a shared ritual calendar, a codified household order, and ideological tools that later governments used for mobilization, as later chapters on empire and total war show.

Historical complexity

Reforms arose from contest among officials, educators, religious leaders, and local communities, and their outcomes depended on regional conditions, money, and resistance. No single policy or leader created the system.
Historical Debate

Was Meiji Educational Reform Social Empowerment, State-Building, or Both?

The question: Historians have long debated whether the Meiji school system chiefly expanded opportunity for ordinary people or chiefly served to build loyalty and obedience to the state.

Interpretation A — Empowerment and modernization: Some scholars emphasize that the Meiji system built on Tokugawa literacy, opened learning across status lines, offered paths of advancement through merit, and supplied skills for a new economy. On this view, schooling widened opportunity, encouraged a culture of self-improvement associated with writers such as Fukuzawa Yukichi, and contributed to economic growth and social mobility.
Interpretation B — State-building and indoctrination: Others, including critics of prewar education, emphasize the growing centralization, textbook control, moral instruction, and school ritual that culminated in the Rescript. On this view, the system was designed to shape obedient subjects and to prepare the population for taxation, conscription, and loyalty to the emperor, and its egalitarian features were constrained by gender, class, and region.
Interpretation C — Negotiated and combined effects: A third group stresses that both processes occurred together and that outcomes were shaped by local communities, teachers, and families, who sought literacy on their own terms and sometimes resisted state aims. Work on the construction of Meiji ideology has highlighted the role of many actors, not only central officials.

Evidence: The interpretations draw on the same body of evidence, including school statistics, the Gakusei preamble, the 1879 and 1880 orders, the 1886 ordinances, the Rescript and its ceremonies, local petitions and protests, and memoirs. They differ in weighting opportunity against supervision.

Why it matters: How historians answer the question affects how they understand the origins of mass literacy, the social base of later political movements, and the role of schooling in mobilization for war.

Section 55.20

Chapter Review and Historical Thinking Checkpoint

This chapter has examined how the Meiji government and many other actors tried to change the people who lived under the new institutions created after 1868. Before proceeding, students should be able to demonstrate understanding of these core insights. The government sought to replace the Tokugawa status order with a population of imperial subjects, but it did so unevenly: legal emancipation in 1871 did not end discrimination, and household registers carried ancestry forward. The school system announced in 1872 was an ambitious statement that took decades to approach universal attendance, and its policy changed in distinct phases in 1872, 1879, 1880, and 1886. Religious reform separated kami and buddhas, devastated many Buddhist institutions, and, after the failure of the Great Promulgation Campaign, gradually produced the arrangement scholars call State Shinto. Household law culminated in the Civil Code of 1898, and policies toward Ainu communities and Okinawans show that the supposedly uniform nation was administered through differentiated and often coercive means. The Rescript of 1890 gathered these strands into a compact moral and ceremonial form.

Chapter Summary

Chapter 55 has argued that the Meiji government did more than create new political and military institutions. It tried to redefine social status, schooling, religion, family obligation, and the relationship between individuals and an emperor-centered state. The chapter's governing distinction is between ambition and accomplishment. Many reforms changed legal categories and official language quickly, while changes in social practice, belief, and everyday life were slower, partial, and uneven.

The first division showed how the government replaced the Tokugawa status order with a simplified scheme of kazoku, shizoku, and heimin and a national household register. The Emancipation Edict of 1871 abolished eta and hinin as legal designations, yet communities of former outcasts lost customary monopolies, were sometimes marked in registers as new commoners, and faced violence and discrimination. The reforms advanced formal legal equality without achieving social equality, and the registers they created preserved ancestry as an administrative fact.

The second division traced the construction of a national education system. Building on Tokugawa terakoya and domain schools, the Education System Order of 1872 announced an ambitious scheme of universal schooling and encouraged practical learning. In practice the system relied on local funds, produced improvised schools, and encountered resistance driven by cost and labor needs. The policy shifted in distinct phases: local flexibility in 1879, stronger supervision and a priority for moral training in 1880, and the comprehensive structure of 1886 under Mori Arinori. Girls and rural children were less likely to attend, and the curriculum increasingly stressed discipline and national purpose.

The third division examined religion. The separation of kami and buddhas in 1868 and the anti-Buddhist movement it encouraged damaged many temples and altered long-standing practice, with sharp regional variation. Government confiscation of temple and shrine lands reshaped religious finance, and the failure of the Great Promulgation Campaign led officials to move toward school-based moral instruction and to separate shrine ritual from religious teaching. State Shinto thus appeared as a gradually assembled arrangement rather than a system complete in 1868. Christianity moved from persecution to a limited and suspect toleration, and new religions such as Tenrikyō and Konkōkyō grew on the margins of official categories.

The fourth division turned to the household and to regional experience. Meiji law moved from piecemeal reforms toward the Civil Code of 1898, which standardized a household system giving the male head broad authority and subordinating wives, drawing on samurai norms and resolving a contested legal debate. Women gained schooling and some new freedoms but were bound by an ideology of good wives and wise mothers and excluded from political life. Ainu communities experienced colonization, land loss, resettlement, bans on customs, and assimilationist schooling, while the Ryukyu Kingdom was annexed in 1879 and its people were integrated through language-centered education and a policy of retaining old customs.

The fifth division showed how the Rescript on Education of 1890 consolidated these strands. It tied loyalty and filial piety to the imperial polity, presented a list of virtues for subjects, and, through school ceremonies, made national participation part of every child's routine. It did not create uniformity, since the experiences of Ainu, Okinawans, former outcast communities, women, and Christians show gaps between ideal and reality, but it supplied a compact ideological frame.

Throughout, the term "new citizen" has required qualification. The population addressed by the state was one of subjects, with rights defined by the state and obligations tied to the emperor. Yet opportunity was real for many: literacy spread, occupations opened, and new forms of association emerged, a point that explains why historians debate whether Meiji education should be read mainly as empowerment, state-building, or both.

These developments were shaped by many actors. Officials such as Mori Arinori and Tanaka Fujimaro, thinkers such as Fukuzawa Yukichi and Motoda Nagazane, religious leaders such as Shimaji Mokurai, founders of new movements, teachers, parents, and local communities all influenced outcomes. Reforms were contested within the government, adapted by local authorities, and negotiated or resisted by ordinary people.

The chapter's findings are foundational for what follows. The economic transformation of Chapter 56 depended on a population increasingly literate and legally mobile, the political movements of Chapter 57 drew on the participants' schooling and status changes, and the constitutional order of Chapter 59 rested on the framework of imperial loyalty developed here. Later chapters on empire, mass culture, and total war return to the school, the shrine, and the household as institutions that governments used to shape and mobilize the population.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows what happens after a political revolution. Dismantling domains and warrior privilege, as Chapter 54 described, left open the question of what kind of people would live in the new state. The answers explored here, through status law, schools, shrines, and the household, reveal that state-building in Meiji Japan was as much about shaping everyday life and belief as about institutions of government.

The chapter also reveals how unevenly reforms land. Legal emancipation, mass schooling, and religious reorganization opened some doors and closed others. They gave many children literacy, and they bound families and women into an emperor-centered household order. They gave Buddhist and Shinto institutions new formal positions, and they inflicted loss on many temples. They offered the Ainu and the people of Okinawa schooling and legal inclusion, and they did so on terms of assimilation and unequal rights. Recognizing these contradictory effects protects students from both celebratory and purely condemnatory narratives.

Many of the developments here would shape later history. The school ritual and moral framework of the Rescript became tools of mobilization in later wars, the household law remained in force until the postwar reforms, the arrangement of shrine and state persisted until 1945, and the legacies of discrimination against Burakumin, Ainu, and Okinawans continued to generate activism and debate into the present. At the same time, the chapter shows people acting within and against the system: communities building schools, Buddhist leaders contesting doctrine, Christians and founders of new religions asserting belief, and women and families adapting.

Finally, the chapter connects the archipelago's regions and its relations with the wider world. Reformers drew on French, American, German, and other models, while foreign pressure on questions such as Christianity and the treatment of bonded labor shaped policy. Hokkaidō and Okinawa were pulled into the national project on different terms from the main islands, showing that the "new citizen" was never a single figure. The next chapter turns to the economic engine of the new state, in which the schooled, mobile population of subjects became workers in railways, mills, mines, and the emerging great business groups.
