Part VII — Tokugawa Japan
44

Villages, Agriculture, Taxation, Famine, and Popular Protest

Japanese History: From Jōmon Japan to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

A Tokugawa village was not simply a cluster of farmhouses. It was a body that kept accounts, wrote rules, shared out taxes, repaired canals, represented its members before officials, and, when pushed far enough, could draft a petition, choose delegates, and walk to a lord’s castle town or even to Edo.

Chapter 43 asked who rural people were within Tokugawa society: how the household (ie) was organized, how status categories such as honbyakushō (registered landholding cultivators) and tenants worked, and why the familiar “four-class” picture is misleading. This chapter asks a different question. What did rural communities do collectively? How did they govern themselves, produce and allocate food, meet the demands of shogunal and domain authorities, survive disasters, and respond when they believed those demands had become unjust?

The central argument of the chapter can be stated briefly. Tokugawa villages were neither powerless collections of peasants nor independent democratic communities. They were corporate local communities with real powers of self-government, and they were also the administrative and fiscal units through which the bakufu and the domains ruled the countryside. Authority over taxation, justice, and land flowed through villages, and so did the capacity to resist. This tension—autonomy inside subordination—runs through every section that follows.

The countryside was also changing. Over the seventeenth century, cultivated land expanded through reclamation, irrigation, and drainage; later, growth shifted toward intensification, with better seed, double cropping where climate allowed, heavier labor inputs, and purchased fertilizers that tied farms to distant fisheries. Rural households grew cotton, rapeseed, indigo, tea, mulberry, and tobacco as well as rice and grain. Commercialization created prosperous farm families and also new dependence on prices, credit, and distant supplies. Rural society was not a timeless agrarian backdrop to samurai government; it was a dynamic economy with its own inequalities.

That dynamism did not protect people from catastrophe. The Kyōhō famine of 1732, the Tenmei famine of the 1780s, and the Tenpō famines of the 1830s killed large numbers of people and exposed regional and social differences in vulnerability. These crises were not simply weather events. Weather produced harvest shocks, but prices, storage, transport, taxation, official decisions, household reserves, and disease helped determine whether a bad harvest became mass death. Famine is therefore treated here as a compound social and environmental process.

The chapter ends with politics from below. Villagers petitioned, negotiated, fled collectively, held mass demonstrations, and sometimes rose in ikki (collective uprisings). The most famous episode, the Gujō or Hōreki disturbance of 1754–1758, shows how taxation, village organization, procedural appeals, and elite politics could intersect. Historians increasingly treat such actions as organized political claims about legitimate government rather than as spasms of desperation. At the same time, most protesters sought relief, fair administration, and the defense of customary rights. They rarely sought to abolish the Tokugawa order.

Historians reconstruct this world from village records preserved in headmen’s households: land registers, tax assessment and receipt documents, village regulations, petitions, boundary agreements, diaries, and agricultural manuals, along with domain and bakufu legal records, trial proceedings, and chronicles written by literate observers. Almost all of these sources were created by village elites or officials, which means the experience of tenants, servants, and many women must often be read between the lines. The chapter sits between Chapter 43 (status and household) and Chapter 45 (roads, markets, and cities); its subject is production and extraction in the countryside, not yet circulation and urban consumption.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why the Tokugawa village is best understood as both a self-governing community and an administrative unit of lordly rule.
  2. Describe the principal village offices, assemblies, and five-household groups, noting regional and domain variation.
  3. Distinguish kokudaka as an assessed productive value from an actual harvest count.
  4. Analyze how nengu was assessed and collected through the principle of murauke, and compare kemmi and jōmen assessment.
  5. Assess how the burden of taxation and village obligations fell unequally on different rural households.
  6. Trace the shift from reclamation-driven expansion to agricultural intensification and commercial cropping.
  7. Evaluate the role of water, forests, and commons in cooperation and conflict between villages.
  8. Explain famine as an interaction of environmental shock, agricultural system, markets, taxation, relief policy, and disease, using the Kyōhō, Tenmei, and Tenpō crises.
  9. Compare petition, collective flight, mass appeal, and uprising as forms of rural political action.
  10. Interpret the Gujō disturbance as a case study in procedure, organization, division, and consequence.
  11. Evaluate what rural protest could and could not accomplish without projecting modern democratic aims onto Tokugawa villagers.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How could the same institution, the village, function both as a self-governing community and as a tool of lordly administration?
  2. Why did Tokugawa authorities govern the countryside through villages and small-group responsibility rather than through a large professional bureaucracy?
  3. How did the system of assessment and collection shape the relationship between rulers and villagers, and why did changes in assessment methods provoke conflict?
  4. Who benefited from agricultural expansion, intensification, and commercialization, and who became more vulnerable?
  5. How did conflicts over water, forests, and commons reveal the limits of village solidarity?
  6. Why is famine better explained by social and institutional conditions than by weather alone?
  7. How did villagers decide among negotiation, petition, flight, and uprising, and what did each tactic assume about rulers?
  8. In what sense was rural protest “political” even when it sought only to restore customary or benevolent government?

Historical Context

Three earlier developments set the stage. First, Chapter 40 explained how Toyotomi Hideyoshi’s land surveys measured fields, recorded cultivators, and converted productive capacity into rice-denominated values, separating warriors from the land and tying cultivators to registered holdings. The Tokugawa inherited that cadastral framework, and this chapter asks how it worked in everyday rural administration rather than how it was created. Second, Chapter 42 described the bakuhan system: the shogun controlled bakufu direct lands (bakuryō/goryō, later commonly called tenryō) directly through intendants (daikan), while some 250 domains (han) governed their own territories under daimyō. A village therefore stood inside either a bakufu district, a domain, or the holding of a bannerman (hatamoto), and the rules varied accordingly.

Third, Chapter 43 established that rural society was layered: registered landholders, tenants and dependent cultivators, servants, village officials, and specialized or marginal groups. Some “peasants” were also fishers, foresters, wage laborers, or small manufacturers. Many regional differences cannot be summarized in a single national model: a rice-irrigation village in the Kinai basin, a mountain village in Mino or Shinano, a cold northern village in Tōhoku, and an island community in the south faced different environments and different lords. This chapter therefore uses examples cautiously and flags variation wherever it matters.

The chapter also needs a long view of the economy. During the seventeenth century, the combined effects of peace, reclamation, and administrative stability coincided with substantial population growth; estimates differ, but the archipelago’s population probably more than doubled across the century before leveling off in the eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries at roughly 26–33 million. Plateaus did not mean stagnation, since regional trajectories diverged and output per hectare kept rising in places. The pressures of a larger population on a land base that could no longer expand easily shaped the intensification, the commercial crops, and the vulnerabilities described below. Ryukyu and Ezo (the Ainu homeland) each had distinct rural and resource economies tied to Satsuma and Matsumae respectively; they appear here where they intersect with the countryside’s fertilizer and sugar economies, and are treated fully in Chapter 48.

Section 44.1

From Household to Village: The Rural Community as Government

Chapter 43 ended with the household as the basic unit of rural life. A household, however, could not irrigate a valley, defend a boundary, or settle a tax bill alone. Between the household and the lord stood the village (mura), the institution that coordinated hundreds of households’ labor, shared its obligations, and spoke for it before outside authority. In a society without a large salaried rural bureaucracy, the village was one of the most important intermediary institutions of the Tokugawa political order.

Rulers had good reasons to rely on it. After the early seventeenth century, samurai were concentrated in castle towns and had no resident presence in most villages. A bakufu intendant might oversee a district of many villages with a small staff, and domain officials were similarly stretched. Governing through existing communities, with their headmen, internal rules, and neighbors responsible for one another, was efficient. It also gave villagers leverage, because rulers depended on their cooperation to collect revenue and keep order.

The sections that follow therefore treat the village as an institution with a dual character. It had powers of its own and also performed functions for others. Neither aspect makes sense without the other.

Section 44.2

What Was a Tokugawa Village? Community and Administrative Unit

The Tokugawa village had two faces. As a community, it was a historically rooted settlement or group of settlements with shared fields, water, forests, shrines, temples, festivals, and customary rules, many of them centuries old. Its inhabitants knew one another, intermarried, and cooperated in work. As an administrative unit, it was recognized by rulers as the entity that was assessed for taxes, registered for land and population, and answerable for public order. The boundaries of these two things did not always match. The administrative village (mura) was sometimes a composite of several hamlets, and in other places a single settlement was split among different lords.

This dual character excludes two caricatures. One is the “free peasant republic,” in which villagers governed themselves and merely tolerated distant samurai. The other is the “helpless village,” in which officials dictated every detail of rural life. Neither fits the evidence. Villages could write binding regulations, keep their own accounts, raise internal levies, and negotiate with officials. Yet they could not refuse assessment, ignore a lord’s orders, or deny that they were subordinate to lordly justice. Rulers expected villages to manage themselves because that made them governable.

Villages also differed widely. Population ranged from a few dozen households to several hundred; some were compact rice-growing nucleated settlements, others scattered mountain hamlets or fishing communities. Domain policy, bakufu practice, and local custom produced varied arrangements, so any account of “the” village is a model, not a rule.

Section 44.3

Village Officials: Nanushi, Shōya, Kimoiri, Kumigashira, and Hyakushōdai

The chief village official was the headman, called nanushi in much of the Kantō and eastern regions, shōya in much of western Japan, and kimoiri in some areas and domains. Assistants and counselors had titles that varied still more. A common pattern included a deputy or group of elders (often called kumigashira or similar), and a representative of ordinary landholders, the hyakushōdai, who monitored the accounts and checked headman power. Readers should not imagine a uniform three-office constitution applied in every village.

The headman’s responsibilities were broad. He received and announced orders, compiled land and population records, assessed the apportionment of dues within the village, delivered taxes, represented the community in legal proceedings, helped resolve disputes, and reported incidents to higher officials. He was, in effect, a villager acting as an agent of lordly government. In some places the office was hereditary within a leading family; in others, it rotated or was elected by landholders. Hereditary headmen often had greater wealth, literacy, and kin ties, which made them influential but also a target of grievance.

That ambiguity matters. A headman could be a shield for the village, a collaborator with the lord, or both, and villagers sometimes petitioned against headmen suspected of padding accounts or favoring relatives. The dispute over whether the village’s accounts were open to ordinary landholders was among the most common internal political issues in the Tokugawa countryside.

Section 44.4

Assemblies, Village Rules, and Local Self-Government

Important village decisions were typically made at meetings (yoriai) of landholding households, often held at a temple, shrine, or the headman’s house. Meetings decided how to share the tax load, scheduled irrigation and repair work, set the opening date for common grass cutting, approved village regulations, and settled disputes among households. Written rules (mura okite or village regulations) survive in large numbers. They prescribed fines for pilfering crops, set wages for communal work, regulated the use of forest and water, forbade gambling and extravagance, and spelled out responsibility for roads and bridges.

Villages also kept their own budgets. Alongside the dues imposed from above, they levied internal charges (mura nyūyō or village expenses) for travel to the intendant’s office, legal costs, festivals, repair work, and hospitality for visiting officials. These charges were often a contentious subject, because they could be inflated, and because who paid how much revealed the village’s internal hierarchy. Villagers who sought to audit these accounts left some of the richest documentation we have.

But autonomy did not mean equality. Established landholders dominated meetings and offices. Servants, tenants, land-poor households, and outsiders typically had little formal voice, and women, though active as workers and sometimes as household heads, rarely sat at meetings. Village self-government was thus a form of corporate self-rule by the propertied. It protected the community against outside interference and shielded elites from direct control by lords, but it also let elites decide how burdens were shared among those below them.

Section 44.5

Five-Household Groups and Collective Responsibility

Below the village level, many communities were organized into neighborhood units conventionally called gonin-gumi (five-household groups), although the actual number of households varied and the institution’s form differed across regions and domains. These groups served several purposes: mutual surveillance against crime and forbidden religion, surety for members’ obligations, cooperation in work and emergencies, and the circulation of official notices. Members could be held collectively liable if a neighbor absconded, committed a crime, or defaulted on dues. The relationship between five-household groups and village officials such as the kumigashira also varied.

The system reflected a broader principle: the Tokugawa order governed through small-group responsibility instead of a large professional state apparatus. Obligations were assigned to groups; groups policed themselves. That economy of administration made rural government cheap, but it also bound neighbors in relations of mutual obligation and mutual suspicion. Collective responsibility could be a source of security when a household fell ill or a harvest failed, and a source of coercion when neighbors were compelled to inform on one another.

Historical Debate

Historical Debate: How Autonomous Was the Tokugawa Village?

The question: Was the Tokugawa village primarily an instrument by which lords extracted resources and imposed order, or primarily a self-governing community with its own logic and interests?

Interpretation A — The village as an instrument of extraction: Older scholarship, especially work influenced by postwar Marxist history, emphasized that lords designed village institutions to collect taxes and control the cultivators. Collective responsibility, registration, and village officers were tools of rule that bound peasants to the land and restricted their mobility.
Interpretation B — The village as a corporate self-governing community: Later research has stressed that villages had strong pre-existing traditions of cooperation, managed common resources, wrote their own rules, and bargained with rulers. Village autonomy was a precondition of Tokugawa government, not merely an accident of weak administration.

Evidence: Both sides draw on village documents, such as regulations, accounts, petitions, and tax-receipt records, together with legal records and administrative orders. The evidence permits both readings because the same document may reflect lordly demands and local initiative.

Why it matters: If villages were chiefly instruments, popular protest appears as resistance to an imposed system. If they were self-governing communities, protest looks more like a conflict over the terms of a partnership. Most current scholarship combines the two: autonomy and extraction were simultaneous and mutually dependent.

Student task: Choose one village function discussed in Sections 44.3–44.5 and explain how it can be read as both an instrument of lordly rule and an expression of village autonomy.

Section 44.6

Land Surveys, Registers, and Kokudaka

The fiscal framework of Tokugawa rural governance rested on the survey tradition described in Chapter 40. By the seventeenth century, village fields had been measured, graded by quality, assigned an estimated productivity, and recorded by cultivator in cadastral registers (kenchichō and related records). Each village’s total assessed productivity was expressed as its murataka or kokudaka, a figure in koku of rice (one koku is roughly 180 liters, or about the amount of rice thought to feed a person for a year). Lords’ holdings, daimyō ranks, and military obligations were also expressed in kokudaka.

Historical Vocabulary
Historical Vocabulary: Kokudaka

Kokudaka was an assessed productive value, expressed in rice-equivalent koku, assigned to a field, village, or domain. It was a fiscal and political yardstick, not a literal annual count of rice harvested. Fields producing barley, cotton, or vegetables were converted into rice equivalents, and values were revised only occasionally, so as yields rose through reclamation or improved methods, actual output often outran the assessed figure.

The term matters because it embedded political rank, military duty, and taxation in a single rice-based vocabulary. A modern reader who treats a 38,000-koku domain as simply a domain that “harvested 38,000 koku” will misunderstand both agriculture and government.

Registers recorded who held and cultivated each plot. They were compiled with the participation of village officials and became legal evidence in disputes. Because the surveys were not constantly repeated, the gap between registered values and actual productivity could grow over time, a source of unplanned relief for cultivators in some regions and a motive for lords to seek new assessments. Hence arguments over measurement and re-measurement were among the most politically charged subjects in the Tokugawa countryside.

Section 44.7

Nengu and Murauke: Taxation Through the Village

The main land tax was called nengu (annual tribute). Its core principle is often summarized as murauke, “village contracting”: authorities assessed obligations on the village as a whole, and the village, through its officials and landholders, apportioned the burden among households and delivered the proceeds. Officials rarely collected from individual households. The village’s collective responsibility for the total meant that its internal arrangements, who paid how much, who covered shortfalls, who was excused, were left largely to local decision.

Historical Vocabulary
Historical Vocabulary: Murauke

Murauke (village-level undertaking of tax responsibility) refers to the practice by which a lord assessed taxes on the village collectively and the village was responsible for fulfilling the total. It is not a formal law with a single date but a historically developed practice that varied by region.

It matters because it explains why headmen had such pivotal roles, why villages kept elaborate accounts, and why internal arrangements could provoke conflict. It also warns against imagining an individual-peasant-versus-lord relationship: the institutional counterpart of the lord was the community.

Nengu was not the only obligation. Alongside the principal land tax, villages owed kōmononari (miscellaneous levies on secondary products and trades), service levies including labor for transport and maintenance (kuniyaku, sukegō, tenma, and similar obligations, with names and burdens varying), and in some areas cash payments. Payment might be in rice, cash, goods, or labor, depending on the region and period. Reclaimed land or commercial crops could be treated differently from registered paddy.

The old textbook formula that peasants surrendered 40 or 50 percent of their rice (the “four lord, six people” or “five lord, five people” rules of thumb) cannot be treated as a nationwide rule. Nominal rates differed across bakufu, domain, and bannerman lands and across time, and effective rates depended on how closely assessments tracked actual yields and how many fields escaped re-measurement. Many scholars argue that effective burdens in some regions declined during the eighteenth century as production rose and values stayed fixed. Others stress that miscellaneous levies and village expenses were heavy. The accurate conclusion is that there was no single tax rate, and that burden depended on place, period, and household.

Section 44.8

Kemmi and Jōmen: Measuring the Harvest or Fixing the Assessment

Authorities used two broad approaches to set the yearly assessment, though neither was universal or exclusive. In kemmi (harvest inspection), officials visited the village before the harvest, judged the standing crop, and set the tax according to the year’s expected yield. This method adapted assessment to conditions: in a poor year, the tax fell; in a good year it rose. In jōmen (fixed assessment), the tax was set for a period of years, usually on the basis of average yields over previous years, so that the village owed approximately the same amount regardless of the year’s harvest.

Each system had advantages and risks. Kemmi offered relief in bad years, but it gave inspectors discretion and invited bribery, negotiation, and suspicion; villagers often regarded inspections as an opportunity for officials to demand more. Jōmen gave rulers predictable income and villagers predictability and incentive to improve yields, since extra output was theirs to keep, but it made bad years painful unless relief was granted. Bakufu officials expanded the use of fixed assessment in the early eighteenth century, in part to stabilize revenue.

Because assessment was a negotiation as much as a calculation, changes in the method could be read as changes in the moral relationship between lord and village. A lord who moved from a stable assessment toward inspection, or vice versa, could be seen as breaking custom. This is why the Gujō case, discussed in Section 44.19, turned so heavily on assessment procedures.

Section 44.9

Who Actually Bore the Burden? Inequality Inside the Village

Because taxation operated through the village, its impact depended on how each community apportioned the total. Landholding honbyakushō usually bore the direct obligations. Landless or land-poor households, often called mizunomi (literally “water-drinkers”) in particular regions, might cultivate rented land, work for larger households, or combine tenancy with wage and nonagricultural work; some held small amounts of land, so the term is not a simple synonym for tenant. Tenants paid rent to landlords, often a large fraction of the crop, and such households typically had little weight on tax registers or in village decisions. Servants and day laborers depended on wages and food from larger households. Officials and wealthy families could lend money, hire labor, and hold the best fields.

The same bad harvest therefore did not mean the same thing to each household. A wealthy family with stored grain, other income, and credit could endure a poor year and even profit by lending at interest or buying land from the distressed. A land-poor household with small plots and little savings faced debt, pawning, sale of land, loss of tools, migration for wage work, or in extreme cases starvation. Over the eighteenth century, in commercially developed regions, mortgage and tenancy relationships grew, and a layer of rural landlords (jinushi) emerged who owned land they did not cultivate. Authorities viewed land concentration with mixed feelings: it threatened the stable base of tax-paying cultivators, but landlords were also frequently village officials on whom the system depended.

Grievances inside villages were therefore not only directed upward. Disputes over who should pay village expenses, whether headmen had misused funds, and whether wealthy landholders were evading obligations generated petitions to officials and, at times, violent attacks on the houses of rural creditors. Understanding the burden of rural government requires attention to these internal conflicts as well as to the demands of lords.

Section 44.10

Expanding the Cultivated Landscape: Shinden, Reclamation, and Irrigation

One reason the seventeenth century saw rapid population growth was that cultivated land expanded dramatically. Lords, wealthy merchants, village communities, and groups of families invested in reclaiming river deltas, coastal wetlands, lakeshores, and upland slopes. New fields (shinden) were opened, embankments and drainage channels built, and irrigation canals dug or extended, sometimes diverting rivers over long distances. Such projects required capital, engineering knowledge, labor, and negotiation over water rights. Many created new villages or enlarged old ones.

This was not a smooth process. Reclamation altered drainage patterns, which could flood neighboring settlements, and deforestation increased erosion and silting in some watersheds. By the late seventeenth century, authorities in some regions became concerned that excessive clearing of hillsides was harming water supplies, and they restricted further development. The agricultural landscape was thus actively built and repeatedly modified by human decisions, not simply inherited.

New fields also raised administrative questions. Newly reclaimed land might be exempt from tax for an initial period, taxed at lower rates, or left unregistered. Such arrangements gave villages incentives to expand and created later disputes about whether new fields should be assessed. Rural development thus created both prosperity and fiscal ambiguity.

Section 44.11

Agricultural Intensification: Seeds, Double Cropping, Tools, and Fertilizer

Once the frontier of easily reclaimable land narrowed, from roughly the early eighteenth century, production growth depended increasingly on working existing land more intensively. Farmers selected rice varieties suited to local climate and soil, including early-ripening kinds for cooler areas. Where climate and water allowed, they planted a second crop, such as barley, wheat, or rapeseed, in the same fields after the rice harvest. They improved irrigation, weeded more carefully, and invested more labor per unit of land. Hayami Akira and other historians have called this pattern an “industrious revolution,” in which households worked harder and more skillfully rather than relying on labor-saving machinery or larger holdings.

Tools improved as well. Iron-bladed hoes adapted to deep tilling, threshing devices with many iron teeth (senbakoki), and winnowing machines spread, often through manuals and traveling craftsmen. Agricultural writings, from Miyazaki Yasusada’s 1697 Nōgyō zensho to later works, circulated advice in printed form and reveal a literate rural elite interested in technique.

Fertilizer was the decisive input. Early farmers relied on night soil, green manure, compost, and grass and leaves from commons. In advanced regions, farmers increasingly bought commercial fertilizers: dried sardines (hoshika), oil cakes made from pressed rapeseed or soybeans, and fish products such as pressed herring cake (shimekasu). These links connected inland farms to coastal fisheries, and in the late Tokugawa period to Ezo, where herring fisheries operated under the Matsumae domain’s system of fishing stations (basho) that relied heavily on Ainu labor under coercive terms (see Chapter 48). Productivity growth in the core therefore depended on supply chains that drew on marginal peoples and distant waters.

Section 44.12

Beyond Rice: Commercial Crops and Rural Production

Rural Japan was not a giant rice field. Many regions grew little rice because of climate or terrain and relied on millet, barley, buckwheat, wheat, sweet potatoes (spread in the eighteenth century), and vegetables. At the same time, farmers in many regions produced goods for sale. Cotton was grown widely in the Kinai region and spun and woven into cloth in rural households. Rapeseed supplied lamp oil and fertilizer; indigo was a key dye crop; tea, tobacco, hemp, paper mulberry, lacquer, and, above all, mulberry for silk production supplied specialized markets. Studies of some regions, such as Shimotsuke, indicate that even rice was increasingly grown as a commodity for sale by the late seventeenth century.

Rural manufacturing grew alongside cash cropping. Households spun cotton, wove silk and hemp, pressed oil, brewed sake, and made paper, charcoal, and other goods, often as by-employment during slack agricultural periods. This supplementary income was frequently essential to household survival and was largely performed by women and children as well as men. Women’s labor in spinning, weaving, and sericulture helped finance taxes and purchased inputs, even though most official records list men as household heads.

The chapter pauses here. Wholesalers, merchant credit, the Osaka rice and commodity markets, the highway system, and urban finance belong to Chapter 45. What matters for this chapter is how these new opportunities changed what villagers planted, how they fertilized, and why many households needed cash and markets to meet obligations.

Everyday Life and Environment

The Rhythms of a Tokugawa Farming Year

In a rice-growing village, work followed a seasonal rhythm: preparing seedbeds in spring, transplanting seedlings in early summer, weeding and managing water through the heat, and harvesting in autumn. Families often worked in exchange-labor groups for peak tasks. Women transplanted, weeded, and harvested alongside men, while also managing food, spinning and weaving, child care, and care of silkworms where relevant. Children herded animals, gathered fuel, and tended younger siblings. Winters brought repairs, straw-weaving, and by-employments, and festivals marked planting and harvest, often organized by the village shrine or temple.

Diet depended on the region and household. Rice was the prestige food and tax commodity, but many farm families ate considerable quantities of millet, barley, and vegetables, and in poor regions or years, foraged foods such as nuts and roots. Housing ranged from thatched farmhouses with earth floors to the larger homes of headmen. Illness, infant mortality, and the risk of crop failure were constant background conditions.

Section 44.13

Water, Forests, Commons, and the Politics of Rural Resources

A village’s livelihood depended on resources it could not own individually. Irrigation water had to be shared along channels and among villages; forests and grasslands provided fuel, building timber, fodder, and green manure; rivers and shores yielded fish and seaweed. Many such resources were managed as commons (iriai), with rules about who could cut, how much, and when. Common-use regulation was a core task of village assemblies, and breaches were punished by fines or exclusion.

Commons were also a frequent source of conflict. Upstream villages could divert water needed downstream; neighboring villages disputed boundaries of grazing land or the right to enter a forest; lords claimed forests for timber supplies or hunting, restricting villagers’ access. Such disputes were resolved by negotiation, mediation by neighboring headmen, appeals to officials, and sometimes by lawsuit, which could last years and be extremely costly. The documents they produced, including maps and written agreements, remain central sources for historians.

Agricultural history is therefore also environmental history. The countryside was managed, contested, and continuously modified; the intensification of rice farming drew grass and leaves from commons for fertilizer, and thus increased pressure on them. Overuse could cause erosion and flooding, and authorities periodically issued rules on forest conservation. The Tokugawa countryside was neither a pristine natural landscape nor a sustainable equilibrium but an actively managed and sometimes strained one.

Section 44.14

Prosperity, Commercialization, and New Vulnerability

Agricultural intensification and commercialization produced a paradox. In many regions they raised living standards, created wealthier village households with better housing, education, and consumption, and generated a layer of rural elites who patronized poetry, scholarship, and local trade. Yet the same developments made households more dependent on things they could not control. Farmers who grew cotton or mulberry rather than grain relied on markets for food; those who purchased fertilizer needed cash and credit; those who borrowed against crops were exposed to price swings. A bad harvest or a sudden collapse in prices could drive households into debt and land loss.

Commercialization was therefore neither simply progress nor simply exploitation. It expanded opportunity and interdependence together. Prosperous villages often had greater capacity to petition, litigate, and organize, because they had literacy, funds, and networks, and the same prosperity could deepen internal divisions between rich and poor, increase tenancy, and make rural society more volatile. The vulnerability was especially acute in regions that had become specialized or that depended on a single staple like rice in a cold climate, a point that becomes tragically visible in the famines of Section 44.16.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Continuity and Change Over Time: The Tokugawa Countryside

What Changed?

  • Cultivated land expanded rapidly in the seventeenth century, then growth shifted toward intensification and commercial crops.
  • Fertilizer shifted from locally gathered materials to purchased products connected to distant fisheries.
  • Tenancy, rural credit, and wealth differences grew in commercially developed regions.
  • Villagers developed more sophisticated documentation, litigation, and petitioning practices.

What Remained Continuous?

  • Taxation through the village, with headmen mediating between lords and villagers.
  • The rice-based fiscal vocabulary of kokudaka, even as farmers grew many other crops.
  • Reliance on collective management of water and commons.
  • Exposure to weather, pests, and disease.

Who experienced change most directly? Households in commercial regions, especially those with cash crops, experienced the largest shifts, both opportunity and risk. Who experienced greater continuity? Subsistence-oriented upland and northern villages saw less change in crops but remained exposed to shocks. Why does the pattern matter? It shows that the Tokugawa countryside was dynamic rather than static, and that growth and fragility developed together.

Section 44.15

What Makes a Famine? From Crop Failure to Social Disaster

A famine is not synonymous with cold weather or too little rain. Tokugawa-era harvest shocks came from abnormal temperatures, prolonged rain, drought, floods, typhoons, insect outbreaks, crop disease, and volcanic effects. Whether a shock became a mass-mortality crisis depended on a long chain of conditions: how much grain households and communities had stored; whether farmers depended on a single vulnerable crop; whether grain could be transported to hard-hit regions; how prices behaved; whether lords demanded tax in kind regardless of the harvest; whether hoarding or speculation occurred; whether officials opened granaries; whether people fled to towns; and whether epidemic disease followed hunger. Malnourished people died more often from dysentery, typhoid, and other infections than from literal starvation.

Records of the Tenmei crisis show why this matters. Mortality varied greatly by region, social position, and local policy; some domains lost large shares of their population, while neighbors with similar weather suffered less. The key teaching point is that weather creates harvest shocks, and institutions and social conditions help determine whether the shock becomes mass death. Famine is thus a historical process, not an act of nature alone.

Historians reconstruct famine from village diaries, temple death registers, domain reports, petitions for relief, price records, and chronicles by literate observers. Each has limits: diarists were often elites who described the poor from outside, and official death counts could be incomplete, politically shaped, or missing, so the numbers discussed below are estimates with substantial uncertainty.

Section 44.16

Three Great Famine Crises: Kyōhō, Tenmei, and Tenpō

Kyōhō (1732). In 1732, prolonged rain during the summer, followed by a massive outbreak of planthoppers (unka) that devastated rice plants, caused severe crop failure in western Japan, particularly in regions around the Inland Sea and Kyushu. Because the shock was concentrated in the west while eastern harvests were more normal, the crisis demonstrated the importance of transport, prices, and relief. The shogunate and domains took emergency measures, and the disaster later contributed to interest in famine-resistant crops such as the sweet potato.

Tenmei (1780s). The Tenmei crisis, usually dated to about 1782–1788, was most severe in northeastern Japan. Cold summers reduced harvests across multiple years; in 1783 the eruption of Mount Asama sent ash across central Japan and may have added to atmospheric cooling and local devastation, but it did not cause the famine on its own. It worsened an already developing climatic and socioeconomic crisis. Northern domains had become heavily dependent on rice for taxation and cash, sometimes exporting grain even as shortages loomed; disease compounded hunger. Mortality in places such as the Tsugaru domain is estimated to have been very high, although numbers vary widely among sources, and some domains with effective relief had much lower losses.

Tenpō (1830s). The Tenpō famines of roughly 1833–1837 again followed poor weather and flooding. Again regional differences were sharp, with the northeast and parts of central Japan suffering heavily, while some areas managed to buffer the crisis through reserves or relief. The social tension of the period, including urban unrest and the 1837 uprising in Osaka, is treated in Chapter 50.

Comparing the three shows that each had different environmental triggers, but all exposed the interaction of regional specialization, rice taxation, commercial networks, reserves, and policy. Later crises were not simply repetitions of earlier ones, because rulers and villagers drew on experience, and commercial integration changed both the supply of food and the speed of price movements.

Cause and Effect

Cause and Effect: Famine as a Compound Disaster

Long-term causes

Population growth on a land base that could no longer expand easily; regional specialization; dependence on rice in climatically marginal areas; fiscal systems requiring tax in rice; uneven household reserves.

Short-term causes

Cold summers, prolonged rain, flooding, insect outbreaks, or volcanic effects reducing a harvest; speculation and price spikes; delayed or inadequate relief.

Event or transformation

A harvest shock turns into food shortage, rising prices, household indebtedness, migration to towns, and epidemic disease.

Immediate effects

Deaths from hunger and disease; abandoned villages; land sales and debt; begging and flight; rural violence and petitions for relief.

Long-term consequences

Population stagnation or decline in affected regions; new granary and relief policies; reform programs; heightened criticism of domains and officials.

Historical complexity

The outcome depended on weather plus institutions. The same cold summer produced very different mortality in different domains, which is why no single cause explains famine.

Historical complexity: The chain “bad weather → crop failure → famine → riot” is too simple. The better model is environmental shock + agricultural system + reserves + markets + taxation + distribution + relief + household resources + disease = a historically specific outcome.

Section 44.17

Relief, Survival, and the Responsibilities of Rulers

People responded to food crises through a range of strategies. Households substituted cheaper foods, ate wild plants, sold or pawned belongings, borrowed, sent members to work elsewhere, or moved to castle towns and cities in hope of relief. Villages used reserves, shared grain, and sometimes organized community granaries (gisō or shasō). Lords could postpone or remit tax, open granaries, lend seed grain, regulate prices, ban exports, purchase grain from other regions, and organize soup kitchens or rice distribution. Temples and wealthy households also gave charity, sometimes from conviction and sometimes under social or official pressure.

Outcomes differed dramatically. Some domains and officials acted early and effectively; others delayed, kept collecting tax, or allowed grain exports while their own people starved. Later Tokugawa tradition remembered a few administrators, such as Matsudaira Sadanobu at Shirakawa during Tenmei, as successful relievers, though such accounts require care because they were also part of later political reputation-making. After the crises, authorities encouraged or required community granaries, a theme revisited in Chapter 50.

The Tokugawa ideal of benevolent rule (jinsei) held that rulers had a duty to protect the subsistence of their people. This was not democratic accountability, but it was an operative moral framework. Villagers invoked it in petitions, and rulers sometimes acknowledged it. People judged lords partly by whether they protected subsistence during crisis, and failure could damage legitimacy.

Section 44.18

Petitioning Power: Grievance, Negotiation, and Collective Action

Rural communities did not move immediately from discontent to violence. Most disputes were settled through a graduated repertoire. Villagers first sought mediation and informal negotiation; then submitted written petitions through the proper chain of command (village to magistrate to higher official); then sent delegates to a higher office or to Edo; and only in extreme cases engaged in mass gatherings, collective flight, or destruction of property. Researchers have shown that even collective flight (chōsan), in which villagers left their fields, could be a structured bargaining tactic: a way to deny the lord revenue and labor until demands were heard, after which people returned.

Historical Vocabulary
Historical Vocabulary: Ikki, Gōso, and Jikiso

Ikki (literally “united in purpose”) was a general term for collective action by people bound together by a common cause; for the Tokugawa period it usually refers to peasant or commoner uprisings. Gōso is often translated “forceful petition”: a mass appeal backed by the threat of collective action. Jikiso refers to a direct appeal by bypassing the normal chain of authority, for example appealing to a senior official in Edo, an act formally prohibited and risky. Scholars use these terms in somewhat different ways, and contemporary usage shifted, so each should be treated as a historically specific category, not a fixed legal definition.

The terms matter because they show a gradation of tactics and a legal framework within which protest took place, one that punished bypassing authority severely even as it sometimes responded to the grievances being expressed.

The key conceptual point is that petitions were claims about legitimate government. Petitioners did not typically argue that lords had no right to tax; they argued that this tax or this procedure violated custom, was unjust, or failed the benevolence that rulers claimed to embody. By invoking the ruler’s own principles, villagers turned the ideology of rule into a resource for bargaining.

Organization mattered. Delegates were chosen, funds raised, and secrecy and solidarity enforced. Historians note the use of pledge documents signed in circular form, so that no single signatory appeared as leader and thus no single person could be easily singled out for punishment. A 1756 example from the Gujō disturbance is examined in the Primary Source Window below.

Section 44.19

From Petition to Ikki: The Gujō Uprising, 1754–1758

The Gujō disturbance, often called the Hōreki disturbance after the era name, brings together taxation, village organization, petitioning, direct appeal, and punishment. Gujō was a mountain domain in Mino Province held by the Kanamori family, with assessed income of about 38,000 koku. The lord, Kanamori Yorikane, had served as a shogunal sōjaban (an expensive court-ceremonial office) since 1747 and presided over a financially strained domain. In 1754 the domain attempted to replace the established assessment arrangement with a form of harvest inspection (kemmi), provoking resistance from villagers who feared heavier and less predictable burdens. Sources differ on the details of the domain’s finances and of the assessment change.

Villagers gathered in large numbers, petitioned repeatedly, and sent representatives, first to the domain and then to Edo. They used extraordinary procedures, including direct appeals to senior officials, which were illegal and dangerous. The movement was not unanimous: accounts describe divisions among villagers, with some communities and leaders favoring compromise or siding with the domain, and the struggle lasted years, with costs, arrests, and hardships. Eventually, the case reached the shogunate’s highest judicial body.

In 1758, the shogunate held a lengthy investigation and trial. The outcome was severe on both sides. Many protest leaders were sentenced to punishment. The judgment condemned fourteen peasant participants to death; because one condemned man died in custody, some reference works count thirteen actual executions. Several leaders’ heads were publicly displayed. At the same time, the shogunate dismissed senior officials implicated in the affair, confiscated the Kanamori domain, and ended the family’s tenure. Gujō was then granted to the Aoyama family. The case is often said to be unusual because the lord was removed, but historians caution against treating it as typical: most protests ended with punishment of leaders and at most partial concessions.

Gujō is instructive because it shows procedure, organization, factional disagreement, appeals to higher authority, and the assumption that rulers were accountable to norms of proper governance. It was not a spontaneous revolt by desperate peasants, and it did not challenge the Tokugawa system itself; the villagers appealed to the shogunate against a lord. The case also shows the price of protest, since leaders paid heavily for actions that were technically crimes.

Section 44.20

Politics from Below: What Rural Protest Could—and Could Not—Do

Over the course of the Tokugawa period, commoners developed increasingly sophisticated repertoires of petition, organization, protest, regional coordination, and collective claims. Historians have counted thousands of protest incidents, though totals vary with definitions. Early protests were often village-level and focused on lords’ tax demands; by the eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries, larger multi-village and regional actions appeared, along with disputes about marketing and prices, such as the 1823 Settsu–Kawachi kokuso against privileged Osaka merchant controls over cotton and oilseed/rapeseed distribution. In crises, urban and rural riots (uchikowashi, “smashing”) targeted rice dealers and rural creditors. Popular legends of martyred leaders, such as the story of Sakura Sōgorō, circulated in later theater and storytelling; historians treat these narratives as evidence of memory, not as straightforward records.

It would be a mistake to turn Tokugawa villagers into proto-liberal democrats or revolutionaries. Most protesters sought tax reduction, relief, fair administration, removal of abusive officials, protection of customary rights, or enforcement of ideals of benevolent government. They typically appealed within the political order. Yet that does not make their actions apolitical. They claimed standards for legitimate rule and organized across communities, and rulers took them seriously enough to monitor, punish, and sometimes accommodate them. Such practices formed a political culture that later generations of activists in the nineteenth century would draw upon in new ways (Chapter 57).

Protest’s limits were equally clear. It rarely changed structures; leaders risked execution; rural elites often sided with authorities; and protest could be directed against fellow villagers as easily as against lords. Reform came more often through negotiation and gradual adjustment than through confrontation.

These increasingly productive villages were deeply connected to castle towns, highways, markets, wholesalers, credit networks, and the great cities of Osaka, Kyoto, and Edo. Chapter 44 has explained production and extraction in the countryside; Chapter 45 explains circulation, commerce, and urbanization.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the political economy of the Tokugawa countryside. Its central claim is that villages were neither powerless nor democratic: they were corporate communities with real self-governing capacities, and they were the units through which the bakufu and domains governed rural society. Authority and autonomy were simultaneous realities.

Village government relied on headmen, assistants, assemblies, written rules, and five-household groups. Headmen mediated between lords and villagers, kept records, and apportioned burdens, and their dual position made them both indispensable and vulnerable to criticism. Participation was unequal: established landholders dominated, while tenants, servants, and women had little formal voice.

The fiscal framework rested on land surveys, registers, and kokudaka, an assessed productive value rather than a count of harvest. Taxation operated through the village under the practice of murauke; nengu was joined by miscellaneous levies and service obligations; and assessment could follow harvest inspection (kemmi) or fixed rates (jōmen). There was no single nationwide rate, and effective burdens varied by region, period, and household.

Inequality shaped how burdens fell. Wealthy households could absorb shocks and lend, while land-poor families faced debt and loss. Commercialization deepened this stratification in some regions, creating landlords and tenants while raising living standards for many.

Agriculture was dynamic. Seventeenth-century reclamation and irrigation expanded cultivated land; later growth relied on intensification through improved seed, double cropping, labor, and purchased fertilizer. Rural households grew cash crops and engaged in by-employments, with women’s labor central. These developments depended on supply chains reaching distant fisheries and, in the case of herring fertilizer, Ainu labor under coercive conditions.

Water, forests, and commons were managed collectively but generated disputes, and intensification increased pressures on them. The countryside was an actively managed and contested environment.

Prosperity and vulnerability grew together. Dependence on markets, credit, and specialization made households sensitive to price and supply shocks, particularly in cold or single-crop regions.

Famine was a compound process. The Kyōhō crisis of 1732, the Tenmei crisis of the 1780s, and the Tenpō famines of the 1830s each had distinct environmental triggers, but outcomes depended on reserves, markets, taxation, relief, and disease. Weather created shocks; institutions and social conditions shaped mortality.

Villagers responded through petitions, negotiation, collective flight, mass appeals, and uprisings. These actions were organized claims about legitimate government that invoked rulers’ own ideals of benevolence. The Gujō disturbance of 1754–1758 illustrates the process and its costs: sustained organization, direct appeals, severe punishment of leaders, and the removal of a lord.

Rural protest was political but not democratic or revolutionary. It operated within the Tokugawa order and had clear limits, yet it created a political culture and shaped how rulers governed. The next chapter turns from production to circulation.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it moves the Tokugawa countryside from the margins of political history to its center. A state that ruled through villages depended on them, and villages used that dependence. The stereotype of the passive, endlessly taxed peasant cannot survive the evidence of accounts, assemblies, petitions, and organized appeals, even though extraction, inequality, and harsh punishment were real.

The chapter also reveals larger processes: expansion and then intensification of agriculture, growing commercial integration, and recurring subsistence crises. These shaped population, regional inequality, and the legitimacy of rulers, and they set up the reform programs of the late eighteenth and nineteenth centuries and the political ferment of the Bakumatsu era.

The countryside also connected regions. Fertilizer from Ezo fisheries, sugar from the southern islands, cotton from the Kinai, and rice from the northeast linked communities across the archipelago, often unequally. Understanding Tokugawa Japan means seeing these connections rather than treating the village as an isolated unit.

Finally, the chapter prepares for what follows. These productive and vulnerable villages were bound to castle towns, highways, markets, and credit networks. Chapter 45 turns from production and extraction to circulation and urbanization.
