Part III — Nara and Heian Japan
17

The Founding of Heian-kyō

Japanese History: From Jōmon Japan to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

In 784 an emperor left a capital that had stood for nearly seventy-five years. Ten years later he left its replacement. The city that became known to later generations as Kyoto was the second capital Emperor Kanmu built, not the first—and its founding makes sense only when the failed first attempt at Nagaoka is placed at the center of the story.

Textbook shorthand often compresses this episode into a single line: in 794 the capital moved to Kyoto and the Heian period began. The line is convenient, and it is misleading. It hides a decade of construction and abandonment at Nagaoka-kyō, an assassination at a building site, the death of a crown prince, anxieties that later generations attributed to a vengeful spirit, a punishing war in the northeast, and a series of reforms aimed at a state whose fiscal and military machinery was showing strain. It also hides continuity. The emperor who founded Heian-kyō did not abolish the ritsuryō system of law, ranks, registers, and provinces that Chapters 13 through 16 described. He tried to make it work differently.

This chapter asks why Kanmu's court left Nara, why the first replacement capital failed, and how the founding of Heian-kyō reshaped—but did not replace—the political order it inherited. Chapter 16 ended with a Nara state that was powerful but strained, ruling diverse regions imperfectly, with an unresolved northeastern frontier and a fiscal system under pressure. Chapter 17 follows the generation of rulers who inherited that state between 781, when Kanmu became emperor, and 806, when he died. It treats the move of the capital not as a single reaction to a single problem—least of all to the old formula that Buddhist monks had captured Nara—but as one element in a wider program of political restructuring that touched court government, provincial administration, military organization, frontier policy, and the relationship between the throne and Buddhist institutions.

The chapter's governing argument is that 794 was not the beginning of an entirely new system. Kanmu inherited institutions, fiscal problems, frontier conflicts, aristocratic rivalries, and religious-political tensions from the Nara state. Heian-kyō preserved much of the ritsuryō framework while changing the geography of government, the relationship between the court and the great institutions of Nara, patterns of aristocratic politics, and the means by which the state attempted to control its provinces and manage frontier warfare. What emerged was neither a fresh start nor a simple continuation, but a reconstruction carried out under pressure, by people who did not know how it would turn out.

The evidence for these decades is richer and more politically loaded than for earlier periods. The Shoku Nihongi, completed in 797 and covering events to 791, was compiled under Kanmu's own government. For the years after 791 the principal court chronicle is the Nihon Kōki, completed in 840, decades after the events, and preserved only in part. Excavations at Nagaoka-kyō and Heian-kyō have revealed palace foundations, roads, wells, tiles, and wooden tablets. Poetry, temple records, and later religious literature add further perspectives, though many of them were shaped by memories of the crises. Students should keep asking who compiled a source, when, and for whom—especially where the chronicle explains the motives of rulers, or attributes disasters to spirits.

The chapter also keeps frontier and religious history in view. Kanmu's northeastern wars, which Chapter 16 carried to the revolt of 780, reached their climax in this reign, culminating in the surrender of the Emishi leaders Aterui and More in 802. Kanmu's court also sponsored the missions to Tang China of 804, in which the monks Saichō and Kūkai took part. But the chapter stops around Kanmu's death in 806. Who accumulated power within the new capital—through marriage politics, regency, and bureaucracy—is the subject of Chapter 18, and the court culture of the mature Heian world belongs to Chapter 19.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain the political, fiscal, and dynastic pressures that Kanmu inherited in 781 and why the capital's location became a political question.
  2. Evaluate competing explanations for leaving Heijō-kyō, and explain why the formula "Kanmu moved because Buddhist monks controlled Nara" is inadequate.
  3. Describe the choice, planning, and construction of Nagaoka-kyō, including its river-transport advantages and the labor it demanded.
  4. Analyze the assassination of Fujiwara no Tanetsugu and the fall of Prince Sawara, separating contemporary accusation, Sawara's death, and later beliefs about a vengeful spirit.
  5. Explain why Nagaoka was abandoned after about a decade and why the court moved to Heian-kyō in 794.
  6. Describe the plan of Heian-kyō and assess its relation to Chinese capital models without treating it as a copy of Chang'an.
  7. Trace Kanmu's reforms in provincial government, land allotment, taxation, and military recruitment, and explain why they left the ritsuryō framework in place.
  8. Interpret the northeastern war from 789 to 802, including the roles of Aterui, More, and Sakanoue no Tamuramaro, within the frontier framework of Chapter 16.
  9. Assess how the new capital reconfigured relations between the throne and Buddhist institutions, including the rise of Saichō and Mount Hiei.
  10. Distinguish what changed and what continued between the Nara state and the early Heian state, and identify what Chapter 18 must explain.
  11. Read chronicle sources critically, noting when they were compiled, by whom, and what interests shaped their accounts.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did Emperor Kanmu move the capital not once but twice, and what does the failure of the first move reveal about the political conditions of the 780s?
  2. How should historians weigh dynastic, fiscal, logistical, religious, and factional explanations for leaving Nara without settling on a single cause?
  3. How can historians treat the death of Prince Sawara and the later belief in his vengeful spirit as historical evidence without treating the spirit as a cause?
  4. In what ways did the geography of Nagaoka and Heian—rivers, basins, roads, and ports—shape the political possibilities of a capital?
  5. To what extent was Heian-kyō a new political order, and to what extent was it a relocation of the ritsuryō state?
  6. What did Kanmu's reforms try to fix, and why did they leave so much of the Nara system intact?
  7. How did warfare in the northeast reshape military organization, and how does the surrender and execution of Aterui and More fit the frontier interpretation developed in Chapter 16?
  8. How did the founding of a new capital alter the relationship between imperial authority and Buddhist institutions?

Historical Context

Chapters 14 through 16 established the Nara state from three vantage points. Chapter 14 examined Heijō-kyō and the organization of court government; Chapter 15 traced the alliance of Buddhist institutions with the throne, from Tōdaiji and the Great Buddha to the provincial temple system; Chapter 16 followed the state into the provinces and northeast, where registers, taxes, governors, district magistrates, forts, and settlers made court authority real but uneven. This chapter begins where that account ended, in the late 770s and early 780s, with the revolt of Iji no Azamaro in 780 and Kanmu's accession the following year.

The court Kanmu inherited had lived through repeated political shocks. The succession had passed among descendants of Emperor Tenmu for much of the eighth century, and the death of Empress Shōtoku in 770, following the rise of the monk Dōkyō (Chapter 15), ended that line. Kōnin, a descendant of Emperor Tenji, took the throne, and Kanmu, his son, continued the Tenji line. Court politics in these decades were marked by accusations of plots, exiles, and posthumous punishments, as competing aristocratic families and imperial branches contested the succession. The state's fiscal position was also difficult. The allotment and tax systems described in Chapters 13 to 16 rested on registers that were increasingly unreliable, on households that could flee or be reclassified, and on labor and grain flows that the capital, its temples, and the frontier all demanded.

The capital itself was a source of pressure. Heijō-kyō had been founded in 710 as a planned city, and a century of courtly and religious building had bound it to great institutions such as Tōdaiji, Kōfukuji, and other temples with substantial landholdings, personnel, and political weight. The Nara basin was a landlocked area whose links to the wider archipelago depended on roads and on water routes that were less convenient than those farther north. Moving a capital was not unprecedented: earlier rulers had moved their palaces repeatedly, and the court had already relocated in the 740s during the reign of Shōmu. But the ritsuryō capital was a much larger investment than earlier palaces, and relocating it was an expensive political act.

Finally, the wider world shaped these decades. Diplomacy with the Tang empire continued through periodic missions, and the court received envoys from Balhae, the kingdom whose ships crossed the Sea of Japan. Relations with Silla had cooled. Immigrant lineages from the Korean peninsula and their descendants, including the Baekje-descended families of Kanmu's mother's side, formed part of the court and provincial elites. The court's frontier wars in the northeast tied its finances and manpower to distant events. Understanding Heian-kyō therefore requires seeing the capital move as an act embedded in domestic politics, regional geography, and a changing set of external relationships.

Section 17.1

The Late Nara Order under Strain

Kanmu came to the throne in 781, when his father Kōnin abdicated because of illness; Kōnin died soon afterward. Kanmu was in his mid-forties and had not been born to the throne in any obvious way. As a prince named Yamabe, he was not initially a leading candidate, and his path to the succession opened only after a court crisis in the early 770s in which Kōnin's consort and their son, the crown prince, were accused of cursing the emperor and removed from position. Kanmu became crown prince in 773. His mother, Takano no Niigasa, belonged to a lineage that traced its ancestry to Baekje royalty, a fact that historians note as a sign of the place of immigrant-descended families in the court, though Kanmu's policies should not be reduced to ancestry.

The reign began amid evident insecurity. In 782, according to the chronicle, a plot attributed to Hikami no Kawatsugu, a descendant of the Tenmu line, was exposed; Kawatsugu was exiled, and others were punished. Whatever the truth of the accusation, the episode shows that the succession still had rivals, and that the new emperor's authority could not be taken for granted. Kanmu appointed his younger brother Sawara as crown prince, an arrangement that would later become fraught. The court also faced the frontier crisis that Chapter 16 described: after the revolt of Iji no Azamaro in 780, the northeast remained unsettled, and the destruction of Tagajō had shaken the court's confidence that its frontier institutions would hold.

Fiscal and Institutional Pressures

Behind the political drama lay structural problems. The system of registration and land allotment described in Chapters 13 to 16 required households to be counted, fields to be assigned, and taxes and labor to be collected. In practice, registers were inaccurate, households evaded obligations, and epidemics and migration altered populations faster than officials could record them. The grain tax (so) attached to allotted rice land; taxable males bore personal obligations including chō, yō, labor service, and military conscription; and provincial governments lent grain at interest through the suiko system. These obligations weighed heavily on households, and the court's own demands—for the capital, for temples, for frontier campaigns—strained the system further. The state's problem was not an absence of institutions but a gap between what the institutions demanded and what the countryside could reliably supply.

The great Nara temples, meanwhile, held land, labor, and prestige, and their relationship to the throne, established in the age of Shōmu and reshaped by the Dōkyō affair, remained politically sensitive. Aristocratic families had built networks around the Nara court, and appointment to office, competition for succession, and control of estates and patronage all ran through the capital. Kanmu inherited this entire complex. Moving the capital was thus not a response to a single problem but a way of reopening the whole question of how the court related to its aristocracy, its temples, its provinces, and its frontier.

Section 17.2

Why Leave Heijō-kyō?

The chronicles record decisions and actions but say relatively little about motive, and historians have offered several overlapping explanations for why Kanmu decided to leave Heijō-kyō. The oldest and most familiar account holds that Kanmu moved because Buddhist monks, and the great temples of Nara, had grown so powerful that the throne needed to escape their influence. That explanation has a basis: the Dōkyō affair of 769–770 had shown how a monk could acquire extraordinary political influence at court, and Kanmu's government later took measures to regulate clergy and temple landholding. But as the sole explanation it is inadequate. It treats Buddhist institutions as a single bloc, ignores the aristocratic, dynastic, fiscal, and logistical dimensions of the move, and reads later assumptions about "monastic politics" back into a period when rulers and temples were deeply interdependent. Kanmu himself patronized Buddhism, and the court in its new capital did not abandon Buddhist ritual.

Several Overlapping Explanations

Political reorientation. After decades of factional struggle, a new ruler might hope to loosen the grip of entrenched networks by moving away from the place where they were strongest. A capital is not just a city but a set of social relationships among officials, families, temples, and suppliers; relocating it forced these relationships to be renegotiated. Kanmu could reward those who followed him and reshape the map of patronage.

Dynastic considerations. Kanmu belonged to the Tenji line, which had taken the throne only in 770. Heijō-kyō was closely associated with the Tenmu line rulers who had dominated the eighth century. Some historians suggest that Kanmu wished to establish a capital associated with his own dynastic identity. The chronicles record that in 785 Kanmu performed a Chinese-style suburban sacrifice to Heaven at Katano in Kawachi Province. In 787 he sent Fujiwara no Tsugutada to conduct another rite there in which his father Kōnin was paired with Heaven. Historians have connected the Katano rites with Kanmu's Tenji-line dynastic self-presentation, but that interpretation rests on inference about their political meaning.

Geography and logistics. A site in Yamashiro offered river access. The Katsura, Uji, and Kizu rivers join to form the Yodo, which flows to Naniwa and the Inland Sea, so a capital there could receive goods and travelers by water more readily than the landlocked Nara basin. The region was also agriculturally productive and was home to influential immigrant-descended and local families, including the Hata, whose resources and connections proved valuable in construction.

Cost and fiscal calculations. Any move was expensive, but a court that already faced fiscal pressure might have believed that a better-located capital would improve the flow of goods and labor. The chronicle's evidence for such calculations is indirect, and the eventual cost of two capitals in a decade cut the other way.

Historical Debate

Why Did Kanmu Move the Capital?

The question: Was the move of the capital primarily a response to the power of Nara's Buddhist institutions, a strategy of dynastic and political renewal, a logistical improvement, or a response to crisis? How can historians weigh explanations when the sources record actions but rarely state motives?

Interpretation A — Escaping Nara's entrenched institutions: On this older and still influential view, the court sought distance from the great Nara temples and the aristocratic networks around them. Supporters point to Dōkyō's rise, to Kanmu's later regulation of clergy and temple land, and to the court's decision not to move Nara's great monasteries into the new capital.
Interpretation B — Dynastic and political renewal: Other historians emphasize Kanmu's position as a ruler of the Tenji line, the Katano rites, and the opportunity to build a capital identified with his own reign, reshaping patronage and reward.
Interpretation C — Logistics and geography: A third approach stresses the river network, the productive surrounding region, and the practical benefits of a capital closer to Naniwa and the Inland Sea, along with local elites who could supply resources.

Evidence: The debate draws on the chronicle record of decisions and rites, archaeological evidence of the sites, the geography of the region, and later accounts of the move. None gives a direct statement of Kanmu's motives, and the chronicles were compiled by officials writing for his court. Most current scholarship treats the explanations as overlapping rather than exclusive.

Why it matters: How historians understand the move shapes how they assess Kanmu's reign: as a reaction against Buddhist power, as a dynastic project, as pragmatic statecraft, or as an experiment made under pressure. It also shapes whether Heian-kyō appears as a clean break or as part of a longer restructuring.

Student task: Explain why the formula "Kanmu moved because Buddhist monks controlled Nara" is too narrow. Identify one piece of evidence for each interpretation, and say what additional evidence would help decide among them.

Section 17.3

Building Nagaoka-kyō, 784

In the fifth month of 784, according to the Shoku Nihongi, Kanmu sent officials, including Fujiwara no Tanetsugu, to inspect a site at Nagaoka in Otokuni District of Yamashiro Province. The choice reflected the geographic reasoning described above. The site lay near the confluence of the rivers that form the Yodo, giving access by water to Naniwa and the Inland Sea, and it was outside the Nara basin, on a plain bounded by hills and rivers. Within months the court committed to the project, appointed officials to oversee construction, and in the eleventh month of 784 the emperor moved into the new palace, even though much of the work remained unfinished.

Primary Source Window

The Shoku Nihongi and the 784 Move to Nagaoka

Creator: Court officials compiling the official chronicle under Kanmu's government · Date: events of 784; chronicle completed in 797 · Type: Official court chronicle (narrative and documentary entries)

Context: The Shoku Nihongi covers the years 697 to 791 and was completed in 797. Its entries for 784 record the dispatch of officials to survey Nagaoka, appointments connected with construction, and the emperor's removal to the new site. The compilers were officials of the same government that had made these decisions, working during the reign of the emperor whose policies they recorded.

Paraphrase of the chronicle's content (not a verbatim quotation): The chronicle presents the move as a series of orderly administrative steps: officials are sent to inspect the site, others are appointed to manage construction, and the court removes to the new palace. It gives dates and names but offers little explicit statement of why the capital was being moved.

Analysis: The chronicle is our principal narrative source for the decision, and it preserves dates, appointments, and the names of officials, including Tanetsugu's role. It shows how the court framed the move: as an act of government, carried out through appointments and orders. But it is a source written from within the government, at a time when the emperor's decisions could not be openly criticized. It gives few reasons. The chronicle was completed in 797, after Nagaoka had already been abandoned for Heian-kyō. Its narrative coverage, however, ends in 791, so the entries for 784 do not themselves explain the later abandonment. The final compilation may nevertheless have been shaped by knowledge of what happened afterward. The account of the years that followed, in the Nihon Kōki, was compiled in 840 and reflects later court perspectives.

  1. What does this source say about how the move to Nagaoka was carried out?

  4. What can historians not establish from this source alone, particularly about motive and about the experience of those who built the city?

Planning and Construction

Nagaoka-kyō was laid out on a grid in the ritsuryō tradition, with a palace enclosure at its northern center and avenues and blocks to the south. Excavations have uncovered the palace's central buildings, gates, roads, and ditches. Construction also reused earlier palatial architecture. In connection with the 784 move, major buildings of the later Naniwa palace were dismantled and transferred to Nagaoka; archaeological finds, including roof tiles, support this reuse. Materials from Heijō were also transferred during later construction phases. Reuse reduced costs and sped construction while showing how heavily new capitals depended on resources taken from older ones.

Construction required enormous labor. The chronicle records the mobilization of very large numbers of workers, with figures in the hundreds of thousands, though such numbers should be treated cautiously as they may reflect cumulative person-days or rhetorical inflation. The labor drew on the provincial system described in Chapter 16, especially the compulsory labor obligation (zōyō) and other mobilizations of households from surrounding provinces. Fujiwara no Tanetsugu was the central figure in supervising construction, and he reportedly had connections to the Hata, whose local resources may have assisted the project. Tanetsugu's influence at court was substantial, and his role would prove both central and dangerous.

Everyday Life and Environment

Building a Capital: A Household's View

Note: the household below is an illustrative composite based on general patterns in law codes and chronicle references to labor mobilization; it is not a specific named family.

Imagine a household in a province within reach of the Yodo basin. When the district office announces a labor levy for the new capital, a taxable adult male is required to go, carrying his own provisions or relying on what the household can supply. The household must decide who remains to plant, weed, and harvest, and the woman of the house and her relatives take on tasks the man would ordinarily perform, alongside the weaving and other work they already do. The journey to the building site takes days on foot. At Nagaoka, the worker joins crews digging drainage ditches, hauling timber and earth, and constructing buildings under the direction of officials and skilled craftspeople. Some of the timber and tiles may have come from dismantled buildings elsewhere.

The length of service is limited by law, but in practice it can extend if the work is urgent or if replacements do not arrive. Illness, injury, and accidents are risks; so is the loss of a season's labor at home. A household that lacks adult men may pay to hire a substitute or fall into debt. For local communities near the site, the arrival of thousands of workers changes the local economy: food, lodging, and materials are in demand, and elite families with land and boats and labor, such as the Hata, are positioned to profit. Some households experience the capital's construction as a burden, others as opportunity.

  2. Who might have benefited economically from the project, and who bore its costs?
  3. What kinds of evidence would historians need to reconstruct the actual working conditions at Nagaoka?
Section 17.4

Assassination, Prince Sawara, and the Crisis of Nagaoka

In the ninth month of 785, while Kanmu was away from the capital, Fujiwara no Tanetsugu was struck by arrows at the Nagaoka building site and died of his wounds. The killing was a political catastrophe. Tanetsugu was the official most closely associated with the new capital, and his death struck at the project's leadership as well as at the court's sense of security.

The court moved quickly to identify those responsible. According to the chronicle, the investigation implicated a group of men connected to the Ōtomo and Saeki families, and it also named Ōtomo no Yakamochi—the poet and official who had died a few weeks earlier—as a figure said to have been involved. Yakamochi was posthumously stripped of his honors and his family was punished; his rank and honors were restored in 806, on the day of Kanmu's death. The investigation also reached the crown prince. Prince Sawara, whose household had connections to the Ōtomo, was accused of knowing of or consenting to the plot. He was confined at a temple, deposed, and sent toward exile on Awaji Island. According to the chronicle, he refused food and died on the way, before reaching his destination. Kanmu's son Ate was made crown prince in his place.

What Can Be Known and What Cannot

The evidence requires careful separation. It is documented that Tanetsugu was killed at Nagaoka in 785; that the court accused a group of men and punished them; that Sawara was deposed and died in custody or in transit; and that Ate became crown prince. It is not established, on the evidence we have, that Sawara ordered or knew of the plot. The accusations come from the court's own narrative, compiled under the emperor who deposed his brother, and the speed and severity of the punishments have led many historians to consider the possibility that the affair also served the purposes of court factions who wished to remove Sawara and secure the succession for Kanmu's son. Others take the accusations more literally. The evidence does not allow certainty. It is therefore inappropriate to say that Sawara "murdered" Tanetsugu; what can be said is that the court accused him of complicity and that historians debate the reliability of the charge.

Historical Vocabulary
Onryō and Goryō

An onryō is a vengeful spirit, the angry spirit of a person who died in circumstances thought to demand redress, especially one who died unjustly or in disgrace. Goryō is a related term for honored or placated spirits, often associated with rituals meant to appease them. In the early Heian period, beliefs about such spirits came to shape court ritual and politics.

Historians study these beliefs as evidence of how contemporaries interpreted illness, disaster, and death, not as explanations of what caused them. An account that attributes a flood to Sawara's spirit tells us how people at court thought about the crisis, not what produced the flood.

Sawara's Afterlife

Over the following years, misfortune struck the imperial family. Kanmu's mother died in 789, and his empress in 790; other deaths and illnesses followed. In the early 790s, when the crown prince Ate fell ill, divination reportedly attributed the illness to Sawara's spirit, and the court responded with rituals of apology. In 800, Kanmu posthumously gave Sawara the title of emperor (Sudō Tennō) and had his burial place honored. Later, in the ninth century, Sawara was among the spirits honored at court ceremonies to appease vengeful spirits (goryō-e). These developments belong to a history of belief and ritual, showing how the court processed political guilt and fear. They do not establish that a vengeful spirit caused the events. They do show that, by the 790s, Nagaoka had become associated in court memory with disaster and unease, an association that helped make the site a place the court was willing to leave.

Section 17.5

From Nagaoka to Heian-kyō, 794

Nagaoka was not meant to be temporary, and it should not be treated as a stop on a predetermined road to Kyoto. The court invested in it, and it served as the capital for roughly a decade. Its abandonment resulted from a convergence of pressures, and the sources do not allow us to assign a single decisive cause.

Why Nagaoka Was Given Up

Political instability. The assassination of Tanetsugu and the fall of Sawara left the site associated with violence and factional bitterness, and the deaths of Kanmu's mother and empress in 789 and 790 deepened the sense of misfortune. Court officials and the emperor himself seem to have grown anxious about the place, and ritual concerns, including the belief that Sawara's spirit troubled the crown prince, fed that anxiety.

Cost and construction problems. The project was expensive, and work dragged on. The northeastern war, which consumed men and resources, competed with construction for the same labor and revenue. Archaeological evidence suggests that parts of the planned city were never fully developed.

Environmental concerns. The chronicle records flooding at Nagaoka in 792, and the site's location near river confluences that made it attractive for transport also exposed it to water. Historians debate how serious the drainage and flooding problems were, since sources emphasizing them may reflect later justifications for the move.

Attractive alternatives. Another site in Yamashiro, at Kadono to the northeast, offered a basin surrounded by hills, with rivers on both sides and a productive hinterland, and the Kamo and Hata families had established presences in the area. In 793, Kanmu sent officials to inspect the site and, according to later tradition, took an interest in it personally.

Cause and Effect

From Nara Problems to the Foundation of Heian-kyō

Long-term causes

Decades of succession conflict and aristocratic rivalry; fiscal strain in the allotment and tax system; the entrenched weight of Nara's institutions; the unresolved northeastern frontier; and the geography of the Nara basin relative to river and sea routes.

Short-term causes

Kanmu's accession and dynastic position; the insecurity of the early 780s; the opportunity to build a capital associated with his own reign; and the resources available in Yamashiro.

Event or transformation

The move to Nagaoka in 784, followed by crisis in 785, doubts through the early 790s, and the move to Heian-kyō in 794.

Immediate effects

A second capital within a decade, ongoing cost and labor demands, changed patterns of patronage, and a new relationship between the court and the institutions left at Nara.

Long-term consequences

A capital that would remain the imperial seat for over a thousand years, the setting for the political developments of Chapters 18 and 19.

Historical complexity

The sequence was not a deterministic chain. Nagaoka might have endured; the move to Heian was a choice made under pressure; and even after 794 the court could have returned to Nara, as an attempt in 810 by the retired Emperor Heizei would show.

Historical complexity: Each stage of the sequence was shaped by contingency. Explaining the founding of Heian-kyō means explaining why the court left Nara, why Nagaoka failed, and why Kadono was chosen, and none of these follows automatically from the others.

The Move of 794

Work at Kadono began in 793, and in the tenth month of 794 (late in the Western year) the emperor moved into the new palace. In the following month, an edict renamed the surrounding province, changing the written form of Yamashiro, and named the new city Heian-kyō, the "Capital of Peace and Tranquility." The edict, as recorded, praised the natural setting, with mountains and rivers forming a kind of natural fortress. The city later called Kyoto was thus originally Heian-kyō; "Kyoto" is simply a later, generic term for "capital" that became the familiar place name. Construction was far from complete in 794. The palace enclosure and its principal buildings were built over the following years, and portions of the planned city would never be densely settled. The founding of Heian-kyō was a beginning, not a finished product.

Section 17.6

Designing Heian-kyō

Chapter 14 explained the planning of Heijō-kyō as a ritsuryō capital modeled in part on Chinese precedents. Heian-kyō followed the same general tradition while differing in scale, site, and history. It was laid out on a rectangular grid, roughly five kilometers north to south and somewhat more than four kilometers east to west, with the imperial palace complex at the northern center. A broad central avenue, Suzaku Avenue, ran south from the palace's southern gate to the city's great southern gate, Rashōmon, dividing the city into a Left Capital (Sakyō) to the east and a Right Capital (Ukyō) to the west. Cross streets numbered from north to south divided the city into wards and blocks, and two officially regulated markets, one in each half, served exchange.

Historical Vocabulary
Heian-kyō

Heian-kyō ("Capital of Peace and Tranquility") was the official name of the city founded in 794 in Yamashiro Province. It is the city later called Kyoto (kyō, "capital," plus to, "metropolis"). The period from 794 to the late twelfth century is called the Heian period after it, though the label is a convenient convention rather than a sharp historical boundary.

The Heian palace complex, the Daidairi, was a large enclosure containing the emperor's residence and the offices of government. Its layout and its name should not be confused with the city as a whole, or with the mature courtly civilization that developed in it.

Chinese Models and Japanese Adaptation

The planners drew on the tradition of Chinese capitals such as Chang'an, the Tang capital, with its grid, central axis, and separation of palace and city. But Heian-kyō was not an exact copy. It was smaller than Chang'an, lacked a surrounding wall of the Chinese type, and adapted the grid to the site's topography and rivers. Its southern gate, Rashōmon, stood without a wall extending from it. The plan also expressed different political conditions: Japanese capitals were not the seat of a huge bureaucratic empire in the Chinese sense, and their populations and functions differed. The city, moreover, changed as it was lived in. The plan on paper and the city on the ground diverged over time, as the sources and archaeology both show.

Palace, City, and Temples

The palace enclosure held the great audience hall and the halls in which court ceremonies were performed, the offices of the state ministries, and the emperor's private residence. Around it, aristocrats built mansions in the wards, officials and craftspeople lived in less prestigious areas, and markets provided goods. Unlike Heijō-kyō, Heian-kyō was not built around a set of great Buddhist monasteries. The court permitted only two official temples within the city, Tōji ("East Temple") and Saiji ("West Temple"), planned near the southern gate as the capital's two state temples; construction administration is documented from the later 790s, and the complexes took years to develop. Other major temples were located outside, on the hills around the city. This decision expressed a political geography in which the court kept the temples of Nara at a distance and controlled Buddhist presence in the capital, a subject taken up in Section 17.9. Shrines associated with local families, including the Kamo shrines to the north, retained their own prestige and would attract later court patronage.

Over time, the city's western half, the Right Capital, proved less attractive, in part because of poor drainage, and settlement concentrated in the east. This process belongs to later decades and centuries, but it is a reminder not to assume that the whole city was completed and densely occupied in 794, or that the plan describes the lived city of later centuries.

Section 17.7

Reforming Court and Provincial Government

Kanmu was not merely a city founder. His reign saw a series of measures aimed at making the existing state work better, or at least at containing its costs and abuses. The core point is that Kanmu tried to make the existing imperial state work differently; he did not invent a new constitution in 794. The ritsuryō codes remained the legal framework, and the offices, ranks, and provinces described in earlier chapters continued.

Oversight of Provincial Officials

Chapter 16 described the provincial governors (kokushi), who came from the capital for limited terms, and the district magistrates (gunji), who were drawn from local elite families. The court had long worried that governors abused their positions, and that transfers of office left accounts and storehouses in disorder. In 797 Kanmu's government established an office, the Kageyushi, to audit the documents by which departing governors certified that they had handed over their responsibilities in good order. The office stood outside the framework of the original codes, a category historians call extra-statutory offices (ryōge no kan), and it illustrates a characteristic pattern of the period: the state responded to problems by creating new offices alongside the old ones rather than abolishing the ritsuryō structure. The court also warned repeatedly against abuses by governors and district officials, restricted certain practices in the provinces, and attempted to reduce expenditure by consolidating or abolishing some posts.

Land, Taxes, and Labor

The land-allotment system, which called for reallotment at regular intervals, had become increasingly difficult to administer. The allotment based on the 794 registers was delayed until 800. In 801 the court responded by ordering allotment once every twelve years rather than every six, effectively omitting alternate redistribution cycles. Other measures sought to reduce the burdens of provincial households, including adjustments to labor and loan obligations, while maintaining the core categories described in Chapter 16: the grain tax (so) on allotted rice land; the levy in textiles or local products (chō); the corvée-exemption levy commonly paid in cloth or other goods (yō); and the additional compulsory labor obligation (zōyō). The provincial loans (suiko), which had become a quasi-tax, were also adjusted. These reforms did not end the ritsuryō system; they modified its details. The gap between legal prescription and actual collection, which Chapter 16 emphasized, continued to shape provincial society, and the movement of people to escape obligations and to seek the protection of powerful patrons continued as well.

The court also tried to control the growth of private landholding and the acquisition of land by temples and elites, though the record shows that these measures had limited effect. Land opened by reclamation and held under the decrees of the 720s and 740s remained a channel through which powerful families and institutions could accumulate holdings, foreshadowing the estates (shōen) that Chapter 20 will examine.

Continuity within Reform

Kanmu's reforms therefore worked within the ritsuryō framework. They aimed at accountability, efficiency, and reduced burden, and they experimented with new offices and practices. But they left the fundamental structure, including dependence on local elites, in place. The reforms also show the continuing importance of the provincial elites described in Chapter 16. The military reforms discussed in the next section, for example, drew directly on the sons of district magistrates and wealthy households.

Section 17.8

Military Reform and the Northeastern War

Chapter 16 ended with the revolt of 780 and the unsettled frontier Kanmu inherited. This section completes the Kanmu-era frontier story, and it continues Chapter 16's interpretation: the northeastern wars were not the triumph of "Japan" over barbarians but a changing frontier involving warfare, incorporation, settlement, negotiated surrender, and competing political actors, narrated for us by the court.

Failure in 789

After 780, fighting and negotiation continued in Mutsu. In 788 the court ordered a large mobilization of troops from eastern provinces to gather at Tagajō, and Ki no Kosami was appointed to lead the expedition. In 789, according to the chronicle, the army advanced into the interior and suffered a serious defeat at the hands of forces led by Aterui, a leader recorded in the chronicle with a kabane title, near the Kitakami River. Many of the court's soldiers were killed, and the chronicle reports heavy losses among those who tried to retreat across the river. The numbers given for the armies, in the tens of thousands, should be treated cautiously, since chronicle figures can reflect the scale of mobilization orders rather than of forces actually fielded. The defeat was nonetheless a shock. It showed that a large, poorly integrated conscript army could be outmaneuvered by a mobile force fighting on ground it knew, and it strained the households that supplied men and provisions.

Changing How the Army Was Raised

In 792 the court abolished the provincial conscript units (gundan) in most of the realm, retaining them only in regions of special military concern such as Mutsu, Dewa, and the western Saikaidō. In their place it created a system of selected recruits (kondei), drawn from the sons of district magistrates and other well-off local families, who served as guards and could be armed and trained. The change reflected several problems: the expense of broad conscription, the burden on ordinary households, the poor performance of conscripts, and the court's reliance on local elites. It also had consequences. It tied military capacity more closely to provincial elite families, giving them a formal role in armed force, and it points toward the development of specialized military households, a subject Chapters 20 and 21 will develop. The kondei system was not a warrior class, but it was a step in the changing relationship between the state and armed local elites.

Historical Vocabulary
Sei-i taishōgun

Sei-i taishōgun ("great general who subdues the barbarians") was a temporary military command title conferred on commanders of campaigns against the Emishi. It was first held by Ōtomo no Otomaro in the 790s, and Sakanoue no Tamuramaro held it from 797.

The title should not be confused with the later office of shogun held by Minamoto no Yoritomo and by the Ashikaga and Tokugawa rulers (Chapters 24, 29, and 41). In the early ninth century it designated a commander appointed for a particular expedition under the emperor, not the head of a warrior government. Its later prestige came from historical memory and from its adoption by warrior rulers centuries afterward.

Campaigns, Tamuramaro, and Isawa

Kanmu increased his commitment. In the mid-790s, an army under Ōtomo no Otomaro, with Sakanoue no Tamuramaro as one of his subordinate commanders, campaigned in the northeast, and the court received reports of success. Tamuramaro, a member of a lineage descended from immigrants, was appointed sei-i taishōgun in 797 and led a further campaign in 801. In 802 the court built a new fort at Isawa, deeper in the interior. Isawa later became the seat of the northeastern military command, the Chinjufu, when that command was transferred from Tagajō in 808, after Kanmu's death. The court also ordered the transfer of large numbers of households from eastern provinces to settle the region, echoing the settlement policies described in Chapter 16. A further fort followed at Shiwa in 803. The frontier had moved northward, and the state's presence in the interior now rested on a chain of fortified sites, settlers, and alliances.

Aterui, More, and 802

In 802, according to the Nihon Kōki, Aterui and another leader, More, surrendered with several hundred followers. Both were recorded with kabane titles that marked their recognized standing. The chronicle reports that Tamuramaro brought them to the capital and argued that they should be spared and used as a means of pacifying their communities. Court officials, however, rejected the plan, arguing, as the chronicle paraphrases them, that releasing the two leaders would be dangerous, and the two were executed in Kawachi Province. The episode is instructive. It shows that surrender and incorporation were real options, and that a commander on the ground could favor them, while officials at the capital could choose punishment. It also shows the limits of the court's promises, which frontier leaders had reason to distrust.

The chronicle presents Aterui as a rebel, and later tradition, particularly in Iwate, has remembered him as a heroic leader of his people. Neither label is adequate. The court's account reflects the language of the state; local memory reflects later concerns. Historians must keep the Chapter 16 framework in view: "Emishi" was a court category, and Aterui's authority rested on local leadership in a region with its own political relationships. The surrender in 802 was not the final conquest of the northeast. Other communities remained outside court control, the court's fort line rested on a thin base of settlers and troops, and further campaigns would be attempted in the following decade, including another major operation in 811, after this chapter's narrative ends. Captured and surrendered Emishi were also relocated to provinces far from their homes, a policy that scattered communities and placed people labeled fushū in provincial society, where some later served in military roles. The final consequences of these policies belong to later chapters.

Section 17.9

Religion in the New Political Geography

Chapter 15 traced the alliance between the throne and Buddhist institutions. The founding of Heian-kyō did not end it, but it altered its geography. The court did not simply transplant Nara's great monasteries wholesale into the new capital. Tōdaiji, Kōfukuji, Yakushiji, and other major temples remained at Nara, which came to be called the "southern capital" (Nanto) and remained a center of Buddhist learning, ritual, and landholding. In Heian-kyō itself, the court allowed only Tōji and Saiji, established as state temples near the southern gate. The decision reduced the immediate presence in the capital of institutions with large followings and landholdings, and it reflected a wish to keep Buddhism within a framework the court could supervise.

Kanmu's government also issued measures regulating ordination, clerical conduct, and temple landholding, and it scrutinized the granting of official recognition to new religious communities. None of this made the court secular. Buddhist rituals continued to protect the state and the imperial family, and Kanmu himself was a patron of temples and monks. The relationship changed in form: rather than an alliance centered on great monasteries at the capital, it became a relationship with communities that were located on mountains around the city and led by monks who cultivated ties with the court.

Saichō, Mount Hiei, and the Missions to Tang

The most important such figure of Kanmu's reign was Saichō, a monk who withdrew to Mount Hiei, northeast of the site of the future capital, in the 780s and established a small community there. Saichō came to the emperor's attention, and Kanmu supported his study of the Tiantai (Tendai) tradition. In 804, a mission to Tang China sailed with four ships; Saichō traveled with it and spent time in China studying Tiantai teachings and receiving esoteric initiations before returning in 805. Kūkai, another monk, was a member of the same mission and traveled on a different ship; he stayed in China longer, returning in 806, and his story, with the Shingon tradition, will be taken up later. In January 806 the court authorized two annual ordinands for Saichō's Tendai program, a decision conventionally treated as official recognition of Japanese Tendai. The rise of Tendai on Mount Hiei gave the new capital a religious complex on its northeastern edge, an arrangement whose implications for court politics, religious institutions, and later warfare would unfold over centuries.

The missions to Tang also reveal continuing connection with the continental world. The court sent envoys, students, and monks to China at considerable expense, and they returned with texts, ideas, and objects. At the same time, the court received missions from Balhae, whose ships crossed the Sea of Japan. The court's international relations, like its domestic politics, were carried out from a capital that was moving.

The broader point is that the move did not secularize government; it reconfigured the relationship between imperial authority and Buddhist institutions. Local shrines and cults also continued. The Kamo shrines north of the city, for instance, were tied to local families and would come to hold a prominent place in court ritual, and the older belief in kami, as well as newer concerns about vengeful spirits, shaped court practice alongside Buddhism.

Section 17.10

What Did the Founding of Heian-kyō Actually Change?

By the early 800s the court had committed itself to two enormous undertakings: building a new capital and prosecuting a long war in the northeast. In 805, according to the Nihon Kōki, Kanmu asked Fujiwara no Otsugu and Sugano no Mamichi to debate the two great burdens of the reign: capital construction and the campaigns against the Emishi. Otsugu argued that both should stop because they were exhausting the people; Mamichi defended their continuation. Kanmu accepted Otsugu's position and ordered the projects curtailed. The episode is significant because it shows a ruler weighing the human cost of his own policies, but it must be read critically: the Nihon Kōki was compiled decades later, and Otsugu himself later participated in its compilation. What is clear is that by 805 the court's resources were strained, and the emperor was old and ill. He died in 806.

Continuity and Change Over Time

The Nara State and the Early Heian State

What Changed

  • The geographic center of government moved from the Nara basin to Yamashiro, closer to river and sea routes.
  • The court's relationship to Nara's great temples changed, with only two official temples permitted in the new capital and new communities growing on Mount Hiei.
  • Urban geography changed, with a new plan, new wards, and new patterns of aristocratic residence.
  • Some administrative and military practices changed, including new oversight of provincial governors, a longer interval between allotments, and the replacement of conscript units by selected recruits in most provinces.
  • The relation between center and frontier changed, as forts and settlers moved the northeastern line to Isawa and Shiwa.
  • Political opportunities among court families shifted with the relocation and the fall of some families and the rise of others.

What Stayed the Same

  • The emperor and the aristocratic court remained the center of legitimate authority.
  • The ritsuryō offices, ranks, registers, and provinces continued, alongside new extra-statutory offices.
  • Taxation in grain, goods, cloth, and labor continued, with its familiar gap between prescription and collection.
  • Provincial administration still depended on governors and local elites.
  • Buddhist institutions continued to participate in political life and state ritual.
  • The tension between legal ideals and actual governing capacity continued.

Who experienced change most directly? Court families, temples, and households near the capital experienced the relocation most directly, as did northeastern communities and eastern households drawn into the wars. Many provincial households experienced greater continuity, since their obligations remained largely the same. Why the pattern matters: It shows that 794 was a reconstruction within a continuing system, not the birth of a new state.

The chapter's central question can now be answered. Kanmu moved the capital first to Nagaoka and then to Heian-kyō as part of a wider attempt to reorganize court government, provincial administration, military policy, and imperial authority. The moves cannot be reduced to a reaction against Buddhist monasteries. They involved dynastic ambition, political insecurity, geography, cost, crisis, and ritual anxiety, and they were carried out together with reforms that adjusted but did not abolish the ritsuryō state. The result was a new capital and a modified system, both created under pressure and neither settled by 806.

Nor was the outcome inevitable. After Kanmu's death, his son Heizei reigned briefly and, after abdicating, tried in 810 to return the court to Nara, a move his brother Emperor Saga suppressed. That episode, which Chapter 18 will discuss, shows that Heian-kyō's permanence was not guaranteed in 806. It also points to the next question. If Heian-kyō established the new center, who actually accumulated power within that court? The answer, involving marriage politics, regency, aristocratic competition, and the changing meaning of imperial legitimacy, is the subject of Chapter 18, and the court culture that flourished in the new capital is the subject of Chapter 19. The estates and provincial military families whose roots this chapter has traced belong to Chapters 20 and 21.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined why Emperor Kanmu moved the capital first to Nagaoka-kyō and then to Heian-kyō, and how those moves formed part of a wider attempt to reorganize court government, provincial administration, military policy, and imperial authority. Its central argument is that 794 was not the beginning of an entirely new system. Kanmu inherited institutions, fiscal problems, frontier conflicts, aristocratic rivalries, and religious-political tensions from the Nara state, and the founding of Heian-kyō changed the geography of government while preserving much of the ritsuryō framework.

Kanmu came to the throne in 781 as a member of the Tenji line, after a succession crisis that had opened his path late. The reign began in insecurity, with plots and punishments, an unresolved northeastern frontier, and a fiscal system strained by unreliable registers, evasion, and heavy demands from the capital, the temples, and the frontier. Moving the capital was a way of reopening the question of how the court related to its aristocracy, its temples, its provinces, and its frontier.

Historians offer several overlapping explanations for leaving Nara: political reorientation, dynastic considerations, geographic and logistical advantages, and fiscal calculation. The old formula that Kanmu moved because Buddhist monks controlled Nara captures one real concern but is too narrow. The chronicles record actions more than motives, and the evidence does not permit a single answer.

Nagaoka-kyō, chosen in 784 for its position near the Yodo river system and outside the Nara basin, was built with enormous labor, reusing materials from earlier palaces. Its early years were shattered by the assassination of Fujiwara no Tanetsugu in 785 and the fall of Prince Sawara, who was accused of complicity, deposed, and died on the way to exile. The evidence does not establish Sawara's guilt, and later beliefs that his vengeful spirit troubled the imperial family are evidence about court belief and ritual, not causes of the events attributed to it.

The court abandoned Nagaoka after about a decade because of a convergence of factors: political instability and ritual anxiety, the cost and slow pace of construction, environmental problems including flooding, and the appeal of a site at Kadono. Heian-kyō, founded in 794, was laid out on a grid with a central avenue, palace complex, two markets, and only two official temples. It drew on Chinese models but was not a copy of Chang'an, and it was far from complete or densely settled at its founding.

Kanmu's administrative reforms worked within the ritsuryō framework. They included an office to audit departing governors, a longer interval between land reallotments, adjustments to burdens on provincial households, and warnings against abuses, while the categories of so, chō, yō, and zōyō continued and the state still depended on local elites. The pattern was to add new offices and practices alongside the old rather than to abolish the system.

The northeastern war reached its climax in this reign. After the defeat of Ki no Kosami's army in 789, the court replaced most conscript units with selected recruits drawn from the sons of local elites, increased its commitment of men and resources, and moved the frontier north with the founding of Isawa in 802. Aterui and More surrendered and were executed in 802 despite the reported advocacy of Tamuramaro for clemency. Consistent with Chapter 16, the wars involved warfare, incorporation, settlement, and negotiated surrender, and they did not conquer the whole northeast.

Religion was reconfigured rather than displaced. The court did not transplant Nara's great monasteries, which remained at Nanto, and allowed only two official temples in the new capital. New Buddhist communities, especially Saichō's Tendai on Mount Hiei, grew on the capital's edges, and the missions to Tang in 804 brought both Saichō and Kūkai to China. The court's relationship with Buddhism remained close but took a new geographic and institutional form.

The founding of Heian-kyō thus changed the center of government, the court's relation to Nara institutions, urban geography, some administrative and military practices, and the relationship between center and frontier, while emperor, aristocracy, ritsuryō offices, taxation, provincial dependence on local elites, and Buddhist participation in politics continued. It ended not with triumph but with a decision in 805 to halt both construction and war, and Kanmu's death in 806. The outcome was not inevitable, as the attempt in 810 to return to Nara would show.

The chapter hands Chapter 18 its question: if Heian-kyō established the new center, who accumulated power within that court? The answer lies in marriage politics, regency, aristocratic competition, and imperial legitimacy.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Heijō-kyō
The Nara capital, founded in 710, whose association with great temples and entrenched networks formed part of the background to Kanmu's move.
Nagaoka-kyō
Capital of 784–794 in Yamashiro Province, near the confluence of rivers forming the Yodo; abandoned after political crisis and doubts about the site.
Heian-kyō
"Capital of Peace and Tranquility," founded in 794 at Kadono; the city later called Kyoto.
Yamashiro
The province north of the Nara basin in which both Nagaoka and Heian were located; its written name was altered in 794.
Daidairi
The large palace enclosure of Heian-kyō containing the emperor's residence and the offices of government.
Suzaku Avenue
The broad central avenue running south from the palace to Rashōmon, dividing the city into the Left and Right Capitals.
Sakyō and Ukyō
The Left (eastern) and Right (western) Capitals of Heian-kyō; the western half proved less attractive and was less densely settled over time.
Tōji and Saiji
The two state temples planned near the southern gate of Heian-kyō; construction administration is documented from the later 790s, and the complexes took years to develop. They were the only major temples permitted within the city.
Nanto
"Southern capital," the later name for Nara and its great temples, which remained centers of learning and landholding.
Onryō
A vengeful spirit, believed to be the angry spirit of a person who died unjustly; a category of belief studied as evidence of contemporary interpretation, not as a cause of events.
Goryō
A placated or honored spirit; ceremonies to appease such spirits developed in the ninth century.
Kokushi
Provincial governors appointed by the court for limited terms.
Gunji
District magistrates drawn from locally rooted elite families.
So
The grain tax on allotted rice land.
Chō
A levy in textiles or local products sent toward the center, owed by taxable males.
Yō
A corvée-exemption levy commonly paid in cloth or other goods, owed by taxable males.
Zōyō
An additional compulsory labor obligation, often imposed for provincial projects such as construction.
Ryōge no kan
Extra-statutory offices created outside the original ritsuryō codes to address new problems.
Kageyushi
An office established in 797 to audit the documents by which departing governors certified handover of their duties.
Gundan
Provincial conscript military units, abolished in 792 in most provinces.
Kondei
Selected recruits, drawn from the sons of district magistrates and other well-off families, who replaced conscripts as provincial guards.
Sei-i taishōgun
A temporary command title for leaders of campaigns against the Emishi; not the later office of shogun.
Chinjufu
The northeastern military command; it remained based at Tagajō through Kanmu's reign and was transferred to Isawa in 808.
Isawa
Fort founded in 802 in the northeastern interior, moving the frontier's focus northward.
Kabane
An ancient hereditary status title borne by central and regional lineages; titles of this kind appear in the recorded names of Aterui and More.
Fushū and ifu
Court status labels for Emishi drawn into submission or incorporation; meanings shifted over time.
Tendai
A Buddhist tradition based on Tiantai teachings, brought from China by Saichō and centered on Mount Hiei.
Shoku Nihongi
Official chronicle covering 697–791, completed in 797 under Kanmu's government.
Nihon Kōki
Official chronicle completed in 840 covering the years from 792, preserved only in part; a principal source for the later years of Kanmu's reign.
Extra-statutory reform
The pattern, characteristic of this period, of adding new offices and practices alongside the ritsuryō system rather than abolishing it.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a political order changes without being replaced. The founding of Heian-kyō is often described as the beginning of a new era, but the evidence shows a ruler and court working under pressure, trying one capital, abandoning it, and building another, while adjusting an inherited system of law, taxation, and provincial government. Seeing this process makes it harder to treat period boundaries as sharp breaks and easier to understand how institutions are rebuilt from within.

The chapter also reveals larger processes that run through the book: the dependence of central authority on provincial elites, the entanglement of religion and politics, the effect of frontier war on military organization, and the ways in which rulers and later writers interpret crises through ritual and memory. The kondei system, the extra-statutory offices, and the fort line at Isawa all point forward to a world in which power would become more dispersed, a story that Chapters 20 and 21 will develop.

The chapter connects regions of the archipelago and the wider world. Households from many provinces supplied labor for the capital and men for the frontier; Emishi communities shaped the course of the northeastern war; Baekje-descended and other immigrant lineages helped build and staff the new state; and missions to Tang and Balhae kept the court in contact with the continent. The capital moved, but its life depended on networks that extended far beyond it.

Finally, the chapter invites careful reading of sources. The chronicles that describe Nagaoka, Sawara, and Aterui were written by officials of the court, and their language of accusation, rebellion, and appeasement reflects court interests. Students who ask who compiled a source, when, and for whom are prepared for Chapter 18, where the question shifts from where the capital stood to who held power within it.
