Part VII — Tokugawa Japan

The Floating World: Kabuki, Bunraku, Ukiyo-e, Geisha, and the Pleasure Districts

Japanese History: From Jōmon Japan to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

In 1794, a few months of work by a designer known only as Tōshūsai Sharaku produced some of the most psychologically intense portraits in the history of Japanese printmaking. They depicted kabuki actors, and they were published by a Yoshiwara-born bookseller who understood that faces, names, and rumors could be sold by the sheet. The prints are now treasured museum objects. When they first appeared they were consumer goods, issued by a businessman, carved and printed by specialists, and bought by city people who already knew the actors, the plays, and the gossip.

That double identity, art and merchandise, is the key to this chapter. Chapter 45 traced how roads, castle towns, merchant houses, and rapidly growing cities created an urban economy unlike anything earlier Japan had known. Edo, Osaka, and Kyoto contained hundreds of thousands of people whose lives were organized by wages, rents, retail prices, and credit. This chapter asks what those city people bought when they had money left over after buying food and cloth. The answer was experience: performances, companionship, fashion, images, reputation, and the pleasure of being part of a shared urban scene. The commodities of the “floating world” were intangible as often as tangible.

The term itself, ukiyo, has long been translated as “floating world,” and the phrase has acquired a glamorous aura in modern popular culture. It conjures lantern-lit pleasure quarters, elegant courtesans, dashing actors, and exquisite woodblock prints. This chapter argues that the glamour is real but incomplete. The floating world was not a carefree zone outside Tokugawa society. It was produced by that society: by its commercial growth, its status distinctions, its gender hierarchies, its household economy, and above all by its regulatory institutions. Authorities licensed, confined, taxed, inspected, restricted, and sometimes punished the very institutions that made the floating world possible. The tension between performance and regulation, and between freedom and inequality, is the central theme of the chapter.

Three distinctions organize the chapter and must be kept clear throughout. First, a courtesan was not a geisha. Licensed courtesans worked in a system centered on regulated sexual commerce, although the highest-ranking women also cultivated extensive artistic and social skills. Geisha were professional entertainers whose skills lay in music, dance, conversation, and hospitality; the two groups shared districts and customers, but their institutions differed. Second, ukiyo-e was not simply “pictures of geisha.” It was a broad commercial visual culture of actors, beauties, famous places, warriors, fashion, and erotica, produced through a collaborative publishing economy. Third, the glamorous image that survives is not a fair sample of the people involved. Most of what survives was made for consumers, and the labor of apprentices, servants, carvers, printers, stagehands, and women bound by contract is far harder to recover.

The chapter proceeds in stages. It begins by explaining what ukiyo meant and how Tokugawa authorities governed urban pleasure. It then examines the licensed quarters, the women who lived and worked in them, the realities of debt and coercion, the history of sexuality in commercial settings, and the emergence of geisha. Next it turns to the theater: kabuki, with its theaters, actors, and fans, and puppet theater (ningyō jōruri, later widely called bunraku) with its great playwright Chikamatsu Monzaemon. Finally it studies ukiyo-e as a print industry, then fashion, censorship, and the social inequalities that sustained the entire system.

The evidence is unusually rich and unusually biased. Historians draw on playbills, actor-critique booklets, courtesan rankings, guidebooks to the licensed quarters, popular fiction, prints, sumptuary edicts, magistrates’ records, theater account books, and the surviving texts of plays. Almost all of it was produced by or for male consumers, publishers, or officials. Women who worked in the licensed quarters left comparatively little in their own voices, and the extant records of their experience often come through brothel owners, authors, and artists with commercial interests. A responsible account must read these sources critically, asking at every point who made them, for whom, and what they leave out.

Chapter 46 stands between neighboring chapters with distinct responsibilities. Chapter 45 supplied the cities, markets, and consumers. Chapter 47 will turn to religion, temples, shrines, Confucian ethics, and the regulation of belief, while Chapter 48 examines the controlled international world, and Chapter 49 treats sumo, martial schools, crafts, festivals, food, and everyday leisure more broadly. Chapter 50 will take up the wider history of literacy, publishing, reform politics, gambling networks, and the criminal underworld. This chapter touches these themes only where they directly illuminate the commercial culture of entertainment. Its subject is the commercialization of pleasure and the contest between cultural freedom and social constraint.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how the term ukiyo changed in meaning and why the “floating world” should be understood as a commercial urban culture rather than an escape from Tokugawa society.
  2. Analyze how Tokugawa authorities licensed, concentrated, and supervised theaters and pleasure quarters as part of urban governance.
  3. Compare the licensed quarters of Edo (Yoshiwara), Kyoto (Shimabara), and Osaka (Shinmachi) and the surrounding unlicensed sex trade.
  4. Distinguish licensed courtesans from geisha, and explain why the two categories are routinely confused in modern popular culture.
  5. Assess the roles of contract, debt, and coercion in the lives of women in licensed quarters, distinguishing cultural prestige from labor freedom.
  6. Trace the development of kabuki from the early seventeenth century and explain how regulation reshaped rather than ended popular theater.
  7. Describe kabuki and puppet theater as commercial institutions with managers, performers, audiences, supporting trades, and celebrity cultures.
  8. Interpret the plays of Chikamatsu Monzaemon as commercial entertainment that dramatized tensions between social obligation and human feeling.
  9. Explain how ukiyo-e was produced and sold, and identify the roles of publisher, designer, carver, and printer.
  10. Evaluate the effects of sumptuary regulation and censorship on fashion, publishing, and performance, and the ways participants adapted.
  11. Evaluate competing historical interpretations of whether the floating world offered genuine social freedom.
  12. Avoid romanticized or purely condemnatory generalizations by recovering the unequal labor and varied experiences within the floating world.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How did the economic growth described in Chapter 45 create the audiences, markets, and institutions of the floating world?
  2. Why did Tokugawa authorities choose to license and concentrate urban pleasure rather than simply prohibit it, and what did this arrangement cost and benefit different groups?
  3. In what ways could theaters and pleasure quarters temporarily blur distinctions of status, and in what ways did they reinforce hierarchy?
  4. How should historians weigh the artistic achievements of the floating world against the coercion and debt that sustained parts of it?
  5. Why did regulation of kabuki change its form rather than eliminate it, and what does this show about the relationship between state power and popular culture?
  6. How did commercial publishing turn actors, courtesans, and places into celebrities and brands?
  7. What can surviving sources, produced mostly by consumers, publishers, and officials, reveal about the lives of performers and workers, and what can they not reveal?
  8. Why is it historically misleading to treat the floating world as either an elegant erotic paradise or nothing but exploitation?

Historical Context

By the mid-seventeenth century the Tokugawa settlement had produced a political order in which warriors were gathered in castle towns, daimyō and their retinues traveled regularly to and from Edo under the system of alternate attendance, and the shogunal capital became an enormous consumer city. Chapter 45 described how this system financed highways, concentrated spending, and fed a merchant economy. Edo’s population included samurai retainers, many of them living away from their families, as well as the artisans, laborers, and shopkeepers who supplied them. Osaka functioned as the great commercial and financial center of the realm, and Kyoto remained both the imperial seat and a center of luxury crafts. Each city developed its own tastes, and the relationship among the three is a recurring theme of this chapter.

The Tokugawa order also produced a legal status system that Chapter 43 examined. Samurai, farmers, artisans, and merchants were distinguished in law, though the neat “four-class” image conceals many overlapping realities, and groups such as the eta and hinin occupied separately regulated positions. Entertainers were not part of this simple scheme. Many actors, puppeteers, and itinerant performers occupied ambiguous and often stigmatized social positions, even when they earned large incomes. Prosperous townspeople, meanwhile, often had more money than many samurai households. Throughout the period, wealth and formal rank were imperfectly aligned, and this misalignment gave the culture of the cities much of its energy and anxiety.

Finally, the floating world depended on technologies and institutions of communication. Commercial printing, which had expanded dramatically in the seventeenth century, made it possible to produce books, playbills, guidebooks, and single-sheet images for paying audiences. The shamisen, a plucked three-stringed instrument that reached the main islands from the Ryukyu Islands in the sixteenth century, became central to theatrical and entertainment music; it is a reminder that Ryukyuan culture contributed to the soundscape of Tokugawa cities. Chapter 45 has already explained how roads and markets circulated people and goods. This chapter explains how they circulated images, performers’ names, and fashions.

Section 46.1

From Consumer Society to the Floating World

Chapter 45 explained the economic infrastructure of urban life: the highways and post stations, the rice and money markets of Osaka, the wholesale and retail networks that supplied Edo, the merchant houses that built fortunes on credit and distribution. This chapter begins where that infrastructure leaves off. A city of several hundred thousand or, in Edo’s case, roughly a million people contains a very large number of households with some surplus income, and also a very large number of people, especially migrant men, living outside conventional family settings. Samurai retainers who accompanied their lords to Edo, apprentices, day laborers, and artisans all lived in a city with many more commercial opportunities than in the villages that many of them had left. Demand for companionship, amusement, and display grew accordingly.

Historians of the period often describe this as the emergence of a consumer culture, but the phrase needs care. Not everyone could afford theater tickets or the fees of a leading courtesan, and large numbers of urban residents lived close to subsistence. What the commercial city created was a graded market. At the top were wealthy merchants, some prosperous samurai, and the rare daimyō retainer with funds to entertain; at the bottom were cheap standing places in a theater, inexpensive prints, and street-level amusements. The result was not a single leisure culture but a ladder of experiences priced for different budgets, and the ladder itself became a source of social meaning. Where one sat, what one wore, and which teahouse one frequented all communicated information about wealth, taste, and connections.

What was new was not that entertainment existed. Court music, temple festivals, wandering performers, and village ritual drama all predated the Tokugawa period. What changed was that performance, companionship, fashion, and images became commodities sold on a continuous basis to a paying urban public by specialized businesses. Theaters sold tickets and refreshments. Licensed houses sold time and hospitality. Publishers sold images of actors and courtesans to people who might never attend the performances or visit the quarter. This is why the floating world is best understood as an institutional and economic phenomenon, not only an aesthetic one. Its commodities were experience, performance, image, fashion, companionship, and reputation, and its institutions were built to produce them at scale.

Section 46.2

What Was the “Floating World”? Ukiyo Reimagined

The word ukiyo has a layered history. Written with characters that can be read as “sorrowful” or “gloomy” world, it had been used in earlier Japanese literature and Buddhist-inflected thought to evoke a transient, unsatisfactory, and impermanent existence. Beginning in the seventeenth century, a different set of characters, read the same way and meaning “floating,” came into use, and the term acquired a new association with present-day pleasures, fashion, and the entertainment of the cities. The shift was partly a pun and partly a change of attitude: if the world is fleeting, one might as well enjoy it. This transformation is often traced through urban fiction of the later seventeenth century, including works by Asai Ryōi and, most famously, Ihara Saikaku, whose stories of merchants, lovers, and pleasure-seekers made him a commercial success in the Genroku era.

Students should be careful not to imagine that every resident of Edo, Osaka, or Kyoto consciously subscribed to a unified “philosophy of the floating world.” Ukiyo was a literary and cultural label that writers, publishers, and later scholars attached to particular spaces and activities: the licensed quarter, the theater district, the fashion trade, and the books and pictures that circulated about them. Many people who attended the theater or bought a print would never have described their lives in these terms. The term also took on different connotations over time, and the modern English phrase “floating world” carries romantic associations that were not necessarily present for contemporaries, for whom the word could be wry, ironic, or cheerfully commercial.

The related term ukiyo-e, “pictures of the floating world,” appears in the later seventeenth century for images of urban life and its entertainments. It was then applied more broadly to a developing commercial print and painting tradition. The category is partly retrospective: modern scholars and collectors have used it to group together works that contemporaries might have described by subject or format. This matters because it explains why ukiyo-e came to include far more than depictions of pleasure quarters, a point developed in Sections 46.15 and 46.16.

Historical Vocabulary: Ukiyo
Ukiyo (浮世)

Ukiyo, translated “floating world,” names the urban culture of commercial pleasure, fashion, theater, and popular publishing that flourished in Tokugawa cities. The older homophone meaning “sorrowful world” expressed Buddhist ideas of impermanence; the newer written form turned that sense toward living for the moment. Its meaning was not fixed: it could suggest wistful transience, wry irony, or celebration of fashionable consumption, depending on author and period.

The term matters because it names a cultural system, not a separate place. A common modern misreading imagines the floating world as a world apart, a bohemian realm outside normal society. In fact, as this chapter shows, its theaters, quarters, and publishing houses were embedded in the same legal, economic, and gender systems as the rest of Tokugawa society, and were governed by many of the same officials.

Section 46.3

Governing Pleasure: Licensed Entertainment as Tokugawa Policy

It is tempting to imagine the Tokugawa authorities as moralists who reluctantly tolerated vice. The evidence suggests a more pragmatic approach. Rather than attempting to abolish urban entertainment and commercial sex, the shogunate and local officials generally sought to concentrate, license, segregate, and supervise them. This approach responded to real concerns. In the early seventeenth century, cities contained large numbers of masterless warriors (rōnin), disaffected young men, and groups sometimes called kabuki-mono, flamboyant, “off-kilter” toughs whose behavior alarmed officials. Brawling, gambling, and unlicensed prostitution were associated, in official thinking, with disorder. Confining pleasure to designated districts allowed authorities to watch it, to tax or otherwise extract revenue from it where possible, and to limit its spread into residential neighborhoods.

The logic of licensing created a particular institutional structure. Licensed quarters were enclosed or clearly delimited and were entered through controlled gates. Operators of houses received permission to run businesses within the quarter, and in return were subject to rules about who could enter and leave, what activities were allowed, and how disputes and crimes were handled. The quarter had its own internal administration, often through headmen and associations, which in turn answered to town magistrates. Theaters likewise operated under official license, were restricted to designated locations, and were subject to regulation over matters such as plays, costumes, hours, and the behavior of actors. The pattern was not identical across cities or periods, but its general shape is clear: pleasure was governed by being given a legal address.

This had two important consequences. First, it makes sense to describe Edo’s Yoshiwara and the theater districts as part of Tokugawa urban governance, not as autonomous countercultures. They were products of political decisions about where and how commercial entertainment should exist. Second, the system created a boundary between licensed and unlicensed activity that shaped everything from prices to risk. The licensed world enjoyed legal protection and prestige, while unlicensed prostitution in other districts and at post stations was periodically suppressed and its participants punished. The licensed quarters were therefore both a privilege for those who held licenses and a source of exclusion for those who did not.

Section 46.4

Yoshiwara, Shimabara, and Shinmachi: The Licensed Quarters

Edo’s Yoshiwara received official permission in 1617, in a location near the commercial heart of the early city. After the Meireki fire of 1657 devastated Edo, the quarter was rebuilt farther north, in the Asakusa area, and became known as Shin-Yoshiwara. It was enclosed by a moat and walls and entered by a main gate, with a central street lined with teahouses and houses of different grades. Inside were the houses where women lived and worked, teahouses that arranged introductions and hospitality, shops, and a population of attendants, cooks, hairdressers, and other service workers. The quarter had its own festivals and annual rituals, including seasonal events when the central street was decorated, and these became subjects for artists and writers. At its height, the population of women in the quarter numbered in the thousands, though figures varied across the centuries and should be treated as approximate.

Kyoto’s Shimabara, established in its later location in 1640, and Osaka’s Shinmachi, established in the 1620s, had somewhat different cultures. Kyoto’s elite quarter had longstanding connections with the old capital’s aristocratic and literary traditions and with its luxury textile trade. Osaka’s Shinmachi was embedded in a merchant city whose wealth rested on commerce and finance; its customers were often wealthy merchants, and its social codes reflected commercial rather than warrior values. Nearby Dōtonbori, with its theaters and restaurants, gave Osaka a dense geography of commercial entertainment. The differences between the “Kamigata” culture of Kyoto and Osaka and the culture of Edo, which developed from a military capital with a male-heavy population, appear again and again in this chapter.

Yoshiwara dominates our evidence because of the quantity of surviving literature, guidebooks, and prints devoted to it, and it is easy to allow Edo to stand for the entire Tokugawa entertainment world. That would be a mistake. Licensed quarters existed in many cities and castle towns, and a great deal of commercial sex took place outside them. Edo had unlicensed districts known as okabasho, and the post stations on the great highways, including those on the outskirts of Edo, hosted inns where women served as both attendants and sex workers. These places were cheaper, less prestigious, and less protected than the licensed quarter, and women working in them faced greater legal risk. Any account of the “pleasure districts” that includes only the elegant licensed world misses the larger commercial sex economy of which it was a part.

Section 46.5

Courtesans: Rank, Training, Performance, and Labor

The women of the licensed quarters were organized into hierarchies. In the early decades, the very highest rank, often called tayū, represented a small elite of women who were expected to be skilled in music, poetry, calligraphy, conversation, tea, and the etiquette of aristocratic and warrior households. Over time, the details of the ranking system changed, and in Edo the top rank of tayū faded by the middle of the eighteenth century while other ranks and terms, such as oiran as a general designation for high-ranking courtesans, became prominent. Cities used different terminology, and the ranks were tied to price and customs about how a client approached a woman: through teahouses, with ceremonial processions, and over multiple visits. These were commercially structured rituals.

Young girls entered houses as attendants to senior women, learning etiquette, music, and the manners of the quarter. The most promising were trained for rank and might be promoted as they matured; others were destined for lower grades. For the highest ranks, performance was central. Elite courtesans were fashion leaders, whose clothing, hairstyles, and ornaments were widely imitated and depicted in prints. They conversed with customers in refined manners and were expected to display cultivated taste. In a society in which access to advanced literary and artistic training varied sharply by gender, household, region, and period, some courtesans were thus among the most accomplished performers and cultural figures of their age, and a few became famous well beyond the quarter. A courtesan of the highest rank was therefore not a “prostitute in fancy clothes”; her role included training, performance, and display as well as sexual commerce.

It is equally important not to let that prestige obscure the structure. Even the highest-ranking women worked within a business whose owners profited from their appearance, accomplishments, and sexual availability, and the great majority of women in licensed houses were not in the top ranks. Lower-ranking women served more customers at lower prices, lived in less comfortable conditions, and had little of the cultural capital that made a few courtesans celebrities. The existence of an elite should be understood as part of how the system worked: the visible prestige of the few helped legitimize and advertise an industry whose daily operations rested on the labor of the many. Section 46.6 examines the legal and financial arrangements that bound them.

Section 46.6

Contract, Debt, Coercion, and the Limits of Glamour

How did women come to work in the licensed quarters? Overwhelmingly, through the economic circumstances of their families. In a society in which poverty, famine, and debt could threaten rural and urban households alike, families sometimes arranged for daughters to enter service in the quarter in exchange for an advance payment. The arrangement was legally framed as a contract of limited-term service rather than the permanent sale of a person, and in principle the term was limited, with ten years commonly cited as a standard maximum. But the contractual form did not guarantee freedom. The advance was a debt that the woman owed to the house, and charges for clothing, bedding, food, training, and other costs were commonly added to the account. Women could find that their obligations extended beyond the original term, and the legal fiction of voluntary service coexisted with considerable pressure.

Women in the quarter lived under severe restrictions. Movement outside the walls was controlled, and attempts to flee were punished. The houses were crowded and subject to fire, and the risks of sexually transmitted disease, pregnancy, and dangerous attempts to end pregnancies were substantial. Historians remain cautious about statistics for mortality and length of service because the records are incomplete and were produced mainly by houses and officials, but the broad pattern of constrained labor is not in doubt. Many women were young when they entered, and the high-ranking women of the surviving imagery were a small minority of those who passed through the quarter.

At the same time, it would be an error to portray every woman as a passive victim without agency. Courtesans negotiated within the system: they could build reputations, cultivate regular customers, develop relationships, and in some cases receive gifts or support that improved their conditions or helped them buy out their contracts. A few women left the quarter to marry or establish businesses. Some became figures of influence in the cultural life of the city. Yet these options were possibilities within a structure that was fundamentally unequal, and the exceptional cases do not alter its nature. A useful way to hold the evidence together is to distinguish cultural prestige from labor freedom. A woman could be admired, imitated, and celebrated and still be bound by debt and confined by contract. The surviving literature and prints, produced mostly for customers, emphasize the first and downplay the second. Later reform movements in the Meiji period, discussed in Chapter 55, would attack contract prostitution directly, a reminder that contemporaries and successors could see the system’s coercive core.

Section 46.7

Sexuality in the Urban Entertainment World

Because sexuality was central to the commercial culture of the Tokugawa cities, it deserves direct and historical treatment. The most visible form was commercial heterosexual encounter in the licensed quarters and the unlicensed districts. But the early modern entertainment world also included a wider range of sexual practices and representations, and historians must be careful not to impose contemporary categories of sexual identity on them. Terms such as nanshoku, often rendered “male love” or “male eroticism,” and wakashu, adolescent youths who occupied a defined position in the sexual and social order, refer to practices, roles, and age-related hierarchies. They do not map cleanly onto modern ideas of heterosexual and homosexual identities. Many men could be described as having relationships with both women and youths, and cultural attitudes were shaped by age, status, and role rather than a single binary of orientation.

Male-male desire was part of the culture of warrior households, temples, and the urban entertainment world alike, and literature of the Genroku era treated it as a recognized subject. Saikaku, for example, wrote about it alongside his stories of heterosexual pleasure. In the theater districts, some young actors in training or in minor roles were also available to clients through arrangements with teahouses, a phenomenon sometimes referred to by the term kagema. Authorities regulated such activities, and the prohibition of wakashu kabuki in 1652, discussed in Section 46.9, was partly motivated by concerns about the sexual attraction of young performers and the disorder it provoked. Sexual commerce involving young males, like that involving women, took place within hierarchies of age, status, and money, and in many cases of coercion.

The visual and literary culture of the floating world also represented sexuality openly. Shunga, erotic prints and books, were produced by many of the major ukiyo-e artists and circulated widely, among readers of different classes. Guidebooks to the licensed quarters ranked and described houses and women. Popular fiction treated erotic adventures with humor and satire. Historians use these sources carefully because they were commercial products, designed to entertain and sell, and their depictions reflect market tastes and the fantasies of their audiences rather than the experience of the people portrayed. Official restrictions on erotic publication, discussed in Section 46.18, were intermittent, and the line between tolerated and prohibited content shifted. The subject for historians is practices, roles, regulation, representation, and commercial settings, not an attempt to assign modern identities to people who did not think in those terms.

Section 46.8

Geisha: Professional Entertainers, Not Courtesans

The word geisha literally means “person of the arts,” and its history is in important respects the opposite of the stereotype common in modern popular culture. The earliest people described by terms related to gei-sha in the eighteenth century were men, entertainers and jesters (often called taikomochi or hōkan) who enlivened banquets with talk, music, and humor. Women performers who danced and played the shamisen at banquets, sometimes called odoriko (dancers) or by related names, became prominent in the mid-eighteenth century, first in Edo’s Fukagawa and Yoshiwara areas, and by the later decades of the century women had largely supplanted men as the principal geisha. In Kyoto and Osaka, comparable entertainers developed, and Kyoto’s Gion, a district of teahouses that grew up near the Yasaka shrine, became one of the best known of the entertainment districts that employed them, with the Kyoto vocabulary of geiko and apprentice maiko.

Geisha were professional entertainers whose products were skill: singing, shamisen, dance, conversation, and the art of making a gathering pleasant and lively. Their relationship to the licensed quarters was complicated. They worked at banquets in houses and teahouses and thus overlapped spatially and commercially with courtesans. But regulations and customs generally distinguished them: in Yoshiwara, for example, geisha were typically registered through the quarter’s own administration and were expected not to compete with courtesans by selling sexual services. This rule was aimed at protecting the licensed houses’ monopoly. In practice, the boundary varied by place and period. Some districts, especially certain unlicensed ones outside the licensed quarters, had reputations for geisha who also provided sexual services, and individual circumstances differed.

The result is that any blanket statement, “geisha were prostitutes” or “geisha never sold sex,” is inaccurate. The more precise historical statement is that geisha constituted a distinct profession of entertainment whose institutions, training, and primary function differed from those of licensed courtesans, that the two groups worked in overlapping spaces and sometimes competed for the same patrons, and that the boundaries between categories were policed unevenly. Geisha were also workers within hierarchies of apprenticeship, debt, and dependence on houses and patrons, and their economic circumstances, like those of courtesans, could be precarious. The later image of the geisha as a timeless embodiment of Japanese tradition is largely a creation of subsequent centuries and is treated in later chapters.

Historical Vocabulary: Geisha
Geisha (芸者) and Geiko (芸妓)

Geisha are professional entertainers trained in arts such as shamisen, singing, dance, and conversation. The term first applied to men and then, from the middle of the eighteenth century, increasingly to women. In Kyoto, geiko is the common local term, and apprentices are called maiko. The English word “geisha” is often wrongly equated with “prostitute” or “courtesan.”

The distinction matters because it clarifies the structure of the entertainment economy. Courtesans were part of a licensed system of sexual commerce, though the top ranks cultivated performance skills. Geisha were part of a market for entertainment skills. They moved in shared spaces, but their roles, training, and regulation differed, and treating the categories as interchangeable erases the history of a profession.

Section 46.9

From Okuni to Kabuki: The Origins of a New Theater

The name kabuki derives from a verb meaning to lean or tilt, and by extension to act in an unconventional, flamboyant way. Later tradition associates the beginnings of the art with Izumo no Okuni, a woman described in sources as a performer connected with the Izumo shrine, who around the first years of the seventeenth century gave dance-dramas in Kyoto in which she dressed in provocative, fashionable costume and sometimes played the part of a dashing young man. The surviving evidence for her career is thin: contemporary diary entries and later accounts offer glimpses, and many details of the traditional story are retrospective. What is clear is that early kabuki emerged from a milieu of itinerant performers, shrine-affiliated entertainers, and the new fashions of the city, and that it was quickly imitated by troupes of women and others.

The early troupes, performing on riverbanks and in temporary theaters, were closely associated with prostitution: performers might offer sexual services to audience members, and the performances were occasions for disorderly competition among spectators. This association helps explain official concern. In 1629 the authorities banned women from performing on the kabuki stage. Troupes of adolescent boys and youths, wakashu kabuki, filled part of the gap, but they presented similar concerns about sexual attraction and rivalry, and in 1652 this form too was prohibited. Officials allowed adult male troupes to continue under conditions, including requirements that performers shave their forelocks, as adult men did, and that the performances focus on plays, not primarily on sensual display.

The important causal point is that regulation changed the institution rather than eliminating popular theater. Demand for theater did not disappear because women and youths were banned; instead, adult male actors developed a rich dramatic art, and specialists in female roles, the onnagata, emerged as artists who made the representation of femininity a central skill. The theater turned toward spoken drama, elaborate plots, and stage craft. Licensed troupes obtained permission to operate in fixed theaters, and the major cities came to have defined theater districts. The story of kabuki’s origins is thus not a simple tale of artistic invention but of an interaction among performers, audiences, and a regulatory state that sought to channel, not abolish, a popular pastime.

Historical Vocabulary: Onnagata
Onnagata (女形)

Onnagata are male actors who specialize in playing female roles in kabuki. The role became institutionalized after women were banned from the stage in 1629 and after the 1652 prohibition of wakashu performance. Practitioners treated the art as a discipline of cultivated technique, not simple impersonation, and noted onnagata influenced women’s fashion and deportment.

The term matters because it shows that the theater treated gender as a matter of performance and training. It is anachronistic to read the onnagata through modern debates about gender identity; the historical interest lies in how a commercial theater, under regulation, made the representation of femininity into a prestigious professional art.

Section 46.10

The Kabuki Theater as a Commercial Institution

Kabuki was not merely dramatic literature; it was a mass entertainment industry embedded in the urban economy. In Edo, the shogunate licensed a small number of principal theaters, traditionally three (the Nakamura-za, Ichimura-za, and Morita-za), whose licensed managers (zamoto) held rights to stage performances, with a few other venues operating in secondary status. In Osaka, the Dōtonbori district housed several theaters, and Kyoto had its own along the river near Shijō. A theater license was valuable and tradable property, and a manager’s role combined business, artistic, and political tasks: engaging actors and playwrights, negotiating with officials, and managing finances. Large theaters were distinctive buildings, recognizable by license emblems and drum towers, and were surrounded by teahouses, food stalls, and shops that depended on the crowds.

The theater year was organized around contracts. Actors were typically engaged for a season, and the annual “face-showing” (kaomise) performances in the eleventh month, when the new company was introduced, were major events. Programs were long: performances began early in the morning and could last through much of the day, with multiple plays and dances, and audiences ate, drank, and socialized during them. Seats were arranged in tiers, from expensive boxes to cheaper floor and gallery places, so that the cost of attendance mapped onto social and economic position. Seating and refreshments were often arranged through theater teahouses, which acted as intermediaries between the theater and affluent customers and added their own fees. The architecture and technology evolved: the hanamichi, a raised walkway through the audience, and mechanisms such as the revolving stage, which came into use in the eighteenth century, allowed spectacle and made the theater an arena of technical innovation.

The commercial structure also made theaters vulnerable. Wooden theaters were prone to fire, and fires destroyed or relocated them repeatedly. Theaters depended on patronage from the public and on official permission, and the removal of a license could be economically devastating. They also generated an ecosystem of related trades: scene painters, costume makers, wigmakers, hairdressers, printers of playbills and illustrated programs, and sellers of food and souvenirs. When Chapter 45 discussed the urban economy of Edo, it described a city of retail and service trades. The kabuki district was one of that city’s most concentrated and visible expressions.

Everyday Life and Environment

A Day at the Theater: Who Was There and What They Did

An outing to the theater was a social event as much as an artistic one. Families, groups of friends, and parties arranged through teahouses attended together, bringing or buying meals, and viewers moved, talked, and commented during performances, responding to favorite actors with calls and applause. Women attended in large numbers, and the theater was among the few public spaces where women of different ranks might see and be seen. Authorities and moralists periodically worried about this, particularly when women of higher status appeared, and the Ejima affair of 1714 is often cited as an example in which a high-ranking woman’s theater outing led to punishments and the closure of an Edo theater.

Behind the spectacle were large numbers of workers: ushers, ticket sellers, stagehands, musicians, dressers, and food vendors. Actors lived and trained in an environment of apprenticeship, in which children of theatrical families and trainees learned roles from senior performers. For the audience, the theater was a place where fashions were displayed and invented, where gossip circulated, and where actors’ names, crests, and styles were shared knowledge. For the performers and workers, it was a demanding and hierarchical workplace. Both experiences belong to the history of the floating world.

Section 46.11

Actors, Roles, and Celebrity: Aragoto, Wagoto, Onnagata

Stylistic traditions in kabuki developed regionally. In Edo, a bold, larger-than-life style called aragoto (“rough” style), featuring heroic figures, powerful gestures, dramatic makeup, and exaggerated costumes, became associated with the actor Ichikawa Danjūrō I (1660–1704) and the hereditary line that bore his name. In Kyoto and Osaka, the Kamigata tradition favored wagoto (“soft” style), portraying gentle, often foolish or vulnerable lovers in realistic contemporary settings, and the actor Sakata Tōjūrō I (1647–1709) is its most celebrated figure. These contrasts reflect the audiences and cultures of the cities: a warrior-dominated capital with a taste for bravura, and a merchant world more attuned to romantic and domestic drama. They should not be treated as fixed national “types”; styles influenced one another, and actors toured and borrowed.

The onnagata tradition reveals the theater’s sophisticated approach to gender. Yoshizawa Ayame I (1673–1729), an early eighteenth-century onnagata, is associated with teachings, later collected, that emphasized disciplined attention to feminine behavior as a lifelong craft, extending even to everyday life. Whether or not every detail of these later accounts is accurate, they show that practitioners and audiences treated the portrayal of women as an art requiring training and commitment. Onnagata were influential in women’s fashion, and their styles were imitated by female fans. The theater therefore offered a space in which gender was visibly performed, and the boundary between performance and everyday identity could be explored, within the strict legal frameworks that governed theater.

Actors’ social position was paradoxical. Legally and socially, actors were often stigmatized and in many places restricted to residence within theater districts, and they were not part of the standard status categories. Yet the leading performers earned large incomes, commanded devoted followings, and were among the most famous people in the cities. Hereditary stage names and lineages allowed celebrity to be reproduced across generations: a name such as Danjūrō carried accumulated reputation, which could be passed to a successor, and successions were themselves public events. The violent death of Danjūrō I, killed on stage by another actor in 1704, shows both the intensity of rivalries and the extraordinary public attention that attached to stars. Later in the period, Danjūrō VII was punished for ostentatious living during the Tenpō reforms, an example of the way fame and expenditure exposed actors to official censure.

Section 46.12

Audiences, Fans, and the Business of Celebrity

Kabuki connected to consumer culture in ways that extended far beyond the theater walls. Fans bought prints of actors in favorite roles, illustrated playbills, souvenirs, and booklets that ranked and reviewed performers in the style of rating guides. Publishers issued annual evaluations of actors, comparable to rankings applied to courtesans and, in a different domain, to sumo wrestlers, and these publications fed gossip and debate. Colors and patterns associated with famous actors, often their crests or the roles they played, became fashionable among urban consumers, so that a fan could wear a pattern linked to a favorite. In this way celebrity became a commodity that could be worn, displayed, and traded.

The point deserves emphasis because it challenges the assumption that celebrity culture is a product of modern mass media. In Tokugawa cities, actors and courtesans were famous for their appearances, reputations, and personal lives, and a commercial infrastructure existed to circulate their images and names. Fan groups organized themselves, and audiences expected performers to cultivate distinctive personas. At the same time, the relationship between fans and stars was unequal. Fans consumed images and information; performers were subject to the demands of contracts, rivals, and officials. The same commercial system that elevated stars could expose them to ruin.

The audience itself was socially varied. Wealthy merchants and some samurai were among the most lavish patrons, although authorities discouraged warriors from attending theaters, and those who did sometimes appeared in disguise. Artisans, shopkeepers, and their families made up much of the broader audience. This mixture is part of what made the theater a space where status distinctions could be blurred: a wealthy commoner in a good seat might be in closer proximity to a samurai than the formal status system would otherwise allow, and expensive places were open to anyone who could pay. But the theater also preserved gradations: the tiered seating mapped out wealth, and officials remained alert to inappropriate mixing. The theater was a place of temporary, conditional blurring, not of abolished hierarchy.

Section 46.13

Bunraku: Puppet Theater and the Power of the Narrator

The puppet theater of the Tokugawa period is usually called bunraku today, but this name is later than much of the tradition it describes. The more precise historical term for the commercial form of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries is ningyō jōruri, “puppet jōruri,” combining puppetry with jōruri, a style of chanted narrative accompanied by shamisen. The name Bunraku derives from a theater established in the early nineteenth century, which became the principal surviving company, and it came to be applied generally to the art. Using “bunraku” for the whole history is common, but students should understand that it is a modern label for an older, commercially diverse theater.

The form was a collaboration among three kinds of specialists. The chanter, or tayū (not to be confused with the courtesan rank of the same pronunciation), narrated the story and voiced all characters, using a wide range of vocal technique. The shamisen player accompanied the narration and established mood. The puppeteers manipulated large, sophisticated puppets, and the three-person manipulation system, conventionally dated to 1734 and said to have been introduced at the Takemoto-za, allowed an extraordinary range of expressive movement. The puppeteers appeared on stage, visible to the audience, and the convention of treating them as part of the art became central to the form. The shamisen, whose ancestry runs from China through the Ryukyu Islands to the main islands, thus became the musical heart of a Japanese theater.

Osaka was the center of this world. Takemoto Gidayū (1651–1714) founded the Takemoto-za in 1684 in Dōtonbori, and the theater became a commercial success with plays by Chikamatsu Monzaemon. Rival companies appeared, and the theaters competed for audiences and playwrights. Puppet theater and kabuki influenced each other: successful puppet plays were adapted for kabuki, as the example of the 1794 print discussed later in this chapter illustrates, and kabuki’s popularity challenged puppet theaters. Like kabuki, puppet theater was a business with managers, performers, and a paying audience, and its plays reflected both historical subjects and contemporary urban concerns. The great collaborative works of the mid-eighteenth century, for example, became staples of both theaters.

Section 46.14

Chikamatsu and the Drama of Duty and Desire

Chikamatsu Monzaemon (1653–1725), born into a warrior family and educated in a courtly environment, became the most celebrated playwright of the early commercial theater. He wrote for puppet theater and also for kabuki, and his plays fall into two broad types: jidaimono, set in earlier historical periods, and sewamono, domestic plays about contemporary townspeople. The latter were innovative. Chikamatsu took stories of merchants, apprentices, and courtesans, often based on recent events, and turned them into tragedies in which the characters’ choices are shaped by money, debt, family obligation, and love.

His Sonezaki shinjū (The Love Suicides at Sonezaki), staged in 1703, is the classic example. It dramatized a recent double suicide, a pairing of a shop clerk and a courtesan, with a speed that shows the commercial theater’s responsiveness to news. Such plays often explore the tension that later commentators describe using the pair giri and ninjō: social obligation, including duties to family, employers, and creditors, versus human feeling, including love and compassion. This framework is useful, but it should not be turned into a timeless “Japanese psychology.” In the plays, giri and ninjō are dramatic pressures within particular economic and social situations, such as debts that cannot be paid or a contract that cannot be broken. Notably, the courtesan characters in these plays are often depicted as trapped by the financial structure of the quarter, a dramatization, for paying audiences, of the very coercion discussed in Section 46.6.

The popularity of lovers’ double suicides, whether in fact or in fiction, alarmed authorities, who in the 1720s restricted the staging of such plays and imposed severe penalties on those who attempted joint suicide. This is a reminder that the commercial theater’s treatment of social conflict was monitored. Chikamatsu also wrote large-scale historical dramas, among them The Battles of Coxinga, which drew on the story of a figure of mixed Chinese and Japanese parentage who resisted the Qing, and which was reported to have enjoyed an exceptionally long run. The play shows that the theater’s audiences were interested in the wider East Asian world, even as the Tokugawa state controlled foreign contact. The commercial city turned conflicts of its own time into paid entertainment, and in doing so also shaped how those conflicts were understood.

Section 46.15

Ukiyo-e: A Commercial Print Culture

Chapter 45 used the landscape prints of Utagawa Hiroshige as evidence for travel and the highway system. This chapter owns the print industry itself. Ukiyo-e is often taught as “great Japanese art,” and its aesthetic achievements are real, but it was also a mass-produced urban commodity. The woodblock print was not the work of a solitary artist. It was the product of a collaborative enterprise involving at least four principal roles: the publisher (hanmoto), who conceived and financed the project, commissioned artists, secured official approval, and handled distribution; the designer, who produced the original drawing; the carver, who cut the design into wooden blocks, typically cherry wood; and the printer, who applied pigments and pressed each sheet by hand. A full-color print required a separate block for each color, and the registration of colors depended on skilled printers. The designer’s name usually appears on the sheet, but the print’s commercial success depended equally on the others, and especially on the publisher.

The history of the form reflects commercial development. Hishikawa Moronobu (active c. 1670s–1694) was among the first Edo artists to establish a reputation for illustrated books and single-sheet prints of beauties and pleasure-quarter scenes. Early prints were often black-and-white or hand-colored. Artists associated with the Torii school specialized in theatrical posters and actor images, and in the eighteenth century the technology of color printing advanced. In 1765, commissioned calendar prints, a fashionable pastime among circles of educated townspeople, led to the production of polychrome “brocade pictures” (nishiki-e) associated with Suzuki Harunobu, and full-color printing became a commercial standard. Print runs varied, and popular designs could be reprinted until the blocks wore out. Prices were low relative to paintings, so that images could reach a wide audience, though estimates of prices and print runs should be treated cautiously because documentation is incomplete.

Publishers were central. The firms that shaped ukiyo-e often also published guidebooks, fiction, and illustrated books, and they cultivated relationships with writers and artists. Tsutaya Jūzaburō (1750–1797) is the best known: starting from a bookshop near Yoshiwara and issuing guides to the quarter, he built a list that included comic fiction and prints by Kitagawa Utamaro and Tōshūsai Sharaku. Other major houses, such as Nishimuraya Yohachi’s Eijudō, issued some of the most celebrated images of the period. Understanding the publisher’s role is crucial to understanding why ukiyo-e looks as it does: publishers identified markets, promoted favored designers, and responded to changing demand and regulation. The broader history of Tokugawa publishing, literacy, and learning belongs to Chapter 50, but publishers matter here because they made the floating world’s images possible.

Section 46.16

What Ukiyo-e Sold: Actors, Beauties, Places, Erotica, and Desire

Ukiyo-e encompassed several major genres. Yakusha-e, actor prints, depicted performers in famous roles and functioned as portraits, advertising, and collectibles. Bijin-ga, pictures of beautiful women, depicted courtesans, teahouse waitresses, and fashionable women of the city. Utamaro, for example, produced large numbers of images of women of Yoshiwara and of famous commoner beauties, such as the group sometimes called the “Three Beauties of the Present Day,” which included a teahouse waitress and the daughter of a rice-cake shop owner alongside a Yoshiwara woman. These images made specific women into local celebrities and, in effect, advertised their workplaces. Prints also advertised fashions: kimono patterns, hairstyles, and accessories were rendered in detail, and publishers produced designs meant to encourage new styles.

Other genres expanded the market. Landscapes and views of famous places, notably by Katsushika Hokusai and Hiroshige in the first half of the nineteenth century, sold to travelers and armchair travelers alike. Imported Prussian blue pigment became widely used by the 1830s, an example of how Chapter 48’s controlled foreign trade supplied materials for domestic art. Warrior prints, comic subjects, and pictures of fashionable goods and entertainments also had audiences. Shunga was produced by many leading artists. The point is that ukiyo-e was not “pictures of geisha”: geisha appear in the prints, but alongside a vast range of other subjects, and the great majority of the print market’s output addressed actors, beauties, landscapes, and popular tales.

These images both reflected and actively created consumer desire. A print of a courtesan or actor made a person famous, and fame stimulated demand for more images and for attendance at theaters or visits to the quarter. Publishers invested in particular artists and subjects, and rivalry among them pushed innovation. At the same time, the images were products of a male-dominated industry catering to the desires of its customers. Images of women were designed for consumption by buyers, many of them male, and they idealize their subjects. A student looking at a courtesan print should ask what the image wants the viewer to feel, who paid for it, and what it leaves out.

Section 46.17

Fashion, Taste, and Sumptuary Regulation

Fashion was central to the floating world. Courtesans, actors, and wealthy townspeople were leaders of taste, and clothing, hairstyles, fabrics, accessories, cosmetics, restaurants, and named establishments conveyed social information. Pattern books for kimono design circulated from the later seventeenth century, enabling customers to select designs. Clothing patterns and colors associated with famous actors became fashionable, and elite courtesans introduced and popularized styles that were copied by women of the city. In Edo, a restrained, subtle ideal of stylishness, often glossed as iki, developed among urban sophisticates, in contrast with ostentation; scholars differ on how consciously contemporaries used the concept, which was theorized extensively only later. Fashion thus operated as a language of urban belonging and distinction.

This matters politically because fashion could blur the visual distinctions on which the status order depended. When commoners wore luxurious silks, or when rich merchants dressed more splendidly than samurai, clothing no longer signaled rank reliably. Authorities responded with sumptuary laws, edicts restricting the fabrics, colors, ornaments, and styles permitted to different groups, repeatedly issued from the seventeenth century onward. Such edicts addressed luxury in clothing, housing, food, and entertainment. The very repetition of these regulations is revealing: it suggests continuing official concern about the gap between wealth and status, and it suggests that enforcement was imperfect. Yet students should avoid the opposite simplification that the laws were meaningless. They had effects, shaping what was displayed in public, encouraging subtle forms of consumption such as expensive linings and understated colors, and exposing violators to penalties. The Tokugawa order exerted pressure on fashion, and fashion responded with adaptation.

Fashion also had a gendered and commercial dimension. Textile merchants, dyers, weavers, hairdressers, and makers of combs and hairpins depended on demand for new styles. Retailers promoted clothing through publications and, by the later period, through large-scale shops, a development connected with the merchant houses described in Chapter 45. The material side of the floating world, from kimono to cosmetics to smoking accessories, was thus a site of significant employment and a major component of the urban economy. Chapter 49 will return to textile crafts and material traditions more generally; here the concern is how fashion, performance, and image interacted.

Section 46.18

Censorship, Reform, and the Negotiated Limits of Entertainment

The floating world operated under constant surveillance. Authorities regulated theater locations and plays, the conduct of actors, the activities of prostitutes and their houses, the content of prints and books, and the display of luxury. Over the course of the period, officials issued repeated edicts on these subjects. The shogunate’s policies in the 1720s restricted plays about double suicide. In 1790 new rules required preliminary print designs to be submitted for inspection, and from late 1790 or 1791 approved prints commonly bore a kiwame censor’s seal. In 1791 the publisher Tsutaya Jūzaburō was penalized and the writer Santō Kyōden was punished for books that authorities found offensive. In 1804, Utamaro was reportedly punished after producing prints that depicted a historical figure in ways officials judged improper. The Tenpō-era measures of the early 1840s restricted prints of actors and courtesans and relocated the Edo theaters to Saruwaka-chō.

Students should not treat these episodes as the whole story. Chapter 50 will examine the broader reform programs of the Kansei and Tenpō periods as political projects, and this chapter mentions them only to show how they affected entertainment. What emerges across the evidence is a pattern of adaptation. Publishers and artists learned to encode meaning, to use allusion and puns, to alter titles, to shift subjects, and to anticipate what the censors would accept. They obtained approvals when required and sometimes evaded them. Theater managers lobbied officials and adjusted performances. The boundary of the permissible was negotiated, not fixed, and it shifted with official priorities and the political climate.

This negotiation shows that neither the “romantic” nor the “purely repressive” interpretation is adequate. The floating world was neither a free zone nor a totally controlled one. It was a commercial culture whose limits were set by the state but which constantly tested them. Regulation also shaped the character of the art: the subtlety of allusion, the indirection of satire, and even the formats of prints reflect the pressure of oversight. The relationship between commercial culture and the state was one of interdependence and conflict at the same time.

Section 46.19

Who Paid, Who Performed, Who Labored? Inequality Behind the Floating World

To understand the floating world as a social system, historians must ask who participated and on what terms. Wealthy merchants and some samurai paid for the most lavish entertainments, and merchants’ money underwrote the elite quarter and the theater. Artisans, shopkeepers, and their families made up the broader audience for theater and prints. The stars, leading actors, top courtesans, and celebrated artists, were visible and sometimes wealthy. But the system depended on a much larger workforce whose names are rarely recorded: apprentices and child attendants in the licensed houses, lower-ranking women, cooks, cleaners, hairdressers, kimono makers, porters, stagehands, musicians, dressers, ticket sellers, scene painters, wigmakers, and the carvers, printers, and shop workers in the publishing trade.

These workers were distinguished by gender, age, and position in hierarchies of apprenticeship and debt. Women in licensed houses faced contractual bondage; child attendants entered service young; theater apprentices endured long training under senior performers; carvers and printers, though skilled artisans, remained anonymous in the public image of ukiyo-e even as their techniques were essential. The glamorous surviving image disproportionately represents consumers and stars, because they were the ones who purchased, commissioned, and preserved the records. Recovering the labor behind the spectacle requires reading sources against the grain: account books, guild regulations, official orders, and incidental references in fiction and legal records.

Finally, the floating world was a gendered economy. Men were its principal consumers and, in theater and publishing, many of its principal producers and owners; women were prominent as performers and subjects but occupied constrained positions in the business structure. Even among performers, the best-documented are those who rose to fame. A responsible history acknowledges that artistic achievement and exploitation coexisted within the same institutions, and that the people who bore the greatest costs often had the least voice in the evidence.

Historical Debate: Was the Floating World a Space of Social Freedom?

Freedom Inside Institutions of Constraint

The question: Did the theaters, licensed quarters, and print culture of the Tokugawa cities create genuine social freedom for participants, or were they instruments of a regulated and unequal system?

Interpretation A — A space of temporary social loosening and commoner cultural authority: Some scholars emphasize that, in the theater and the quarter, money could outweigh formal rank, commoners could become celebrities, fashion and taste moved upward from townspeople to elites, and gender could be performed in creative ways. In this reading, the floating world gave urban commoners an arena for cultural experimentation and self-definition otherwise limited by the status order.
Interpretation B — A regulated, unequal system dependent on the Tokugawa order: Other scholars emphasize licensing, surveillance, debt bondage, gendered exploitation, the stigma attached to performers, and the dependence of the entertainment economy on the status system it appeared to subvert. In this reading, the freedom on offer was bought by customers and bounded by regulations, and for many of the people who worked in the floating world it was largely illusory.

Evidence: Both interpretations rely on the same bodies of evidence: guidebooks, prints, fiction, and theater records, which show vibrant cultural activity, and legal and financial records, regulations, and incidental testimony, which show constraint. They differ in whose experience they treat as central. Interpretation A draws more on the perspectives of consumers, writers, and stars; Interpretation B on those of workers and on the institutional framework.

Why it matters: How historians answer this question shapes how they teach Tokugawa urban culture, whether as a story of vibrant popular creativity, of structural inequality, or of the inseparability of the two. It also affects how students approach the art that survives: as evidence of liberty or as an artifact of a system that included coercion.

Section 46.20

Pleasure, Regulation, and the Meaning of Urban Culture

The floating world neither overthrew Tokugawa hierarchy nor simply reproduced it. It created spaces where money could sometimes outweigh formal rank, gender could be performed in unusual ways, commoners could become celebrities, and fashion could move upward through society. It generated art, literature, and theater of lasting significance and produced a visual and performing culture that engaged audiences across social lines. Yet these spaces depended on licensing, unequal labor, gender hierarchy, debt, and political supervision. They existed because the Tokugawa order permitted and profited from them, and they were constantly shaped by the limits that order imposed.

The chapter’s three distinctions bear repeating. The licensed courtesan and the geisha belonged to different institutions even where their worlds overlapped. Ukiyo-e was a broad commercial visual culture, not a catalogue of beautiful women. And the glamorous surviving record is not a representative sample of the people who made the floating world. Keeping these distinctions in view prevents two opposite errors: the romantic version, in which Yoshiwara, geisha, and ukiyo-e add up to an elegant and sexually liberated old Japan, and the equally flattening reaction, in which the floating world was nothing but exploitation. A commercial cultural system can contain artistic innovation, pleasure, sociability, celebrity, coercion, hierarchy, aspiration, and regulation all at once, and the historian’s task is to show how.

Chapter 47 turns to a different dimension of the same society. The people who bought theater tickets, visited the quarters, and collected prints also lived in a world organized by temples, shrines, Confucian ethics, popular belief, ritual communities, and religious regulation. The same state that licensed pleasure also required households to register with temples and suppressed Christianity, and the same cities that supported the theater also supported festivals, pilgrimage, and temple fairs. The commercial pursuit of pleasure and the organization of religious life were two faces of one social order, and understanding the Tokugawa world requires seeing them together.

Cause and Effect: The Rise of the Commercial Floating World

Why a Commercial Culture of Pleasure Emerged

Long-term causes

Urban growth driven by sankin-kōtai and castle-town concentration; merchant wealth and monetization; expanding road and shipping networks; a stable political order after the wars of the Sengoku period; and a status system that separated wealth from formal rank.

Short-term causes

Official decisions to license and segregate entertainment (1617 and after); the regulation of kabuki (1629, 1652); fires that reorganized city geography; commercial publishing and woodblock technology; and dense audiences of migrant men and prosperous townspeople.

Event or transformation

The emergence in the Genroku era and after of commercial theater, licensed quarters, celebrity culture, fashion markets, and a mass print industry organized around the pleasures of the city.

Immediate effects

New professions and institutions (actors’ lineages, theaters, publishers, geisha); the circulation of images and names; blurring of visual markers of status; and recurring official intervention.

Long-term consequences

A durable urban popular culture; later traditions of theater, print, and entertainment; and a legacy of debate about contract prostitution and gender that reformers confronted in the Meiji period.

Historical complexity

No single factor explains the floating world. Wealth without publishing, or publishing without dense audiences, or audiences without licensed districts would have produced something different. The regulatory state helped create the system it tried to control.

Historical complexity: Urban growth, merchant wealth, monetization, publishing technology, dense audiences, and licensed entertainment districts together produced commercial theater, celebrity, fashion, print culture, and pleasure markets. None of these causes alone is sufficient.

Continuity and Change Over Time: From Genroku to Tenpō

The Floating World, c. 1680–1850

What Changed

  • Women geisha replaced men as the principal professional entertainers, and the highest courtesan ranks changed or disappeared
  • Prints evolved from black-and-white and hand-colored sheets to full-color nishiki-e, with landscapes becoming major genres
  • Theater technology and staging developed, and Edo gradually matched Kamigata as a cultural center
  • Regulation intensified at moments of fiscal and moral anxiety, culminating in the Tenpō measures of the 1840s

What Stayed the Same

  • The principle of licensing and spatial concentration of theater and the quarters
  • Contract-based labor and debt as the basis of the licensed sex trade
  • The commercial logic of celebrity, publishing, and fashion
  • The persistent tension between wealth and formal status

Who experienced change most directly? Publishers, artists, and performers felt shifts in taste and regulation most sharply, and the geisha profession was reshaped substantially. Who experienced greater continuity? Women bound by contract in lower ranks saw little change in the underlying structure of debt and constraint.

Why the pattern matters: A culture can change dramatically in style and technology while its underlying economic and social structures persist, and this is why the floating world should be studied both as an evolving art and as a stable system of labor.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that the “floating world” was a commercial urban culture produced by the economic growth, status distinctions, gender relations, and regulatory institutions of Tokugawa society, and not a carefree realm outside it. The cities described in Chapter 45 created dense audiences with money to spend and a large population of migrant men living outside conventional households. Theaters, licensed quarters, publishers, and fashion trades turned performance, companionship, image, and reputation into commodities, and the word ukiyo became the label for this urban world of pleasure.

Tokugawa authorities did not simply tolerate this world. They concentrated, licensed, segregated, and supervised it. Yoshiwara in Edo, Shimabara in Kyoto, and Shinmachi in Osaka were licensed quarters embedded in urban governance, and theaters were licensed to designated locations. The system established a boundary between licensed and unlicensed activity, and a large commercial sex economy operated outside the licensed quarters in unlicensed districts and at post stations, where participants faced greater legal risk and less protection.

Within the licensed quarters, courtesans were organized in hierarchies, and the highest ranks combined sexual commerce with training in music, poetry, etiquette, and fashion. This prestige must be distinguished from labor freedom. Most women entered service through family poverty and a contractual advance that became debt, lived under restrictions on movement, and faced health risks and conditions far removed from the elegant imagery of prints. Some exercised negotiation and agency, and a few gained fame and influence, but the system was structurally unequal.

Geisha were a different profession. Originating as male entertainers and becoming predominantly female from the mid-eighteenth century, they were professionals of music, dance, and conversation, regulated so as not to compete with licensed courtesans, though the boundary varied in practice. Treating courtesans and geisha as interchangeable erases a distinct history of work, and equating geisha with prostitution is inaccurate. Sexuality in the entertainment world also included male-male desire and the roles of youths, which must be understood in terms of practices, roles, and hierarchies rather than modern identity categories.

Kabuki began in the early seventeenth century in a milieu of itinerant and urban performance. Regulation, including the bans on women performers in 1629 and adolescent boys in 1652, changed the institution rather than eliminating popular theater, and adult male kabuki, with its onnagata, became a major art. Theaters were commercial enterprises with licensed managers, annual contracts, tiered seating, teahouses, supporting trades, and fire risk. Stylistic contrasts such as aragoto and wagoto reflected regional audiences, and hereditary stage names allowed celebrity to be reproduced across generations.

Puppet theater (ningyō jōruri, later widely called bunraku) developed in Osaka around chanters, shamisen players, and skilled puppeteers. Chikamatsu Monzaemon’s plays turned recent events, debts, and love suicides into drama, illustrating the pressures often described as giri and ninjō while remaining tied to particular social situations. Authorities monitored the genre, restricting double-suicide plays in the 1720s. The shamisen, which reached the main islands through the Ryukyu Islands, reminds us that the soundscape of the cities was shaped by wider regional connections.

Ukiyo-e was a collaborative commercial industry in which publishers, designers, carvers, and printers produced prints sold to urban consumers. It encompassed actors, beauties, famous places, warriors, fashion, and erotica, and prints both reflected and created desire, making actors and teahouse women into celebrities. Sharaku’s 1794 portrait of Ichikawa Ebizō (formerly Danjūrō V), with its publisher’s and censor’s marks, illustrates how celebrity was constructed through a regulated commercial channel.

Fashion carried social meaning and could blur status distinctions, prompting repeated sumptuary legislation whose partial effectiveness shaped styles of display. Censorship, from the 1720s restrictions on double-suicide plays through the 1790 publication controls, the punishments of 1791 and 1804, and the Tenpō measures of the 1840s, formed the context in which publishers, artists, and managers adapted, encoded, and negotiated. The relationship between commerce and the state was interdependent and conflictual at once.

The social base of the system was unequal. Wealthy merchants and some samurai paid; stars and celebrated artists were visible; and an anonymous workforce of apprentices, lower-ranking women, servants, stagehands, carvers, printers, and shop workers made the spectacle possible. The glamorous surviving record overrepresents consumers and stars. Historians must read sources critically and recover labor where they can.

The floating world therefore neither overthrew nor simply reproduced Tokugawa hierarchy. It offered conditional spaces of cultural experimentation and social blurring, within institutions of licensing, debt, gender hierarchy, and supervision. This understanding avoids both the romantic image of an elegant, sexually liberated old Japan and the equally flattening image of nothing but exploitation, and it prepares for Chapter 47, which turns to the religious and ethical institutions that organized the same society.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows that the Tokugawa peace did not produce a static, tightly regulated society but a dynamic commercial culture whose creativity and constraints grew together. The theaters, quarters, and print shops of Edo, Osaka, and Kyoto were products of urban growth and official policy, and they generated forms of entertainment, celebrity, and visual culture whose influence extended well beyond their own era. Understanding them as regulated marketplaces, rather than as escapes from the political order, links cultural history to the economic and social history of the preceding chapters.

The chapter also reveals continuing processes. The concentration of consumer culture in cities, the commercialization of images and reputation, the negotiation between popular media and state censorship, and the tension between cultural prestige and unequal labor did not end with the Tokugawa period. Later chapters will show how Meiji officials attacked contract prostitution, how theater and print traditions were reinterpreted in a modernizing nation, and how new media, from newspapers to film, reorganized popular entertainment in similar commercial and regulatory frameworks.

Regionally, the chapter has connected the cultures of Edo, Kyoto, and Osaka as a network rather than a single center, and it has noted links to the wider world: the shamisen’s passage from the Ryukyu Islands, the pigments that came through regulated foreign trade, and the Coxinga story’s East Asian setting. These are reminders that even the most “domestic” urban culture drew on materials and ideas from beyond the main islands.

Finally, the chapter asks students to hold two truths together: that the floating world produced art and performance of extraordinary originality, and that it depended on debt, constraint, and unequal labor. Chapter 47 now turns to the temples, shrines, ethical teachings, and religious regulation that organized the same society, and the student who has understood this chapter will be prepared to see commerce and religion as parts of one social order.
