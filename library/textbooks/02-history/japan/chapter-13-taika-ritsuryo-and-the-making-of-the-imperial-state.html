Part II — Yamato and the Formation of Ancient Japan
13

Taika, Ritsuryō, and the Making of the Imperial State

Japanese History: From Jōmon Japan to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

In 645, in the presence of an empress and envoys from the Korean kingdoms, one of the most powerful men at the Yamato court was cut down in a ceremonial hall. Fifty-seven years later, a court that now called its ruler tennō and its realm Nihon had enacted a written legal code (701–702) and sent an embassy to Tang China, which reached the Chinese court in 703. Between those two dates lay reforms, registers, a devastating military defeat, a civil war, and a slow and contested invention of the state.

This chapter studies that transformation: the passage from a lineage-based Yamato court, in which kings governed through the leaders of hereditary kin groups, toward a more centralized imperial state that claimed to count its people, measure its land, rank its officials, divide its territory into provinces and districts, and rule by written law. Chapters 11 and 12 traced the political competition among the Soga and their rivals and the experiments in rank, diplomacy, and Buddhist patronage associated with the era of Empress Suiko and Prince Shōtoku. Chapter 13 picks up after Suiko's death in 628 and follows the court through some seventy-five years of violence and institution-building, ending with the Taihō Code of 701–702. Chapter 14 then begins where this chapter leaves off, with the imperial state operating from its new capital at Nara.

The governing question is deceptively simple: how did a court of competing lineages become a more centralized imperial state, and how gradual was that change? The traditional answer, still repeated in some older books, is that Prince Naka no Ōe and Nakatomi no Kamatari overthrew the overbearing Soga in 645 and promptly issued the "Taika Reforms," which created a Chinese-style centralized government almost overnight. This chapter argues that the truth is more interesting. The events of 645 mattered a great deal, but they were a coup and a redistribution of power, not the founding of a bureaucracy. The measures associated with the Taika era unfolded unevenly across several reigns. They were later narrated, in the Nihon Shoki completed in 720, from the vantage point of a mature eighth-century state that had reason to present its origins as a single act of deliberate design. The decisive consolidation came later, above all after the Jinshin War of 672, and its main monuments were the Asuka Kiyomihara Code of 689, the new capital at Fujiwara-kyō in 694, and the Taihō Code of 701.

Three pressures drove the process, and students should watch for all three. The first was domestic: competition among aristocratic houses and among royal princes made succession dangerous and made the old system of governing through uji (lineage groups) unstable. The second was continental: the Sui reunified China, and the succeeding Tang consolidated a powerful continental empire, the Korean kingdoms fought for survival, and in 660–663 Yamato's ally Baekje was destroyed and a Yamato fleet was defeated at the Battle of Baekgang. Fear of a Tang–Silla invasion gave urgency to fortification, conscription, and record-keeping. The third was intellectual and ritual: rulers and their advisers, many of them descended from immigrants from the Korean peninsula or trained in continental learning, drew on Chinese and Korean models of law, rank, and monarchy, and they gradually elaborated a new language of sovereignty in which the ruler became tennō, "heavenly sovereign," and the realm acquired the name Nihon.

The chapter also reminds students that state-building was not experienced only from the top. A household in a river valley in what is now Mino or Buzen encountered the new state as a census-taker, a tax demand in cloth or grain, a labor levy for a road, fortress, or palace, or a summons to military service, and sometimes as a reassignment of land. Local magnates, many of whom had previously governed as kuni no miyatsuko (regional chiefs), became district officials and mediators between court and countryside. Baekje refugees were settled in the east. Communities on the frontiers, including the Emishi in the northeast and the Hayato in southern Kyushu, were drawn into the court's calculations as subjects, tributaries, or enemies. The imperial state was built with and against all of these groups.

The evidence for this period is unusually rich and unusually problematic. The principal narrative, the Nihon Shoki, was compiled under the direction of the very dynasty that emerged victorious from the struggles it describes, and it was written in Chinese and shaped by Chinese historiographical conventions. Its account of the 646 edicts contains vocabulary that scholars have shown to reflect later usage. Against this, historians now have thousands of wooden tablets (mokkan) excavated from palace and administrative sites, fragments of household registers, inscribed stelae, the archaeology of fortresses and capital cities, Chinese dynastic histories, and Korean records. Comparing the chronicle with these materials is the central method of the chapter.

By the end of the chapter, students should understand the imperial state of the early eighth century as a real and substantial achievement, dramatically stronger in its legal and administrative reach than anything in Yamato's earlier history, and also as a partial and negotiated one, dependent on aristocratic families, local elites, and the labor of ordinary households. They should also recognize that the terms "Japan," "emperor," and "Japanese" acquire firmer meaning here than they had in any earlier chapter, while remaining historical constructions rather than timeless facts, a caution that runs through the entire textbook.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why the concentration of Soga power and succession conflict after 628 made the coup of 645 possible.
  2. Analyze the Isshi Incident as a political coup and redistribution of elite power rather than an automatic act of centralization.
  3. Evaluate the Taika reform edicts as a source, distinguishing what the Nihon Shoki reports for 646 from what can be shown about actual seventh-century practice.
  4. Describe the household registration, land allocation, taxation, and labor systems the court attempted to build, and distinguish reform ideals from demonstrated implementation.
  5. Trace the gradual movement from lineage authority toward territorial administration, ranked officials, and written records.
  6. Assess how the destruction of Baekje and the defeat at Baekgang in 663 intensified state-building.
  7. Interpret the Jinshin War of 672 as a principal turning point in which the imperial state was forged partly through civil war.
  8. Connect the reigns of Tenmu and Jitō to the Asuka Kiyomihara Code, the new ranking systems, Fujiwara-kyō, and the emerging language of tennō and Nihon.
  9. Distinguish ritsu from ryō, and explain what is and is not known about the Ōmi, Asuka Kiyomihara, Taihō, and Yōrō codes.
  10. Compare the views of ordinary households, local elites, aristocrats, refugees, and frontier peoples on the new state.
  11. Contextualize Yamato state-building within continental models, especially Tang law, without treating it as simple copying.
  12. Evaluate competing answers to the question "when did the ritsuryō state begin?" by identifying the criteria each depends on.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did a court that had functioned through lineage leaders for generations become vulnerable to a violent reordering in 645, and why is it misleading to portray the Soga simply as tyrants?
  2. Are the Taika edicts a literal blueprint issued in 646, or an eighth-century account that organizes a longer and messier process?
  3. How did rulers turn people, fields, and labor into things the court could count, tax, and mobilize, and how much of this can be shown to have worked?
  4. How did military vulnerability after Baekgang change the pace and purpose of state-building?
  5. What does the Jinshin War reveal about the relationship between centralization and elite conflict?
  6. How did the language of monarchy change between the era of the ōkimi and the era of the tennō, and what did the name Nihon mean?
  7. How did the court adapt Chinese and Korean institutions to Yamato circumstances rather than merely copy them?
  8. How did local elites, ordinary households, immigrants, and frontier peoples shape and experience the new state?
  9. How should historians read a civil war through a chronicle compiled under the political order created by its winners?
  10. Why is "the ritsuryō state was created in a single year" a poor description of what happened, and what better descriptions are available?

Historical Context

By the early seventh century the Yamato court, centered in the Asuka region of the Nara Basin, was the most powerful political formation in the archipelago but not its sole governing authority. As Chapters 7 and 8 explained, Yamato kings had built their preeminence through alliances with regional chiefs, monumental burial traditions, and the control of prestigious resources and continental contacts. Chapter 11 described how the court was organized around uji, hereditary groups headed by lineage leaders who held titles (kabane) and who supplied the court with officials, craftsmen, soldiers, and tribute through associated dependent groups called be (occupational or service groups) and through estates. Great houses such as the Soga, Ōtomo, Mononobe, Nakatomi, and Abe were both servants of the king and rivals for influence over him and over succession.

Chapter 12 showed how, in the reign of Empress Suiko (592–628), this system began to be supplemented. The Twelve-Level Cap Rank system of 603 introduced ranks awarded to individuals rather than inherited by lineages, at least in principle. Missions to Sui China from 600 and 607 introduced continental ideas of sovereignty, and traditions later attached to Prince Shōtoku associated the court with Buddhist patronage and with the idea of a ruler who stood above competing houses. Students should recall, however, that ranks of this kind coexisted with the uji system for decades and did not create an administration. The court still lacked a comprehensive census, a uniform tax system, a permanent capital, or a body of written statutes.

Two developments in the wider world shaped the seventh century. First, China was reunited by the Sui in 589. After the Sui collapsed, the Tang dynasty was founded in 618 and consolidated control over the competing regimes during the following years. The Tang built a formidable administration based on written law codes, household registration, land allocation, and taxation in grain, cloth, and labor. It also built an ambitious foreign policy in Korea. Second, the Korean peninsula was divided among Goguryeo in the north, Baekje in the southwest, and Silla in the southeast, with the smaller Gaya polities having been absorbed earlier. Yamato's ties with Baekje were especially close, involving diplomacy, Buddhist teachers, craftsmen, scholars, and migrants. Silla's alliance with the Tang in the 640s and 650s would eventually destroy Baekje and Goguryeo and confront Yamato with a hostile and victorious neighbor. Chapter 9 developed these connections in detail.

The archipelago itself was not politically uniform. The Yamato court's authority was strongest in the Kinai (the home region around the Nara Basin and Ōsaka Bay), substantial across western Honshu, northern Kyushu, and parts of the east, and variable elsewhere. In southern Kyushu the people later called Hayato, and in the northeast the people the court called Emishi, lived in communities that were partly independent of the court and interacted with it through trade, tribute, warfare, and negotiation. The Ryukyu Islands and Hokkaido lay outside the court's political reach, though island and northern peoples appear occasionally in court records. Chapter 16 will examine the frontiers in fuller detail; here the important point is that the "imperial state" being built in the late seventh century was an aspiration and a claim as well as a fact, and that it defined itself partly against those it labeled as outsiders.

Finally, students should be aware of the nature of the evidence at the outset. The Kojiki (712) ends with Suiko and is of little use for this chapter's events. The Nihon Shoki (720) provides the main narrative, which we can supplement with the Shoku Nihongi (797), which begins with Monmu's reign, as well as with archaeology, mokkan, and foreign records. Every one of the written narratives was composed after the state they describe had matured, and this shapes what they emphasize.

Section 13.1

From Suiko's Court to the Crisis of the Soga

When Empress Suiko died in 628, she left no clear successor. Sovereignty at the Yamato court was not yet governed by a fixed rule of succession. Candidates were drawn from a broad pool of princes and princesses of the royal line, and the choice among them was made through negotiation among the leading lineages, whose chiefs served as the great ministers (ōomi and ōmuraji) or court counselors. The Nihon Shoki records a dispute between two principal candidates: Prince Tamura, supported by the Soga leader Emishi, and Prince Yamashiro no Ōe, a son of Prince Shōtoku. Tamura was enthroned as Jomei in 629. Jomei's reign saw the founding of a great temple, Kudara Ōdera, associated with Baekje, and continued diplomatic contact with the continent, including the return of students and monks who had studied in Sui and Tang China.

Jomei died in 641, and his consort, Takara, was enthroned in 642 as Kōgyoku. Women had ruled before, and the model was Suiko. Female rulers in this period were mature royal women who had been consorts or daughters of earlier sovereigns, and they were not mere regents; they held ritual and political authority, though as the chronicle presents matters they operated in dense networks of male ministers and princes. In 643, according to the Nihon Shoki, Soga no Iruka, acting without waiting for imperial approval, sent troops against Prince Yamashiro no Ōe and his household at Ikaruga, and the prince and his family died. Even if details are colored by later hostility to the Soga, the killing removed the most prominent alternative claimant and revealed how fragile the relationship between king and minister had become.

The chronicle presents the Soga as arrogant beyond measure. It says that Emishi and Iruka built residences on the Amakashi hill overlooking the palace, called them by names appropriate to royal palaces, and treated their children as princes. It accuses them of using the labor of the people to build tombs on a royal scale. These accounts must be handled with care. They come from a tradition produced under the descendants of the coup's victors, and the Soga's enemies had every reason to justify what was done to them. At the same time, the underlying picture may not be wholly invented. Soga power had grown across the previous century through marriage alliances with the royal line, control of Baekje-linked immigrant specialists in writing, finance, and construction, and patronage of Buddhism. Umako, Emishi's father, had died in 626, and the family's position was unusually strong. What made it dangerous was not that it was un-Yamato but that it was so fully Yamato: it worked through exactly the mechanisms of marriage, kinship, and control of the throne that all great houses used.

Other elites had good reason for concern. The Soga's control of succession threatened royal princes such as Naka no Ōe, whose father Jomei had been enthroned with Soga help but whose own claims were in doubt. It threatened rival houses, including the Nakatomi, who held ritual offices, and branches of the Soga itself who resented Emishi and Iruka's dominance. The Abe, the Ōtomo, and others had to decide whether to support the Soga or to look elsewhere. Meanwhile, Kōgyoku's reign was troubled by drought in 642, when the court conducted rain rituals of both Buddhist and indigenous kinds, an episode that underscores the ruler's dual role as a ritual and political figure. Finally, returning scholars such as the monk Min, who returned in 632, and Takamuko no Kuromaro, who returned in 640, later served as advisers and brought detailed knowledge of the Tang order. Students of the period sometimes overlook that continental knowledge was available to all factions, including the Soga, who had their own connections with Baekje and with Buddhism. The question was not "reform or no reform" but who would control the court and on what terms.

Section 13.2

The Isshi Incident of 645

On the twelfth day of the sixth month of 645 (in the chronicle's reckoning), the court gathered at the Itabuki palace to receive tribute envoys from three Korean kingdoms, Goguryeo, Baekje, and Silla. Iruka attended, as did Empress Kōgyoku. According to the Nihon Shoki, Naka no Ōe and Kamatari had planned the assassination with care, enlisting Soga no Kurayamada no Ishikawa no Maro, a member of a Soga branch who was to read a memorial to the throne, as well as warriors including Saeki no Komaro and Kazurakibe no Tsuwako. The plot's most dramatic moment, in the chronicle, came as the reader's voice trembled while the assassins hesitated, before Naka no Ōe himself burst in and struck Iruka. The empress demanded to know what was happening, and Naka no Ōe answered that Iruka was plotting to destroy the imperial line. Iruka, who had appealed to the throne, was killed, and his body was thrown into the courtyard.

This is a vivid narrative, and it should be treated as a chronicle tradition rather than an eyewitness report. The essential fact, that Iruka was killed at court by a coalition led by a royal prince and a Nakatomi adviser, is not seriously doubted. Details of speeches, hesitation, and the accusation against Iruka reflect the narrative shaping of the Nihon Shoki, which had a stake in presenting the coup as a rescue of the throne from a usurper. The incident is known in Japanese as the Isshi no hen, after the sexagenary year name of 645. Students should learn the term but keep in mind that it is a modern label.

What followed was swift. Soga no Emishi, besieged in his own residence, took his own life the next day, and the chronicle says that records of the emperors and of the state were burned, though a text called the Kokki was saved. Kōgyoku abdicated, an unusual step that was apparently the first recorded abdication of a sovereign, and her brother Prince Karu was enthroned as Kōtoku. Naka no Ōe became crown prince rather than taking the throne himself, perhaps because his claim was weak or because it served his interests to keep a senior figure in front. Abe no Uchimaro was named Minister of the Left and Soga no Kurayamada no Ishikawa no Maro Minister of the Right, and Kamatari became a key adviser. The monk Min and Takamuko no Kuromaro were appointed as state scholars (kuni no hakase). In the twelfth month the court moved to Naniwa, on the coast of Ōsaka Bay, where a large palace was built. Naniwa's location gave the court access to sea routes and continental diplomacy, and its palace, as excavations show, was on a scale not previously seen. The court also adopted a new era name, Taika ("great change"), traditionally regarded as the first Japanese era name, though whether era names were used continuously in this period is debated.

It is important to see the coup for what it was. It was a political action by a coalition that included a royal prince, a ritual-religious house, and a segment of the Soga itself. It eliminated the main branch of the most powerful house and redistributed offices and influence. It did not abolish the uji, it did not end aristocratic power, and it did not immediately change how the court governed. Indeed, the Minister of the Right, a Soga relative and a key conspirator, was himself forced to suicide in 649 after being accused of treason, a reminder that the new regime remained unstable. Kurayamada had built Yamada-dera, a major Buddhist temple, and his fall shows that Buddhist patronage remained enmeshed in elite rivalry. Nor did the coup make Naka no Ōe secure. It is best understood as the opening of an era of experimentation, not the completion of a design.

Section 13.3

What Were the Taika Reforms?

The name "Taika Reforms" refers to a set of measures announced by the Kōtoku court between 645 and roughly 650, of which the best known is a four-article edict that the Nihon Shoki dates to the first month of Taika 2 (646). The edicts are among the most cited documents in Japanese history, and they are often summarized as the birth certificate of the centralized state. That summary is not wrong about the ambitions that the text expresses, but it goes beyond what the evidence supports about the year 646.

As the chronicle presents it, the first article calls for abolishing private holdings in people and land attached to the royal house and elite lineages: the koshiro associated with imperial princes, the court estates (miyake), and privately controlled lands and workers held by ministers, local nobles, and village chiefs. In their place it provides allotments of households as stipends for ministers and officials. The second article establishes a capital region, the Kinai, with defined boundaries, and prescribes a hierarchy of provinces (kuni), districts, and villages, along with post stations and barrier guards. The third article orders the compilation of household registers and tax registers and the periodic allocation of rice fields to the population. The fourth article replaces earlier levies with a new set of taxes, described as levies of textiles on land and households and labor service, with corresponding rules for how much labor could be demanded. It also includes an ordering of villages, with fifty households forming a unit under a headman.

Read on its face, the edict describes a state that controls land and households in order to draw taxes and labor directly. It is closely parallel to the Tang system of equal-field allocation, household registration, and a triple tax in grain, cloth, and labor. Historians generally agree that Tang institutions were a principal model, and they have pointed to the presence at the Yamato court of scholars who had studied in China. They are also generally agreed that the Yamato court did not copy the Tang wholesale. It adjusted institutions to a court based on aristocratic lineages, to the availability of local elites, and to Yamato's own scale and resources.

The difficulty lies in the transmission of the text. The Nihon Shoki was completed in 720, nearly three quarters of a century after the events, at a time when the Taihō and Yōrō legal systems were in place. Scholars have long noticed that the edict uses institutional terms, such as the character for "district" (gun, 郡), that appear to reflect the usage of the early eighth century. The mokkan evidence discussed in the vocabulary box below shows that the earlier term was different. Historians therefore doubt that the edict as we have it can be a verbatim record of a decree of 646. Some hold that it was substantially revised or even composed by the compilers to present the origins of the ritsuryō state as a coherent program. Others argue that a core of genuine provisions underlies the later wording. Still others regard it as a mixture: a real edict of some kind, expanded and retouched. What can be said with confidence is that the court in the 640s and 650s did undertake some measures aimed at registration, land, and the reduction of private control over people, and that the chronicle's account is shaped by the eighth-century perspective. The primary source window in this chapter invites students to read the edict with these problems in mind.

Other reforms associated with the reign of Kōtoku are attested by the chronicle. These include the appointment of officials to conduct registers in the provinces in 645 and the establishment of a system of ranks and cap colors in 647 and 649. Kōtoku's court also created ministries and offices and is said to have set up complaint boxes, following a continental model, to hear the grievances of the people. The chronicle also mentions a Hakuchi ("White Pheasant") era from 650, after a white pheasant was presented as an auspicious omen. This illustrates how much the court's political vocabulary depended on continental ideas of omens and virtue. In all these cases, historians must decide how much is reliable narrative and how much retrospective ordering.

Historical Vocabulary
Ritsuryō (律令)

Ritsuryō refers to the system of government based on ritsu (penal statutes defining crimes and punishments) and ryō (administrative statutes defining offices, ranks, taxation, land allocation, and procedure), together with the supplementary rules (kyaku and shiki) that amended and implemented them. The model was the Tang legal system, especially its codes of the mid-seventh century. The term is used both for the codes themselves and, more loosely, for the state and period they governed, roughly the late seventh through the tenth centuries.

Students should not read "ritsuryō state" as a modern bureaucracy or a meritocracy. Office and rank depended heavily on birth and family; the system worked through aristocratic families, local elites, and negotiated compromise; and the codes described an ideal that the court often could not fully realize. The concept matters here because the entire chapter is a story of the assembly of this system, and because Chapters 14 to 20 trace its functioning and gradual erosion.

Section 13.4

Land, People, Census, and Taxation

The central problem of any early state is legibility: a ruler who wishes to draw resources from a population must first know who and where they are. Before the mid-seventh century, the Yamato court drew resources indirectly. Lineage heads supplied laborers, soldiers, and goods from their be and estates, and the court received tribute from provincial chiefs. The court could not easily know how many households there were, what land they worked, or how much they produced, and much of the surplus was retained by intermediaries. The measures associated with the Taika era, and pursued more systematically later, aimed to change this by registering households directly and assigning them obligations to the court.

The core instrument was the household register. Registration in this period meant recording each household and its members by name, sex, and approximate age category, and organizing households into units, of which the fifty-household village (ri) became standard. The chronicle records a first attempt in 645, when officials were sent to the provinces to compile registers, and it records more successful efforts under Tenji and Jitō: the Kōgo nenjaku of 670, named for the sexagenary year, and the Kōin nenjaku of 690. The later register is said to have been revised every six years thereafter under the Taihō system. None of the seventh-century registers survive. The earliest surviving examples are from 702, preserved in the Shōsōin repository at Nara, and record households from Mino, Chikuzen, Buzen, and Bungo provinces. They are among the most valuable documents for ordinary life in ancient Japan, listing household heads, women, children, the elderly, and dependents, and they show that large composite households were common in some regions and that women appear regularly among the registered population.

The second instrument was land allocation. The system as described in the codes, called handen shūju, involved periodically allotting rice fields to registered persons according to age, sex, and status, and taking them back upon death, so that in principle all arable land was public. The Taika edict describes the idea; the Taihō system fixed the details. How far it worked in practice has long been debated. Land measurement and allocation required surveys, and the chronicle's evidence for surveys before the 680s is thin. Surviving eighth-century land records suggest that allocation was often incomplete or approximated, and that pre-existing patterns of cultivation persisted. Nor did private landholding vanish. Temples, shrines, and high aristocrats were granted or claimed land, and the court itself recognized categories of private land. Students should avoid the older textbook statement that Taika "nationalized" all land; the accurate statement is that the court asserted a public claim over rice land and attempted to administer it, while many forms of control by elites continued.

The third instrument was taxation, in kinds that the Tang model had provided: a grain tax on land (so), a tax in local products, especially cloth, from households or individuals (chō), and yō, a corvée-exemption levy commonly paid in cloth or other goods. Actual compulsory labor was imposed through zōyō and related service obligations. This is the mature ritsuryō arrangement; the seventh-century evolution toward it was gradual. There were also military obligations. The precise structure was worked out in the later seventh century and defined in the codes, and the Taika edict's version reflects the eighth-century formulation. What is clear is that the burden fell on ordinary households, and that different provinces supplied different products: silk, hemp, salt, iron, fish, and other local goods, later documented on mokkan tags. Households were obliged to carry goods or labor to the capital, and hardship on these journeys is attested in later sources. The state's expenditure supported palaces, temples, fortresses, and the ranks and stipends of officials.

Historians distinguish carefully between reform ideals and demonstrated implementation. That the court proclaimed a system of universal registration in 646 does not show that it had one. Evidence such as the reference to the 670 register, mokkan naming individuals and households, and the 702 registers shows that registration became real by the late seventh century, and the Taihō code assumed it. In between, the process seems to have been gradual, region by region, with tension between the court and local magnates who resisted the loss of control. This chapter's approach is therefore to treat the land and tax system as an unfolding project rather than an event.

Section 13.5

Building an Administrative State

If registration and taxation were the fiscal foundation of the new state, territorial administration was its skeleton. The old order was organized by lineage: a person belonged to a uji and served the court through it. The new order aimed to organize people by place and by office, in a hierarchy that ran from the court to provinces, districts, and villages. The change was gradual. It involved the creation of new territorial units, the appointment of officials, the development of ranks, and the growth of a literate class capable of keeping records.

At the local level, the court relied on existing elites. The chief of a region, formerly styled kuni no miyatsuko and typically a hereditary leader whose authority rested on local kinship and religious ties, was in many cases confirmed as head of a new territorial unit called a district. The unit was written in the seventh century with a character read kōri (評), and its heads and staff were drawn from local families, who thereby received state recognition and, in exchange, took on responsibility for taxation, labor, and order. Above them, governors sent from the court supervised territories called kuni (provinces); the mature provincial system, with fixed boundaries and resident governors, took shape only in the later seventh century and belongs to the account of Chapter 14. What matters for now is the logic: the state did not eliminate local elites but transformed them into office-holders with their positions tied to the court.

Evidence from a stele at Nasu in modern Tochigi Prefecture, dated 700, illustrates this dynamic. It commemorates a local magnate who had served as a district head and it shows a local elite taking on the language of the state, including a continental-style inscription, while retaining a leading position in the region. Students should treat this as one example, not as proof of uniformity. Elsewhere, local families may have been displaced or absorbed, and the extent to which governors from the court actually controlled the provinces is a matter of debate.

At the court, the chronicle describes successive rank systems. Suiko's Twelve-Level Cap Rank system was replaced or extended in 647 with a thirteen-level system and in 649 with a nineteen-level one, and in 664 under Tenji with a system of twenty-six ranks. These ranks were awarded to individuals, though they were heavily influenced by family status. Later systems under Tenmu and Jitō further reorganized ranking, and the codes of 689 and 701 set out the principle that rank determined office, stipend, and privileges. Court offices developed, including a Council of State and ministries that later took the standard form described in Chapter 14. Precisely when each office was established is debated, and the Nihon Shoki may sometimes name institutions in an anachronistic way. Some, such as the Minister of the Left and Right, appear in 645; the eight ministries of the Taihō system are not securely dated before the 690s.

Administration also required record-keeping and communications. The court used written documents, including wooden tablets that carried labels for goods, records of personnel, and orders. Thousands of such mokkan have been excavated from Naniwa, Asuka, and Fujiwara-kyō, and their number grows from the 680s onward, an important indicator of increasing bureaucratization. Roads linking the capital with the provinces and post stations provided communication, though the network built in the fully developed form belongs to Chapter 14. The state also relied on specialists in writing and calculation, many of them immigrants or their descendants from Baekje and elsewhere in the peninsula. Continental learning reached the court through these individuals, and their role reminds us that "Chinese influence" was mediated through Korean and immigrant communities and was carried by real people, not diffused by abstract borrowing.

Historical Vocabulary
Kōri (評) and Gun (郡)

Both characters were used for the district, the local administrative unit beneath the province, and in the eighth century both were read as kōri. The Nihon Shoki's version of the 646 edict uses the character 郡 (gun). But wooden tablets excavated at Fujiwara-kyō and other sites, dating from before the Taihō Code, use the character 評 for the district. Scholars debated for years whether the chronicle's usage was original or later, and the discovery of these mokkan, beginning in 1967, showed that the seventh-century unit was written 評 and that the character 郡 was standardized in the Taihō system of 701–702. Some tablets from Naniwa suggest that the unit existed as early as the mid-seventh century, though its form and function are debated.

The point for students is methodological. A single character in a chronicle helped historians show that the text of the 646 edict was edited in the early eighth century, and it also shows that some real administrative unit existed earlier. The episode is often called the gun–kōri (or kōri–gun) controversy, and it is a model of how archaeology can be used to test a written tradition.

Section 13.6

Korea, Baekje, and the Crisis of 663

The reforms of the late 640s and 650s were carried out against a background of expanding warfare on the continent. The Tang emperors Taizong and Gaozong undertook campaigns against Goguryeo, and by the 650s the Tang and Silla had formed a partnership aimed at eliminating Baekje and Goguryeo. Yamato's diplomatic ties with Baekje were old and strong, and the court had hosted Baekje princes, including Prince Buyeo Pung (Hōshō in Japanese), who resided in Yamato from around 631. The court also maintained relations with Silla and the Tang, sending envoys to the Tang in 653 and 654, and it appears to have hoped to balance among the powers. That balance ended in 660, when a Tang and Silla force took the Baekje capital Sabi and captured the king.

Baekje nobles, led by figures including Buyeo Pung's supporters and the general Gwisil Boksin, continued resistance and appealed to Yamato for aid and for the return of Prince Pung. The court, then under the aged Saimei (Kōgyoku), decided to intervene. The empress and Naka no Ōe moved to Tsukushi in northern Kyushu to organize an expedition, and Saimei died there in 661 at the temporary Asakura palace. Naka no Ōe governed without formally taking the throne, in a style the chronicle calls shōsei. In 662–663 forces were sent across the sea, and Prince Pung was installed as king of a Baekje restoration. In the eighth month of 663 (traditionally the twenty-seventh and twenty-eighth days), a Yamato–Baekje fleet was defeated by Tang naval forces, within the wider Tang–Silla campaign, in the Baekgang (Hakusukinoe in Japanese) estuary. Chinese sources report a decisive victory, and the Yamato army withdrew, carrying with it many Baekje refugees. Goguryeo fell in 668, and the Tang subsequently fought Silla for control of the peninsula, with Silla emerging by the 670s as the dominant power.

The defeat at Baekgang was a shock, and its consequences are visible in the archaeology and the chronicle. In 664 the court established beacon stations and frontier guards (sakimori) in Tsushima, Iki, and Tsukushi, and built a great embankment and moat, the Mizuki (Water Fortress), near what became the Dazaifu, to defend the approach to northern Kyushu. In 665 fortresses were built at Ōno and Ki in Kyushu under the direction of Baekje officers who had taken refuge, and other mountain fortresses appeared along the Inland Sea and in the Kinai in the following years. The court reorganized its ranks in 664 and admitted Baekje refugees into them, and it settled thousands of Baekje people in Ōmi and eastern provinces, many of them given ranks and responsibilities. The move of the capital to Ōtsu in Ōmi in 667, away from the exposed coast, is often read as a response to fear of invasion, though internal politics may also have played a part. Tang envoys visited Yamato in the mid-660s, and the court sent its own envoys, seeking to avoid conflict.

Historians disagree on how far the security crisis explains state-building. Some see 663 as the decisive catalyst, converting the court's reform program into an urgent effort to register and mobilize the population for defense. Others emphasize that the institutions were already developing and that the crisis merely accelerated them. No invasion ultimately followed, since the Tang and Silla turned to fight one another, and historians should distinguish the court's evident perception of danger from evidence that a Tang–Silla invasion was concretely imminent. The perception itself seems to have been real. It helps to explain why the court invested in fortifications, conscription, and household registration, and why Tenji's court in particular was so active in administration. It also shows again that Yamato was a participant in a regional international system and was affected by events across the sea. Baekje refugees, with their technical and administrative skills, contributed to the culture of the court in the late seventh century, including its architecture, letters, and learning.

Section 13.7

Tenji and the Deepening of Court Administration

After Baekgang, Naka no Ōe continued to rule as regent-like leader until he was formally enthroned in 668 as Tenji, the year Goguryeo fell. The intervening years mark the most intense phase of administrative activity in the chronicle. In 664 an edict reorganized the ranks and, according to the record, classified lineage heads of great, small, and associate rank, granting each a kind of formal recognition and specifying which groups they were responsible for. This is often read as a step toward converting lineage authority into a structure of officially sanctioned status, keeping the aristocracy but placing it inside the court's own system. In 670 the court compiled the Kōgo nenjaku household register, sometimes called the first nationwide register, and the chronicle reports efforts to demarcate fields and assign obligations. In 669, on his deathbed, Nakatomi no Kamatari was granted the highest rank and the surname Fujiwara, a moment that marks the origin of the house that would dominate Chapters 17 and 18.

Tenji's reign is also traditionally associated with the Ōmi Code. Later sources, including an eighth-century tradition, credit Tenji with the promulgation of a set of administrative statutes, called the Ōmi ryō, in twenty-two volumes, in 668 or thereabouts. The status of this code is uncertain. No text survives; the sources that mention it are late; and some scholars doubt that a comprehensive code existed in this period. Others suppose that a body of rules, perhaps not systematically organized, was compiled under Tenji and provided a basis for later work. The most cautious position, adopted here, is that the Ōmi Code is best treated as a traditional stage in legal development, a claim that Tenji's court was moving toward written law, rather than as a securely known code whose contents can be described. The first codification that later sources place on firmer ground is the Asuka Kiyomihara ryō of Tenmu and Jitō, though its text too is lost.

Students should also note Tenji's more general political style. The chronicle presents him as an energetic and at times ruthless ruler who dominated the court and who was concerned to organize military and administrative resources. He moved the capital, built fortresses, and extended the register system. He also married his daughters to his brother and to his son, binding the royal house tightly together, and he appears to have preferred his son, Prince Ōtomo, as successor over his brother Prince Ōama, the crown prince. The chronicle attributes this to a decision made late in Tenji's reign. In 671 Ōtomo was appointed Daijō-daijin (Grand Minister), the first person known to hold that office, and this set the stage for conflict. Tenji died in the twelfth month of 671. The tensions in the royal house, which the chronicle describes with the benefit of hindsight, came to the surface within months.

Section 13.8

The Jinshin War of 672

The Jinshin War, named for the sexagenary year 672, was a major civil war and one of the most consequential conflicts in the archipelago's early recorded history. It pitted two members of the royal family against one another: Prince Ōtomo, Tenji's son, who held the court at Ōmi, and Prince Ōama, Tenji's younger brother and the former crown prince. It was fought by armies drawn from across the Kinai, Mino, Owari, Ise, and other provinces, and its result determined not only who would rule but which branch of the royal family would define the dynasty and its chronicle for centuries.

According to the Nihon Shoki, the crisis began when Tenji, gravely ill, summoned Ōama and asked him to take the throne. Ōama, suspecting a trap, declined and proposed that he take the tonsure as a Buddhist monk and retire to Yoshino, in the mountains south of Asuka. The chronicle presents this as a shrewd stratagem: he withdrew from danger and prepared for the contest. In the summer of 672, learning that the Ōmi court was mobilizing labor for what looked like a preparation for military action against him, Ōama left Yoshino with a small party, including his consort Uno no Sarara (the future Jitō) and his sons, and went east through Iga to Mino and Ise. There he secured the loyalty of local chiefs and the important Fuwa barrier, a key strategic pass, and raised forces from the eastern provinces. His son Prince Takechi commanded his troops.

The Ōmi side attempted to mobilize the east and Tsukushi (Kyushu), but its envoys were blocked or refused, and the chronicle reports that the governor of Tsukushi declined to provide troops, citing the need to guard against foreign threats. The war was fought on several fronts. In the Nara Basin, forces led by Ōtomo no Fukei supported Ōama and took Asuka. In the north, Ōama's armies advanced from Mino toward Ōmi and fought a series of battles. The decisive engagement took place near the Seta bridge, and Ōtomo's forces were beaten. Prince Ōtomo took his own life, in the seventh month of 672, and the Ōmi court collapsed. Ōama returned to Asuka, established his court at a new palace at Kiyomihara, and was enthroned in 673 as Tenmu. Many of the losing side's officials were exiled or punished, while the winning side's commanders received rewards. Ōtomo's reign is not counted in the traditional imperial genealogy. Only in 1870 did the Meiji government award him a posthumous title as Emperor Kōbun, an example of how the memory of the war continued to be politically consequential.

The Jinshin War is a principal turning point of the chapter for several reasons. First, it demonstrated that centralization had not eliminated elite conflict. The institutions of the late seventh century, such as the registers and the ranks, provided Ōtomo with resources but did not prevent a civil war, and it was won by a prince who mobilized regional followers using personal and local ties. Second, the war's outcome punished, displaced, or weakened important supporters of the Ōmi court, giving Tenmu greater room to reorganize the elite. Third, the war gave Tenmu an unusual legitimacy of victory, and his reign shows an unprecedented concentration of authority. It is no accident that his government was more forceful, and that it took more decisive steps toward a written code.

Finally, the war illustrates the problem of sources. The Nihon Shoki gives a detailed and generally favorable account of Ōama's campaign, organized by day, and it draws on records kept by participants. In 681 Tenmu ordered princes and officials to compile imperial genealogies and historical records, a court historiographical project that formed part of the tradition eventually culminating in the Nihon Shoki, and Tenmu's line, through his sons and grandsons, occupied the throne for much of the eighth century. Students should therefore read the chronicle's version as the account of the victors, shaped to show that Ōama was compelled to fight in self-defense and that Heaven favored him. We know Ōtomo and his court largely through their enemies' narrative.

Section 13.9

Tenmu, Jitō, and the Late-Seventh-Century State

Tenmu's reign (673–686) is the period in which the institutions of the state became markedly more concrete. Tenmu did not appoint a Grand Minister and instead governed with a small circle of royal princes and close advisers, an arrangement later remembered as "government by the imperial house." This concentration of authority in the royal family, together with the prestige of victory, allowed him to press forward on several fronts. In 675 the court abolished remaining kakibe attached to elite lineages and separately tightened court control over privately held mountains and uncultivated lands, a step that echoes the first article of the 646 edict, and it is a sign that such policies, if proclaimed in 646, were enacted only later. In 684 the court promulgated a new system of eight honorific titles (yakusa no kabane), headed by mahito, asomi, and sukune, that reorganized the old kabane titles around the royal house and ranked families in a hierarchy that placed royal descendants first. This reshaped the aristocracy: lineage rank was preserved but now derived from the court. In 681, the chronicle reports, Tenmu ordered the compilation of a code and of imperial genealogies and historical records, projects that fed into the Asuka Kiyomihara ryō and into the tradition that eventually produced the Nihon Shoki.

Tenmu's reign also saw an unusually explicit attempt to give the monarchy a sacral and ritual character. The court promoted the shrine at Ise, where Tenmu's daughter Princess Ōku served as high priestess, and cultivated the myth of the sun goddess Amaterasu as ancestor of the royal house, though the full elaboration of this tradition appears in the chronicles of the following generation. It also patronized Buddhism on an unprecedented scale. Under Tenmu the earlier Kudara no Ōdera, founded under Jomei in 639, was transferred to the Takechi area and, according to temple tradition, renamed Daikandaiji in 677; he also vowed the construction of Yakushiji for his wife's recovery, ordered Buddhist altars in provincial households, and regulated the clergy through the state. The combination of Buddhist and native ritual, discussed in Chapter 15, made religion a state matter. In the same period, the compilers of the earliest court histories began to shape genealogies and myths that presented the royal line as descended from the sun goddess and as legitimate rulers from time immemorial. As Chapter 8 explained, these narratives must be read as products of their time.

Tenmu died in 686. His consort, Uno no Sarara, acted as regent, and her nephew and stepson Prince Ōtsu, a talented and popular son of Tenmu, was accused of treason and forced to die, a reminder that succession problems persisted. Her own son, Crown Prince Kusakabe, died in 689, and she ascended the throne in 690 as Jitō. Her reign (690–697) completed many of Tenmu's projects. The court promulgated in 689 what later sources identify as the Asuka Kiyomihara ryō, the first systematic body of administrative statutes associated with the court. Its original text is lost, its precise scope is debated, and it probably did not include a complete penal ritsu. It does not survive, and its content must be inferred from later codes and from the practice it shaped. In 690 the Kōin register was compiled, and in 694 the court moved to Fujiwara-kyō, the first capital planned on a grid pattern with a central palace, avenues, and blocks, modeled on continental cities but adapted to local conditions. Fujiwara-kyō, which held the court until 710, is important because it was the first full-scale planned capital, an attempt to establish a stable, planned seat of government and a physical embodiment of the state, though the court abandoned it for Heijō-kyō after only sixteen years. Before this time, palaces were moved with each reign, and the concept of a fixed capital was new.

Jitō's reign also produced a precedent of great importance. In 697 she abdicated in favor of her grandson Karu, who reigned as Monmu, and continued as a retired sovereign wielding influence. Because Kusakabe had died young, Jitō supported the succession of his son, and her arrangement reflects an effort to protect the direct line of Tenmu's descendants against other claimants. It also shows how flexible the concept of sovereignty remained: succession by a grandson, by a woman ruler, and by abdication were all improvisations that the state later regularized. Fujiwara no Fuhito, son of Kamatari, became prominent in this era as an administrator and as a key figure in the compilation of the Taihō Code. The ties between Fujiwara and the royal house began to develop through marriage, laying the groundwork for the later political dominance of the family.

The late seventh century also saw a transformation in the language of monarchy. The older title ōkimi ("great king"), used by Yamato rulers, was gradually supplemented by tennō, written with characters meaning "heavenly sovereign," a term with Chinese Daoist and imperial associations. The chronology of its adoption is debated. Older accounts attributed it to Suiko or Shōtoku, but most scholars now place its institutional establishment in the reigns of Tenmu and Jitō or slightly earlier, and a wooden tablet found at the Asukaike site is often cited as an early contemporary use in Tenmu's era. The name of the realm also changed. Yamato rulers and their subjects had long been known to the Chinese as Wa. Around the time of the Taihō Code the court began to use the name Nihon, "origin of the sun," and an embassy led by Awata no Mahito departed in 702 and reached the Chinese court in 703; Chinese accounts of this renewed contact use the state name Nihon and report some skepticism about its origin. Neither the title nor the name should be given a single invention date; they emerged over several decades and were formalized in the codes. Nor should they be read back into earlier centuries, when rulers were ōkimi of Yamato and their realm was known as Wa.

Historical Vocabulary
Tennō (天皇)

Tennō is the Japanese term usually translated as "emperor," though its characters mean "heavenly sovereign." It became the standard title of the ruler in the late seventh century, and it was defined in the codes. Before then, rulers were called ōkimi (great king) in Japanese, and the Chinese called them kings of Wa. The Chinese-derived term suggested a ruler who stood above other kings, and it suited a court that wanted to present itself as the center of its own world. It is not the same as the Chinese "emperor" (huangdi), and the court did not claim equality with Tang in all respects.

Students should not assume that the tennō held the same power in every period. The ritsuryō codes envisioned a ruler at the apex of a bureaucratic state, but actual power was shared with royal relatives and aristocratic families, and it shifted dramatically in later centuries. Chapters 18, 24, and later will show the tennō in very different roles. Historians also debate whether, and for how long, the earlier title was ōkimi in usage, and how to describe Yamato rulers before Tenmu; this textbook uses "king" or "sovereign" for earlier rulers where accuracy requires.

Section 13.10

Taihō, Ritsuryō, and the Imperial State

Under Monmu, the court completed a legal project that its predecessors had begun. A commission that included Prince Osakabe, Fujiwara no Fuhito, and Awata no Mahito, along with scholars of continental law, compiled a comprehensive set of statutes, the Taihō Code, enacted in 701 (Taihō 1). The administrative statutes (ryō) took effect in 701, while the penal statutes (ritsu) followed in 702. Unlike the Asuka Kiyomihara Code, it included both a ritsu, a penal code of six volumes defining crimes and punishments, and a ryō, an administrative code of eleven volumes defining offices, ranks, land allocation, taxation, and procedures. Its model was the Tang legal tradition, especially the codes of the mid-seventh century, but it adapted them, for example by placing a separate Office of Deities (Jingikan) for state kami rites alongside the Council of State (Daijōkan), rather than placing those rites inside one of the ordinary administrative ministries, and by adopting a hereditary element in the awarding of ranks that had no exact parallel in China.

The original Taihō Code does not survive intact. Its text has to be reconstructed from later sources. In 718 a revised code, the Yōrō Code, was compiled, and it went into effect in 757; it survives, in part, through commentaries from the ninth century, such as the Ryō no gige and Ryō no shūge, which sometimes quote the Taihō version for comparison. Scholars therefore know Taihō mainly through Yōrō, and they generally hold that the differences were modest in structure though not always in detail. Students should avoid asserting specific provisions of the Taihō Code as if the original text were available. The best statement is that the surviving ryō, and the ritsu, are those of the Yōrō system, and that they provide a good guide to the Taihō framework.

The ritsuryō order set out a structure with the tennō at its apex, a Council of State (Daijōkan) directing eight ministries, an Office of Deities (Jingikan) handling state kami rites, and a hierarchy of ranks determining officials' privileges and stipends. It defined the capital region and the provinces, with governors sent from the court and district heads drawn from local elites. It provided for registration, allocation of rice land, taxation in grain, goods, and labor, and conscription for military service, including the frontier guards. The ritsu prescribed punishments in grades from beating with light or heavy sticks through penal servitude, exile, and death, and it defined offenses against the sovereign, such as rebellion and sacrilege, as the gravest. Historians also note that the code protected aristocratic privilege by allowing rank to reduce or commute punishments, an important sign of the class order it embodied.

The wider ritsuryō state that emerged had features that students should keep in mind. It maintained a system of slaves and unfree persons (nuhi), although the codes limited some forms of bondage, and it distinguished between commoners (ryōmin) and various status groups. It relied on the labor of women, who wove cloth for taxes and worked in households. Its distribution of rice land was gendered: women received a smaller allotment than men, but women did receive allotments. Its treatment of frontier peoples was distinct: Emishi and Hayato were sometimes treated as tributaries and sometimes as enemies, and the court was engaged in expansion at the northern edge, with forts and settlement, that Chapter 16 will examine. On the southern edge, court records note that islanders from Tane and Amami visited court in the later seventh century, and in 699 the court sent envoys to explore islands to the south, connections that anticipate later relations with the Ryukyu Islands (see Chapter 32). Hayato communities in southern Kyushu resisted and were later brought under administrative control. The state's frontier policy was thus part of its self-definition as a center with peripheries.

The essential conclusion is a balanced one. By 702 the court possessed a legal-administrative framework that was dramatically stronger than that of 645: written law, registers, defined offices, a planned capital city, and a new vocabulary of monarchy. But the ritsuryō state remained dependent on aristocratic families who filled its offices and who held rank by inheritance, on local elites who mediated between court and countryside, on households whose labor and goods supported it, and on negotiated implementation. Its reach varied by region and over time. Centralization was substantial, not absolute. In 710 the court moved to Heijō-kyō (Nara), and Chapter 14 examines how this state functioned there.

Primary Source Window

The Reform Edict Traditionally Dated to 646

Creator: Attributed to the court of Emperor Kōtoku; preserved in the Nihon Shoki · Date: traditionally the first month of Taika 2 (646); text transmitted in the chronicle completed in 720 · Type: Royal edict in Chinese, transmitted within an official chronicle. English translations are available in several scholarly editions and in the Columbia University "Asia for Educators" collection.

Context: The chronicle presents the edict as a New Year's proclamation, introduced by a statement of principles about the rule of the sovereign, and followed by four numbered articles. The Nihon Shoki is the only source of the text. It was compiled by court scholars who had access to older records but who also edited and organized them in the light of the mature legal system of their own time.

Paraphrase (not a verbatim quotation): The first article ends the holding of people and land by princes and lineages and substitutes support for officials through allotted households; the second sets up a capital region and hierarchies of provinces and districts; the third calls for household and tax registers and for allocating rice land; the fourth replaces older exactions with levies of cloth and goods and defined labor service, and organizes households into villages.

Analysis: The text is invaluable as evidence of what the eighth-century court believed its own origins to be, and it shows the institutional program that the ritsuryō system embodied: public registration, allocation of land, territorial units, and standardized taxes. It may preserve genuine elements of the Kōtoku court's ambitions. But it is not a straightforward contemporary record. It uses terminology, such as the character for district, that appears to belong to a later period, and its comprehensiveness suggests editing. It also tells us nothing about implementation. It does not tell us how officials responded, how local elites and households reacted, or how many of its measures were carried out in the 640s. Its perspective is that of the court, and the voices of those who were taxed and registered are absent.

Historical Debate

Was There Really a "Taika Reform"?

The question: No serious historian denies the coup of 645 or that the Kōtoku court took some administrative measures. The debate concerns how to understand them: as a program, a beginning, or a retrospective construction.

Interpretation A — A rapid and deliberate reform program: Older scholarship, and much popular history, treated the edicts as a genuine and comprehensive blueprint issued by Naka no Ōe and Kamatari that immediately began to transform the state. On this view, the chronicle's account is broadly reliable, and the reforms represented a decisive break, shaped by Tang models and by returned students.
Interpretation B — An important beginning realized only gradually: Many historians accept that the 645 court launched real measures but argue that most provisions were realized over decades, under Tenji, Tenmu, and Jitō, as capacity and political opportunity allowed. The edicts, on this view, express aspirations more than accomplishments.
Interpretation C — An eighth-century retrospective framework: A more skeptical position holds that the compilers of the Nihon Shoki substantially organized or composed the edicts, projecting the developed ritsuryō system back onto 646 to give it a unified, heroic origin and to legitimize the dynasty. Many scholars take an intermediate position, accepting that some genuine elements lie under later wording.

Evidence: The debate turns on the language of the edicts (for example, the gun/kōri problem), on mokkan and archaeological evidence for administrative practice before 701, on the surviving registers and stelae, and on comparison with Tang sources. It also depends on how one weighs the chronicle, whose narrative shaping is well established.

Why it matters: If the reforms were a rapid program, the state was founded in the 640s and the later seventh century was a period of refinement. If they were gradual or retrospective, then the Jinshin War, Tenmu, and Jitō become the decisive builders, and the story of a heroic reform in 645 becomes part of the state's own founding myth. It also matters for how students read every later court chronicle.

Student task: Choose one piece of evidence from this chapter that supports a rapid-program view and one that supports a gradual or retrospective view, and explain which interpretation better accounts for both.

Historical Debate

When Did the Ritsuryō State Begin?

The question: Historians have proposed several starting points: 645/646, 672, 689, and 701–702. Each corresponds to a different criterion.

If the criterion is political rupture and program: 645–646 is the natural date, since it marks the overthrow of the dominant lineage and the proclamation, at least in the chronicle, of a new administrative program.
If the criterion is the concentration of monarchical power and elite reorganization: 672–673 is decisive, because the Jinshin War produced a ruler able to reorder the aristocracy and initiate codification.
If the criterion is written law and functioning administration: 689 (the Asuka Kiyomihara ryō) or 701–702 (the first complete ritsu and ryō) marks the beginning, since only then can we point to systematic statutes and a demonstrably operating system.

Evidence: The choice depends on what counts as a "state": intentions, personnel, laws, or practice. Mokkan and registers show practice growing from the 680s; the codes show formalization; the chronicle shows ambitions from 645.

Why it matters: The question shows that there was no magic founding date, and that historians' periodization reflects their definitions. Students should understand the ritsuryō state as an assembly over roughly six decades.

Multiple Perspectives

Living Through State-Building

Note: these perspectives are reconstructed from the categories of evidence available. They are not quotations, and the groups did not have equal power.

Court aristocrats and royal princes: For the great houses, the new state offered offices, ranks, and stipends, but it also meant subordination to court rank and the risk of losing dependent groups. Those on the winning side after 645 and 672 gained; those on the losing side, such as the main Soga line or Ōtomo's ministers, lost offices and sometimes lives. Royal princes competed for succession, and the arrangements of Tenmu and Jitō were designed to protect one line.
Local elites and district heads: Regional magnates, formerly kuni no miyatsuko, could become district officials whose authority was recognized by the court. This gave them legitimacy and often perpetuated their local power, but it made them answerable for taxes and labor and subject to court oversight. Some benefited; others may have lost control of people and land.
Ordinary households: For farming families, registration and taxation meant new and predictable obligations in grain, cloth, goods, and labor, and the possibility of conscription. Their experience varied by region, household size, and distance from the capital, and their voices survive mainly in registers and tax tags. Women's labor in weaving was central to the tax system.
Baekje refugees and immigrant specialists: After 663, thousands of Baekje people came to the archipelago. Some, with technical or administrative skills, received ranks and posts and helped design fortifications and records; others were settled in frontier regions. They contributed to the court's continental learning while being dependent on the court's favor.
Emishi and Hayato communities: For peoples on the northern and southern margins, the expanding state meant tribute demands, forts, settlement, and warfare, but also trade and diplomacy. The court's records were written by those who wished to define them as barbarians, and they left few written accounts of their own.
Continuity and Change Over Time

From Lineage Court to Ritsuryō State

What Changed

  • A stronger and more sacralized monarchy, with the new title tennō and the name Nihon
  • Written law and codes, culminating in the ritsu and ryō of 701
  • Household and land registration and a tax system of grain, goods, and labor
  • Territorially defined offices, with provinces and districts
  • Systematic ranks and a planned capital city, an attempt at a stable seat of government
  • Increasing reliance on writing and records, visible in mokkan

What Stayed the Same

  • The power of aristocratic families, now expressed through rank and office
  • Local elites as mediators between court and countryside
  • The agricultural household as the economic base
  • Uneven court reach across regions
  • Succession conflict and political violence
  • Dependence on continental models and immigrant expertise

Who experienced change most directly? Court aristocrats and local elites, whose status was reorganized, and farming households, who newly faced registration and taxation. Who experienced greater continuity? Communities beyond the reach of the registers, and many local practices of land and ritual. Why the pattern matters: It shows that the imperial state was an overlay on older structures, and it prepares students for the tensions of Chapters 14 to 20.

Cause and Effect

The Making of the Ritsuryō State

Long-term causes

Lineage competition and a weakly institutionalized succession; Soga concentration of power; accumulating continental knowledge through immigrants, students, and Buddhism; the Sui reunification of China and subsequent Tang consolidation.

Short-term causes

Rivalry over the throne after 628; the killing of Yamashiro no Ōe; Tang–Silla pressure on Baekje and Goguryeo; the threat perceived after 660; the succession crisis after Tenji's death.

Event or transformation

The coup of 645 and reform program; experiments in registration and administration; the 663 defeat; the Jinshin War; Tenmu and Jitō's consolidation; the codes of 689 and 701.

Immediate effects

Elimination of the main Soga line; a more powerful ruler after 672; reordering of ranks and titles; fortifications and settlement of refugees; the planned capital at Fujiwara-kyō.

Long-term consequences: The ritsuryō framework shaped court government through the Heian period, established the tennō and Nihon as enduring political concepts, gave rise to the Fujiwara house, and created tensions between public land and private estates that Chapter 20 will explore.

Historical complexity: The process is best summarized as a chain: lineage competition, Soga concentration, and continental institutional knowledge produced the coup and reform program of 645; census, tax, and administrative experiments combined with the continental military crisis to intensify state-building; the Jinshin War then redirected the dynasty; and Tenmu and Jitō's consolidation led to the Asuka Kiyomihara Code and the Taihō order. No single link, and no single person, accounts for the outcome.

Everyday Life and Society

Households, Work, and Belief under the New State

Most people in the late seventh century were farming households, cultivating rice, millet, and other crops, gathering, fishing, and making cloth and tools. The new state added to the rhythm of the seasons a calendar of obligations. Households prepared textiles, especially hemp and silk, salt, and other goods to be submitted as taxes and carried, often on foot, to the capital. They provided labor for construction, including palaces, roads, canals, and fortifications; the chronicle mentions great labor levies under Saimei, and the Mizuki and Kyushu defenses required large numbers. Men were liable for military service and some were sent as frontier guards to Tsushima, Iki, and Kyushu, far from home.

The surviving registers of 702 provide a rare window onto family life. They show households of varied sizes, some with dozens of members including slaves and dependents, and they record women and children by name. They suggest that women were a large part of the recorded population, and that many households included several generations. The Buddhist temples and the growing cult of the royal ancestors added a religious dimension to life: local shrines and rituals, honoring kami and ancestors, continued alongside Buddhist observances. Alongside these, Chinese-influenced ideas of omens, divination, and calendar-keeping entered court practice. Famine, disease, and drought, which the chronicle records in various years, fell hardest on those who lived close to the margin.

Elite culture also changed. The court at Asuka and Fujiwara-kyō supported poets, including Nukata no Ōkimi and Kakinomoto no Hitomaro, whose poems in the Man'yōshū reflect the court's sense of itself. Tomb art shifted as well: the painted chambers of Takamatsuzuka and Kitora tombs near Asuka, dating from around the turn of the eighth century, show Chinese and Korean-influenced imagery and testify to elite continental taste. The end of the old monumental keyhole tombs and their replacement by smaller, differently designed burials is another sign of changing ideas of royal power.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced the transformation of the Yamato court from a lineage-based political order into a more centralized imperial state between roughly 628 and 702. Its central argument is that 645 mattered but did not create the imperial state in a single stroke. The Isshi Incident was a coup that eliminated the main Soga line and redistributed power, and it opened a period of experimentation. The state that emerged by the early eighth century was the product of decades of institutional development, external crisis, civil war, and legal codification.

The succession disputes after Suiko's death and the concentration of power in the Soga house created the conditions for the coup. The Soga were not simple tyrants; they were skilled participants in the politics of kinship, marriage, and patronage, and their fall was the result of rivalry with a royal prince and other elites. The chronicle's hostile portrayal reflects the perspective of those who overthrew them.

The measures associated with the Taika reforms, above all the four-article edict traditionally dated to 646, describe a program of land allocation, household registration, territorial administration, and taxation modeled partly on Tang practice. But the text survives in the Nihon Shoki of 720 and shows signs of later wording, most famously the use of the character for gun where contemporary mokkan use kōri. Historians therefore treat the edict as evidence of eighth-century understanding and of real seventh-century ambitions, rather than as a literal blueprint implemented at once.

Land, census, and taxation were gradually built. Household registers were compiled in 670 and 690, and the earliest surviving registers date from 702. Land allocation and taxation in grain, goods, and labor were defined in the codes, but implementation was uneven, and private landholding by temples and elites continued. The court transformed local elites into district officials rather than eliminating them, and it developed ranks, offices, and record-keeping, evidenced by the growth of mokkan from the 680s.

The destruction of Baekje and the defeat at Baekgang in 663 was a crisis that intensified state-building. The court fortified Kyushu, established frontier guards, reorganized ranks, settled Baekje refugees, and moved the capital to Ōmi. Tenji's reign deepened administration and is traditionally associated with the Ōmi Code, though that code is not securely documented. Immigrant and refugee expertise from the peninsula shaped the court's administrative and cultural life.

The Jinshin War of 672 was a principal turning point. Prince Ōama's victory over Prince Ōtomo demonstrated that centralization had not ended elite conflict, punished, displaced, or weakened important supporters of the Ōmi court, and gave Tenmu the authority to reorder the aristocracy. Because the Nihon Shoki was produced under the victors' line, its account must be read critically.

Under Tenmu and Jitō, the state became markedly more concrete. The remaining kakibe were abolished and private mountain and waste lands brought under tighter court control, new titles and ranks were issued, the Asuka Kiyomihara ryō was promulgated in 689, the Kōin register was compiled in 690, and the court moved to the planned capital of Fujiwara-kyō in 694. The language of monarchy shifted from ōkimi to tennō, and the name Nihon began to replace Wa, though the chronology of both remains debated. Sacral and Buddhist patronage strengthened the ruler's authority.

The Taihō Code, enacted in 701 with the ryō in effect that year and the ritsu following in 702, brought together a ritsu and a ryō and completed the legal framework of the ritsuryō state. The original text is lost, and the system is known mainly through the later Yōrō Code and commentaries. The resulting state was substantially stronger than its predecessors, yet it depended on aristocratic families, local elites, and the labor of households. It was neither a modern bureaucracy nor a merit-based system.

The chapter has also emphasized that no single date, person, or cause explains the transformation. Debates over when the ritsuryō state began show how the answer depends on the criteria used, and the debate over the Taika reforms shows how a state's founding narrative can be shaped by later needs. Frontier peoples, refugees, women, and farming households experienced the state differently from aristocrats, and their experiences remain visible in the surviving evidence, though mostly through the records of the state.

Finally, Chapter 13 sets the stage for Chapter 14. The imperial state now had a legal framework, a capital tradition, and a vocabulary of sovereignty. How it functioned from Nara, how its provinces, roads, and taxes worked, and how its ideals confronted reality are the subjects that follow.

Chapter Reflection

Chapter 13 matters because it shows how a state is made. The imperial state of the early eighth century was not the product of a single decree or a single reformer but of decades of political violence, administrative improvisation, military anxiety, and legal craftsmanship. That process linked events at the court with events across the sea, and it drew on the skills of immigrants, the loyalties of provincial chiefs, and the labor of farming households.

The chapter also demonstrates a lesson about historical writing. The Nihon Shoki gave later generations a powerful story of reform and rescue, and that story has shaped Japanese and international views of the period for centuries. Recognizing it as a narrative composed by the heirs of the winners, and testing it against wooden tablets, registers, and fortifications, is a method students will use again when they read the chronicles of the Heian court and the warrior histories of later chapters.

The developments studied here shaped much that follows. The ritsuryō codes provided the legal vocabulary for court government through the Heian period, the tennō became an enduring institution whose role would change dramatically, and the Fujiwara house began the rise that Chapter 18 describes. At the same time, the tensions between public claims and private land, between the court and its provinces, and between ideal and practice, planted the seeds of the estates and provincial warriors of Chapter 20. Frontier peoples, including the Emishi and Hayato, and the island communities to the south were drawn into the court's field of vision, anticipating the histories of Chapters 16 and 32.

Students who understand the imperial state as an assembly, achieved with violence and compromise, and as a partial success rather than a completed design, are well prepared for Chapter 14, where the state they have just seen built begins to operate from the new capital of Nara.
