The Rice Riots of 1918 and the Politics of the Crowd

Japanese History: From Jōmon Japan to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

In the last days of July 1918, women from fishing households on the Toyama coast gathered where rice was being readied for shipment out of their communities and asked, collectively and in public, that it stay where it could be bought at an affordable price. Within weeks, crowds in Nagoya, Kyoto, Osaka, Kobe, Tokyo, and scores of other towns, villages, and mining districts were confronting rice dealers, police, and soldiers. By late September the prime minister had resigned.

The Toyama coast, on the Sea of Japan side of central Honshu, was an unlikely place for a national crisis to begin. Its small ports, fishing hamlets, and rice-growing hinterland lay far from the great political and commercial centers of Tokyo and Osaka. Yet it was in communities such as Uozu, Mizuhashi, and Namerikawa, in the last third of July and the first days of August 1918, that women whose households depended on purchased rice organized meetings, appealed to merchants and officials, and sought to halt shipments of rice from their own district. Their actions were reported in newspapers, discussed in other regions, and imitated, adapted, and radicalized by people in very different circumstances. The historian's task is to explain why a local demand for fair prices on a northern coast could, within a few weeks, become a national confrontation.

The answer begins with the wartime economy. The First World War had made Japan richer in the aggregate: exports expanded, shipping and shipbuilding earned extraordinary profits, and Japan shifted from a debtor to a creditor in international accounts. Chapter 66 examined that transformation. This chapter asks what it meant for households that bought their food with wages, or that lived by fishing, casual labor, mining, small trade, or tenant farming. For many of them, the price of rice rose faster than their incomes, and rice was not an ordinary commodity. It was the staple of the diet, the largest single item in many household budgets, and the object of long-standing popular expectations that merchants and authorities owed the community a fair price. When prices surged in the summer of 1918, partly because of a long wartime inflation and partly because of speculation connected with the Siberian Intervention, those expectations collided with an economy that rewarded profit and a state that was not organized to guarantee subsistence.

The events that followed are known in Japanese as the kome sōdō, the "rice disturbances," and in English as the Rice Riots of 1918. Neither label is neutral. "Disturbance" and "riot" reflect the vocabulary of officials and newspapers, who tended to emphasize disorder; the word ikki, applied by the press to the Toyama women, evoked older traditions of collective rural protest. This chapter uses the common names while insisting on a distinction the labels can obscure. The episodes ranged from quiet negotiations and petitions to mass meetings, from the pressured sale of rice at reduced prices to the burning of merchants' property, and from tense standoffs to armed suppression in which soldiers fired on crowds. The participants included fishing-community women, industrial workers, day laborers, miners, small shopkeepers, tenant farmers, and residents of discriminated Buraku communities. Their goals, methods, and political language varied by place. The chapter therefore avoids two simplifications: that the events were merely spontaneous hunger riots, and that they were a coordinated revolutionary movement.

Historians reconstruct the Rice Riots from an unusually rich but unusually partial body of evidence. Newspapers carried the first reports and, after a government ban in mid-August, became a case study in censorship and self-restraint. Police, prefectural, municipal, and military reports record the authorities' view of crowds and the measures taken against them. Court and prosecution records survive for some defendants. Diaries, such as that of the communications minister Den Kenjirō, show how members of the government perceived the crisis. Local histories, memoirs, and oral recollections preserve memories shaped by decades of subsequent political argument. The scholarship that interprets this evidence includes the multivolume study by Inoue Kiyoshi and Watanabe Tōru, which treated the riots as a mass uprising with revolutionary significance, and Michael Lewis's Rioters and Citizens: Mass Protest in Imperial Japan, which emphasizes variation among localities and the emergence of popular claims to citizenship. Each source speaks from a position, and the chapter repeatedly asks whose voice is recorded and whose is missing.

The chapter also marks a threshold in the textbook's treatment of mass society. Earlier chapters showed how industrialization, empire, and war created new social groups and new forms of public life: urban wage earners, newspaper readers, factory and mine workers, and a politically alert middle stratum. Chapter 67 traced the constitutional and party politics of the Taishō era, including the formation of Hara Takashi's cabinet. Chapter 68 provides the national history of the crisis that preceded that cabinet. It does not, however, retell the broader political history, nor does it narrate the organized labor, suffrage, and reform movements that Chapter 69 will address. Its responsibility is to explain how the crisis arose, how it spread, who took part, how the state responded, and what difference it made.

The chapter is organized in seven divisions. The first examines the economic foundations of the crisis: wartime inflation, rice as a social necessity, the structure of the rice market, and the Siberian Intervention. The second follows the origins of protest on the Toyama coast. The third traces the spread of disturbances through the major cities. The fourth turns to rural districts, coalfields, and the unequal social geography of unrest. The fifth examines local government, police, the army, the press, and the courts. The sixth considers the political and social-policy consequences, including the fall of the Terauchi cabinet. The seventh asks what the crowds demanded and how historians have understood the event. Throughout, the principal narrative concentrates on 1917 to September 1918, with particular emphasis on July to September 1918.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how wartime growth, inflation, speculation, and the Siberian Intervention combined to produce the rice-price crisis of 1918, distinguishing long-term from immediate causes.
  2. Distinguish wholesale from retail prices, nominal from real purchasing power, and producers of rice from households that depended on buying it.
  3. Trace the chronology of the disturbances from the Toyama coast in late July 1918 to the urban outbreaks of August, separating the July 22–23 Uozu events from the August 3 Nishimizuhashi episode and from the later city riots.
  4. Analyze the role of women in originating the movement and evaluate how the press represented their actions.
  5. Compare disturbances in Nagoya, Kyoto, Osaka, Kobe, Tokyo, and other cities, explaining local differences in crowd composition, targets, and official response.
  6. Contextualize rural, mining, and Buraku participation, and explain why grievances and opportunities for collective action differed by class, occupation, gender, and discrimination.
  7. Assess the actions of municipal officials, police, the army, censors, and the courts, distinguishing negotiation, relief, coercion, and lethal force.
  8. Evaluate the riots' contribution to the fall of the Terauchi cabinet and the formation of the Hara cabinet while weighing other factors.
  9. Interpret the competing historical arguments that treat 1918 as a food riot, a moral-economy protest, a popular political movement, or a demonstration of the state's coercive capacity.
  10. Analyze primary sources, including price series, newspaper reports, official records, and diaries, identifying their authors, purposes, limitations, and silences.
  11. Connect the crisis to later developments in social policy, labor activism, and debates over citizenship without assuming that democratization followed automatically.

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  2. What distinguished the Toyama coast protests from the later urban riots, and why does the difference matter for explaining the origins of the movement?
  3. How did news, rumor, and shared grievance spread protest across hundreds of localities without a central organization?
  4. Why did crowds in different places choose different targets and tactics, and what do those choices reveal about local social relationships?
  5. How did class, gender, occupation, and discrimination shape who participated, what they demanded, and how they were treated by the authorities?
  6. When and why did local officials negotiate with crowds, and when and why did they turn to police and soldiers?
  7. To what extent did the riots cause the fall of the Terauchi cabinet, and what other factors must be weighed?
  8. Should the Rice Riots be understood as a traditional food protest, a response to industrial capitalism, a form of popular politics, or some combination, and what evidence would help decide?

Historical Context

By 1918 Japan was a constitutional monarchy governed under the Meiji Constitution, with an elected House of Representatives, a House of Peers, an appointed bureaucracy, a military with direct access to the throne, and a group of senior statesmen, the genrō, whose influence over the choice of prime ministers was fading but still real. Since October 1916 the cabinet had been led by General Terauchi Masatake, a former army minister and governor-general of Korea, whose government drew its strength from the bureaucracy, the army, and the support of Yamagata Aritomo rather than from a party majority. The Terauchi government had presided over wartime expansion, loans to China, and, in the summer of 1918, a decision to send troops to Siberia. These developments belong to Chapters 66 and 67 and appear here only as context.

Japan's cities had grown rapidly. Industrial employment in factories, shipyards, and mines expanded during the war, drawing migrants from the countryside into districts of tenements, rented rooms, and company housing. Many urban households bought nearly all their food. Rural Japan, by contrast, was not a uniform society of self-sufficient farmers. Landlords collected rents in rice from tenants, who often paid a large share of their crop; many cultivators sold rice to meet rent, taxes, and debts and then bought other grains or rice later in the year. Coastal communities, forest villages, and mining settlements depended on markets for staple food. The food-supply system linked these groups through merchants, rice exchanges, railways, and coastal shipping, and through a colonial empire that supplied additional rice from Korea and Taiwan.

Popular protest was not new. Tokugawa-period villagers and townspeople had petitioned, struck bargains, and sometimes smashed the property of rice dealers and officials in actions known as ikki and uchikowashi (Chapter 44). Crowds had also appeared in the politics of the modern city: the Hibiya riot of 1905 (Chapter 61) and the crowd demonstrations of the 1913 political crisis showed that urban people could assemble, march, and attack symbols of authority. Nor were the Toyama women's actions entirely without precedent in the history of food protest. What was distinctive in 1918 was the combination of a national press, railways and telegraphs, a large wage-earning population, and a government that had few instruments for managing food prices.

The chapter's conceptual vocabulary draws on social history. The historian E. P. Thompson argued that eighteenth-century English food crowds acted on a "moral economy," a shared understanding of fair prices and the obligations of merchants and authorities. Applying such ideas to Japan requires care. Japanese crowds of 1918 existed in a society of newspapers, parties, and wage labor, and some participants expressed claims that went beyond neighborly obligation. Historians debate how far such claims amounted to a new language of rights, a subject the chapter takes up in its final division.

Division I — War, Inflation, and the Economic Foundations of the Crisis

The first division establishes that the Rice Riots were a crisis of the distribution and affordability of an essential food, not necessarily a collapse of agricultural production. It begins with the wartime economy, moves to the social meaning of rice and the arithmetic of household budgets, and ends with the commercial and diplomatic shocks of the summer of 1918.

Section 68.1

Japan in 1918: Wartime Prosperity and the Cost of Living

The First World War transformed the Japanese economy in ways that Chapter 66 has described. European industrial powers, preoccupied with war, withdrew from many Asian markets and demanded munitions, ships, and supplies. Japanese manufacturers of textiles, chemicals, and metal goods expanded exports. Shipping companies earned unprecedented freight rates, and shipbuilders could not meet demand. The result was a trade surplus and a shift in Japan's international financial position: from a debtor nation dependent on foreign loans, Japan became a creditor. Business profits soared, and the press coined the term narikin, borrowed from the game of shōgi, for the newly rich who had risen in a season by fortunate investments in shipping, trade, or speculation.

This was growth with a pronounced distributional edge. Factory employment grew, and in some trades nominal wages rose, particularly for skilled workers in shipyards, steelworks, and machine shops. But general prices rose faster than wages for large parts of the working population, and the price of necessities such as rice, charcoal, fish, and rent moved sharply upward. Historians generally judge that real wages, meaning the purchasing power of earnings, stagnated or fell for many wage earners during 1917 and 1918, though the calculation depends on which occupation, region, and price series is used. Workers in newly established heavy industries often did better than casual laborers, textile workers, or the families of fishermen. A household's experience depended less on the national averages than on whether its income was fixed, paid in kind, or tied to the prices of the goods it sold.

The war boom therefore widened the visible distance between rich and poor. In Osaka, Kobe, Tokyo, and Nagoya, urban residents saw new mansions, expensive entertainments, and the conspicuous consumption of the war profiteers, while their own food bills climbed. This visual contrast helps explain why later crowds directed anger at merchants and trading houses that seemed to symbolize the new wealth. It also warns against treating inflation as a purely technical matter. Prices carried moral meaning when they were perceived as the fruit of speculation rather than of production.

Historians trace these conditions through several kinds of evidence: price indices compiled by the Bank of Japan and local governments, wage surveys, municipal household-budget investigations, and contemporary reporting. Each is partial. Surveys of household budgets were few and typically concentrated on urban workers, while rural, mining, and fishing households were less systematically studied. A student should therefore read statements about the cost of living as inferences from selected series, and the next sections show how to read those series critically.

Section 68.2

Rice as Food, Commodity, and Social Necessity

Rice occupied an unusual position in early twentieth-century Japan. It was simultaneously the staple food, the main agricultural crop, a store of value, and a symbol. In the Tokugawa period the wealth of domains had been measured in koku of rice, and tax obligations had been paid largely in grain. Although the Meiji land tax converted that obligation to money, rent paid by tenants to landlords continued to be calculated and often paid in rice, so that rice moved from farm to market as both a food and a rent receipt. The national crop was on the order of tens of millions of koku, supplemented by imports from Korea and Taiwan, a flow that grew with colonial development (Chapter 62).

For consumers, rice was not uniformly consumed. Wealthy and urban households ate polished white rice as the central element of meals, and expected to be able to buy it. Poorer households, especially in rural and mountainous districts, often mixed rice with barley or millet, or ate less rice and more cheaper grains and vegetables. As rice prices rose, households faced a choice of reducing consumption, shifting to cheaper substitutes, or sacrificing other expenditures. The substitution options were limited for city dwellers accustomed to rice and for families whose cooking facilities and food habits were organized around it. Food, and rice in particular, formed a large share of the spending of working-class households; a rise in the staple could thus consume a significant proportion of a family budget, while the same rise had a smaller effect on affluent households.

A crucial distinction separates those who produced rice from those who needed to buy it. Landowning farmers who sold surplus rice might benefit from high prices. Tenants who paid rent in kind often retained little after rent, taxes, fertilizer, and debts, and some had to purchase rice or other grain later in the year. Agricultural laborers received wages that rarely rose in step with prices. Fishing households along the coast, like those of Toyama Bay, produced fish rather than rice and relied on purchase. Miners and industrial workers bought their food at stores, markets, or company outlets. In this sense the 1918 crisis produced losers even in a prefecture such as Toyama that was a rice-producing region, because local production was exported while local consumers faced high prices.

Popular expectations about rice were also moral. In the long Japanese tradition of subsistence protest, communities regarded rice dealers as having obligations beyond profit. A merchant who sold rice away from a hungry community, or who stored it to await higher prices, might be accused of violating a legitimate expectation. This expectation was not unchanging, and it was not the only language available in 1918. But it provided a powerful vocabulary for the earliest protests.

Historical Vocabulary
Kome sōdō, ikki, shō, and koku

Kome sōdō (米騒動) literally means "rice disturbance" and is the standard Japanese name for the events of 1918. The term sōdō carries connotations of tumult and disorder, a vocabulary favored by officials; historians who emphasize popular claims sometimes prefer to speak of a rice "uprising" or "movement," while others retain the standard term while analyzing the diversity it covers. Ikki originally referred to a league or band of people united for a common purpose, and in the Tokugawa period came to denote collective peasant action. When the press called the early Toyama events nyōbō ikki, it invoked this older vocabulary and, often, a note of curiosity or condescension about women's participation.

Shō and koku are units of volume used in the rice trade. One shō is roughly 1.8 liters, ten shō make one to, and ten to make one koku, so a koku is about 180 liters, traditionally the amount of rice believed sufficient to feed one adult for a year. Retail prices were commonly quoted per shō and wholesale or exchange prices per koku. Mixing them is a frequent error.

Subsistence in this chapter refers to the ability of a household to obtain enough staple food to live. Claims that merchants and authorities have obligations to protect subsistence are central to the interpretation of the riots as a form of "moral economy" protest.

Section 68.3

Prices, Wages, Household Budgets, and the Crisis of Affordability

The central economic fact of the summer of 1918 was the pace of the rise in rice prices. By the commonly cited price series, wholesale and exchange prices more than doubled in the two years before the crisis and reached a peak in the late summer, with further rises still to come in 1919. Retail prices followed, with local variations and delays. Because wholesale quotations were set in great exchanges while retail prices were set by local shopkeepers dealing with customers, the two measures could diverge, and consumers experienced the retail price, in the particular shop where they bought. Differences between shops, neighborhoods, and regions could therefore matter as much as national averages.

Wages are harder to summarize. Nominal wages, the money paid per day or month, did rise in many sectors from 1915 to 1918, particularly in industries experiencing labor shortages. But nominal rises must be compared with the price of necessities to measure real purchasing power, and the comparison is complicated by the fact that wages were paid in different forms, by piece or by day, with bonuses and seasonal fluctuations. For a household with several earners, the cost of rice relative to combined earnings mattered more than any single wage. For casual laborers, whose employment was irregular, a daily wage that might look adequate on paper represented a smaller monthly income. Rents in industrial cities rose as migrants poured in, further squeezing budgets.

Historians therefore make four distinctions when they analyze the affordability crisis. They distinguish wholesale from retail prices; nominal from real values; one place and date from another; and one occupational group from another. A statement that "rice prices doubled" is incomplete until the series, the unit, the location, and the dates are identified. The documentary exercise below shows the procedure.

Section 68.4

Rice Merchants, Wholesale Markets, Transport, and Speculation

Rice reached consumers through a chain of intermediaries. Farmers and landlords sold or shipped rice to local dealers and regional collectors. Merchants in port towns and regional centers stored it in warehouses and sent it by railway or coastal ship to larger markets. At the top of this system stood the great rice exchanges, especially the Dōjima exchange in Osaka and the exchange at Kayabachō in Tokyo, where contracts for future delivery were bought and sold. Telegraph lines carried market quotations across the country in hours, so that a price movement in an exchange could affect a rural dealer's decisions the same day. Retail rice sellers, who bought from wholesalers and sold by the shō to customers, stood at the end of the chain and were the most visible to consumers.

Speculation and hoarding had long been suspected in such a system, and the public suspected them in 1918. Merchants could legitimately hold stocks in anticipation of price changes, and a shipment out of a region was a normal part of trade. But the same acts could be perceived as hoarding, kaishime, when prices were rising and households were struggling. The line between prudent inventory management and profiteering was drawn differently by merchants, officials, and consumers. In the crisis, newspapers and speeches named particular firms as hoarders, and rumors circulated, some probably accurate and some exaggerated, about stocks hidden in warehouses.

It would be a mistake to explain the price rise solely by speculation. Demand for rice grew with urbanization and the growth of the wage-earning population, while production was increasing slowly. Labor leaving agriculture for factories affected farm output, and the harvest in individual years varied with weather and local conditions. Colonial imports added to supply but could not entirely close the gap. The financial environment of the war, with abundant money and rising prices of all goods, made storing commodities attractive. Speculation amplified these underlying pressures, especially in the short run, and the public perception of speculation was therefore historically real even where it was imperfect as an explanation.

The structure of the trade also shaped the protests. A local rice dealer was a neighbor, known by name, and could be held accountable in face-to-face confrontation. A great trading house or an exchange speculator was distant, and crowds could reach it only by symbolic action, such as attacking a visible shop or head office. This difference helps explain why the earliest protests in small coastal communities took the form of appeals to local merchants, while the later urban riots targeted a variety of commercial figures.

Section 68.5

The Siberian Intervention, Rumors of Military Demand, and the Immediate Price Shock

In 1917 the Russian revolutions and the withdrawal of Russia from the war created a diplomatic and military problem for the Allied powers, and in 1918 Britain, France, and the United States debated intervention in Russia, including the protection of stores and the evacuation of Czechoslovak troops. Japan's decision, discussed in Chapter 66, to send forces to Siberia was announced on August 2, 1918, after months of cabinet debate and negotiation with the Allies. The announcement was the culmination of a period in which the expectation of intervention had already circulated in the press and the business world.

The relevant point for this chapter is the commercial effect of that expectation. A military expedition needs food, and rice is the staple of the Japanese army's rations. Traders and speculators anticipated that the army would buy large quantities of rice, and in July prices moved upward sharply as buyers sought stocks. The sums involved, and the scale of any army purchase, are matters of record and rumor rather than precise knowledge; what is documented is that contemporaries, including officials, linked the price spike to the expected expedition. The effect was to convert a steady inflation into a visible shock at the moment when public anxiety about food was already high.

It is useful to distinguish the long-term from the immediate causes. The long-term causes were wartime inflation, urban growth, slow growth in domestic production relative to demand, and the commercial structure that allowed speculation. The immediate catalysts were the July spike and the intervention announcement. Neither alone is a sufficient explanation. Inflation without the July shock might have produced hardship without a national movement; the shock without the prior inflation would have found households with more margin. Together, in a population primed by experience of rising prices, they made the summer decisive.

The Rice-Price Shock of Summer 1918

Long-term causes

Meetings, petitions, and attacks on merchants; emergency sales and relief in many localities; military deployment and arrests; a government ban on press coverage.

Historical complexity: The riots cannot be reduced to speculation, to the Siberian Intervention, or to hunger alone. Division II — Toyama Bay, Women's Collective Action, and the Origins of Protest

The second division reconstructs the origins of the movement on the Toyama coast. It treats the women's actions as the beginning of a political event, not as a picturesque prelude, and takes care to distinguish the several episodes of late July and early August.

Section 68.6

Fishing Communities of Toyama Bay: Women, Work, and Household Survival

Toyama Prefecture lay on the Sea of Japan coast, a region of rice agriculture on alluvial plains, and of fishing communities along the shore of Toyama Bay. Its port towns, among them Uozu, Mizuhashi, Namerikawa, Fushiki, and Takaoka, had long served as shipping points for goods moving along the coast, and in earlier centuries the Hokuriku region had been tied by sea to the northern coast and to Hokkaido. By the early twentieth century, rail and steamship connections shipped rice and other goods out of the prefecture to the industrial cities of the Kinki and elsewhere. Toyama was, in short, a rice-exporting region.

The economy of the coastal households, however, depended on fishing and on supplementary work. Fishing households were not usually landowners with rice to sell. They bought rice, and they paid for it from the proceeds of the catch, wage labor, and other earnings. Historians often note that in the summer many men of the region were away for seasonal work, including fishing in northern waters, which left women to manage households and make decisions. The extent and pattern of this seasonal migration should be understood from local studies rather than assumed uniform, but it provides one explanation for the prominence of women in the early actions. Women also performed a variety of paid and unpaid tasks: processing and selling fish, working on the shore, and in port communities, handling and carrying cargo, including rice, as part of the local labor economy.

The export of locally grown rice in a season of rising prices therefore had immediate meaning. The same rice that passed through the village on its way to the ship was what local households needed and could not afford. Seeing rice loaded for departure while local prices rose created a particular grievance. It was not hunger in the abstract, but the visible removal of food from a community, and a perceived choice by merchants to prefer distant, high-paying markets over local neighbors.

Section 68.7

The Meetings and Actions at Uozu, July 22–23, 1918

The earliest events widely treated as part of the Rice Riots took place at Uozu in the final third of July 1918. Accounts based on local records and the historical literature describe a meeting of women from fishing households on July 22 and a gathering at the shore on July 23, when women confronted the loading of rice for shipment. These are related but distinguishable milestones, and the chapter keeps them separate. The first was a discussion among community members about the price and shipment of rice; the second was a public collective action aimed at preventing, or at least challenging, an actual shipment. The careful historian treats dates and details as dependent on the sources used, since local histories, later recollections, and contemporary newspaper reports do not agree in every particular.

The sources indicate that participants sought to persuade or compel those handling the rice to stop the shipment and to arrange for rice to be sold locally at a lower price. The women appealed to merchants and shipping interests, and they were met by local officials and police. In the accounts, the initial confrontation did not take the form of widespread looting or arson; it involved assembling in numbers, speaking, and pressing demands. The encounter also illustrates how quickly a local issue could involve officials: police were called, and the local authorities and notable figures were drawn into mediation. The result was not a lasting resolution but an event that was reported, discussed, and imitated.

Students should note what historians can and cannot establish. The existence of a gathering of women at Uozu in late July and its connection to the shipment of rice is supported by local and national sources. The precise number of participants, the identities of leaders, and the exact words spoken are less secure, and later retellings, including journalistic and political accounts, embroidered the story. In some retellings the Uozu actions became the single "origin" of the riots; modern historians prefer to see them as a significant early episode in a longer sequence of local meetings and actions, and to take the coastal region, not one village, as the setting of origin.

Section 68.8

Preventing Rice Shipments: Collective Bargaining, Local Authority, and Community Justice

The methods of the early Toyama protests are as significant as their timing. Participants did not initially seek to overthrow authority or to seize goods. They asked, in public and in numbers, that rice shipments be stopped and that rice be sold locally at a price households could pay. This combination of petition and pressure resembles a form of collective bargaining: the crowd's size and its presence at the site of the shipment gave weight to a request that individual households could not have made effectively.

Several elements of community authority operated. Reputation mattered; a merchant who had to live among his neighbors had reason to hesitate before refusing them. Local notables and officials could mediate. The presence of women, who were mothers and household managers, gave the protest a moral standing that officials found hard to dismiss as mere disorder. At the same time, the crowd's pressure rested on an implicit possibility of escalation, and local authorities knew that a stalemate could turn violent.

The moral logic is often described as that of a moral economy: a belief that in time of scarcity the community's need for staple food took precedence over a merchant's right to sell wherever he could obtain the highest price. Applied to Toyama, this expectation helps explain why the demand was to keep rice in the locality and sell it at fair prices, rather than to redistribute wealth in general. The idea should not be applied mechanically. Merchants and officials had their own claims about legal rights of trade, and the women's demand was also an assertion that the community was entitled to a voice in how local resources were used.

What the protests did not do is also instructive. In many reports they did not specify demands for national government reform, and they did not aim to change political institutions. They were nonetheless political in the broad sense that they challenged an allocation of resources and demanded accountability from those with local power. That tension between a narrow subsistence demand and its wider political implications runs through the whole event.

Section 68.9

Nishimizuhashi, Namerikawa, and the Spread along the Toyama Coast

In the first days of August the protests intensified and spread along the coast. The disturbance at Nishimizuhashi on August 3 is among the best known and is highlighted in archival chronologies. Accounts describe a gathering of women, numbering in the hundreds by some reports, who confronted rice dealers in the community and pressed demands for the reduction of prices and the sale of rice to residents. Police and local officials attended, and the situation was more tense than in the earlier Uozu actions. Namerikawa and other communities along the shore saw similar gatherings in the same days, and nearby villages and towns followed.

As protests spread, their character varied. In some places crowds of women and men petitioned dealers and wealthy residents; in others, tension turned to damage of property. Local authorities differed as well: some officials arranged emergency sales and negotiated with merchants, while others relied on police to disperse assemblies and arrest leaders. The coastal movement, in other words, had already begun to develop the diversity that would characterize the national events. It did not follow a single script.

This episode also marked the point at which the events reached beyond the region. Reports of the Toyama gatherings were transmitted to newspapers in Osaka and Tokyo and entered national discussion, an important step in the diffusion examined in Division III. Contemporary journalists linked the Toyama events to broader price and supply concerns, and in doing so framed what was happening on the coast as a sign of a national problem.

Section 68.10

The "Women's Uprising": Press Representation and the Question of Political Agency

When national newspapers reported the Toyama events, they gave them a name: nyōbō ikki, or the "wives' uprising," often with the regional label Etchū, the old name of the Toyama region. The phrase conveyed the novelty of women acting collectively and in public, and it drew on older images of peasant uprisings. It also had effects that historians now regard critically. By presenting the events as the desperate response of housewives to hunger, the press tended to treat women's actions as emotional or instinctive, and to minimize their organization, strategy, and political content. The phrase made the movement seem exotic and local, and it distracted attention from the structural economic causes.

Reading beyond the press labels, historians see in the Toyama actions evidence of women's political agency. The participants calculated where and when to gather, whom to approach, and what to ask. They used the resources available to them: numbers, the moral standing of mothers, community networks, and public visibility. They were not passive victims. Their actions were also constrained, because women in Japan could not vote or hold office, and the Public Order and Police Law of 1900 had barred women from political associations and assemblies of a political character. The protest was thus conducted by people formally excluded from conventional politics, and this exclusion shaped both their methods and the authorities' response.

It is important to avoid two errors. One is to treat the Toyama women as the sole movers of the national riots; as the urban events show, men formed a large part of later crowds, and the national movement drew on many social groups. The other is to treat their role as merely symbolic. The coastal actions were significant as an early episode, as an influence on public attention, and as a demonstration of what organized household women could do. The distinction between the female leadership of the early coastal actions and the broader social composition of later disturbances is one of the keys to a careful account.

Section 68.11

How Protest Spread: Newspapers, Networks, Rumor, and Shared Grievances

The speed with which disturbances spread in August 1918 invites the question of how. One answer is communication. Japan in 1918 had mass-circulation newspapers, a telegraph and telephone network, an extensive railway system, and a literate urban population. Reports from Toyama reached Osaka, Tokyo, and other cities within days, and the press in turn reported subsequent events in other places. Rumor supplemented the printed word. Stories of hoarding by particular firms, of shipments of rice leaving a district, and of what crowds in other places had done circulated by word of mouth in markets, workplaces, tenement districts, and tea shops.

A second answer is shared grievance. Because the price rise affected many households at the same time, residents of different places perceived similar problems and responded in similar ways. They did not need a centralized organization to conclude that merchants should reduce prices or that officials should intervene. The coincidence of experience made imitation easy. A crowd that learned that elsewhere merchants had reduced prices under pressure had reason to think the tactic could work locally.

A third element was existing local networks. Neighborhood associations, workplace groups, religious and festival communities, and the informal ties of kin and acquaintance provided means to call people together. Public parks and temple grounds served as customary gathering places. In some cities, individuals with oratorical skill or local standing, including labor activists, ward notables, or men associated with local entertainment and construction groups, played roles in drawing crowds and leading them, though historians debate how far such figures organized events as opposed to riding them.

The distinction between diffusion and coordination is central. The evidence does not show a national organization directing the riots, and the authorities themselves, searching for instigators, found no evidence of a single directing center. This does not mean the events were mere chaos. Crowds had aims, chose targets, and often showed restraint in some respects and violence in others. The picture that emerges is of a movement spread by communication and imitation, shaped by local organization, and therefore highly varied.

Section 68.12

Nagoya: Public Meetings, Crowds, and Conflict with Police

Nagoya was one of the first major cities to experience large-scale unrest. The local chronology begins on the evening of August 9, when a rumor of a meeting on the rice-price question drew a gathering to Tsurumai Park, a municipal park and established place for public assembly. A contemporary Nagoya Shinbun report counted roughly 300–400 people that first evening, when the crowd largely dispersed without speeches or major damage. On August 10 a much larger gathering assembled, speeches were delivered at the park music hall, and groups subsequently moved into the city. Nagoya City Archives preserves a municipal memorandum estimating approximately 20,000 on August 10, while the contemporary newspaper gave a different and larger estimate. These are divergent contemporaneous descriptions, not an audited attendance total.

After the larger August 10 assembly, portions of the crowd moved toward rice merchants and administrative targets. The next nights saw demands directed at dealers to reduce prices, attacks on property, and confrontations with police. Police attempted to disperse the assembly and to protect shops, producing confrontations. The disturbances in Nagoya continued for several days through about August 13, with fluctuating intensity. Municipal authorities and merchants responded with emergency measures, including the sale of rice at reduced prices, and troops were deployed to maintain order in the city, as the national state moved toward greater reliance on military force.

Nagoya shows several features that recur elsewhere. First, a public meeting and a riot were not opposites; the meeting provided the occasion and the assembled mass, and the shift from speech to action depended on the crowd's reaction to what was said and to police efforts to disperse it. Second, the targets were local rice sellers, who were identifiable and accessible. Third, the relief measures offered by the city were a response to the pressure of the crowd and were understood as such by participants. These patterns allow comparison with Kyoto, Osaka, and Kobe, where different local conditions produced different results.

The distinction between August 9 and 10 is particularly instructive about crowd formation. The Nagoya City Archives uses both contemporary newspaper reports and a municipal document called the Temporary Incident File (Rice Price Surge), volume ID 7480 (A-So T230). The municipality reported speeches and a very large attendance on the second night and described a group moving toward the governor’s residence, with stones breaking windows at the nearby Waterworks Division. The city council also considered relief sales of imported rice at cost. These details show that protest could develop from rumor into a mass public meeting, then into pressure on administrators and property, while officials simultaneously discussed concession and policing. The widely differing estimates of attendance in contemporary sources should be shown as evidence of uncertainty, not blended into an authoritative national total.

Section 68.13

Kyoto: Neighborhood Mobilization, Rice Merchants, and Buraku Communities

In Kyoto the disturbances began on about August 10, according to the records of the Kyoto Prefectural Archives and the standard accounts, and a major locus was the Higashi-Shichijō district, a Buraku community in the south of the city. The Buraku communities were the descendants of groups classified in the Tokugawa period as eta and hinin, whose members continued after the abolition of formal status categories in 1871 to experience social discrimination in marriage, employment, residence, and everyday life (Chapters 43 and 55). In Kyoto, as in other cities, such communities often lived in crowded districts with a high proportion of casual laborers, small tradespeople, and workers in leather, footwear, and other occupations, and so were especially exposed to rice-price increases.

Residents of the district gathered, and crowds went to rice dealers' shops in the vicinity and elsewhere in the city, demanding reduced prices and, in some cases, damaging property. The authorities responded with emergency relief, including arrangements for the sale of rice at lower prices, and with police and troops. The deployment of soldiers followed a pattern repeated in other cities: as the police proved unable to control large crowds, prefectural authorities requested military assistance.

Buraku participation requires careful treatment. Historians have documented the involvement of Buraku residents in Kyoto and some other localities and have shown that discrimination shaped both the experience of the participants and the official response. Authorities and the press, in some cases, portrayed Buraku communities as the instigators or the criminal element in the riots, and arrest and prosecution patterns in some localities fell heavily on such communities. At the same time, the Kyoto crowds were not composed solely of Buraku residents, and the community was not homogeneous. Its residents included people of different occupations and different views, and not all joined protests. Historians therefore warn against both erasing Buraku participation and treating it as the sole explanation for urban disturbance. The significance of the episode for later history is considerable, since the experience of 1918 and its aftermath contributed to the discussions that led to the formation of the Suiheisha in 1922 (Chapter 69).

Section 68.14

Osaka: Industrial Workers, Urban Poverty, and Mass Confrontation

Osaka, Japan's great commercial and industrial city, was the home of the Dōjima rice exchange and of a large population of factory workers, casual laborers, and small tradespeople. Its rapid growth during the war created districts of dense housing and low incomes. The disturbances in Osaka occurred in the middle of August, in the days after the events in Nagoya and Kyoto, with large gatherings in public places, among them Tennōji Park, and attacks on rice shops and other commercial premises.

The social setting of the Osaka crowd was urban industrial. Participants included wage earners from factories and workshops, day laborers, small shopkeepers and artisans, and residents of the poor districts of the city. For these people, the connection between the rice price and the wartime profits made by Osaka's merchants and manufacturers was immediate. Osaka was also the center of the rice trade, and its brokers and exchange figures were targets of suspicion.

The authorities responded with police, with emergency relief arrangements, and with military forces from the garrison. The confrontation between large crowds and troops produced some of the most serious clashes of the urban disturbances. As elsewhere, the course of events varied by district and by day, and the crowd was not a single entity. Different groups moved through the city, some pursuing particular targets and others joining as events developed. The number of participants and casualties in Osaka, as for the riots generally, is a matter on which reports differ, and the chapter does not adopt a single figure.

Osaka illustrates the relationship between industrial growth and protest. The riots were not an expression of an organized labor movement, since labor unions were weak and legally constrained, but the same workers who would soon be drawn to the labor organizations of the postwar period took part in the crowds. The overlap between the experiences of the shop floor and of the street is a theme Chapter 69 will develop.

Section 68.15

Kobe: Suzuki Shōten, Commercial Power, and the Destruction of Property

Kobe, the great treaty port of the Kansai, produced one of the most dramatic incidents of the riots: the burning of the premises of Suzuki Shōten on August 12. Suzuki Shōten was a trading house that had expanded dramatically during the war, becoming one of the largest commercial enterprises in Japan, with interests in sugar, steel, shipping, and other commodities. Under the direction of its manager Kaneko Naokichi, it had profited immensely from the wartime boom. For many Kobe residents the firm symbolized the new wealth, and rumors circulated that it had hoarded rice and other goods. The historian must treat the hoarding charge cautiously: the rumors were widely believed and widely reported, and they shaped crowd action, but the extent to which they were accurate is a separate question that sources do not settle neatly.

On August 12 crowds attacked and set fire to the firm's buildings. Other targets in Kobe included newspaper premises, reflecting hostility toward a press that some crowds regarded as aligned with merchants or the government, and other commercial property. The authorities, at first unable to control the crowd, relied on police and then troops. The destruction was considerable, and the images of burning buildings in a port city circulated in public discussion and in the memory of the event.

The Suzuki incident shows how crowd anger could address concentrated commercial power, not just local retailers. Participants evidently understood the firm as part of a system of wartime profit. At the same time, the destruction of property was not the only form of action in Kobe, and the crowd's behavior combined attacks with demands. The later history of the firm, which suffered failure in the financial crisis of 1927 (Chapter 73), belongs to another chapter, but the 1918 incident marks an early confrontation between popular anger and the new economic elites. The government also treated it as significant: the diary of the communications minister Den Kenjirō records concern about Kobe.

Section 68.16

Tokyo, Hiroshima, Kure, and Other Cities: Comparing Urban Disturbances

The disturbances reached Tokyo in mid-August, and the capital experienced crowd actions in several districts, with attacks on rice dealers and other shops and confrontations with police. Tokyo's disturbances were in some respects less concentrated than those of Osaka and Kobe, reflecting the capital's more dispersed neighborhoods and a different relationship between crowds and the authorities; the capital's police were large, and the national government was at hand. The disturbances in the capital nonetheless demonstrated that protest had reached the center of the state.

In western Honshu, Hiroshima experienced disturbances, and the naval base town of Kure saw particularly serious crowd action, in a setting shaped by the navy's dockyards and a large workforce dependent on purchased food. Other cities in the Kansai, the Inland Sea, northern Kyushu, and elsewhere saw outbreaks of varying scale, and some smaller towns and villages experienced actions that were small in scale but locally significant. The pattern was uneven: some cities had major riots, others only meetings or negotiated price reductions, and others saw little documented action at all.

Comparison reveals what local conditions mattered. Where large concentrations of wage earners lived in districts with weak neighborhood notables and a history of crowd action, riots tended to be larger. Where municipal governments acted quickly to arrange relief sales, crowds were sometimes placated, though not reliably. Where merchants were visible and associated with wartime profit, they were targeted. Where police and troops were present in force, violence could escalate or be forestalled depending on tactics. Division IV — Rural Protest, Mining Communities, and Unequal Social Experience

The fourth division moves beyond the cities. Villages, coalfields, and the empire's peripheries experienced the crisis differently, and the social composition of participants reveals how class, occupation, gender, and discrimination shaped collective action.

Section 68.17

Rural Japan: Tenant Farmers, Villages, and the Price of Rice

It might be assumed that rising rice prices would benefit the countryside. For landowners who sold rice, this was often true. But the agrarian population was highly stratified. Tenant farmers, who cultivated land owned by others and paid rent in rice, typically handed over a large share of the crop and retained a small remainder for household consumption, seed, and the payment of other costs. Many had to purchase rice or other grain later in the year, when the previous year's harvest was exhausted and prices were highest. Agricultural laborers worked for wages that did not follow prices. Smallholders who produced less than they needed also became buyers.

The summer was a vulnerable season. The previous year's rice had been consumed or sold, and the new crop was not yet harvested. Households that depended on purchased rice in these months were exposed to price rises at the moment when their own stocks were at their lowest. Rural dwellers who were not cultivators, such as woodcutters, fishermen, and artisans, relied entirely on markets.

The experience of rural households thus differed from that of the urban poor in being embedded in village relationships. Landlords, village officials, and merchants were known figures, and relations of obligation and dependency shaped what villagers could expect and demand. The same hardship might produce petitions, appeals for relief, or collective pressure on local dealers, depending on the balance of power and the character of local leadership. The actions of tenants against landlords, which became a major theme of the following years, were not yet the main form of village protest in 1918, but the conditions that produced later tenant disputes were visible.

Everyday Life and Environment

Buying Rice in 1918: Households and Unequal Exposure to Inflation

For most households, rice was bought in small quantities, by the shō or even less, from a neighborhood dealer, often on credit recorded in the dealer's ledger. Responsibility for purchasing and preparing food fell largely to women, who therefore experienced price changes daily and negotiated directly with shopkeepers. A household might respond to higher prices by mixing more barley or other grain into the rice, eating less, buying inferior rice, delaying other purchases, borrowing from the dealer, or sending children or additional family members to work. The ability to adjust differed enormously. A prosperous household could absorb the increase with little change in diet. A casual laborer's family might have no reserve.

These everyday strategies are poorly recorded in official sources, and historians reconstruct them from household-budget surveys, memoirs, and local accounts. Their significance is that crowds were composed of people who had already made such adjustments and had reached the limit of what they could do individually. The turn to collective action marked the failure of household strategies.

Section 68.18

Landlords, Rents, and the Moral Economy of Village Protest

In village protest, demands were directed at several kinds of people: rice dealers, brewers, wealthy landowners, and local officials. Villagers sought reductions in the price of rice, the sale of rice at fair prices, relief from those with stocks, and, in some places, concessions on rent. Some protests resembled the moral-economy actions of earlier centuries: assemblies that visited the houses of the wealthy to request or require contributions, often with an expectation that those who had profited from the crisis would share in relief. The community often accepted such pressure as legitimate when conducted in customary forms.

Relations between landlords and tenants were tense but varied. In some regions landlords were close to village life and regarded as patrons with duties; in others they were absentee owners who took rice from distant estates. Landlords who sold rice at high prices profited, and their wealth was visible. Tenants' grievances about rent were expressed in the 1918 disturbances in some places, though the major tenant movements came later. The immediate subsistence grievance and the broader dispute about the economic power of the village elite were intertwined and not always separable.

The village disturbances also illustrate the role of local authority in managing conflict. Village headmen, landlords, and notables often acted as mediators, offering relief or persuading dealers to lower prices, and thereby defusing crowds. Where such mediation failed or where outside authorities intervened, violence could follow. The rural disturbances were thus less visible in the national press than urban riots, but they were numerous, and a full account of the Rice Riots requires attention to them.

Section 68.19

The Coalfields: Ube, Kyushu, and the Escalation of Mining Protest

Among the most serious episodes of the Rice Riots occurred in the coal-mining districts. Coal was the principal industrial fuel and a major export, and the wartime boom had expanded production in Kyushu's Chikuhō and other fields, in the Ube field of Yamaguchi Prefecture, and in Hokkaido. Mining communities were distinctive social worlds. Miners lived in company housing or dormitories, often in isolated settlements; they bought food at company-affiliated stores or from merchants who served the mine; and they were subject to the discipline of company management and, in many fields, of labor bosses and subcontracting systems. Work was dangerous and wages were often paid in ways that left workers in debt (Chapter 63).

In such settings the rise in rice prices became part of a larger set of grievances. The price at the company store, the level of wages, hours and conditions of work, and the authority of the company over daily life were all at issue. Protests in mining districts began as demands for price reductions or rice relief but could escalate into attacks on company offices, stores, and managers' houses. The stakes were higher than in an urban crowd because the company controlled both workplace and community, and because the police and the company's own security forces were often closely linked.

The mining disturbances were, by many accounts, among the most violent episodes of the riots, and some involved the use of lethal force by police or troops. The confrontations in mining districts suggest how a protest over food could become a struggle over industrial authority, and how the state's coercive power was used to protect the property and discipline of the large companies. Reports of casualties differ among sources, and students should treat numbers cautiously and record their provenance. The mining protests also bring forward the issue of labor organization: the miners' actions were not led by unions, but they exposed grievances that unions would later seek to address.

Section 68.20

Class, Occupation, Gender, and Discrimination among Participants

A comparison of the participants shows why the Rice Riots cannot be assigned to a single social group. Fishing-community women, as in Toyama, acted as household managers whose primary concern was the provision of food, and their methods emphasized assembly and appeal. Industrial workers in cities such as Osaka and Kobe experienced inflation through the wage relationship and often took part in large urban crowds, in which men predominated. Day laborers, whose employment was irregular, were especially vulnerable and prominent among those arrested in many places. Miners combined food grievances with grievances against the company. Tenant farmers and village residents acted within the framework of local relationships. Small merchants and artisans, who bought rice for their households and were squeezed by rising costs, sometimes joined crowds even though they might also be victims of attacks. Residents of Buraku communities took part in several places, in circumstances shaped by poverty and discrimination.

These groups did not have the same motives or opportunities. Women, excluded from formal politics and constrained by legal prohibitions on political assembly, had fewer public channels and relied on community pressure. Men with wage employment faced the risk of dismissal and of arrest, but also had workplace networks. Miners lived under company authority that could dismiss and evict them. Buraku residents confronted police and prosecutors who were predisposed to suspect them. The state's responses also differed with the status of the participants.

Historians therefore speak of the riots as a convergence of diverse social movements, not a unified popular uprising. The convergence was real, however. The shared experience of rising prices and the shared language of fair dealing created a common vocabulary that united people in particular places and moments. The challenge for students is to hold both ideas together: unity of grievance and diversity of social experience.

Section 68.21

The Uneven Geography of Unrest: Hokkaido, Regional Differences, and the Imperial Periphery

The disturbances were widespread but not uniform. The documented events cluster in the Kinki region, the Inland Sea and western Honshu, northern Kyushu, and parts of the Tokai and Hokuriku. They are less densely documented in much of the north, in Tōhoku, and in some interior districts. Several reasons are plausible: differences in urbanization and industrial concentration, the density of wage earners dependent on purchased rice, local market structures, patterns of landholding, the speed of relief by local authorities, and the extent to which news and rumor reached a district. Differences in documentation also matter, since a district with few records may have experienced more unrest than later historians can show. A map of recorded disturbances is a map of what the sources capture.

Hokkaido, the northern island that had been colonized by the Japanese state since the 1870s (treated further in the textbook's continuing history of the Ainu), had coalfields, fishing towns, and ports, and reports indicate that disturbances occurred in some Hokkaido localities. The island's population, composed largely of settlers from Honshu alongside Ainu communities, had its own economic structure and did not necessarily follow the pattern of the main islands. The Ainu, who had been subjected to dispossession and assimilation policies, appear little in the sources on the rice disturbances, and their experience of the crisis remains poorly documented, a silence that students should note rather than fill with assumption.

The imperial periphery presents further problems. Korea and Taiwan supplied rice to Japan, and the expansion of colonial rice exports affected local consumption and prices in the colonies (Chapter 62). The Korean peninsula was under colonial rule, with strict controls on assembly and the press, and reports of rice-related unrest there are limited. It would be a mistake to assume that colonial societies experienced the same movement as the Japanese main islands, or that a parallel movement occurred. At the same time, the rice trade connected the colonial and metropolitan economies, and the policies that drew rice from the colonies bore on colonial hardship, which would become a political issue in later years. The Rice Riots in the strict sense were primarily a phenomenon of the home islands, and the colonial dimension appears chiefly through the rice trade and the questions it raises.

Division V — Local Government, Police, Military Repression, and the Courts

The fifth division examines how the state at its several levels responded. Municipal officials, police, soldiers, censors, and judges did not act as a single body, and their responses ranged from negotiation to lethal force. Section 68.22

Municipal Authorities, Petitions, and Emergency Relief

City and prefectural governments were the first officials to confront crowds, and they had a limited repertoire. Mayors, prefectural governors, and local assemblies met with delegations, received petitions, and negotiated with merchants. Their most common response was emergency relief, usually arranged in the form of the sale of rice at reduced prices to residents. The price was often fixed below the market, the difference being covered by public funds, donations from wealthy residents, or contributions from merchants who were pressured to participate. Some cities distributed relief rice or money to the poor, set up temporary sales outlets, or arranged to buy rice in bulk from outside the locality.

These measures show that local authorities understood the crisis as one of subsistence and not simply of public order. They also show political calculation. Officials who appeared unwilling or unable to protect residents risked losing legitimacy, and the pressure of crowds gave them reason to act quickly. The donations of wealthy people, whose contributions were publicized, served both as relief and as a way of recovering the standing of the rich in a time when they were targets of anger.

The weakness of these measures was equally clear. Local governments had limited funds and could not control the national price. Relief sales lasted only as long as the money did, and they treated symptoms and not causes. In many places local authorities coupled relief with the call for troops, and so combined concession and coercion. The pattern anticipated the national response described in Division VI.

Section 68.23

Police, Local Officials, and the Escalation of Crowd Conflict

Police in prewar Japan were a national service organized through the Home Ministry and deployed by prefecture, with officers who carried swords as part of their equipment. They were responsible for public order, for enforcing laws on assembly, and for investigating political and criminal offenses. In August 1918 their task was to prevent or disperse unlawful assemblies, to protect property, and to arrest those who attacked it. The scale of crowds often exceeded what local forces could handle, and the police, spread thin across a prefecture, were sometimes overwhelmed.

The police were not a single actor. Local police chiefs and prefectural officials differed in judgment and temperament. In some places police attended meetings and negotiated, sometimes encouraging merchants to reduce prices to forestall violence. In others they attempted to break up assemblies at the outset, producing clashes. The same force might negotiate with one crowd and charge another depending on perceived threat, the presence of valuable property, the availability of reinforcements, and the pressure of superiors.

Escalation often depended on interaction. A crowd that met police resistance could be provoked into attacking them or the property they guarded; police who lost control could call for troops, producing a more dangerous confrontation. Historians therefore resist treating violence as an inherent property of crowds or of the state. It emerged from specific encounters, in which choices by both sides mattered.

Section 68.24

Military Deployment, Armed Suppression, and Civilian Casualties

When police could not control crowds, prefectural governors and other civil officials could request military assistance, and army units were dispatched under procedures that placed the troops in a supporting role to the civil authorities. During the Rice Riots army units from divisions across the country were deployed in many localities. An army report preserved in the National Archives of Japan lists dispatches of troops and provides an official record of the scale of military involvement. Its categories and counts are those of the military administration and do not give a complete picture of events, but the broad conclusion is clear: the army was used on a large scale, in cities, towns, and mining districts.

The presence of troops did not always mean shooting. In many places soldiers were deployed to guard buildings, patrol streets, or deter crowds, and the display of force sufficed. In other places, soldiers or police fired on crowds. The lethal confrontations were concentrated in specific localities, notably some mining districts, where force was used against large and angry crowds. Reports of the number killed and wounded vary with the source, in part because official and unofficial accounts differ, because the dead and wounded were counted by different agencies for different purposes, and because the boundaries of what counts as part of the Rice Riots are uncertain. Students should avoid adopting a single total without documentation.

The military's role raises the question of the state's willingness to use force against its own citizens in a domestic crisis. The deployment was not unprecedented, since troops had been used in suppressing disturbances earlier in the Meiji period, but the scale in 1918 was striking and was widely noted. It left a legacy of memory, and for some contemporaries the sight of soldiers in city streets demonstrated both the seriousness of the crisis and the character of the regime.

Section 68.25

Press Coverage, Censorship, Public Opinion, and the Politics of Information

Newspapers were central to the Rice Riots as carriers of information and as participants in the controversy. As Section 68.11 noted, press reports helped spread awareness of the events. As the disturbances escalated, the government acted to limit coverage. In mid-August the Home Ministry, using the authority of the Newspaper Law, restricted the publication of reports on the disturbances, a formal reporting prohibition imposed on August 14, 1918. The aim was to prevent further imitation and to control the public image of the crisis. Some papers complied, and others sought ways to report within the limits, and the effect on public knowledge was considerable.

It is important to distinguish between formal orders and voluntary restraint. Some newspapers practiced self-censorship, anticipating official displeasure, while others used euphemism or published accounts under different labels. Political newspapers interpreted the riots in line with their allegiances: papers sympathetic to opposition parties emphasized the government's failure, while government-friendly papers stressed disorder and the need for firm action. Some newspapers were themselves targets of crowds, as in Kobe, because they were perceived as allied to merchants or to the government.

The conflict between the press and the government came to a head in the "white rainbow incident." An August 17 Osaka meeting protested the news restrictions; at a larger gathering of Kansai newspaper representatives on August 25, journalists again condemned the government. The Osaka Asahi Shinbun report, issued in its August 26-dated edition, used the classical image of a “white rainbow piercing the sun,” which authorities interpreted as a threat to imperial dignity. Prosecution and pressure on the newspaper followed. The episode belongs to the wider history of the press examined in Chapter 65, but here it reveals how the riots became a confrontation over the boundaries of public criticism. Dates on evening editions require particular care because a paper might carry the following day’s date.

Section 68.26

Arrests, Prosecutions, Trials, and the Criminalization of Collective Action

The legal response to the Rice Riots was extensive. Police arrested participants in large numbers, and prosecutors brought charges under the criminal code, including riot or tumultuous assembly (sōjō), arson, robbery, and theft. Published figures for arrests, prosecutions, and convictions are of very different orders and are sometimes confused; commonly cited totals run to tens of thousands of arrests, a much smaller number of indictments, and fewer convictions, but definitions and archival bases vary, and the chapter does not adopt a single number. Students should always ask what a figure counts, who counted it, for which places and dates, and whether it is a count of arrests, prosecutions, or sentences.

Sentences varied. Many defendants received relatively short terms or lesser penalties; some received heavy sentences, and the harshest penalties fell on participants in the most violent incidents. Defendants were drawn largely from the urban and industrial poor: laborers, casual workers, miners, small tradespeople, and residents of Buraku communities, in some localities in numbers that exceeded their share of the population. Women were a smaller share of those prosecuted, though the Toyama women were among the first to be questioned and detained. Historians have used the trial records to reconstruct who participated, and the social profile they provide is a counterweight to the contemporary image of the crowd as the "dangerous classes."

The prosecution of collective action as crime was itself a political decision. By classifying the riots as criminal disorder, the state declined to treat the crowd's demands as legitimate. At the same time the government's relief measures implicitly acknowledged the legitimacy of the grievance. This dual stance, concession to the grievance and punishment of the protest, is a defining feature of the state's response.

Historical Debate

Public Protest or Criminal Disorder?

The question: Authorities, courts, and much of the contemporary press classified the actions of 1918 as criminal riots. Later historians have asked whether this classification captures what the crowds were doing, or whether it was a political label that concealed legitimate protest. How should historians categorize collective actions that included both demands and destruction?

Interpretation A — Criminal disorder: This view, associated with the authorities' contemporary perspective, emphasizes the arson, looting, and violence that occurred in many places, the threat to life and property, and the legal duty of the state to maintain order. It notes that victims included small shopkeepers and bystanders, and it cautions against romanticizing the crowd.
Interpretation B — Legitimate protest: This view emphasizes the grievances, the restraint shown in many episodes, the demands for fair prices, and the disproportion between the crowd's actions and the state's response. It argues that the criminal label obscured the political character of the events and justified repression.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on the same body of evidence: police and court records, press reports, and local histories. They differ in the weight assigned to destructive acts as against the structure of demands and in the point of view from which the sources are read.

Why it matters: The question affects how historians describe the relationship between the state and its citizens, how they treat the participants, and whether they regard 1918 as part of the history of popular politics. It does not require denying either the violence or the legitimacy of the grievance; most scholars accept both and debate their proportion and meaning.

Division VI — National Political Consequences and the Expansion of Social Policy

The sixth division turns to the national government and the longer consequences. It does not retell Taishō party politics, which Chapter 67 has treated, but explains how the riots affected the Terauchi cabinet and how they contributed to new debates about public responsibility for living conditions.

Section 68.27

The Terauchi Government's Emergency Response

The Terauchi cabinet responded to the crisis on several fronts at once. It authorized the import of foreign rice, drawing on supplies from Southeast Asia and from Japan's colonies and on China, and adjusted customs duties to encourage imports. It supported emergency sales of rice at reduced prices through local governments and made available funds for relief. It encouraged contributions from the wealthy. The imperial household announced a large donation for relief in mid-August, and business houses and prominent individuals made contributions, which were publicized as evidence of the solidarity of the nation. It also took measures against hoarding and sought to influence the rice trade.

These measures show a government improvising. Japan did not have a standing system for the management of rice prices, and the cabinet drew on existing administrative tools. The relationship between relief and coercion was uneasy. The same government that arranged cheap rice also ordered troops into the streets and restricted the press. Observers at the time saw this as evidence of incoherence or of a primary concern with order. The government's interest was in ending the disturbances and restoring confidence, and relief served that purpose as well as the purpose of aiding the hungry.

The effects were mixed. Imports and relief sales helped reduce tension in some places, though prices did not fall quickly and remained high into 1919. Contributions provided immediate aid but did not address structural causes. The experience led officials to consider whether permanent institutions of food management were needed, a question taken up in Section 68.30.

Section 68.28

The Crisis of Government Legitimacy: Political Criticism and Public Distrust

The Terauchi cabinet was a non-party government in an age when parties were increasingly assertive. It had been formed outside the House of Representatives majority and rested on the army and the bureaucracy. Critics, including the opposition Kenseikai and sections of the press, had attacked the government for its Siberian policy, its financial dealings with China, and what they portrayed as a distant and authoritarian style. The riots gave these criticisms new force. They showed a government that had failed to protect households from the consequences of its own wartime economy and that responded to protest with troops.

Public distrust took several forms. Newspapers connected the riots with the government's alleged indifference and, in some cases, with its connections to wartime profiteers. Political speakers and writers argued that the government was responsible for the failure to control prices, and that a government accountable to the people would have acted differently. The press restrictions, rather than silencing criticism, provoked it, as the white rainbow incident showed. Even officials within the government recognized that the crisis had damaged its standing.

Here the household issue of subsistence became linked to the wider question of political accountability. This link did not require the crowd to have articulated a theory of government. It was made by commentators, politicians, and newspapers who used the riots to argue for broader changes. Historians distinguish between the intentions of the participants, which were largely focused on prices and local justice, and the uses to which the events were put in national debate.

Section 68.29

The Fall of Terauchi and the Formation of Hara Takashi's Cabinet

Terauchi Masatake resigned on September 21, 1918, and Hara Takashi, president of the Rikken Seiyūkai, was appointed prime minister on September 29. The sequence invites an inference that the riots brought down the government, but historians are more cautious. Terauchi's health was poor, and he had reportedly discussed resigning for health reasons before the riots. The government also faced pressure over Siberia, finance, and parliamentary politics. The riots deepened a political crisis and made continuing in office more difficult, and they are properly regarded as a major contributing factor, but not necessarily the sole cause.

The choice of Hara reflected the changed political situation. The senior statesman Yamagata Aritomo, who initially wanted to avoid a party cabinet, first sought to have Saionji Kinmochi return as prime minister. Saionji repeatedly declined, citing his health; Yamagata then reluctantly turned to Hara, president of the Seiyūkai and an influential member of the House of Representatives, as the most practicable successor. The selection reflected a political impasse and Hara’s reputation for pragmatic negotiation, not the sudden conversion of all elder statesmen to parliamentary supremacy. He was widely noted as the first prime minister without a peerage title and the first sitting member of the elected House of Representatives to hold the premiership. Chapter 67 examines the Hara cabinet and its policies; here the point is that the riots were part of the context in which the choice was made and in which the political elite's attitude toward parties shifted.

The result was both a change and a limit. The party cabinet was not a democratic revolution: the franchise remained restricted, the House of Peers, the military, and the bureaucracy retained power, and Hara's government itself would resist rapid expansion of the vote and take a hard line against labor. The riots did not produce a new regime; they altered the balance within the existing one.

Section 68.30

Beyond the Riots: Social Administration, Public Relief, and the Problem of Popular Welfare

The riots helped move the problem of living conditions into the center of public debate. In the months that followed, officials and reformers discussed the need for permanent institutions to provide relief and to manage essential goods. The Home Ministry's engagement with what contemporaries called social policy (shakai seisaku) and social work expanded. Its Relief Work Investigation Council (Kyūsai Jigyō Chōsakai) had already been established in June 1918, before the rice disturbances, and therefore cannot properly be represented as an institution founded in direct response to them; the crisis strengthened the urgency of the broader discussion. Municipal governments, particularly in Osaka and other large cities, developed public markets and public dining facilities intended to supply food at moderate prices, extending the emergency relief into continuing practice. Later institutional reforms included the Rice Law (Beikokuhō) of 1921, which authorized government operations in the rice market, including purchases and storage when rice prices were low and sales when prices were high. Its original emphasis was on adjusting quantities in circulation; later amendments developed more explicit price-adjustment mechanisms. It was not an immediate 1918 guaranteed-price program.

These developments should be understood with care. They were limited. Public markets and relief programs served only some of the urban poor, and national policy was shaped by fear of disorder as well as by concern for welfare. Many officials continued to regard poverty as a matter of individual failing or charity and not of rights. But the shift was real: the riots had demonstrated that the market alone could not be trusted to feed the population in the manner expected and that the state would be held responsible when it failed.

The riots also fed the formation of movements that Chapter 69 will examine: labor organizations that found a larger audience, reformers who argued for suffrage and for social legislation, and minority and women's movements that sought to organize on their own behalf. It would be a mistake to treat these as automatic results of 1918. They had their own origins and faced strong opposition. But the riots changed the setting in which they operated, by making visible the discontent of the urban poor and by encouraging elites to consider concessions.

The timing of reforms helps students distinguish prior reform activity from the political effects of the riots. The June 1918 investigation council shows that official interest in organized relief predated the August crisis. Subsequent unrest heightened the significance of such initiatives but did not create social administration from nothing. The 1921 Rice Law likewise belongs to a later institutional response to unstable rice markets, not to a quick sequence of reforms enacted during the demonstrations themselves. Its mechanism differed from the small, emergency sales undertaken by cities in August 1918. Local officials were trying to get affordable rice to immediate purchasers; national lawmakers were developing a lasting instrument for purchasing, holding, and releasing rice in response to fluctuations in supply and price. These different timescales should remain visible throughout the account.

Section 68.31

What Did the Crowds Demand? Subsistence, Justice, Rights, and Political Accountability

The demands recorded in the sources were overwhelmingly concrete: lower rice prices, the sale of rice to local residents, relief for the poor, and, in some places, concessions on rent, wages, or the conduct of particular merchants. They were demands for fair dealing within a local community and for the prevention of shipments that deprived it of food. They also included demands against particular individuals and firms accused of profiteering. In a few places crowds directed demands at officials or at the government.

Behind these demands lay a language of justice. Participants drew on expectations of mutual obligation, on the belief that merchants who profited from scarcity violated a community's trust, and on an idea that those who were rich owed help to those in need. This is the moral economy discussed earlier. Some historians, including Naoko Tomie in “Two Notions of ‘Right to Live’ in 1918 Rice Riots: Moral Economy and Citizenship” (Journal of Welfare Sociology 14 [2017], 95–119), have suggested that in 1918 these older ideas began to merge with newer claims about the entitlements of members of the nation, a shift from the language of customary obligation toward the language of citizenship and rights. The evidence for this shift is suggestive and not conclusive: the sources record few explicit claims to "rights," and the historian must infer them from patterns of action and from the commentary of contemporaries.

Distinguishing the motives of participants from the interpretations placed on them is essential. Politicians and newspapers read the riots as evidence of misgovernment; socialists and labor activists read them as evidence of class struggle; officials read them as evidence of threats to order; and later historians have read them in the light of their own questions. The crowd itself left few self-explanations. This silence is not emptiness but an invitation to caution. The chapter's own interpretation should be read as an attempt to infer what is likely from evidence, not as a final account of what participants meant.

Section 68.32

Rebellion, Moral Economy, or Popular Politics? The Historical Meaning of 1918

Historians have offered several interpretations. The postwar Marxist-influenced scholarship, represented by Inoue Kiyoshi and Watanabe Tōru, treated the riots as a mass uprising of the people against the capitalist order and the state, a movement of national scope that revealed the strength of popular discontent and that marked a turning point toward organized class politics. This interpretation drew attention to the scale and social breadth of the events and to the state's repression, and it shaped a generation of understanding. Critics have noted that it risks reading unity and revolutionary intent into a diverse set of local actions.

A second interpretation, drawing on E. P. Thompson, stresses the continuity of a moral-economy tradition of food protest, in which crowds enforced customary standards of fair prices and merchants' obligations. It emphasizes the restraint, the focus on rice dealers, and the language of justice. A third, associated with Michael Lewis, emphasizes variation by locality and the emergence of crowd action as a form of popular politics, in which ordinary people asserted claims as members of a community and, increasingly, as citizens, in a society with newspapers, parties, and public meetings. A fourth line of interpretation emphasizes the state's coercive capacity and the limits of political reform, viewing the riots as revealing both the vulnerability of the government to popular discontent and its readiness to use force.

These interpretations are not mutually exclusive. Most scholars would accept that the riots included traditional elements and modern ones, that they differed by place, and that the state's response combined relief and repression. They differ in emphasis and in what they consider the most significant dimension. The question of consequences is also contested. The riots contributed to the fall of a cabinet, to new discussion of social policy, and to a changed political atmosphere, but they did not by themselves produce democracy, and the following years saw continued repression alongside expanded participation. A careful account avoids teleology, the assumption that later democratization followed automatically.

Historical Debate

Food Riot, Moral Economy, or Political Movement?

The question: What kind of event were the Rice Riots? Was 1918 the last great traditional food protest, a response to industrial capitalism, the first modern mass political movement, or something that combined these elements?

Interpretation A — Moral-economy protest: The crowds enforced customary ideas of fair prices and merchant duties, targeted dealers and profiteers, and generally sought a restoration of just dealing, not a change of regime. The event belongs in a long tradition of subsistence protest adapted to a new setting.
Interpretation B — Popular politics and citizenship: The crowds acted in a society of newspapers, public meetings, and wage labor, in which people increasingly claimed a place in public life. Their actions, their targets, and the national response show an emerging politics of citizens' claims, whatever the participants' own vocabulary.

Evidence: Both draw on the pattern of demands, the choice of targets, the role of meetings and newspapers, the state response, and the explicit commentary of contemporaries. Interpretation A relies more heavily on the form of local actions and the language of fairness; Interpretation B on the national scale, the urban setting, and the political uses of the events. A further body of work emphasizes the state's coercive response, which both interpretations must explain.

Why it matters: The answer shapes how 1918 is placed in the history of popular movements: as an end of an old pattern, as a beginning of a new one, or as a bridge. It also bears on how students understand the relationship between everyday grievance and political change.

Continuity and Change Over Time

The Rice Riots and Later Popular Movements

What Changed

  • Protest became national in scale and communication, spread by newspapers and railways rather than confined to a single domain or district
  • The state was expected to take responsibility for food supply and living conditions to a degree it had not been before
  • Social policy and the problem of urban poverty entered the center of political debate
  • Political leaders reconsidered the role of parties and popular opinion in government

What Stayed the Same

  • The moral-economy language of fair dealing and merchants' obligations persisted
  • The state's readiness to use police, press controls, and military force against crowds continued
  • Women remained excluded from formal political participation, and discrimination against Buraku communities persisted
  • The franchise remained restricted, and the structure of power in the Meiji Constitution was unchanged

Who experienced change most directly: Urban wage earners, miners, and the poor saw the most direct shift in how the state and elites regarded them; officials and party politicians saw changes in the political calculus. Who experienced greater continuity: Rural tenants, women, colonial populations, and Buraku communities saw less immediate change in their legal and social position.

Why the pattern matters: Understanding 1918 as a mixture of change and continuity guards against treating it as either a failed revolution or a smooth step toward democracy.

Naoko Tomie offers a particularly useful refinement to this debate. In her 2017 study of two notions of the “right to live,” she analyzes how intellectuals subsequently interpreted the riots through the language of citizenship. Her argument is not proof that every participant in 1918 already demanded parliamentary rights. Rather, it asks how immediate, community-centered claims to survive could be translated into a new political vocabulary by commentators. That distinction between contemporary crowd demands and retrospective interpretation is central to historical method. A riot can become a landmark in the history of citizenship partly because later actors make it one, even if those who first acted were focused on the price of tonight’s meal.

Chapter Summary

The Rice Riots of 1918 were a national crisis in which a question of food prices became a confrontation over economic justice, public authority, and the obligations of government. The central argument of the chapter is that the riots exposed a widening divide between Japan's wartime prosperity and the living conditions of ordinary households, and that this divide, once made visible by a sharp price shock, produced collective action of many kinds across the country.

The economic foundations lay in the First World War boom, which enriched exporters, shipowners, and trading houses while prices of necessities rose faster than the earnings of many wage earners. Rice, the staple food and the largest item in many household budgets, carried moral as well as economic meaning. The rice trade, with its local dealers, regional merchants, and great exchanges, was open to speculation, and the public suspected hoarding. The Siberian Intervention, announced on August 2, 1918, and the rumors of army purchases that preceded it converted a steady inflation into an immediate shock.

The protests began on the Toyama coast, where women from fishing households met and acted collectively in late July and early August to stop shipments of rice from their communities and to press for affordable local sales. The chapter has distinguished the July 22–23 events at Uozu from the August 3 episode at Nishimizuhashi and has treated the women as political agents, while noting that the press label of a "wives' uprising" tended to minimize their organization. Their actions were an early and influential episode, not the sole origin of a national movement.

From the coast the disturbances spread, carried by newspapers, telegraph, railways, and rumor, and shaped by shared grievance and local networks, not by central direction. In the cities, large meetings and crowds appeared in Nagoya, Kyoto, Osaka, Kobe, Tokyo, and other places in the second week of August. The cities differed in setting and crowd composition, in targets, and in official response, and the burning of Suzuki Shōten in Kobe, the participation of Buraku residents in Kyoto, and the confrontations between large crowds and troops in Osaka showed different dimensions of the crisis.

Rural and mining districts experienced the crisis in their own ways. Tenants and village residents faced hardship even when rice prices rose, because many did not have surplus rice to sell. Miners, who lived under company authority and bought food through company-linked channels, combined food grievances with grievances about wages and discipline, and some of the most violent confrontations occurred in the coalfields. The geography of documented unrest was uneven, with fewer recorded events in much of the north and with the colonies and Ainu communities poorly represented in the sources, a silence that should not be filled with assumption.

The state responded at several levels. Municipal authorities arranged emergency sales and relief. Police, whose behavior varied by locality, negotiated or dispersed crowds. The army was deployed in many localities, and in some, soldiers or police used lethal force. The Home Ministry restricted press coverage, producing a confrontation with newspapers that culminated in the white rainbow incident. Thousands were prosecuted, and published figures for participants, casualties, arrests, and convictions vary and must be handled with attention to definitions.

At the national level the Terauchi government imported rice, supported relief, and received donations, but it could not restore its legitimacy. Terauchi resigned on September 21, and Hara Takashi became prime minister on September 29. The chapter has argued that the riots intensified a political crisis and contributed to the fall of the cabinet without being its sole cause, since Terauchi's health and other pressures also mattered. The change brought a party cabinet but not democracy: the franchise remained restricted and the structure of the Meiji Constitution was unchanged.

The riots also moved the problem of popular welfare to the center of public debate. Relief investigations, public markets, and later rice legislation pointed toward a larger public role in food supply and social administration, while the experience of discrimination and exclusion contributed to the organizing efforts of workers, women, and Buraku communities that Chapter 69 will examine. These consequences were not automatic, and the state's reliance on coercion continued alongside concession.

Historians interpret the event in several ways: as a last great tradition of moral-economy protest, as a response to industrial capitalism, as an early form of popular politics and citizenship, and as a demonstration of the state's coercive capacity. These readings are not exclusive. The best account recognizes variation among localities, the diversity of participants, the continuity of older ideas of fairness, and the novelty of a national, newspaper-connected movement in an industrial society.

The enduring lesson is that a crisis in the affordability of food could raise questions that reached beyond prices: who is entitled to subsistence, who is responsible for ensuring it, and what means ordinary people may use to press their claims. Those questions shaped the history of the following decade and remain part of how historians understand the relationship between the Japanese state and its people.

  5. Write your findings. In a 4–6 page essay, compare your locality with the national pattern and with one other locality, using careful language such as "the evidence suggests" and avoiding single figures for contested numbers.

Chapter Reflection

The Rice Riots matter because they show how a society changed by war, industry, and commerce could be shaken by a crisis of everyday life. The event connected a coastal fishing community in Toyama to the great cities, the coalfields, and the cabinet in Tokyo. It showed that households, workers, and neighborhoods could act collectively and that their actions could alter the behavior of local governments and the standing of a cabinet. It also showed the limits of that power in a political system that responded to protest with relief and force at once.

The chapter reveals processes that will recur in the textbook: the tension between economic growth and distribution, the role of communication networks in the formation of public life, the unequal ways in which class, gender, occupation, and discrimination shape participation, and the state's dual reliance on concession and coercion. It also draws attention to the historian's problems of evidence, since the event is documented chiefly by authorities, journalists, and later memory, and since the voices of women, miners, Buraku residents, and colonial subjects must often be inferred.

Later developments built on, but were not determined by, the events of 1918. Chapter 69 will examine the organized struggles for labor rights, suffrage, party reform, and minority and women's activism that emerged in the following years, and later chapters will return to rural crisis, the power of big business, and the strains on parliamentary politics. The continuities, including moral-economy expectations, the state's coercive repertoire, and the exclusion of women from formal politics, survived long beyond 1918, and the question of what a government owes its people in times of scarcity remained part of Japanese politics.

Finally, the riots connect regions of the archipelago and its wider empire. They linked the Sea of Japan coast to the Kansai and Kyushu, and the rice economy of the home islands to Korea and Taiwan, and they occurred amid the wartime intervention in Siberia. Understanding 1918 therefore requires attention to local communities and to the imperial and international economy that shaped their prices. It is one more example of the book's insistence that Japanese history be understood as the history of diverse communities whose experiences were joined, unevenly, by markets, states, and shared crises.
