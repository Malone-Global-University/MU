The Modern City: Cafés, Department Stores, Film, Radio, and Consumer Culture

Japanese History: From Jōmon Japan to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

On December 30, 1927, a short stretch of electric railway opened beneath the streets of Tokyo, linking Ueno and Asakusa in a few minutes at a flat fare of ten sen. Newspapers reported large crowds eager to descend the stairs and ride, not because they needed to reach somewhere urgently, but because the ride itself was a novelty. Japan's first subway was a piece of infrastructure, and it was also an attraction: a way of experiencing the city as something new.

The subway's opening is a useful doorway into this chapter because it joined two of the city's most popular districts: Ueno, with its museums, park, and railway terminal, and Asakusa, the great entertainment quarter of temple fairs, theaters, cinemas, and cheap amusements. The people who rode that first line were doing something that would have been hard to imagine a generation earlier. They were using a mass transit system, paid for with small coins, to move between sites of commerce and leisure that existed to be visited, watched, and enjoyed. This chapter asks how that way of living in a city came into being, who took part in it, and who was left at its edges.

Earlier chapters examined the political and social conflicts of the Taishō years. Chapters 67 through 69 followed party politics, popular protest, labor unions, tenant movements, and the suffrage campaigns. Chapter 70 turns to a different register of the same period: the physical spaces, commercial institutions, technologies, and daily routines through which millions of urban residents experienced modern life. It is a history of shopping, commuting, working in offices and shops, dressing, eating, dancing, listening, and watching. These were not trivial matters to contemporaries. Newspaper columnists, social scientists, feminists, moralists, police officials, advertisers, and filmmakers argued constantly about what the new urban habits meant and whether they were good for the nation.

The chapter's central argument is that the modern Japanese city transformed everyday life by connecting mass commerce, new communications technologies, expanding leisure industries, and changing social identities, and that the modernity celebrated in advertising, cinema, and fashionable districts was distributed unequally. It was constrained by class, gender, occupation, region, and imperial hierarchy. A salaried clerk riding a suburban railway to a department-store restaurant on a Sunday, a textile worker confined to a company dormitory, a café waitress dependent on tips, a Korean laborer living in a crowded neighborhood of Osaka, and a farmer in a Tōhoku village who had never seen a film all lived in the same country in the same decade, but they did not inhabit the same modernity.

Students should therefore resist two tempting images. One is the glittering image produced by the commercial culture itself: the fashionable stroller on the Ginza, the bobbed-haired modern girl, the department-store banner, the jazz-age café. These images were real as representations, and some people did live in ways that resembled them, but they were also advertisements, journalism, and fantasy. The other tempting image is the opposite one, in which mass culture is dismissed as a shallow Western veneer over an unchanged society, or as a mere prelude to the militarism of the 1930s. Neither view survives close inspection. Urban mass culture was an important historical development in its own right, built by Japanese entrepreneurs, performers, designers, workers, and audiences who borrowed selectively, adapted freely, and invented a great deal.

The evidence is unusually rich and unusually tricky. It includes department-store publications and posters, newspaper and magazine advertising, popular magazines aimed at housewives and young people, film trade journals, early radio schedules, government regulations, police and census records, social surveys by contemporary researchers such as Kon Wajirō, memoirs, novels, and surviving films, records, and buildings. Much of it was produced by those who were trying to sell something or to persuade readers about what modern life should be. Historians therefore read it for what it reveals about commercial ambitions and public anxieties, and then test it against evidence of what people actually earned, bought, and did. The historian Miriam Silverberg, whose study of interwar mass culture shapes much of the scholarship discussed here, argued that this culture deserves to be understood on its own terms rather than only as a symptom of something else.

The chapter's chronological core runs from roughly 1918 to 1930, with foundations laid between 1904 and 1917. Tokyo and Osaka are the principal case studies, with comparisons drawn from Kyoto, Kobe, Nagoya, and Yokohama. The Great Kantō Earthquake of September 1923 reshaped Tokyo's commercial and entertainment geography, and its consequences are noted where they matter for consumer and leisure life; the disaster itself, the reconstruction administration, and the social violence that accompanied it belong to Chapter 71. Chapter 65 examined the origins of newspapers, benshi, and early cinema; here the focus falls on the mature commercial cinema, radio, and recorded-music culture of the 1920s. Chapter 72 will turn to artistic and literary experimentation, while Chapters 73 and 74 return to economic crisis and the breakdown of parliamentary restraint, the context in which this exuberant commercial culture was soon to be tested.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Describe the demographic, economic, and spatial growth of Japan's major cities after the First World War, distinguishing urban from rural Japan.
  2. Explain how railways, streetcars, and the first subway altered access to employment, shopping, and entertainment.
  3. Trace the transition from kimono-merchant houses to department stores, distinguishing Mitsukoshi's 1904 declaration from its 1914 building and from the railway-terminal store of 1929.
  4. Analyze advertising, window displays, and magazines as evidence of commercial persuasion rather than of actual purchasing behavior.
  5. Distinguish the early café movement of 1911 from the commercialized café culture of the late 1920s, and the café waitress as a worker from the waitress as a media image.
  6. Evaluate the modern girl (moga) and modern boy (mobo) as both lived social phenomena and media constructions.
  7. Explain the development of the 1920s film industry, the decline of the onnagata convention, new film genres, and film censorship, building on Chapter 65.
  8. Trace the beginning of radio broadcasting in 1925, the 1926 consolidation of broadcasting, and the emergence of a commercial popular-music market.
  9. Compare Asakusa, Ginza, and the railway-based leisure culture of the Hanshin region as different models of commercial entertainment.
  10. Assess who could and could not afford modern consumption and leisure, including migrants, colonial subjects, Buraku communities, and the urban poor.
  11. Interpret competing historical arguments about whether urban mass culture represented adaptation, innovation, liberation, or commercial discipline.
  12. Evaluate primary sources for their creators, commercial purposes, and limits, recognizing whose perspectives they omit.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How did changes in transportation, housing, and employment reorganize the daily lives of urban residents between 1918 and 1930?
  2. Why did department stores, rather than other kinds of retailers, become the most visible symbols of modern consumption, and what did they sell besides goods?
  3. How should historians use advertisements, magazines, and films as evidence when these sources were designed to create desires rather than to record behavior?
  4. What was the relationship between the café waitress as a historical worker and the café waitress as a symbol of urban modernity?
  5. To what extent was the modern girl a social reality, and to what extent was she a creation of journalists, advertisers, and critics?
  6. How did cinema, radio, and recorded music reinforce one another, and how did they change the experience of leisure and shared public life?
  7. Who was excluded from the consumer city, and how did class, gender, migration, discrimination, and empire shape access to modern life?
  8. Was Japanese urban mass culture best understood as Westernization, as adaptation, or as something new, and why did commercial modernity not necessarily produce political liberalism?

Historical Context

Japan's cities were already changing before the First World War. After the Meiji Restoration, Tokyo had been transformed from the shogun's capital of Edo into a national capital with railway termini, government offices, universities, newspapers, and a growing industrial belt along its bay. Osaka, the old commercial center of the Tokugawa economy, became a manufacturing city, especially for cotton textiles, and was sometimes called the "Manchester of the East." The experiences of industrial labor in these cities were traced in Chapter 63, and the rise of newspapers, early film, and mass-circulation print was examined in Chapter 65. By the end of the Russo-Japanese War in 1905, urban residents were accustomed to electric streetcars, gas and electric lighting, daily newspapers, and the first moving pictures, although these were distributed unevenly by neighborhood and income.

The First World War accelerated these processes. Wartime exports, shipbuilding, and heavy industry brought a boom, as Chapter 69 described, and employment in factories, trading houses, banks, and government offices expanded. The expansion created a social group that contemporaries began to call the "new middle class": salaried employees (kyūryō seikatsusha, later more commonly sararīman) with secondary or higher education, who worked in offices, earned monthly salaries, and lived in rented houses or apartments. They were a minority of the working population, but a highly visible one, and advertisers and magazine editors increasingly addressed them. Alongside them lived the much larger populations of factory operatives, artisans, shopkeepers, day laborers, domestic servants, and small proprietors whose lives were organized differently.

The legal and political environment shaped commercial culture in several ways. The Home Ministry and its police supervised theaters, cinemas, cafés, publications, and public morals, and local authorities licensed many commercial entertainments. The Ministry of Communications controlled wireless technology and, as Section 70.28 explains, broadcasting. At the same time, the reforms and movements of Chapters 67 through 69 expanded the idea that ordinary people had a legitimate place in public life, which encouraged advertisers, publishers, and entertainers to address a mass audience. A commercially oriented public sphere and an increasingly assertive state existed side by side, and their relationship was one of negotiation and friction rather than simple opposition.

Finally, the city must be understood within the empire. Japanese retailers and publishers extended into Korea, Taiwan, and Manchuria; colonial migrants worked in Osaka factories and Tokyo construction sites; and the prosperity of metropolitan consumers rested in part on colonial rice, sugar, and raw materials, as later chapters on empire explain. Images of the modern city circulated across the empire, but the colonies had their own urban cultures and their own hierarchies. This chapter concentrates on the metropole, while keeping in view the people who lived there as subjects of an unequal empire.

Section 70.1 · Division I — The Modern Metropolis: Space, Movement, and Everyday Life

Japan's Urban Transformation after the First World War

The first national census, taken in 1920, counted a little under 56 million people in Japan proper, and it revealed a society that was still overwhelmingly rural in its occupations, though not in its direction of change. The city-classified (shi) share was 18.0 percent in the 1920 census and 24.0 percent in 1930; changes to municipal boundaries affect the interpretation of these administrative totals. Those figures are approximate and depend on how city boundaries were drawn, a point that matters throughout this chapter because municipalities repeatedly annexed their suburbs. Even so, the direction is clear. Between the end of the First World War and the onset of the depression, a growing minority of Japanese lived in cities, worked for wages or salaries rather than from household farms or workshops, and bought a larger share of what they needed in commercial markets.

The wartime boom explains much of the timing. Shipbuilding, steel, machinery, chemicals, and textile exports expanded employment in the Tokyo–Yokohama (Keihin) and Osaka–Kobe (Hanshin) regions, in Nagoya, and in northern Kyushu. Trading companies, banks, insurance firms, and government ministries added clerical staff. After the postwar recession of 1920, some firms retrenched, but the structural change persisted: the employment mix of the largest cities shifted toward commerce, services, public administration, and light and heavy manufacturing, and the cities kept attracting migrants from villages, many of them younger sons and daughters for whom the family farm offered no future. Chapter 63 described how factory work was organized; here the point is that urban employment generated cash incomes, and cash incomes generated markets for retail goods and commercial entertainment.

Household structure changed unevenly. In the cities, a growing number of couples set up nuclear households in rented homes, often at some distance from parents, a pattern that advertisers and reformers described as the "new household" or "cultured life" (bunka seikatsu). This did not displace the multigenerational household, which remained the ideal in law and in much of rural society, and many urban families combined elements of both. It is nevertheless significant that commercial culture was built partly around the nuclear family: its meals, its leisure outings, its purchases for children, and the housewife (shufu) as manager of a household budget. Chapter 64 examined the education and ideals of women within such families; this chapter follows the same women, and their husbands and children, into shops, theaters, and restaurants.

It is important not to let the visible city stand in for the whole country. The great majority of Japan's farm households, textile workers' home villages, and fishing communities experienced modern commerce chiefly through traveling peddlers, local shopkeepers, mail-order catalogues, and, increasingly, the products and magazines that circulated beyond the cities. A village schoolteacher might read Shufu no Tomo and a farm family might buy a gramophone record, but the department store, the café, and the film palace were largely urban institutions. The regional differences described in Chapter 69's treatment of tenancy remain essential: a tenant household paying roughly half its crop in rent did not shop in Ginza. The urban culture described in this chapter should be read as the leading edge of a changing society, not as an average of Japanese experience.

Section 70.2 · Division I

Tokyo, Osaka, and the Geography of Urban Modernity

Tokyo and Osaka were the two poles of Japanese urban life, and their characters differed. Tokyo was the political and administrative capital, the seat of the imperial court, ministries, military headquarters, universities, and the major newspapers, and it was ringed by an industrial zone along the bay. Within the city, districts specialized. Marunouchi, the stretch of land west of Tokyo Station that the Mitsubishi interests had developed since the 1890s, was an office quarter of banks and trading houses, and its Marunouchi Building, completed in early 1923, symbolized the arrival of the salaried office worker. Nihonbashi, the old merchant core near the bridge from which Tokugawa highways were measured, held wholesale houses, banks, and the old kimono merchants that became department stores. Ginza, a short distance south, had been rebuilt in brick after a fire in 1872 and became the best-known shopping and café district. Asakusa, to the northeast near Sensōji temple, was the popular entertainment quarter. Ueno combined a park, museums, and a major railway terminal, and the western railway terminals at Shinjuku and Shibuya were beginning to grow into secondary centers.

Osaka had a different history and a different self-image. Its merchants had dominated the Tokugawa rice and credit markets, and the city retained a reputation for commerce, practicality, and a lively popular culture centered on the Dōtonbori canal district with its theaters and restaurants. The Midōsuji boulevard, widened in the 1920s, and the Shinsaibashi shopping street became the focus of fashionable retail, while Umeda, in the north, was the terminal for the railways to Kobe, Kyoto, and Takarazuka. In 1925, after Osaka annexed adjoining towns, its census population of roughly 2.1 million surpassed Tokyo's postearthquake count of about two million, making it Japan's largest city for several years. Between Osaka and Kobe, along a corridor served by competing private railways, a continuous urban region developed that historians call the Hanshin zone, with its suburbs, resorts, and schools. Kyoto, the old imperial capital, retained its textile and crafts industries and became a major film-production center, while Nagoya and Yokohama grew as industrial and port cities.

Why did districts acquire such distinct identities? The answer lies in land ownership, transport nodes, and the strategies of firms. Mitsubishi developed Marunouchi as a corporate enclave. Retailers clustered where merchants had always clustered, then pulled customers toward them with elaborate stores. Entertainment entrepreneurs concentrated in places with large floating crowds and permissive traditions, such as temple precincts. The railways produced new centers where lines met. A district's identity was therefore not an accident of taste but the result of investment, regulation, and habit, and it shaped who went there: Ginza attracted students, shoppers, and strollers with some money to spend, while Asakusa drew a much broader and poorer crowd. Section 70.31 compares them directly.

Section 70.3 · Division I

Streetcars, Suburban Railways, Subways, and the Changing Experience of Distance

Electric transport reorganized how far people could live from where they worked and how readily they could reach shops and entertainment. Tokyo's streetcars, consolidated under municipal ownership in 1911, carried large numbers of passengers daily and tied the old merchant districts to the outer wards. Steam and then electric trains on the national network, together with the loop of the Yamanote line, which closed in 1925 when the Ueno and Tokyo Station sections were joined, concentrated commuters at central stations. After the earthquake, municipal buses and the cheap taxis that appeared in the mid-1920s, so-called one-yen taxis (en-taku, introduced in Osaka in 1924 and soon after in Tokyo), further widened mobility for those who could pay. Fare levels and wages are discussed in Section 70.34, because transport that was cheap for a salaried clerk could still be a significant cost for a factory laborer.

Private railways reshaped metropolitan geography most profoundly. In the Hanshin region, companies such as Hanshin, Hankyu, Keihan, and the lines that became part of the Kintetsu network built electric railways connecting Osaka to Kobe, Kyoto, Nara, and the surrounding countryside, and developed suburban housing and amusement facilities along their routes. In the Tokyo region, Den'en Toshi developed a planned garden suburb, and the Tōyoko line to Yokohama (completed in 1927) and the Odakyū line toward Odawara (opened in 1927), together with other lines radiating from Shinjuku, Shibuya, and Ikebukuro, drew new residential districts out into former farmland. These railways earned money not only from fares but from land sales, housing, and the commercial attractions at their terminals and endpoints. The consequence was a distinctive pattern: the private railways were as much real-estate and retail enterprises as transport companies.

The subway extended this logic into the center. The Tokyo Underground Railway, promoted by the entrepreneur Hayakawa Noritsugu, opened its line between Ueno and Asakusa on December 30, 1927, a distance of about two kilometers, at a flat fare of ten sen. It was Asia's first subway, and it offered a ride of a few minutes between two of the city's major attractions. Contemporary reports emphasize the crowds and the novelty. For historians, the line shows how infrastructure and entertainment merged: the subway was built where large numbers of people already wished to travel for leisure, and it advertised itself, in effect, as part of the city's pleasures. Osaka's own municipal subway came later, in the early 1930s.

Railways also changed how people experienced time. Commuting introduced a daily rhythm of rush hours, season tickets, station kiosks, and crowded cars, and timetables made punctuality a habit. Stations became places to shop and eat. Women and students rode in numbers, and newspapers debated the manners of crowded trains and the problem of harassment, which shows that the new mobility created new anxieties along with new freedoms. For many urban residents, distance became a matter of minutes and coins rather than of walking, and the idea of a Sunday excursion to a suburb, a shrine, a hot spring, or a department store became ordinary for those with the money and the free time.

Everyday Life and Environment

A Salaried Commuter and His Family, c. 1927

A composite reconstruction of patterns documented in social surveys, magazines, and contemporary commentary. It describes no specific family.

A clerk at a trading company in Marunouchi might live in a rented two-story house in a western suburb of Tokyo, served by a private railway. He leaves in the morning by train, transferring at a terminal, and works hours that are fixed by the firm and paid by monthly salary. His wife, responsible for the household budget, may keep a household account book of the kind promoted by women's magazines, and shops at neighborhood stores for daily goods. On a Sunday, the family might travel by rail to a department store, where the children eat in the restaurant and watch the rooftop amusements, and where the wife looks at fabrics and household goods without necessarily buying. Evenings might include listening to a radio if the family can afford a set, or, less often, a trip to a cinema.

This family is not typical of Japan, nor even of all of Tokyo, and the household's comfort is precarious. A cut in salary, illness, or the dismissals of the early depression could end it. Their housing is rented, their savings small, and their possessions largely modest. But the pattern of commuting, budgeting, and weekend outings that they follow is exactly the one that advertisers, railways, and retailers had in mind when they designed the modern city's attractions.

Section 70.4 · Division I

The Changing Urban Landscape after 1923

The earthquake and fires of September 1, 1923, the subject of Chapter 71, destroyed much of the commercial core of Tokyo and Yokohama, including large parts of Nihonbashi, Ginza, Asakusa, and the low-lying eastern wards. For the history of consumer culture, the disaster was a turning point because it destroyed old buildings and habits and forced merchants, theater owners, and cafés to improvise and rebuild. Several features of the rebuilt city matter here, and the reconstruction administration, relief, and the social violence of those weeks are treated in the next chapter.

First, commercial life resumed quickly in makeshift form. In Ginza and elsewhere, shops reopened in temporary wooden barracks, and a group of artists and designers, among them Kon Wajirō, painted and decorated these barracks, treating the improvised city as an opportunity for experimentation. Second, the rebuilding favored reinforced concrete and steel-frame construction, and modern architecture, some of it influenced by European and American styles, became more visible. Department stores that had been housed in older buildings rebuilt on a larger scale, and several adopted the policy of allowing customers to enter in street shoes, which eliminated the old practice of removing footwear at the door and made shopping more like a stroll. Matsuzakaya's new Ginza store, opened in 1924, is credited by the company's own history with pioneering shoes-on access throughout the building; its Ueno store had been destroyed in 1923 and was rebuilt later. Other department stores subsequently adapted their premises and customer practices.

Third, the geography of leisure shifted. Asakusa lost its twelve-story tower, the Ryōunkaku, which had been a famous landmark, and some of its older entertainments were destroyed, though the district recovered and expanded in new forms. Ginza, by contrast, gained prestige as the symbol of the rebuilt city, as stores from other districts opened branches there. Shinjuku and Shibuya grew as suburban commuters and relocated residents concentrated around the western terminals, and new retail and entertainment grew with them. Many residents of the destroyed eastern wards moved to the western and southern suburbs, accelerating the outward spread already underway. The earthquake did not create the modern city, but it accelerated trends already present, particularly suburbanization and the rise of the western districts.

Historians therefore treat the rebuilding with caution. It is tempting to describe a clean break, in which the modern city rose from the ruins. The evidence suggests a more complex picture: many merchants and entrepreneurs who rebuilt were the same people as before, many practices continued, and rebuilding favored those with capital while the poor, as the next chapter shows, faced severe difficulties. For consumers, however, the visible result was a more modern-looking and more commercially intense center, with new buildings, shopping streets, and an image of renewal that advertisers eagerly adopted.

Section 70.5 · Division I

Salaried Workers, Working-Class Neighborhoods, and the Unequal City

The consumer city rested on a social foundation of very unequal living conditions. Salaried employees formed a visible but limited fraction of the population. They were paid by the month, usually had at least a middle-school education, and could expect, in good times, some stability and promotion, though bonuses and security varied by firm. Their wages, like all wages, were affected by inflation and by the postwar recession, and by the late 1920s the phenomenon of the educated unemployed or underemployed became a theme of popular film and commentary. A well-known example is Ozu Yasujirō's 1929 comedy about a university graduate who cannot find work, whose title is usually rendered I Graduated, But….

Most urban residents were not salaried clerks. Factory workers, artisans, day laborers, rickshaw and cart pullers, shop assistants, domestic servants, and small shopkeepers made up the great bulk of the city's working population, and many lived in rented tenement rows (nagaya) in the older neighborhoods and in the crowded industrial districts of the eastern and bayside wards. Housing was cramped, often a room or two per family, sometimes shared with lodgers, and sanitation, water supply, and fire safety were limited. Dormitories housed young unmarried workers, particularly women in textile and other mills, as Chapter 69 described, and lodging houses served migrant men. In Osaka, districts such as Nishinari and Kamagasaki developed cheap lodging houses (kichin'yado) for day laborers. Municipal authorities and social-affairs bureaucrats surveyed these neighborhoods, and their reports are an important source for the living conditions that advertisements concealed.

Reformers and builders proposed alternatives. The Tokyo Peace Exposition of 1922 in Ueno displayed a "culture village" of model houses with Western-style features, which popularized the idea of the bunka jūtaku, or "cultured house," a compact home with a Western-style reception room and modern kitchen. Such houses were more common as images than as realities, and they were out of reach for most households. After 1923 housing associations built apartment blocks and the state developed some public housing, which the next chapter discusses. For the purposes of this chapter, the key point is that access to the modern city's pleasures depended on a household's income, housing, hours of work, and location, and that these varied enormously.

Section 70.6 · Division II — Department Stores, Advertising, and the Consumer Revolution

From Kimono Merchants to Department Stores: Mitsukoshi and the New Retail Economy

The department store did not appear suddenly in the 1920s. Its roots lay in the Tokugawa-period commercial houses that sold textiles, above all kimono cloth, to samurai, wealthy townspeople, and later to a wider urban public. The firm that became Mitsukoshi began in the seventeenth century as the Echigoya, a dry-goods shop in Edo associated with the Mitsui family, whose founder is remembered for adopting practices of selling for cash at a fixed price and cutting cloth to the customer's required length, innovations that distinguished the shop from merchants who sold on credit and bargained. After the Meiji Restoration, the Mitsui family separated its banking from its drapery business, and the retail firm, renamed Mitsukoshi, entered the twentieth century with a strong reputation, a loyal clientele among the upper classes, and a stock tied largely to traditional clothing.

In late 1904, shortly before the end of the Russo-Japanese War, Mitsukoshi publicly announced its intention to become a "department store" (depātomento sutoa) on the model of Western retailers, an announcement circulated to customers and in the press and widely dated to the turn of 1904–1905. Executives, among whom Hibi Ōsuke is usually named, had studied foreign stores, and the declaration committed the firm to broadening its goods beyond clothing, reorganizing the shop into sales departments, and cultivating a wider clientele. The announcement is better understood as the beginning of a long process than as an overnight transformation. The firm continued to sell textiles to its established customers, and the full adoption of the new model took years. It also experimented with publicity, commissioning popular designs, running exhibitions, and publishing a house magazine.

The architectural statement came in 1914, when Mitsukoshi completed a large new five-story building on its Nihonbashi site, designed in a Renaissance-inspired style with a grand central hall. The building featured elevators and what is often described as Japan's first escalator, and it offered customers an experience unlike that of an old drapery shop: a bright, climate-managed interior in which they could browse displays, buy a wide range of goods, and, increasingly, eat, rest, and be entertained. The distinction between the 1904 declaration and the 1914 building is important. The first announced a business model and the second embodied it in stone and steel, and the gap of a decade shows how gradually the new retail economy was built.

Mitsukoshi was also not alone. Other merchant houses, among them Matsuzakaya, Shirokiya, Daimaru, and Takashimaya, were converting from kimono and dry-goods shops to department stores during the 1910s and 1920s, and each had its own history and customers. Together they transformed shopping from a transaction negotiated between a merchant and a known customer, often at the customer's home, into a public activity in which anonymous customers entered a large building, looked at goods displayed for their inspection, and paid fixed prices. It is this shift in the social relationship of buying and selling, as much as the architecture, that justifies the term "retail revolution."

Section 70.7 · Division II

The Department Store as an Urban Destination

What distinguished the department store from earlier shops was that it sold an experience as well as goods. Customers entered through grand doorways into a hall lit by large windows or electric lights, moved through floors by elevator or stairs, and encountered displays arranged to catch the eye. Display windows facing the street turned goods into spectacle for passersby who might never enter. Inside, stores added restaurants serving Western and Japanese food, tea rooms, exhibition halls for art and craft shows, lecture and concert spaces, reading rooms, and, on the roof, gardens, small shrines, playgrounds, and observation decks. A family could spend a day without buying anything substantial, and many did, but the store hoped that the day's pleasure would generate sales and loyalty.

The change of footwear policy illustrates how the stores adapted to a new kind of customer. In older drapery shops customers removed their shoes at the entrance and sat on raised tatami platforms while clerks brought out bolts of cloth. Department stores that allowed customers to walk through in street shoes (dosokuiri) turned the shop into a space like the street, in which people could walk, glance, and leave. After the earthquake, several stores adopted this practice, often reinforced by new concrete floors. The effect was to make browsing socially easier and to encourage impulse purchases, as well as to attract customers who would have felt uneasy in the more formal atmosphere of the old shop.

Department stores also staged events: seasonal sales, exhibitions of kimono patterns and new fashions, art shows, children's days, and special displays tied to festivals and national occasions. They hosted fashion shows and, in the later 1920s, began to feature Western clothing departments and exhibitions on modern household life. They offered services such as delivery, mail order, and, for the wealthy, home visits. By drawing together shopping, dining, culture, and entertainment under one roof, the stores created a social setting for the family outing, the courtship stroll, and the lone woman's afternoon, and they became a key institution of what contemporaries called modern life.

The work that sustained the spectacle was done by employees whose experience differed from the customers'. Stores hired large numbers of clerks, many of them young men who entered as apprentices under a traditional system of training and discipline, and, increasingly, young women as sales clerks, elevator attendants, and telephone and clerical staff. Their hours were long, their conduct was supervised closely, and their wages and chances of promotion were limited, particularly for women. The department store was thus a place where an affluent public saw the modern woman as a customer, while the women who staffed it were working under strict rules to maintain that image. Chapter 63's account of labor conditions helps place store employees among the many categories of urban service workers.

Section 70.8 · Division II

Retail Competition, Railway Companies, and the Expansion of Shopping Districts

The department stores competed vigorously, and their different histories shaped their strategies. Mitsukoshi, with its Mitsui lineage and prestigious customer base, positioned itself at the upper end of the market and built branches in Osaka and other cities, and later in Japan's colonies. Matsuzakaya, originating in Nagoya, and the Tokyo houses of Shirokiya and Daimaru, as well as the Osaka and Kyoto firm Takashimaya, followed their own paths, often combining traditional fabric trade with new departments. In the mid-1920s, large stores opened or reopened in Ginza, including Matsuzakaya and Matsuya, which fit the district's emerging image as the capital's retail promenade, and the Osaka store Daimaru built a large new building in the Shinsaibashi district during this period. The retail landscape was thus one of many competing firms, not a single national chain.

A second, and structurally different, model came from the railways. Kobayashi Ichizō, the founder of the Hankyu group, understood that a railway terminal funneled large numbers of commuters through a single place every day. In 1929 Hankyu opened a department store inside its Umeda terminal building in Osaka, a pioneering example of a retail enterprise built directly into a commuter railway station. The company sold the idea of the suburb together with the means of getting to the city, built housing along its routes, and used the terminal store to capture the spending of its commuters. Its dining hall, which served inexpensive meals, is often described in company lore as an appeal to ordinary customers rather than only the wealthy; such accounts should be checked against business-history studies before being treated as fact. The terminal department store became a model that other private railways later followed in Tokyo.

The result was a reorganization of urban retail geography. The older stores clustered where wealthy customers had traditionally shopped, while the railway stores arose at the junctions where commuter flows met. Transit companies, retailers, and developers formed interlocking enterprises, and the places they built became the major shopping districts of the later twentieth century. This pattern continued, with interruptions, through the depression and the war, and it was revived and expanded after 1945, as Chapter 96 will show for the late Shōwa period.

Section 70.9 · Division II

Advertising, Posters, Window Displays, and the Creation of Consumer Desire

Modern advertising grew alongside the retail revolution. Newspapers, whose circulations rose steeply in the 1910s and 1920s, sold space to advertisers, and magazines, posters, handbills, streetcar placards, illuminated signs, and sandwich-board men all vied for attention. Department stores were among the most inventive advertisers. Mitsukoshi commissioned artists and designers, ran poster competitions, and published fashion and household magazines. In the same period, manufacturers of cosmetics, soap, toothpaste, confectionery, beverages, patent medicines, and processed foods developed branded products and recognizable trademarks. Shiseidō, which began as a Ginza pharmacy and developed a famous cosmetics line and a design department, became known for stylized, elegant advertising, and companies such as Kao, Lion, Morinaga, and Glico used trademarks, jingles, and premiums to build loyalty.

The designers who made this visual culture formed a new profession. Commercial artists drew on the Art Nouveau and Art Deco styles of Europe, on Japanese woodblock traditions, and on the posters of cinema and theater, and they created images of women, goods, and urban scenes that became familiar. Typography, layout, and color were deployed to make a product seem fashionable. The slogans were often simple and memorable. A well-known example is Mitsukoshi's pairing of the Imperial Theatre with its own shop in a slogan that suggested an outing to the theater one day and to the store the next, linking the store to elite cultural pleasure. Section 70.9's source window below examines the way such slogans fused shopping with leisure.

Advertising claimed to show what people wanted, but it also helped to create wants. It taught consumers to see their appearance and household as subjects of improvement, to desire branded goods, and to associate products with modernity, health, hygiene, or beauty. Historians nonetheless caution against reading advertisements as evidence of what people bought. A poster could depict an electric fan, a gramophone, or a Western-style dress that most households could not afford. The advertisement shows what sellers hoped to sell and how they imagined the buyer, which is itself valuable evidence, but it does not by itself show who actually bought the goods. Sales records, household budget surveys, and memoirs are needed to test the images against practice.

Section 70.10 · Division II

Fashion Magazines, Household Advice, and the Imagined Modern Consumer

Popular magazines were the principal channel through which advertisers and reformers addressed households. Women's magazines of the 1910s and 1920s, among them Fujin Kōron, Fujin Kurabu, and above all Shufu no Tomo (Housewife's Friend, founded in 1917), combined fiction, advice, fashion, and practical instruction. Shufu no Tomo became famous for its household-budget ledgers, which were distributed with the magazine, and for articles teaching readers how to manage money, cook, sew, and organize the home. Mass-circulation general magazines such as King, first released by Kōdansha on November 28, 1924 as a January 1925 cover-dated issue, aimed at a broad audience with entertainment and moral uplift, and their circulations reached extraordinary levels by the standards of the day. Illustrated weekly newspapers and photo-magazines, such as Asahi Graph, made visual images of fashion and city life widely accessible.

These publications constructed an image of the modern consumer, and the image was strongly gendered. The housewife was presented as a rational manager of household resources, responsible for thrift, hygiene, children's education, and tasteful presentation of the home. Advertisers for soaps, cosmetics, foods, and household goods addressed her directly, and magazines advised on dress and beauty. A male counterpart appeared as the salaried husband who earned the family's income and whose appearance and leisure were also objects of commercial attention, though in a different register. The ideal of the "cultured household" overlapped with the state-sponsored ideal of the "good wife, wise mother" examined in Chapter 64, but it also differed: it emphasized consumption and comfort as well as duty, and it gave women a role as arbiters of taste.

Historians read these magazines as both prescriptive and descriptive sources. They prescribed ideals, and in their letters pages, reader contributions, and surveys they sometimes record readers' circumstances and anxieties. The editors and writers were largely educated middle-class men and women, and the targets were urban households with modest means. Poorer women, farm women, and factory workers appear mostly as objects of concern or occasional subjects of exposés, and their reading of the magazines, which some of them did, remains difficult to reconstruct. The magazines thus tell us a good deal about commercial ideals and the aspirations of an educated urban readership, and less about the unglamorous routines of the majority.

Historical Vocabulary
Historical Vocabulary: Depāto, Gofukuya, and Consumer Culture

Depāto is the Japanese rendering of "department store," a retailer organized into sales departments with fixed prices and open display. Gofukuya designates the older dealer in kimono cloth from which Mitsukoshi and several rivals descended. Dosokuiri refers to entering a shop in street shoes. Shufu (housewife) and sararīman (salaried employee) name the two household roles that advertisers addressed most often. Together these words describe a retail system, not merely a set of goods.

The English phrase "consumer culture" can mislead if it suggests that everyone shared in consumption. In 1920s Japan, the term describes the commercial institutions, images, and habits centered on a minority of urban households, while most people purchased goods in limited quantities and through older channels. Students should ask who is meant whenever sources speak of "the consumer" or "the modern household."

Section 70.11 · Division II

Household Consumption, New Products, and the Limits of Purchasing Power

The range of goods available to urban households expanded considerably. Machine-made and mass-marketed clothing, including inexpensive silk kimono fabrics such as meisen with bold modern patterns, reached a broad market. Western-style clothing spread first among men in offices, schools, and the military and later among schoolgirls, whose sailor-style uniforms, adopted by girls' schools beginning around 1920, became familiar in cities. Processed foods, among them caramels and chocolates, canned goods, seasonings, soft drinks, and mayonnaise, entered ordinary kitchens. Cosmetics, toothpaste, and soap spread as hygiene campaigns and advertising reinforced one another. Furniture in Western style, electric light fixtures, and gas stoves appeared in middle-class homes, and the electric lamp was by the late 1920s a common feature of urban households, though not a universal one.

But the distance between advertisement and household was great. Electric appliances were the clearest example. Irons and fans were sold and used, but other devices, such as domestic refrigerators and washing machines, were produced in Japan only around 1930 and were priced far beyond the reach of most families; research on household electrification emphasizes how the idealized electrified home of advertisements contrasted with the limited ownership in practice. Radios and phonographs were more widely sold, but a quality receiver or gramophone cost a substantial fraction of a modest household's monthly income. Many urban families rented their housing, shared facilities, and bought goods in small quantities on credit from neighborhood shops. The consumption described in magazines was concentrated among a minority and aspired to by many more.

Wages, working hours, and household budgets set the limits. Surveys by municipal authorities and social researchers showed that a very large share of a working-class household's income went to food and rent, leaving little for discretionary spending, while salaried households spent a smaller share on necessities and could afford some clothing, education, and entertainment. Housewives often practiced intensive thrift, saving and recycling and managing small loans, and the household account book was a tool for control as much as for planning. The shift in the early depression, when wages were cut and unemployment rose, as Chapter 73 will describe, further narrowed spending. The history of consumption is therefore a history of both expansion and constraint.

Section 70.12 · Division III — Cafés, Restaurants, Nightlife, and Commercial Sociability

Café Printemps, Café Lion, and the Early Urban Café Movement

The Japanese café (kafē) was a commercial institution with several overlapping origins. Coffeehouses and tea rooms of the late Meiji years served foreign residents and a small number of Japanese patrons, and the term kafē arrived with the expectation that it would evoke Paris. The most famous early venture was Café Printemps, which opened in Ginza in 1911 on the initiative of the painter Matsuyama Shōzō and became associated with the circle of writers and artists around the Pan no Kai, a group that had met since 1908 to promote a new, urban, and Western-inflected literary and artistic sensibility. Printemps offered coffee and light refreshments in a setting designed to resemble a European café, and its clientele were intellectuals, painters, writers, and actors who valued the space as a place to gather, talk, and be seen. Café Lion, associated with a beer company, and Café Paulista, which promoted Brazilian coffee, opened around the same time, and together these establishments mark the early café movement of 1910 and 1911.

These early cafés mattered for the history of public sociability. Japanese cities had long possessed commercial spaces for meeting: teahouses, restaurants, pleasure quarters, and bathhouses. The café differed in being an everyday place for a mixed, relatively informal conversation, open to people not bound by the rules of the pleasure districts, and associated with a Western aesthetic. It provided a setting for intellectuals and artists to cultivate a bohemian image, and it offered middle-class patrons a place to consume sociability without the obligations of a formal restaurant or the stigma attached to the licensed quarters. The presence of female servers, even in these early establishments, was a distinctive feature that would later become central to the café's reputation and to the anxieties surrounding it.

Historians distinguish carefully between these early artistic cafés and the commercialized café boom of the late 1920s. The earlier ventures were small in number, concentrated in Ginza, and tied to a particular milieu of artists and writers. They did not constitute a mass phenomenon. The much larger expansion came after the First World War and especially after the earthquake, when cafés spread across Tokyo, Osaka, and other cities and developed a broad range of types, from quiet coffeehouses to establishments whose main appeal was the company of female staff. The fame of Printemps and its rivals sometimes led later writers to project the character of the early artistic café onto the whole café phenomenon, a simplification that obscures the diversity of the later period.

Section 70.13 · Division III

Ginza, Osaka, and the Geography of Café Culture

By the mid-1920s, cafés were widespread, and the term covered establishments of very different kinds. Some, often called kissaten or coffee shops in later usage, served coffee, tea, and light food in a relatively quiet setting and attracted students, writers, and office workers. Others were large, ornamented venues with orchestras or phonographs, many tables, and numerous waitresses. Still others were essentially bars or hostess establishments in which alcohol and companionship were the principal attractions. Regulators eventually recognized these differences by creating separate licensing categories, though the boundaries in practice were blurred.

In Tokyo, Ginza was the best-known café district, with establishments of every type clustered along its main streets and side lanes. After the earthquake the district's rebuilding attracted new venues, and large cafés competed in elaborate interiors and in the appeal of their staff. The writer and social observer Kon Wajirō and his collaborators documented the streets of Ginza in 1925, counting passersby by clothing, accessories, and behavior, and their survey shows how the district drew a fashionable and relatively well-dressed crowd. Cafés also flourished in Asakusa, Shinjuku, and other entertainment quarters, with a more varied and often lower-income clientele. Osaka's café culture, centered on Dōtonbori, Shinsaibashi, and the northern districts, was noted for its large and showy establishments and its commercial energy, and the city was often presented as a rival or counterpart to Tokyo in these pleasures.

Clientele differed by district and by type. Literary figures and students favored quieter coffeehouses and certain well-known cafés. Salaried workers visited at lunchtime or after work, some seeking a drink, some a meal, some the company of waitresses. Entertainers, journalists, and those working in the culture industries also formed part of the clientele. Women customers were far fewer, and respectable women generally avoided establishments associated with alcohol and hostessing, though tea rooms and department-store restaurants provided alternative settings. The social meaning of the café therefore varied: for some it was an intellectual salon, for some a place of sociable escape, and for some an erotic marketplace, and these functions coexisted, sometimes within a single establishment.

The café was also tied to the broader commercial ecology of the city. Cafés bought coffee, beer, and food from suppliers, hired musicians, and advertised through newspapers and posters. Department stores and railway stations, for their part, operated their own restaurants and tea rooms, in competition with independent cafés. The café boom of the later 1920s needs to be understood as part of this commercial system of leisure and not as a purely cultural phenomenon: it generated employment, supported suppliers, and attracted the attention of police and moralists in equal measure.

Section 70.14 · Division III

Café Waitresses: Gender, Labor, Service, and Sexualization

The café waitress (jokyū) became one of the most conspicuous figures of the new city, and also one of the most misunderstood. Journalists, novelists, artists, and advertisers represented her as the embodiment of urban glamour or moral danger. Painters and illustrators depicted waitresses in Western dress, and the Metropolitan Museum of Art and other collections hold works of the period that make waitresses a recognizable subject. The writer Tanizaki Jun'ichirō's serialized novel Naomi (1924–1925), whose heroine begins as a café worker and becomes a fashionable, willful young woman, helped to give the type literary currency, though the novel's depiction is a fictional and male-authored fantasy. The historian Miriam Silverberg emphasized that these images should be distinguished from the working lives of the women to whom they referred.

As workers, café waitresses typically came from modest backgrounds, including young women from rural families, urban working-class households, and in some cases daughters of declining middle-class families. Many were paid little or no regular wage and relied on tips (chippu) from customers, sometimes supplemented by shares of drink sales or by food and lodging arranged by the establishment. This structure of pay gave waitresses a financial incentive to please customers and made their income unpredictable, and it exposed them to pressure to provide companionship that blurred the boundary between service and sexual availability. Managers set rules about dress, conduct, and fees, and fines or deductions for infractions were common. Some waitresses earned relatively good incomes, particularly at fashionable establishments, and they could be financially independent in a way few other women could manage. Others struggled with debts, harassment, and instability. Generalizing about "the waitress" is therefore misleading.

The relationship between waitresses and sex work requires careful treatment. Licensed prostitution in the pleasure quarters was a separate, legally regulated institution, as earlier chapters described, and the café waitress was formally an employee of an eating and drinking establishment. In practice, however, customers, journalists, and police frequently assumed that waitresses were sexually available, and some cafés encouraged that expectation. Some waitresses did engage in sexual relationships with customers, whether by choice, pressure, or economic need, while others did not. Historians avoid reducing the waitress to either a victim or a sexual commodity: she was a worker negotiating unequal conditions, with varying degrees of agency, in a commercial environment that sexualized her labor.

Police and moral reformers were deeply concerned. In the late 1920s, local police issued rules for cafés that provided female companionship, regulating hours, requiring registration of waitresses, and in some cases limiting the manner in which they served customers. Newspapers published exposés, and social surveys examined waitresses' backgrounds and wages. These sources show a pattern in which commentators often spoke about the waitresses rather than allowing them to speak for themselves. A few published memoirs and first-person accounts exist, as well as interviews and letters, and they provide valuable glimpses of how some women understood their work, though they too were shaped by editors and publishers and must be read critically.

Section 70.15 · Division III

Restaurants, Western-Style Food, and the Social Meaning of Dining Out

Eating out expanded markedly in the cities. The Tokugawa period had already produced a lively culture of noodle stalls, teahouses, and restaurants, but the 1910s and 1920s saw the spread of new kinds of establishments serving dishes adapted from Western and Chinese cuisines. The category known as yōshoku, literally "Western food," included pork cutlets, croquettes, curry rice, stews, and other dishes that originated in Western models but were adapted to Japanese ingredients, rice-centered meals, and tastes. Croquettes, in particular, became so popular that a comic song about them, associated with the Asakusa Opera of the late 1910s, entered popular memory. Curry rice, brought through the British and developed in Japan, spread through military messes, restaurants, and department-store dining halls. In 1927 the Shinjuku bakery and restaurant Nakamuraya began serving a curry associated with the exiled Indian nationalist Rash Behari Bose, a case of hybrid cuisine produced by migration and politics as well as taste.

Dining out had social meanings that varied with price and setting. A formal Western restaurant or hotel dining room signaled sophistication and wealth, and department-store restaurants offered affordable ways for families to taste fashionable food in a respectable setting. Beer halls, where a growing market for beer had been developed by Japanese brewers, provided a more convivial environment, and workers' eateries and street stalls served simple, cheap meals. Chinese-style noodle shops, such as Asakusa's Rairaiken, founded around 1910, sold inexpensive noodles to a broad clientele, and the dishes later known as ramen emerged from this urban food culture. Restaurants were therefore part of the class geography of the city: the same district could contain a luxury dining room, a department-store tea room, a café, and a stall, each addressing a different segment of the population.

Historians caution against describing this change as simple Westernization. Dishes that originated in foreign models were rapidly adapted: bread and rice coexisted, Western dishes were served with chopsticks and with rice, and Japanese diners shaped menus to local preferences. Meanwhile much of the population continued to eat primarily traditional meals at home, and the nutrition and hygiene campaigns of the period encouraged particular changes, such as more protein and vegetables, that were not always connected to restaurants. The restaurant was an important arena of change, but the household kitchen, where most food was prepared, changed more slowly.

Section 70.16 · Division III

Nightlife, Dance, Jazz Venues, and the Regulation of Urban Pleasure

Urban nightlife developed gradually, and chronology matters. The late Taishō and early Shōwa years saw growth in cafés, bars, dance halls, music venues, and amusement districts, but many features associated with the nightlife of the early 1930s, such as the proliferation of hostess-centered bars, large dance halls, and a recognized jazz scene, developed through the late 1920s and should not be projected back onto the early Taishō period. In the 1910s, evening entertainment in the cities was more likely to mean the theater, the temple fair, the pleasure quarters, or the variety hall than a café or dance hall. The expansion of electric lighting made streets brighter and encouraged evening activity, and the growth of the salaried class provided customers with disposable income.

Social dancing is a revealing case. Dance instruction and dance halls appeared in Japanese cities in the 1920s, with Western-style ballroom dancing taught in schools and practiced in clubs, hotels, and, increasingly, commercial dance halls staffed by professional dancers (dansā) who danced with customers for tickets. These women, like café waitresses, occupied an ambiguous position between respectable employment and sexual commentary. Musicians playing in dance bands performed in hotels, ocean liners, and halls, and early jazz was often played by Japanese bands and by Filipino musicians who worked in the ports and entertainment districts. The phonograph and radio spread jazz and dance music further, as Section 70.30 explains. Dance and jazz were thus tied to both transnational musical networks and the local commercial structure of the entertainment industry.

Authorities regulated these pleasures. Police licensed cafés, bars, theaters, and dance venues, set closing hours, and monitored public morals. Newspapers carried campaigns against "decadent" entertainments, and local governments debated limits on dance halls and late-night establishments. In the later 1920s regulation of cafés and hostess establishments increased, and in the 1930s further restrictions followed as part of a broader moral and ideological campaign. The tension between a growing commercial entertainment industry and a state that regarded public morals as its responsibility is a recurring theme. Regulation did not suppress nightlife, which continued to expand, but it shaped its forms, its risks, and the conditions under which entertainers worked.

Section 70.17 · Division IV — Modern Girls, Modern Boys, Fashion, and the Politics of Gender

The Modern Girl (Moga): Fashion, Consumption, and Public Visibility

The phrase "modern girl" (modan gāru, abbreviated moga) entered Japanese journalism around the middle of the 1920s and quickly became a catchall for a kind of young urban woman: fashionably dressed, often with short hair, visible on the streets, comfortable in cafés and cinemas, and imagined as independent in her tastes and her relationships. The term spread through newspapers, magazines, cartoons, advertisements, and popular fiction, and it was applied both admiringly and disapprovingly. Its prominence peaked in the later 1920s. The key analytic point is that moga was a label produced by commentators, not a self-identification adopted by a clearly defined group of women, and it combined observations of real behavior with fantasies and fears.

Real changes lay behind the image. A minority of young urban women, particularly those with wages of their own, chose Western-style dress, bobbed their hair, wore cosmetics, and went out in public alone or with friends, often using the new railways and shopping in department stores. Girls' schools adopted sailor-style uniforms from about 1920, popularizing Western dress among adolescents, and beauty salons offering permanent waves began to appear. Hairstyle was especially symbolic: short hair broke with the elaborately dressed hair of earlier generations and could provoke open disapproval. Yet the great majority of Japanese women continued to wear kimono in public throughout the 1920s. Kon Wajirō's 1925 Ginza street survey counted about 67 percent of men but only 1 percent of women in Western dress; these figures describe that observed Ginza sample, not Japan nationally, a striking contrast that warns against imagining a universal change in women's clothing. The modern girl was visible far out of proportion to her numbers.

Historians therefore distinguish the historical women who adopted aspects of modern fashion and behavior from the symbolic moga of magazines and advertising. The symbolic figure was often a young, slim, fashionable woman with short hair, drawn in a stylized manner and used to sell cosmetics, clothing, cigarettes, and entertainment. Advertisers found her useful because she represented novelty and desire. Commentators found her useful because she could stand for cultural change, national anxiety, or moral decline. Neither representation tells us how women who were never photographed or illustrated lived. Historians such as Miriam Silverberg have argued that the modern girl was both a recognizable social presence and a commercial construction, and that the Japanese modern girl was not a mere imitation of the American flapper but a figure shaped by Japanese commerce, Japanese debates about womanhood, and a transnational circulation of styles.

Historical Vocabulary
Historical Vocabulary: Moga, Mobo, Kafē, Katsudō Shashin, and Hōsō

Moga and mobo abbreviate modan gāru and modan bōi ("modern girl" and "modern boy"). They were media labels for fashionable young urban women and men, associated most strongly with the mid and late 1920s, and they described a style and a perceived attitude more than an actual social class. Kafē (café) designated establishments ranging from coffeehouses to hostess bars. Jokyū meant a woman working as a waitress or server. Katsudō shashin ("moving pictures"), later shortened to katsudō, was the older term for film, gradually replaced by eiga, the term that became standard. Hōsō means broadcasting, from the verbs for sending and releasing. Chikuonki, literally "sound-storing machine," meant the gramophone.

These words mattered because they shaped how people thought about the changes. A word like moga could turn a diverse set of behaviors into a single, easily criticized type, and a term like jokyū could group very different workers under a label with sexual connotations. Students should treat such terms as part of the historical evidence, asking who used them and for what purposes, rather than as neutral descriptions.

Section 70.18 · Division IV

The Modern Boy (Mobo) and the Changing Culture of Young Men

The modern boy (modan bōi, or mobo) was the male counterpart of the modern girl, though he received less moral attention. The image combined Western-style clothing, with suits, hats, wide trousers, and sometimes striking accessories, with fashionable behavior: frequenting cafés, cinemas, dance halls, and shopping districts, adopting slang and foreign loanwords, and enjoying jazz and Hollywood films. The round-framed glasses worn by the American comic film actor Harold Lloyd were widely imitated in Japan, an example commonly cited of the influence of cinema on style. Like the moga, the mobo was both a visible minority and a media type, and the two were often paired in cartoons and commentary.

The social background of young men who followed these styles varied. Students at universities and higher schools, young office workers, apprentices in the culture and media industries, and sons of well-off merchant households were prominent. Western clothing for men, however, was far more widespread than for women: it was normal for office workers, officials, students, and many professionals, because of its association with modern institutions, and the Ginza survey reflected this. Being a mobo was therefore a matter of degree, a stylish extension of a dress code already common for educated urban men, in contrast with the stronger break that Western clothing represented for women. The male modern was less transgressive, and so less visible in moral debate.

Masculinity itself was changing. The ideal of the salaried breadwinner who devoted himself to his firm and his family coexisted with the ideal of the fashionable young man who enjoyed leisure and urban pleasures, and the tension between these ideals appeared in films, cartoons, and essays. The educated unemployed, the underpaid clerk, and the student radical were additional figures in this changing field. Military service and its prestige continued to shape male identity as well, and the contrast between the commercial dandy and the disciplined soldier became a theme in later criticism, especially as the political climate hardened around 1930. Chapter 74 and later chapters follow the way those tensions were resolved.

Section 70.19 · Division IV

Ginza Strolling, Public Space, and the New Freedom of Movement

The practice of strolling in Ginza, for which journalists coined the term ginbura (a contraction suggesting "wandering in Ginza"), became emblematic of the new urban leisure. A folk etymology links the word to coffee from Brazil served at a Ginza café, but the origin is uncertain and the story is best treated as legend. What is clear is that Ginza's main street, rebuilt after the earthquake, lined with department stores, cafés, bookstores, and display windows, and lit in the evening, was a place where people went to see and be seen. Strolling required no purchase and offered entertainment: window shopping, people watching, chance encounters, and a sense of belonging to the modern city.

Strolling was a distinctive form of public behavior. In older Japanese cities, people tended to move through streets with specific purposes and within the social frameworks of neighborhood, family, and workplace, and the pleasure quarters were the principal places set aside for leisure and display. The new promenades, department stores, cafés, and parks permitted young men and women to walk and mix with relative anonymity, away from the watchful eyes of neighbors and relatives. This opened possibilities for courtship outside arranged introductions, for friendships among students and workers, and for personal experimentation, and it explains why moralists feared it. Magazines advised young people on how to dress and behave on the street, and in cartoons the Ginza stroller became a stock figure.

Observers such as Kon Wajirō approached these streets with a sociologist's curiosity. His "modernology" (kōgengaku), which he developed with collaborators in the 1920s, treated the city streets, the interiors of rooms, the clothes people wore, and the objects they carried as archaeological evidence of contemporary life, and recorded them in sketches and counts. Their documentation is an unusual source for the everyday, though like all such surveys it reflected the choices of observers about what to record. Its 1925 observations found about 67 percent of men and 1 percent of women in Western dress on the sampled Ginza streets; that sample alone cannot establish a numerical comparison with other districts, but it reveals stark gender differences in the use of Western clothing, and that freedom of movement was unevenly distributed by class, gender, and age. A factory worker with a fourteen-hour day, a rural migrant uneasy in the city, or a woman without a respectable escort might not feel free to stroll at all.

Section 70.20 · Division IV

Working Women, Consumer Independence, and Social Constraints

The women who went out in the city included many who had jobs. Contemporaries used the term "professional women" or "career women" (shokugyō fujin) for women who worked outside the home in the new service and clerical occupations: shop assistants, telephone operators, typists, office clerks, elevator attendants, bus conductors, teachers, nurses, journalists, and entertainers, in addition to the vast numbers of women who worked in factories, in agriculture, and as domestic servants. Women in the new occupations were a small proportion of the female workforce but received disproportionate attention, as they seemed to embody women's new role in modern society. The female bus conductor in Tokyo, introduced in the city's emergency bus service after the earthquake, and the "department-store girl" in a uniform became public symbols.

Earning wages could give women a measure of independence and the capacity to purchase clothing, cosmetics, magazines, and entertainment, and some used their wages to support family members, while others saved or spent on themselves. But the constraints were severe. Women earned considerably less than men in comparable roles, and employers often expected them to resign at marriage or after a few years. Workplace rules governed dress and behavior, and some employers imposed restrictions on women's personal conduct. Sexual harassment was widespread, and a woman's reputation could be damaged by public exposure. Newspapers frequently criticized working women for supposed vanity or immorality, and the same press that sold magazines to them also indulged in sensational stories about their supposed sexual misadventures. Women who earned money lived with expectations that they would ultimately marry and become housewives, and the options for independent adult life were narrow.

Not all working women could participate in commercial leisure. Women factory workers confined to dormitories, as Chapter 69 described, rarely had the time, money, or freedom to stroll in Ginza, though some saw films or visited shopping streets on rare days off. Rural women worked on farms and in households with little access to the city's pleasures. The "modern" working woman of magazine imagery was typically educated, urban, and employed in a visible occupation. Historians therefore speak of the uneven geography and class structure of women's modernity, and they treat the wage-earning woman consumer as one figure among many rather than as a representative of her sex.

Section 70.21 · Division IV

Moral Anxiety, Respectability, and Debates about the Modern Generation

The modern girl and boy provoked sustained commentary. Conservative critics, including educators, officials, and some religious and community leaders, interpreted the new styles as signs of moral decay, selfishness, and loss of discipline, and they associated them with foreign influence and commercialism. Newspapers published articles and cartoons attacking bobbed hair, short skirts, makeup, and public displays of affection, and school authorities in some places tried to restrict students' clothing and behavior. Concerns about marriage were prominent: the discourse of romantic love (ren'ai), popularized by writers such as Kuriyagawa Hakuson in the early 1920s, raised questions about arranged marriage, premarital relationships, and the family system, and commentators worried that young people were abandoning obligations to parents and the nation.

Not all commentary was hostile. Some liberal and socialist writers welcomed greater freedom of movement and self-expression, while criticizing the commercial exploitation of women's image. Feminist writers divided: some saw greater public visibility and economic independence as advances, while others, particularly socialist feminists, argued that consumer fashion distracted from deeper inequalities of class and gender and encouraged women to see themselves as objects. Humorists and cartoonists satirized both the modern girl and her critics. The debates shaped the way later writers remembered the 1920s, and the same arguments reappeared in later decades.

Historians have asked how far the modern girl was a social phenomenon and how far a media creation. The most persuasive answers recognize both. Journalists, advertisers, and publishers amplified a small number of real women into a symbolic type, and used that type to sell goods and attract readers. At the same time, women's changing education, employment, and public presence were real, and the types referred to actual changes. Studying the debates also reveals who held power over representation: the images and arguments were mostly produced by men, though women contributed as writers, activists, and designers. Understanding the modern girl requires reading these sources against the evidence of women's incomes, jobs, and lives.

Historical Debate

The Modern Girl: Liberation or Commodification?

The question: Did the modern girl represent a genuine expansion of women's freedom, or was she primarily a commercial image that harnessed women's desires for profit and reinforced existing gender hierarchies?

Interpretation A — A real and significant social change: Some historians emphasize that women's presence in public, their wages, their changing dress, and their participation in leisure and the labor market marked a real shift in expectations about women's behavior. They point to the intensity of the debate as evidence that something had changed, and they stress women's own agency in adopting new styles and pursuing new lives.
Interpretation B — A commercial and discursive construction: Others stress that the image of the modern girl was constructed by journalists, advertisers, and male commentators, that it was applied to women who did not live in these ways, and that the commercial culture that promoted it also sexualized and disciplined women. They emphasize that wage inequality, family expectations, and the legal limitations on women's rights, including the lack of the vote, remained largely unchanged.

Evidence: Both interpretations use magazines, advertisements, cartoons, surveys of clothing and occupations, wage data, and the arguments of contemporary critics. Miriam Silverberg's scholarship has been central in arguing that the modern girl was both a recognizable presence and a commercial construction, which suggests that the two interpretations are complementary rather than strictly opposed.

Why it matters: How historians answer the question shapes how they assess the extent and meaning of social change in the 1920s, the relationship between consumer culture and women's rights, and the continuities between interwar and postwar gender politics.

Section 70.22 · Division V — Film Studios, Cinema Audiences, Stars, and the Modern Imagination

Japanese Film Production in the 1920s: Nikkatsu, Shōchiku, and Studio Growth

Chapter 65 followed the arrival of moving pictures in Japan, the rise of the benshi, and the early film companies. By the 1920s, cinema had become a mature commercial industry, and historians often call the decade a golden age of Japanese silent film. Nikkatsu, formed in 1912 by the merger of several earlier producers and distributors, remained one of the largest companies, with studios in Tokyo and Kyoto. Shōchiku, which had begun as a theater-management firm in Kyoto and expanded into kabuki and other stage productions, entered filmmaking in 1920 and established a studio at Kamata, in the southern part of Tokyo. Other companies, including Taishō Katsuei (Taikatsu), Makino Productions, and later several independent production firms, competed for audiences and talent, and many were short-lived.

Shōchiku's entry is illustrative. The company hired figures with experience abroad, among them the filmmaker Kotani Henry, who had worked in Hollywood, and it aimed to modernize production with improved lighting, camera work, and storytelling, and to draw on its stage resources, including actors and theaters. The Kamata studio, led from the mid-1920s by the producer Kido Shirō, became known for a style of films about contemporary urban life and ordinary people. Taikatsu hired the celebrated novelist Tanizaki Jun'ichirō as a literary adviser, signaling an attempt to raise the status of film as an art. In Kyoto, which retained a large concentration of period-drama expertise, Nikkatsu, Makino, and others produced swordplay and historical films in large numbers. The two cities thus developed distinct production cultures: Tokyo for contemporary-life dramas and Kyoto for period films, a division that remained influential.

The industry's structure rested on integration of production, distribution, and exhibition. Companies owned or contracted with theaters, controlled the flow of films, and developed star systems. Theaters showed programs that often combined several films, newsreels, and live performances, with the film accompanied by a benshi and musicians. Competition for audiences encouraged companies to specialize, to produce quickly, and to cultivate genres. Output was large by the standards of the time, with Japanese companies producing hundreds of films per year in the later 1920s, much of it period drama, though a large proportion of this output has not survived. The scarcity of surviving prints is a fundamental limit on historical knowledge: many films are known only through reviews, scripts, stills, and memoirs.

Section 70.23 · Division V

The Transformation of Silent Cinema: Benshi, Actresses, and the Pure Film Movement

Early Japanese cinema had inherited many conventions from the stage. Women's roles were played by male specialists (onnagata), as in kabuki, camera positions often mimicked a spectator's view of a stage, scenes were long and static, and a live narrator, the benshi, explained the story. Critics and filmmakers associated with the "pure film" movement (jun'eiga geki undō), among them the critic and director Kaeriyama Norimasa, argued that cinema should develop its own methods, borrowing techniques of editing, close-ups, and realistic acting from foreign films, and abandoning stage conventions. Kaeriyama's writings on film production are among the foundational texts of this movement. The movement drew on admiration for Hollywood and European filmmaking while making a claim for the artistic seriousness of the medium.

The first change visible to audiences was the casting of actresses. Shōchiku and Taikatsu employed women in female roles from 1920, and Nikkatsu's Tokyo-area studio, which retained onnagata longer, moved toward actresses in the early 1920s, though some period-film productions kept the older convention longer. The change marked a break with the stage tradition and made films more realistic, but it also opened a new profession for women, who attracted audiences and public scrutiny. Early actresses faced social prejudice: acting remained a disreputable occupation, and women on screen were subjected to the same moral commentary as other women in public roles. They also became among the first female celebrities, with their images circulating in magazines and advertising.

Visual technique evolved too. Directors used more varied camera positions, editing for continuity and rhythm, location shooting, and intertitles in addition to the narrator. The experimental film A Page of Madness (1926), directed by Kinugasa Teinosuke with the novelist Kawabata Yasunari among its collaborators, drew on European avant-garde films, and his Crossroads (1928) was distributed abroad. Itō Daisuke's period dramas of the late 1920s used fast-paced action, and the star Bandō Tsumasaburō created his own production company. Yet the benshi remained powerful. Many audiences came as much for the narrator's performance as for the film, benshi were stars, and distributors and theater owners relied on them. The persistence of the benshi after the arrival of sound is a topic for later study; for the 1920s, it should be remembered that silent cinema in Japan was a performance event rather than a purely visual one, and that attempts to reform it met resistance from performers with commercial interests.

Section 70.24 · Division V

New Film Genres: Domestic Dramas, Urban Youth, Comedy, and Melodrama

Japanese cinema of the 1920s was divided between the period film (jidaigeki), set in the Tokugawa or earlier eras, and the contemporary-life film (gendaigeki). The period film, with its swordfights, loyal retainers, and outlaw heroes, was extremely popular and was produced especially in Kyoto. It also became a vehicle for social commentary, as directors used historical settings to explore rebellious individuals and resistance to authority. Contemporary-life films, centered in Tokyo, dealt with modern urban settings and fell into several categories: melodramas of love and sacrifice, stories of women's suffering and endurance, comedies about young people and city life, and a genre that Japanese critics came to call shōshimin eiga, films about ordinary lower-middle-class people.

The shōshimin eiga gave attention to salaried workers, small shopkeepers, and their families: their jobs, housing, tensions between spouses or generations, and the anxieties of finding or keeping employment. Shimazu Yasujirō, working at Shōchiku's Kamata studio, was a pioneer of the genre, and the directors Gosho Heinosuke and Ozu Yasujirō, both associated with Shōchiku, made comedies and dramas of everyday life. Ozu's 1929 comedy I Graduated, But… treats a university graduate struggling to find work in the changing economy, and it captures the sense of insecurity beneath the surface of consumer prosperity. Although some of these films survive only in fragments, those that do show the way contemporary cinema reflected and shaped self-images of the new middle class.

Melodramas often centered on women and used urban settings to explore tensions between tradition and change, while comedies mocked the fads of the modern boy and girl. Directors such as Mizoguchi Kenji, who made films for Nikkatsu in the 1920s, depicted women's lives in the entertainment quarters and the city, and he later became one of the major figures of Japanese cinema. His 1929 film Tokyo March, discussed in Section 70.30, was linked to a popular song. Genre cannot be reduced to class, but the popular film gave different groups images of themselves: middle-class audiences saw their domestic anxieties, working-class audiences found heroic outlaws, and women found stories of heartbreak and endurance. Historians who study these films treat them as imaginative works that reflect changing sentiments, not as documentary records.

Section 70.25 · Division V

Movie Theaters, Film Stars, Fans, and the Experience of Spectatorship

Going to the cinema was a commercial leisure activity with its own geography, prices, and etiquette. Cities contained clusters of theaters, especially in entertainment quarters such as Asakusa's Rokku district and Osaka's Dōtonbori, and the larger cities also had first-run theaters in fashionable districts where new films opened and where admission was more expensive. Smaller neighborhood theaters showed older films at lower prices. Seating was divided into classes, and in many theaters audiences sat on benches or mats, though the larger new theaters introduced upholstered seats. Prices varied considerably, from a few dozen sen in cheap theaters to a yen or more for reserved seats in prestigious halls, and a visit to the cinema could be a significant expense for a low-income family. Rural areas had far fewer theaters, with traveling exhibitors and occasional screenings in schools or halls.

Audiences varied by class, age, and gender. Young men and women, students, and salaried workers were regular customers, while families attended special programs. Children formed an important audience for adventure films and cartoons, and the content of films prompted debates about their influence on young people, which led to the establishment of educational and moral guidelines. Women were a significant part of the audience for melodramas, and companies created women's pictures and published fan magazines to attract them. Different types of theaters attracted different crowds, with some, especially those showing foreign films, regarded as fashionable and others as plebeian.

Star culture and fan magazines helped sustain the industry. Kinema Junpō, a film trade journal founded in 1919, published reviews, essays, and eventually annual rankings of the year's best films, and a growing range of fan publications carried photographs, gossip, and serialized stories. Stars such as the swordplay actor Bandō Tsumasaburō and others became household names, and fans wrote letters and bought postcards, posters, and magazines. The commercial publicity apparatus tied films to other media: songs, novels, and advertisements. A fan could encounter a star in a magazine, hear a theme song on a record, and see a film in a theater, and these overlapping channels deepened loyalty and generated additional revenue. The film industry was thus a hub of the mass-culture economy.

Section 70.26 · Division V

Hollywood, Japanese Cinema, Censorship, and Cultural Competition

Foreign films, above all American ones, were a large part of what Japanese audiences saw. Hollywood comedies, adventures, and romances, with stars such as Charlie Chaplin, Douglas Fairbanks, and Mary Pickford, drew large audiences in the cities, and European films, especially from Germany and France, appealed to more specialized audiences and critics. Distributors imported and exhibited foreign films, often with Japanese titles and benshi explanations, and film magazines devoted much space to foreign productions. The popularity of imports raised concerns among nationalists and some officials about the cultural influence of Hollywood, even as Japanese filmmakers studied foreign methods and copied some of their successes. The competition was not simply between foreign and Japanese films, but within a commercial system that distributed both and sought profit from each.

The state supervised cinema through censorship. The Metropolitan Police Board had issued film regulations in the 1910s, and in 1925 the Home Ministry introduced national film-censorship rules that standardized procedures across prefectures. Censors examined films for content considered harmful to public morals, offensive to the imperial house, or ideologically dangerous, and they could demand cuts or ban films. Scenes involving sexuality, violence, or political radicalism were frequent targets, and films depicting class conflict or criticizing authority were treated with suspicion. Censorship thus influenced production as filmmakers anticipated restrictions, and as critics later noted, it encouraged certain kinds of indirectness. At the same time, censorship was uneven and negotiated, and many films that touched on contemporary tensions reached the screen.

Anxieties about cinema's influence extended beyond politics. Educators and social workers worried about the effect of films on children and young people, and local authorities restricted attendance by students in some places. Some critics condemned Hollywood's emphasis on romance and spectacle; others argued that foreign films had improved Japanese filmmaking. The arrival of sound, beginning with imported sound films in the late 1920s and with Japanese sound films around 1930, threatened the benshi and changed the industry, but this transformation lies largely beyond the chapter's chronological core; Shōchiku's 1931 film The Neighbor's Wife and Mine, a landmark of Japanese sound production, is best understood as an endpoint that marks the end of the silent era's dominance rather than a feature of the 1920s.

Section 70.27 · Division VI — Radio Broadcasting, Recorded Music, and the New Soundscape

The Beginning of Radio Broadcasting in Japan, 1925

Japan's first public, provisionally authorized radio broadcasts began on March 22, 1925, from a temporary studio at Shibaura in Tokyo; full regular service began on July 12 after the move to Atagoyama. Test transmissions had begun earlier that month, and the Tokyo station that took to the air that day was operated by the newly formed Tokyo Broadcasting Association (Tokyo Hōsō Kyoku). The date matters because it is sometimes misstated: broadcasting did not begin in 1924, and the Tokyo service had to be authorized and organized after a period of regulatory negotiation. The station moved to its permanent Atagoyama facility in July, inaugurating regular service there. Osaka followed, with its station starting in June 1925, and Nagoya in July, each operated by its own regional association and each reflecting local commercial and civic interests.

The technology of early radio was straightforward in principle but demanding in practice. Listeners received amplitude-modulated signals, and in the early years many used inexpensive crystal sets that required headphones and picked up signals from nearby stations, while more expensive vacuum-tube receivers could power loudspeakers and receive signals from greater distances. Hobbyists built their own sets from parts and magazine instructions, and the radio magazines that appeared in the mid-1920s combined program schedules with technical advice. The programs were limited in hours and consisted of a mixture of news bulletins, weather reports, lectures, music, storytelling, and later drama and sports. Early reception was unreliable, with interference and variations in signal quality, and regional differences in service were large.

Radio's promoters presented it as an instrument of education, culture, and national integration. The first head of the Tokyo organization was the statesman Gotō Shinpei, a former mayor of Tokyo and colonial administrator, whose prestige lent weight to the enterprise. Supporters spoke of bringing information and culture to every home and of linking city and countryside. Critics, including some in the press, worried about the loss of newspaper readership and about government influence. The historian must distinguish the experimental and temporary arrangements of early 1925 from the regular services that followed, and both from the consolidated national organization of 1926 described in the next section.

Section 70.28 · Division VI

Broadcasting Institutions, Government Regulation, and the Public Audience

Japan chose a different path from the United States, where commercial stations dominated early broadcasting. The Ministry of Communications, which controlled wireless telegraphy under earlier law, licensed broadcasting through non-profit associations rather than commercial companies, and funded them through listener fees rather than advertising. Each receiving installation required a permit, and listeners paid a monthly fee of one yen. Two yen had been proposed, but the fee was reduced to one yen for provisional broadcasting and remained at that level when regular broadcasts began, a significant cost for many households. A separate government permit charge also applied. The arrangement reflected officials' concern about private control of a powerful medium and their wish to supervise content.

In August 1926 the three regional associations were merged by government direction into a single national organization, the Nippon Hōsō Kyōkai (Japan Broadcasting Corporation, known abroad as NHK). The merger gave the new body a monopoly on broadcasting in Japan, under the supervision of the Ministry of Communications, which appointed or approved its leadership and reviewed its programs. Over the following years it built relay stations and extended the network, and in 1928 it linked stations across the country for the broadcast of ceremonies surrounding the enthronement of the Shōwa emperor, an event that illustrated broadcasting's potential as a medium of national ritual. The centralized structure meant that mass communication in a modern medium was organized under state supervision at a time when the press, though also censored, remained commercially independent.

Centralization had consequences for content. Programs were reviewed by officials and by the organization's own administrators, and political commentary was limited. News was supplied largely through arrangements with the established news agencies and newspapers, which sought to protect their own business. Commentators then and later have noted that Japanese broadcasting developed as a public-service institution with a strong state presence, in contrast with commercial and competitive models elsewhere. Historians emphasize, however, that listeners were not passive: they chose what to hear, wrote letters, and shaped programming through their preferences, and the organization depended on fees from tens of thousands, and later hundreds of thousands, of subscribers.

Section 70.29 · Division VI

News, Music, Lectures, Sports, and the Organization of Daily Listening

Radio programming introduced new routines into daily life. Scheduled broadcasts meant that people could listen at the same time to the same content, whether a morning weather report, a midday news bulletin, an afternoon lecture on household management, or an evening concert. The mix included Western classical music, Japanese traditional music and storytelling, popular songs, language lessons, and talks by scholars and public figures. Sports events became a major attraction. The national middle-school baseball tournament at Kōshien Stadium was broadcast in 1927, and baseball's popularity grew with radio coverage, as Section 70.33 explains. In November 1928 radio calisthenics (rajio taisō) were introduced in connection with the enthronement celebrations, encouraging households and schools to exercise together to the broadcast, and they became a lasting feature of Japanese daily life.

Reception varied by household and place. Families with their own sets listened at home, gathering around the receiver in the evening, and shops, cafés, and inns used radios to attract customers. Schools, village offices, and community halls provided shared listening in places where private receivers were rare, and the gathering of neighbors around a set for a special broadcast became a recognizable scene. The cost of licenses and receivers limited ownership, and subscriber numbers grew from a few hundred thousand in the mid-1920s to nearly a million by the early 1930s, so that for much of the decade a majority of households did not own a licensed set. Rural areas were underrepresented, because signals were weaker and incomes lower. As a result, radio's integration of the nation was partial, and its geography overlapped with the geography of urban and middle-class life.

The medium's cultural effects are hard to measure precisely. It increased access to music and information and gave national events a shared immediacy, and it encouraged the use of standardized speech by announcers, which later contributed to the spread of standard Japanese. It also competed with newspapers, theaters, and cinemas for audience attention, and it linked with other media: radio performers recorded for the gramophone, songs from films were broadcast, and magazines published program guides. But claims that radio instantly created a national community should be qualified by the evidence of limited ownership, regional disparity, and the persistence of local and oral cultures.

Section 70.30 · Division VI

Gramophone Records, Popular Songs, Jazz, and the Commercial Music Market

The gramophone predated radio in Japan and followed a different commercial logic. Where broadcasting was licensed, supervised, and financed by listener fees, records were sold in shops like other commodities, and the record companies competed for hits. Japanese branches of foreign firms, notably the companies that became Nippon Columbia and Nippon Victor, dominated the market by the late 1920s, and electrical recording introduced in the West in 1925 reached Japan through a 1927 Columbia technology partnership; domestically issued Japanese electrical recordings followed in 1928, improving sound quality and expanding the market. Records were purchased by households that could afford a gramophone and by cafés, which used them to attract customers, and the market included Western classical music, traditional Japanese music, comic monologues, and a growing category of popular songs. The term later applied to this genre, kayōkyoku, came into use over time.

The relationship among records, cinema, and radio shaped popular music. Songs were written for films, recorded for sale, and performed on the radio, and the same performers moved between media. The early example of a theme song linked to a stage play, such as a famous song of 1914 associated with a theatrical production of Tolstoy's Resurrection, had already shown the potential of cross-promotion. By the late 1920s, the practice was more systematic. Jazz, played by dance bands and heard on imported records and radio, influenced the sound of popular songs and gave rise to a distinct jazz culture in dance halls and cafés, though jazz in this period meant a broad range of dance and popular styles rather than the improvisational jazz that later fans would recognize.

The song Tokyo March (Tōkyō kōshinkyoku) of 1929 is the best-known case study. It was produced as part of a commercial campaign involving a novel by Kikuchi Kan serialized in the mass-circulation magazine King, a film adaptation by Nikkatsu directed by Mizoguchi Kenji, and a record on the Victor label with lyrics by Saijō Yaso, music by Nakayama Shinpei, and vocals by Satō Chiyako. The lyrics, in a lively march rhythm, describe a tour of fashionable Tokyo, naming districts such as Ginza, Marunouchi, and Asakusa and invoking the pleasures of café, dance, and the commuter railway. The record sold in very large numbers by the standards of the time, and the campaign demonstrated how a company could coordinate print, film, and recorded sound to create a popular success. The song also reveals the idealized image of Tokyo as a city of glamour, and its cheerful tone contrasts with the economic anxieties of 1929, when the early depression was beginning to be felt.

Section 70.31 · Division VII — Mass Leisure, Commercial Entertainment, and the Limits of Urban Modernity

Asakusa: Popular Theaters, Variety Entertainment, and Mass Amusement

If Ginza was the capital's fashionable promenade, Asakusa was its great popular playground. The district grew around the temple of Sensōji, whose precincts had hosted fairs, stalls, and shows since the Tokugawa period. In the late Meiji and Taishō years the entertainment zone known as Rokku, the "sixth district," filled with cinemas, theaters, variety halls, restaurants, shooting galleries, and small shops, and the ornate Ryōunkaku tower, a twelve-story landmark of the 1890s, dominated its skyline until the 1923 earthquake destroyed it. Asakusa Opera, a popular form of light opera and revue that flourished in the late 1910s, performed adaptations of European works and comic songs for a mass audience, and left a legacy of performers, songs, and styles that fed into later revues and comedy.

Asakusa's clientele was broad, and its social character differed from Ginza's. Visitors included working-class families from nearby districts, laborers, students, soldiers on leave, shopkeepers, and migrants from the countryside, as well as curious middle-class visitors and writers in search of urban color. Admission to cinemas and shows was inexpensive, and the district offered amusements for every purse. It also had a reputation for rougher entertainment, gambling, and prostitution at its edges, and writers fascinated by the "low" side of the city found material there. Kawabata Yasunari's serialized novel The Scarlet Gang of Asakusa (1929–1930), a literary evocation of the district's underworld, is one example, though it should be read as a fiction rather than a documentary account and belongs to the literary histories of Chapter 72.

After the earthquake, Asakusa recovered and reinvented itself. New cinemas and theaters were built, and in 1929 the Casino Folies, a revue company modeled on Parisian music-hall entertainment, opened in the district and became famous for its mixture of music, dance, sketches, and comedy. The comedian Enomoto Ken'ichi, known as Enoken, rose to fame in this setting. The revue culture of Asakusa reflected the district's pursuit of novelty and its appeal to an audience that valued fast-paced, inexpensive amusement. Observers coined the phrase "erotic grotesque nonsense" to characterize the sensational entertainment of the era, a label that captured the fascination with sensuality, the bizarre, and the absurd, and that historians such as Miriam Silverberg have examined as a window onto interwar mass culture.

Section 70.32 · Division VII

Osaka, Hanshin, Takarazuka, and Leisure Connected to Private Railways

The Osaka–Kobe region offered a distinct model of commercial leisure, built by railway entrepreneurs rather than by temple precinct merchants. Kobayashi Ichizō's Hankyu enterprise began in 1910 with a line from Osaka to Takarazuka and Minoo, then largely countryside. To create passenger demand, Kobayashi developed suburban housing, sold it with attractive financing, and built amusements at the end of the line. At Takarazuka, a hot-spring resort, the company opened pleasure facilities, including a swimming pool that proved a failure, and the ingenious conversion of its indoor pool into a stage led to the founding of an all-female chorus whose first performance took place in 1914. The troupe grew into the Takarazuka Revue, which staged musical shows with women in all roles, male and female, and developed a devoted audience, especially among women and girls. A large theater opened in 1924.

The revue's most celebrated early production, Mon Paris (1927), was a pioneering Western-style revue with spectacular staging and music, inspired by a trip to Europe by the director Kishida Tatsuya. It helped define the Takarazuka style, with its stylized romance, elaborate sets, and the tradition of women performing male roles. The revue demonstrates how a transport company created a cultural product to bring customers to its line, and how it built a female audience for whom the company created a new form of entertainment. It is also a source of interpretive debate: some observers have emphasized the company's promotion of a wholesome, education-oriented version of femininity under male management, while others stress the pleasures and opportunities it offered to performers and fans, and its unconventional play with gender roles.

Takarazuka was one element of a wider regional network. Private railways built amusement parks, zoos, hot-spring hotels, baseball fields, and shrines and temples as destinations, and sold excursion tickets and guidebooks. Kōshien Stadium, built by the Hanshin railway and opened in 1924, hosted the national middle-school baseball tournament, a major spectator event. Osaka's own commercial culture of Dōtonbori theaters, Shinsaibashi shops, and the cafés and dance halls of the south continued alongside. The Hanshin region thus fostered a leisure culture in which transport, real estate, retail, and entertainment were closely linked, and in which the suburban household and its excursions were central.

Section 70.33 · Division VII

Sports, Recreation, Parks, and the Commercialization of Free Time

Spectator and participatory sport expanded in the 1920s, tied to schools, companies, newspapers, and railways. Baseball, introduced in the Meiji period, became the most popular team sport. The national middle-school tournament, sponsored by the Osaka Asahi newspaper from 1915, moved to Kōshien Stadium in 1924, and the university league in Tokyo, the Big Six, drew large crowds, helped by newspaper coverage and, from 1927, radio. Jingū Stadium in Tokyo opened in 1926. Professional baseball in the form later familiar was established only in the 1930s, so the baseball of the 1920s was an amateur and collegiate culture. The relay race from Tokyo to Hakone, begun in 1920, became an annual university event followed by newspaper readers and, later, by radio listeners. At the Amsterdam Olympics of 1928, Japanese athletes won their first gold medals, and the sprinter and runner Hitomi Kinue won a silver medal in the women's 800 meters, a result widely publicized in the press.

Public parks, zoos, and recreation grounds provided less expensive settings for leisure. Hibiya Park, opened in 1903, Ueno Park, and other urban parks hosted concerts, exhibitions, and strolling, while temple and shrine festivals continued to draw crowds. Municipal authorities and reformers promoted outdoor recreation, hiking, and physical education as healthy alternatives to commercial vice, and youth associations organized group excursions. Hot-spring resorts and scenic spots drew tourists by rail, and guidebooks and railway advertising promoted travel as a pleasure. Commercial amusement parks, such as those built by railway companies, combined rides, gardens, and refreshments.

The commercialization of free time was shaped by work. Many factory workers had few holidays, and the long hours of shops and small businesses limited leisure for owners and employees alike. Salaried workers had more regular schedules and Sunday holidays, and the weekend excursion became a middle-class habit. Schools, companies, and neighborhood associations organized sports days, picnics, and trips that mixed leisure with discipline, and the newspapers that sponsored tournaments combined entertainment with promotion. Leisure was therefore both a market and a field of social management, and the question of who controlled it, whether commercial interests, the state, or voluntary groups, was open.

Section 70.34 · Division VII

Who Could Afford Modern Leisure? Work Hours, Wages, Gender, and Class

A comparison of five hypothetical urban residents clarifies the limits of consumer culture. A salaried commuter with a regular monthly salary and a Sunday holiday could afford occasional department-store visits, cinema outings, and a radio or gramophone, though the family would calculate carefully. A factory worker, whether a skilled machinist or a textile operative, typically worked long hours, often had only a few days off per month, and had wages that covered necessities, so a visit to a cinema or a café was an occasional treat rather than a routine. A café waitress might earn a variable income through tips, spend her days and nights in the commercial entertainment world, and have little leisure of her own. An affluent shopper, such as the wife of a company executive or a landowner, had both income and time, and was the customer the department stores most courted. A migrant day laborer, living in a cheap lodging house and working irregular hours, had very little disposable income and often faced discrimination in the entertainment venues he could not afford or was not welcomed in.

The costs of participation were considerable. A subway ride cost ten sen, a cup of coffee in a café cost a similar amount or more, a seat at a cheap cinema cost a few dozen sen, and a better seat could cost a yen or more, while the monthly radio fee was a yen or two. Taxis at a flat yen, introduced in the mid-1920s, were affordable only occasionally for most households. For a worker whose daily wage was in the range of one to two yen, these expenses mattered. Gender affected both income and time: women usually earned less and carried the main burden of housework, and married women with children had limited opportunities for evenings out. Age mattered too, as students and young unmarried workers often had more freedom to spend than family heads.

Housing and distance imposed further limits. A family living in a tenement in the eastern districts or in a distant suburb faced travel costs and time to reach Ginza or Asakusa, and commercial entertainment was more accessible to those living near the centers. Social norms also mattered: respectable women avoided some venues, and some entertainments were considered inappropriate for children or the poor. Thus consumer culture was graded by price and location, and its spread through society was uneven. This is why historians treat the modern city as a set of overlapping publics rather than a single mass audience.

Section 70.35 · Division VII

The Unequal and Imperial City: Migrants, Discrimination, Poverty, and Social Visibility

The modern city was also an imperial city. After Japan annexed Korea in 1910 and acquired Taiwan in 1895, colonial subjects traveled to the metropole as students, laborers, and migrants. Korean migration grew steeply in the 1920s, driven by poverty and displacement in Korea, including the pressures of colonial agricultural policy, and by Japanese labor demand. The number of Koreans in Japan rose from some tens of thousands around 1920 to several hundred thousand by 1930, depending on the source, and many settled in Osaka, which became the largest center, as well as in Tokyo, Kyoto, Kobe, and industrial districts. They worked in factories, construction, mines, and as day laborers, usually at lower wages than Japanese workers, and they were concentrated in poor neighborhoods where housing was substandard and landlords often refused to rent to them. Taiwanese students and some merchants also lived in Tokyo and other cities, and Chinese workers and merchants formed communities in ports such as Yokohama and Kobe.

Discrimination shaped access to the commercial city. Colonial migrants faced prejudice in employment, housing, and public life, and the violence against Koreans in the aftermath of the earthquake, treated in Chapter 71, revealed the vulnerability of these communities. Buraku communities, whose members were descended from outcast groups of the Tokugawa period, continued to face discrimination in marriage and employment despite formal legal equality, and many lived in segregated neighborhoods with poor housing and sanitation; the Suiheisha founded in 1922, discussed in Chapter 69, was a response to this exclusion. Poor urban residents in tenements and lodging houses, often described by social reformers, had little access to the amenities that advertisers celebrated. The visible city of Ginza and Marunouchi thus coexisted with a less visible city of cramped housing and low wages.

Commercial images tended to obscure these realities. Advertisements, films, and popular songs presented an affluent, cheerful, ethnically homogeneous modern city and rarely depicted the poor, the colonial migrants, or the minority communities who lived there. Retail and media enterprises also extended across the empire: Mitsukoshi established a subbranch office in Seoul in 1906, which was upgraded to a proper branch in 1929 and acquired a purpose-built department-store building in 1930, and Japanese films, records, and magazines circulated in the colonies, where they shaped urban tastes but also reinforced colonial hierarchies. Colonial cities had their own consumer cultures and their own intellectuals, writers, and entertainers, and their histories belong to the chapters on empire. The point here is that the metropolitan modern depended on and was embedded in an imperial economy, and that access to its pleasures was shaped by categories of ethnicity and subject status.

Section 70.36 · Division VIII — Interpretation, Contradictions, and Chapter Review

What Was Japanese Urban Modernity? Consumption, Identity, and Historical Debate

How should historians characterize the urban culture of the 1920s? One long-standing view treated it as Westernization: a superficial adoption of foreign fashions and technologies by an urban elite, atop a society whose deeper structures remained unchanged, and later swept away by nationalism. Another treated it as a prelude to the militarism of the 1930s, a period of excess that provoked reaction. Recent scholarship, in the line of Miriam Silverberg and others, rejects both views. It argues that interwar mass culture was a significant historical phenomenon in its own right, formed by commercial enterprises, performers, designers, workers, and audiences in Japan, that it drew on foreign models but transformed them, and that it did not simply anticipate any particular political outcome.

The meaning of consumer culture is also contested. Some interpret it as a source of liberation, expanding choices, offering new identities, and enabling women and young people to experiment. Others see it as a form of social discipline, in which commercial and state institutions shaped desires and behaviors, directing energy toward work and family. Others stress commercial construction, emphasizing the role of advertisers and publishers in creating images that served profit. These interpretations are not mutually exclusive. Consumer culture could open possibilities and constrain them, and the same café, magazine, or film could be liberating for some and exploitative for others. A further point is that consumer modernity did not necessarily produce political liberalism or economic equality: the same cities that supported cafés and cinemas produced the Peace Preservation Law and the growth of nationalist movements, and the prosperity of the consumer class rested on inequalities that persisted.

Placing this culture within the empire and the global economy adds complexity. Japanese retailers, filmmakers, and musicians were part of transnational networks of styles, technologies, and commodities, and Japanese consumer culture influenced the colonies and was influenced by Hollywood and European models. The question of originality, sometimes framed as whether Japanese mass culture was adaptation or innovation, depends on definitions: individual elements, such as the department store, the café, or jazz, had foreign antecedents, but their combination, their institutional forms, and their social meanings were distinctively Japanese in their urban context. The final chapters of this part will show how the economic crisis and political changes of the late 1920s and early 1930s tested and transformed these institutions.

Historical Debate

Japanese Mass Culture: Adaptation or Innovation?

The question: Was the mass culture of 1920s Japan primarily an adaptation of foreign models, or an innovation with its own character and significance?

Interpretation A — Selective adaptation of imported forms: Some scholars emphasize the foreign origins of many institutions and styles, including the department store, the café, Hollywood-influenced cinema, jazz, and Western clothing, and examine how Japanese enterprises borrowed, modified, and localized them. This view highlights transnational connections and the processes of translation and selection.
Interpretation B — Innovation within Japanese conditions: Others emphasize that the institutions that emerged were new combinations that arose from Japanese conditions: the railway-terminal department store, the all-female revue, the benshi-accompanied silent cinema, the cross-media marketing of songs and films, and the particular meanings of the modern girl. This view stresses that mass culture was created by Japanese actors for Japanese audiences and cannot be explained by imitation alone.

Evidence: Both views draw on business histories, company records, films, songs, magazines, and comparisons with other countries. Historians such as Miriam Silverberg have stressed that interwar mass culture should be studied as a significant development in its own right, while comparative studies of consumer culture in other societies help to identify what was distinctive. Many scholars now treat adaptation and innovation as aspects of one process.

Why it matters: The answer shapes how historians view the relationship between Japan and the West, the claim that modernity is simply Westernization, and the continuity between interwar and postwar popular culture.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Everyday Urban Experience, c. 1910 and c. 1930

What Changed

  • Shopping moved from negotiated, home-delivered transactions toward browsing in department stores and window shopping.
  • Electric railways, the subway, buses, and taxis widened the range of daily travel and created commuting suburbs.
  • Cafés, dance halls, cinemas, and revues provided new commercial settings for sociability.
  • Radio and records brought scheduled and recorded sound into homes and cafés.
  • Young women and men appeared in public in new ways, wearing Western clothing and moving through the city independently.

What Remained Continuous

  • Most women continued to wear kimono in public, and most households cooked and ate largely traditional meals.
  • Class and gender hierarchies, wage inequality, and cramped housing persisted.
  • State supervision of public morals, entertainment, and communications remained strong.
  • Rural life and many urban working-class neighborhoods changed more slowly than the fashionable centers.
  • Colonial and ethnic hierarchies shaped who could enter and enjoy the commercial city.

Who experienced change most directly? Salaried households, students, young urban women and men with money to spend, and entertainers and service workers in the new industries. Who experienced greater continuity? Farm families, factory workers confined by long hours, the urban poor, and colonial migrants. Why the pattern matters: It shows that modernity was a graded and selective experience, and that the visible face of the city understated the persistence of older constraints.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how urban modernity was experienced in everyday life in Japan between roughly 1918 and 1930. Its central argument is that the modern Japanese city transformed daily life by connecting mass commerce, new communications technologies, expanding leisure industries, and changing social identities, and that the modernity displayed in advertising, cinema, and fashionable districts was distributed unequally. It remained constrained by class, gender, occupation, region, and imperial hierarchy. The chapter therefore treated consumer culture as an economic and social system, not as a catalogue of fashionable goods.

The setting was a society still largely rural but with a growing urban minority. The wartime boom expanded employment in industry, commerce, and administration, created a visible salaried middle class, and drew migrants to Tokyo, Osaka, and other cities. Osaka's census population passed Tokyo's in 1925, though boundary changes complicate the comparison. Districts acquired distinct identities: Marunouchi as an office quarter, Nihonbashi as a merchant core, Ginza as a promenade, Asakusa as a popular playground, and the western terminals as emerging centers. Electric railways, private suburban lines, buses, taxis, and the 1927 subway altered the experience of distance, and the 1923 earthquake accelerated suburbanization and modern building.

Department stores were the most visible commercial innovation. Mitsukoshi's 1904 declaration announced a new business model, and its 1914 Nihonbashi building embodied it. Stores offered fixed prices, browsing, restaurants, exhibitions, and rooftop attractions, and after the earthquake several adopted shoes-on shopping. Competitors converted from merchant houses, while the railway companies, led by Hankyu's 1929 terminal store, developed a second model that tied retail to commuter flows. Advertising, posters, slogans, and magazines such as Shufu no Tomo constructed an image of the modern consumer, but historians must read these sources as evidence of commercial aspiration rather than of actual purchasing, since household budgets limited most families' access to advertised goods.

Cafés, restaurants, and nightlife created new spaces of commercial sociability. The early café movement of 1911, represented by Printemps, was small and artistic; the later café boom was far larger and more varied. Café waitresses, paid largely through tips, were both a significant category of female labor and a heavily sexualized symbol, and police and moralists sought to regulate them. Yōshoku dishes, beer halls, and Chinese-style noodle shops illustrated adaptation rather than simple Westernization, while dance halls and jazz venues grew mainly in the later 1920s and were subject to licensing and moral campaigns.

The modern girl and modern boy were media types as much as social groups. Some young urban women adopted short hair, Western dress, and independent movement through the city, but the great majority of women continued to wear kimono, and Kon Wajirō's Ginza survey showed how rare women's Western dress remained. Working women in shops, offices, telephone exchanges, and cafés gained wages but faced discrimination and surveillance. Debates about the modern generation reflected anxieties about marriage, sexuality, consumption, and national identity. Historians such as Miriam Silverberg have argued that the modern girl was both a recognizable presence and a commercial construction.

Cinema matured into a commercial industry in the 1920s. Nikkatsu and Shōchiku, with studios in Tokyo and Kyoto, produced period and contemporary dramas; actresses replaced onnagata in many productions; filmmakers experimented with new techniques; and genres such as the shōshimin eiga reflected the concerns of the lower middle class. The benshi remained central to the silent film experience. Theaters, fan magazines, and star publicity tied cinema to other media, while Hollywood imports and Home Ministry censorship shaped the market. Sound cinema, marked by Shōchiku's 1931 film, lay at the chapter's chronological edge.

Radio and records changed the soundscape. Provisional public radio broadcasting began on March 22, 1925, in Tokyo (regular service followed in July), followed by Osaka and Nagoya, and in 1926 the three stations merged into NHK under government supervision, financed by listener fees rather than advertising. Programming reorganized daily routines through news, lectures, music, sports, and calisthenics, though ownership remained limited and uneven. Gramophone records, dominated by foreign-affiliated companies, expanded after electrical recording, and the 1929 campaign for Tokyo March showed how songs, films, and print could be marketed together.

Mass leisure took diverse forms. Asakusa offered inexpensive cinema, variety, and revue to a broad public, and its character differed sharply from Ginza's. In the Hanshin region, private railways created leisure empires of suburbs, terminals, stadiums, and the Takarazuka Revue. Baseball, relay races, and parks expanded sport and recreation. Yet who could afford these pleasures depended on wages, working hours, gender, housing, and location. Colonial migrants, Buraku communities, and the urban poor lived in the same cities but with far less access, and commercial images largely omitted them.

Historians interpret this culture in several ways: as Westernization, as a prelude to militarism, as liberation, as social discipline, or as commercial construction. The chapter's position is that mass culture was a significant development in its own right, that it combined adaptation and innovation, and that it expanded possibilities without eliminating inequality or guaranteeing political liberalism. These developments set the stage for the earthquake's social consequences in Chapter 71, the artistic experiments of Chapter 72, and the economic and political crises of Chapters 73 and 74.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how modernity entered ordinary life: through the fare paid for a subway ride, the afternoon spent in a department store, the café where a waitress worked for tips, the film watched with a benshi's narration, and the radio voice that reached a household at a fixed hour. These habits changed how millions of people understood time, space, desire, and community, and they did so through commercial institutions rather than through political programs.

The chapter also reveals how unevenly modernity was distributed. The glittering Ginza of advertisements coexisted with tenements, dormitories, lodging houses, and segregated neighborhoods, and the consumers celebrated in magazines were a minority. By keeping workers, women, colonial migrants, and the urban poor in view, the chapter resists a story in which "the Japanese people" became modern together. It also shows that consumer pleasure and state supervision developed side by side, from film censorship to the organization of NHK, and that commercial modernity did not guarantee political liberalism.

Later developments drew on these foundations. The railway-based model of retail and leisure became central to postwar urban Japan, as later chapters show; the star system, cross-media marketing, and popular-song industry anticipated postwar entertainment; and the debates over youth, gender, and consumption reappeared in new forms. At the same time, the economic crises of Chapters 73 and 74 and the wartime mobilization of Chapters 75 through 80 would constrain and redirect this culture.

Finally, the chapter connects Japan to a wider world of exchange. Hollywood films, European revues, American record companies, and Western retail models all reached Japan, but they were reshaped by Japanese entrepreneurs, performers, and audiences, and Japanese styles in turn circulated through the empire. Students who understand urban culture as contested, graded, and open-ended are prepared to approach the earthquake and its aftermath in Chapter 71 and the cultural experiments of Chapter 72 without assuming that the outcome was fixed.
