Japan and the First World War

Japanese History: From Jōmon Japan to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

In the Kalkara Naval Cemetery on the island of Malta, a memorial commemorates Japanese sailors who died there in 1917. They had come some ten thousand kilometers from home to protect British and Allied troopships from submarines in the Mediterranean. In the same war, other Japanese soldiers dug siege lines outside a German-leased port on the Shandong coast of China, and Japanese warships moved through the German colonial islands of Micronesia. In Tokyo and Osaka, meanwhile, ship owners, textile exporters, and bankers were making fortunes, while many wage earners found their pay failing to keep up with the price of rice.

These scenes belong to a single historical episode, yet they point in very different directions. Japan fought the First World War as one of the Allied powers, but it did not fight the war that Britain, France, Germany, and Russia fought. Its armies never entered the trenches of the Western Front, and its civilian population did not endure the casualties, bombardment, blockade, or mass conscription that transformed European societies. The war nonetheless changed Japan profoundly. It enlarged Japan's territorial reach in the Pacific, strengthened its position in northern China, turned the country from a heavy debtor into a creditor for the first time in the modern era, expanded its shipyards, steelworks, and chemical plants, and gave Japanese diplomats a seat among the powers that drew up the postwar settlement in Paris. At the same time it intensified Chinese resistance to Japanese influence, raised suspicion in the United States and the British dominions, and generated the inflation and social strain that exploded in the Rice Riots of 1918.

This chapter therefore treats the war as four things at once: a military engagement, an imperial opportunity, an economic turning point, and a diplomatic transformation. Its chronology runs principally from 1914 to 1919, with necessary background in the Anglo-Japanese Alliance and carefully bounded references to consequences after 1919. The organizing question is not simply what Japan did during the war, but how Japanese leaders, businesses, soldiers, sailors, workers, and farmers, and also Chinese officials and students, Russian villagers, Pacific islanders, and Allied diplomats, experienced and shaped the decisions that Japan made. Japan's entry in August 1914 was carried out within an alliance framework, but it was also an act of Japanese policy. Understanding the difference between the two is one of the chapter's first tasks.

The chapter also asks students to keep several peoples visible. China is not merely the arena in which Japanese diplomacy operated. Chinese officials negotiated, delayed, leaked documents, appealed to Britain and the United States, and ultimately organized a nationalist protest that would reshape Chinese politics. The Chamorro, Carolinian, Palauan, Yapese, Marshallese, and other peoples of Micronesia passed from German to Japanese military control without being asked. Russian civilians in the Far East and Siberia encountered Japanese troops whose stated purpose changed repeatedly. Korea and Taiwan, already Japanese colonies, supplied rice, sugar, and labor to the imperial economy, and Korea's own national movement erupted in March 1919 while Japan was claiming equality at the Paris conference. Okinawa, Hokkaido, and the Ainu communities were also affected, though the surviving evidence on their wartime experience is thinner than for the great trading cities, and the chapter says so where that is the case.

The evidence is rich but must be handled with care. Diplomatic archives preserve the August 1914 ultimatum, the text of the Twenty-One Demands, the Chinese and Japanese cases at Paris, and the Lansing–Ishii notes. Naval and army histories, including those compiled by Japan's own defense institutions, document the campaigns. Customs and financial statistics trace the boom, but they were compiled in nominal yen during a period of rapid price change and cannot be read at face value. Newspapers, diaries, and the memoirs of statesmen show how events were understood, but they also show censorship, self-interest, and hindsight. Chinese sources, including the protest literature of 1915 and 1919, record a perspective that Japanese official sources often marginalize. The chapter will identify, section by section, whose view each kind of evidence represents.

Chapter 66 stands at a hinge in the textbook. It follows the account of Japan's rise to great-power status in Chapters 60 and 61, the colonial consolidation described in Chapter 62, and the industrial and cultural changes of Chapters 63 to 65. It sets up the political history of Chapters 67 to 69, which examine Taishō democracy, the Rice Riots, and the labor and suffrage movements, as well as the economic instability of Chapter 73 and the military and imperial crises of Part XI. The chapter should not become a general history of Taishō Japan. Its subject is the war and the consequences of Japan's participation in it.

By the end of the chapter, students should be able to explain why 1919 can legitimately be described as a moment of Japanese diplomatic triumph, and why it can equally be described as the beginning of deeper contradictions. Japan emerged from the war with greater industrial and international power. It also faced a more hostile Chinese public, wary Western partners, a dissatisfied domestic population, and the unresolved tension between its claim to equality among nations and its own imperial hierarchy.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why Japan entered the First World War in August 1914, distinguishing alliance obligations from Japanese strategic and political objectives.
  2. Trace Japanese military and naval operations at Qingdao, in the German Pacific islands, in the Pacific and Indian Oceans, and in the Mediterranean, and assess their strategic significance.
  3. Distinguish the original Twenty-One Demands from the revised proposals of April 1915 and the agreements actually signed in May 1915.
  4. Analyze Chinese responses to Japanese pressure, including the diplomacy of Yuan Shikai, the boycotts of 1915, and the May Fourth Movement of 1919.
  5. Evaluate the shift from military and ultimatum diplomacy toward financial influence under Terauchi Masatake, including the Nishihara Loans.
  6. Interpret wartime trade, industrial, and financial statistics accurately, distinguishing nominal from real values and business profits from general prosperity.
  7. Compare how factory workers, women, farmers, tenants, merchants, and industrialists experienced the wartime economy.
  8. Explain the origins and course of the Siberian Intervention and why Japanese forces remained after the 1918 armistice.
  9. Assess the Shandong settlement, the South Seas Mandate, and the racial equality proposal as episodes in the postwar order, noting the legal arguments made by Japan and China.
  10. Contextualize Japan's wartime expansion within the longer history of Japanese imperialism and the unequal treaty system in East Asia.
  11. Evaluate competing historical interpretations of whether the war strengthened Japan's long-term position or created strategic contradictions.
  12. Connect the wartime economy to the social tensions explored in Chapters 67 to 69 and 73.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Did Japan enter the war because of the Anglo-Japanese Alliance, or did the alliance provide a framework for decisions that Japanese leaders made for their own reasons?
  2. How did the military campaigns of 1914 create the legal and political conditions for the diplomatic disputes over Shandong and the Pacific islands?
  3. In what ways were the Twenty-One Demands continuous with earlier imperial practice in China, and in what ways did they represent a new level of ambition?
  4. How did Chinese officials, merchants, students, and intellectuals respond to Japanese pressure, and how did those responses reshape the relationship between the two countries?
  5. Why did the wartime boom produce both extraordinary profits and severe hardship, and what does that coexistence reveal about the structure of the Japanese economy?
  6. What were the competing Japanese, American, Russian, and Czechoslovak aims in the Siberian Intervention, and why did Japan's participation last far longer than that of its allies?
  7. What did Japan gain and fail to gain at the Paris Peace Conference, and how should historians weigh the Shandong settlement against the rejection of the racial equality proposal?
  8. How should historians evaluate a diplomatic position in which Japan sought equality among the great powers while exercising imperial control over Korea, Taiwan, and newly occupied territories?

Historical Context

By 1914, Japan was a constitutional monarchy with a modern navy, a conscript army, an industrializing economy, and an overseas empire. Earlier chapters have traced the process. The First Sino-Japanese War of 1894–1895 brought Taiwan under Japanese rule (Chapter 60). The Russo-Japanese War of 1904–1905 secured Japanese predominance in Korea, transferred Russia's lease of the Liaodong peninsula, including Port Arthur and Dalian, and the southern branch of the Manchurian railway to Japan, and brought the southern half of Sakhalin (Karafuto) under Japanese control (Chapter 61). Korea became a protectorate in 1905 and was annexed in 1910 (Chapter 62). The Ryukyu Kingdom had been absorbed into Japan as Okinawa Prefecture in 1879, and Hokkaido, once known as Ezo, had been colonized and settled during the Meiji era, with severe consequences for the Ainu. Japan in 1914 was therefore already an imperial power, with territories and economic interests on the Asian mainland, long before the war began.

Japanese foreign policy was shaped by alliances and rivalries. The Anglo-Japanese Alliance, first signed in 1902 and renewed in 1905 and 1911, tied Japan to Britain, the dominant naval power. After 1905, Japan also reached a series of understandings with Russia, its former adversary, and with France, and it maintained uneasy relations with the United States, where anti-Japanese immigration measures, especially in California, caused resentment in Japan. China, meanwhile, had undergone the Revolution of 1911 and the replacement of the Qing dynasty by a republic whose president, Yuan Shikai, governed a weak and divided state. These conditions made China the principal field of Japanese ambition and of great-power competition alike.

Domestic politics were unsettled. The Taishō Political Crisis of 1912–1913, when the army's refusal to supply a minister helped to topple a cabinet and public protest then forced out a successor, had shown the weakness of the genrō (the surviving Meiji oligarchs) and the growing power of political parties and public opinion (see Chapters 59 and 67). In early 1914, a naval bribery scandal brought down another government. The cabinet that took office in April 1914 under Ōkuma Shigenobu, a veteran statesman who had founded Waseda University and led an earlier party cabinet in 1898, included Katō Takaaki as foreign minister. Japan's finances were strained. Years of foreign borrowing to pay for the empire had left large external debts, and the country usually imported more than it exported. Economic historians have estimated the debt at around ¥1.9 billion on the eve of the war, a very large share of it owed abroad. Chapter 66 begins from this condition of imperial confidence combined with financial vulnerability.

The chapter draws on several kinds of evidence. Diplomatic documents, including the published records of the United States, Britain, and Japan, preserve the text of ultimatums, notes, and agreements. Military and naval histories, such as those produced by Japan's National Institute for Defense Studies, give operational detail. Economic evidence comes from customs returns, bank records, and price and wage series, many compiled after the fact. Chinese memorials, newspapers, and student declarations record the nationalist reaction. Memoirs and diaries, including the diary of the politician Hara Takashi, illuminate decision-making but reflect their authors' purposes. Historians have also produced a substantial scholarship, among which Frederick Dickinson's study of Japan's wartime "national reinvention," Xu Guoqi's work on China and the Great War, Naoko Shimazu's study of the racial equality proposal, Paul Dunscomb's account of the Siberian Intervention, and Michael Lewis's study of the 1918 riots are representative. The chapter has been written to draw on this body of evidence while noting where it disagrees.

Division I · Section 66.1

Japan in 1914: Great-Power Ambitions and the International Order

Japan's international position in the summer of 1914 was the product of two decades of war, diplomacy, and colonial expansion. The victory over Qing China in 1895 had brought Taiwan and opened Korea to Japanese influence. The victory over Russia in 1905 had been far more consequential. It established Japan as the first non-Western power to defeat a European great power in modern war, secured recognition of Japan's predominant position in Korea, and transferred to Japan Russia's leasehold on the southern tip of the Liaodong peninsula, the Kwantung Leased Territory, along with the southern branch of the Chinese Eastern Railway. That railway became the South Manchuria Railway Company, founded in 1906, which was far more than a transport enterprise. It also ran coal mines, ports, hotels, research bureaus, and a railway zone policed by Japanese forces, making it one of the principal instruments of Japanese economic and strategic penetration of Manchuria. Chapters 60 and 61 describe these developments in detail; here they matter as the foundation of what Japanese leaders in 1914 regarded as their legitimate continental interests.

The empire that Japan governed in 1914 was larger and more varied than a map of the four main islands suggests. Taiwan had been a colony for nearly twenty years. Karafuto, the southern half of Sakhalin, had been Japanese since 1905, and the Ainu and other peoples who lived there had experienced displacement under both Russian and Japanese rule. Korea had been annexed in August 1910 after a five-year protectorate, and Korean resistance, including the guerrilla "righteous armies" suppressed between 1907 and 1911, had been met with force (Chapter 62). The Ryukyu Islands had been Okinawa Prefecture since 1879, and Hokkaido had been systematically settled since the 1870s, with devastating consequences for Ainu land and livelihood. When this chapter speaks of "Japan" in 1914, it means a state that governed a multiethnic and unequal empire, not a homogeneous nation-state.

Militarily, Japan possessed a large conscript army, which maintained roughly seventeen to nineteen active divisions in this period, and a modern navy built largely on British designs and, increasingly, on domestic shipbuilding. The navy's battle fleet had been strengthened since 1905 by dreadnought-type battleships and, in 1913, the battlecruiser Kongō, built in Britain. The army and navy were both semi-autonomous institutions. Under the Meiji Constitution, the service chiefs had direct access to the emperor, and the army and navy ministers had to be serving officers, which allowed the services to influence or destroy cabinets, as the events of 1912 had shown. Competition between the army, with its focus on the continent, and the navy, with its interest in the Pacific and in budgets for capital ships, would shape Japan's wartime choices.

Japan's relations with other powers were mixed. With Britain it was an ally. With Russia, once its enemy, it had by 1907 and later negotiated agreements dividing spheres of interest in Manchuria and Mongolia, and the two states would strengthen that relationship in 1916. With France it had reached an understanding in 1907. With the United States relations were more difficult. The American acquisition of the Philippines and the stated American commitment to an "Open Door" in China, meaning equal commercial access, collided with Japanese efforts to secure preferred positions in Manchuria. In addition, the treatment of Japanese immigrants, notably the school segregation controversy in San Francisco in 1906 and the California Alien Land Law of 1913, gave Japanese newspapers and politicians a sense of racial slight. With China, the situation was fluid. The Qing collapsed in 1911–1912, Yuan Shikai became president, and a weak Beijing government lacked the capacity to resist foreign demands.

Japan was thus already a significant regional power before 1914, with colonies, a substantial navy, and an alliance with the world's leading naval power. It was also financially fragile, internally divided about the relationship between the elected Diet and the services, and uncertain whether the great powers would continue to treat it as an equal. These tensions are the starting point for understanding what the outbreak of war in Europe meant to Japanese leaders. It was a crisis, but also an opportunity: for the first time in a decade, the European powers that had checked Japan's ambitions in East Asia were turning their attention elsewhere.

Division I · Section 66.2

The Anglo-Japanese Alliance and the European Crisis

The Anglo-Japanese Alliance was the central diplomatic instrument linking Japan to the European war. It was first signed in London on 30 January 1902, at a time when Britain wished to reduce the burden of defending its Asian interests against Russia and Japan wished to secure support against Russian pressure in Korea and Manchuria. The original treaty recognized British interests in China and Japanese interests in China and Korea, and provided that if either signatory became involved in war with two or more powers, the other would assist. The alliance was renewed and strengthened in August 1905, during the final stage of the Russo-Japanese War. It was extended to cover India, Britain recognized Japan's paramount political, military, and economic interests in Korea, and each party would help the other if attacked by even a single power in the area covered by the treaty. The third version, signed in July 1911, was altered at British insistence to exclude any obligation to fight a country with which either party had a general arbitration treaty, a provision intended to make it impossible for the alliance to apply against the United States.

Britain's motives for maintaining the alliance changed over time. In 1902 and 1905 it provided protection against Russia. By 1911, as Britain concentrated its battle fleet in the North Sea against Germany, the alliance allowed it to rely on Japanese naval power in the Pacific and the Indian Ocean. The British dominions were less convinced. Australia, New Zealand, and Canada, with strong anxieties about Asian immigration and Japanese naval strength, regarded the alliance with some suspicion and wished to keep Japanese influence away from the Pacific territories near them. British diplomats in Tokyo and in China also worried that a Japan freed from European observation would press its advantages in Manchuria and Shandong.

When Europe went to war at the end of July 1914, the alliance's formal terms did not compel Japanese entry. The treaty obliged each party to come to the other's aid when attacked in the region it covered, which meant East Asia and India. Germany had not attacked Britain's possessions in Asia. Britain declared war on Germany on 4 August 1914. On 7 August, the British government asked Japan for limited assistance, specifically help in hunting down the German armed merchant cruisers that threatened British commerce in Chinese waters. London soon began to regret the request, since a Japanese declaration of war could be used to justify Japanese operations beyond that narrow purpose, and on 11 August it tried to withdraw or limit the request. The Japanese government, however, had by then concluded that limited aid was not enough, and Foreign Minister Katō Takaaki insisted that Japan must either decide to act fully as an ally or not at all. Britain, having already asked, could not easily refuse. This sequence matters. It shows that Japan's participation was neither a simple discharge of alliance duty nor a simple Japanese seizure, but the result of British need, British hesitation, and Japanese determination.

The European crisis also affected Japanese perceptions at a deeper level. The Great War was a general European conflict in which the powers that had been the arbiters of Asian affairs were suddenly fighting each other. Japanese journalists and officials quickly called it the Ōshū Taisen, the "Great European War," a name that reflected the way many in Japan understood it: as a European conflict in which Japan chose how far to participate. The language of alliance loyalty was used publicly, and Japanese leaders repeatedly appealed to it, but it was accompanied from the beginning by the calculation that victory by the Allies would settle the future of German interests in East Asia and that Japan should be present when it did.

Historical Vocabulary
Alliance, Neutrality, Leased Territory, and Sphere of Influence

An alliance is a treaty commitment to assist another state under specified conditions. It is not a blanket promise of automatic war: its terms define when obligations arise, which is why the wording of the Anglo-Japanese Alliance, and whether the circumstances of 1914 fell within it, matters to understanding Japan's decision. Neutrality is a legal status of non-participation in a war, with rights and duties under international law. China proclaimed neutrality in 1914, and Japanese operations in Shandong therefore raised questions of Chinese rights that Japanese and Chinese officials interpreted differently.

A leased territory was an area that a Chinese government had been compelled by treaty to lease to a foreign power for a fixed term (Germany at Jiaozhou in 1898, Russia and later Japan at Lüshun and Dalian, Britain at Weihaiwei and the New Territories of Hong Kong). In law, sovereignty was said to remain with China, while the foreign power exercised administration. In practice, as the Shandong dispute demonstrates, lessees behaved as though they held something close to sovereign power. A sphere of influence was a region in which a foreign power claimed preferential economic and political rights, often without formal legal basis, and which it sought to protect against rivals. Historians use the term for the informal division of China among the powers, and the Twenty-One Demands may be understood as an attempt to turn Japan's de facto sphere in southern Manchuria and Shandong into something more permanent.

Division I · Section 66.3

German Interests in East Asia: Jiaozhou, Qingdao, and the Pacific Islands

Germany's East Asian interests in 1914 consisted of a leased territory on the Shandong coast, a set of commercial and railway rights on the Shandong peninsula, and a scattering of Pacific island colonies. The leased territory, in the region around Jiaozhou (Kiaochow) Bay, had its origins in November 1897, when German forces occupied the area after the killing of two German Catholic missionaries in Shandong. A convention signed in March 1898 gave Germany a ninety-nine-year lease over the bay and its surrounding land, together with railway and mining concessions in the province. The leased territory was legally part of China, but the German imperial navy, not the foreign office, administered it. At its center was the port of Qingdao (Tsingtao), which Germany built into a modern city and a naval station. It also became the home port of the German East Asia Squadron, built a European-style grid of streets, a university-level school, and a brewery that remains one of its best-known products.

The Shandong Railway, completed in 1904, linked Qingdao to the provincial capital Jinan and extended German commercial influence inland. The railway was operated by a German company, which also controlled mining concessions along the line. German Shandong was therefore a combination of colonial enclave and economic penetration. The distinction between the two is critical for this chapter. The leased territory around Jiaozhou Bay was a defined area under German administration. The railway and mining rights were commercial and legal privileges held within Chinese territory. When Japan captured Qingdao in 1914, it could argue that it had conquered a German possession. It had a much weaker case that it had automatically inherited the rights that Germany had obtained by treaty from China, and Chinese diplomats argued exactly that point from 1915 onward. Chinese Shandong, in other words, remained Chinese.

In the Pacific, Germany held islands that it had acquired largely from Spain in 1899 after the Spanish-American War, when Madrid sold the Mariana Islands (except Guam, which the United States took), the Caroline Islands, and Palau for a sum of money. The Marshall Islands had been a German protectorate since 1885. Together these northern Pacific possessions, usually called Micronesia, spanned a very large area of ocean but a small land area. Their inhabitants included Chamorro and Carolinian peoples in the Marianas, Palauans, Yapese, the peoples of Chuuk (Truk), Pohnpei, and Kosrae, and Marshallese, and they had been integrated into a German colonial economy based on copra and phosphate. Their experience of German rule was not uniformly peaceful: the German administration suppressed a Sokehs rebellion on Pohnpei in 1910–1911. Further south, Germany held northeastern New Guinea, parts of the Solomon Islands, Nauru, and Samoa. The Pacific islands also had strategic value. The island of Yap was an important node in the world's cable network, and German wireless stations on Yap, Nauru, and elsewhere allowed communication with German warships.

The German East Asia Squadron, commanded by Vice-Admiral Maximilian von Spee, was the military instrument that made all these possessions dangerous to the Allies. Its armored cruisers Scharnhorst and Gneisenau, together with light cruisers, could threaten British trade, troop convoys from Australia and New Zealand, and Allied possessions across the Pacific. For this reason, and not just for territorial ambition, destroying or neutralizing Germany's Asian naval and communications bases mattered to Britain, France, and Russia. It also explains why, in August 1914, a British request for Japanese help was credible rather than merely a pretext.

Division I · Section 66.4

Japan's Decision for War: Ōkuma, Katō, and the August 1914 Ultimatum

The government that decided Japan's response was led by Ōkuma Shigenobu, then seventy-six years old. Ōkuma had been a leading figure of the Meiji era, twice foreign minister and, in 1898, the head of Japan's first party cabinet. He had returned to office in April 1914 after the Siemens scandal, in which revelations of bribery in naval procurement brought down the preceding cabinet of Yamamoto Gonnohyōe. His support in the Diet came chiefly from the Rikken Dōshikai, the party founded by Katsura Tarō in 1913 and later led by Katō Takaaki, who held the foreign ministry. Katō, a former ambassador in London and a believer in an Anglo-Japanese partnership and in constitutional government, was determined to centralize decisions in the cabinet and the foreign ministry and to reduce the long-standing influence of the genrō over foreign policy. The two men, along with the navy and army ministers, were the main actors in the decision.

On 8 August 1914, Katō told the cabinet that Japan should enter the war on Britain's side. The cabinet agreed, and the genrō, who were consulted only after the fact, expressed varying degrees of unease about the procedure. Yamagata Aritomo and others criticized Katō for deciding without them, although they generally accepted the outcome, and the genrō Inoue Kaoru reportedly welcomed the crisis as a gift from providence that Japan should exploit to secure its position in East Asia. Japanese officials, including the navy, wanted to seize Germany's Pacific islands and the army and foreign ministry wanted to eliminate German power in Shandong. Katō himself argued that Japan, if it stayed out, would be isolated when the postwar settlement was made and could lose the benefits of the alliance.

On 15 August, Japan sent Germany an ultimatum, copied to Allied governments. It demanded that Germany immediately withdraw its warships from Chinese and Japanese waters or disarm them, and that it deliver the entire leased territory of Jiaozhou to Japan by 15 September "with a view to eventual restoration to China." No reply was received by the deadline of noon on 23 August, and Japan declared war on Germany that day by imperial rescript. Two days later, because the Austro-Hungarian cruiser Kaiserin Elisabeth remained at Qingdao, Japan also declared war on Austria-Hungary. Britain's final position was a compromise: it accepted a Japanese declaration but sought assurances that operations would be confined to the Far East and would not extend to the Pacific islands. Japan in practice read the limitation flexibly, and its navy moved against the German islands north of the equator in October.

Two features of the ultimatum deserve attention. First, it was addressed to Germany but was clearly also a message to China and the other powers about Japanese intentions. It did not state any time by which the territory would be returned, and the phrase "eventual restoration to China" was ambiguous as to conditions. Second, by demanding the whole territory of Jiaozhou, the ultimatum implied that Japan's subsequent war aims in Shandong would be larger than anything required to protect shipping from German raiders. In China, Yuan Shikai's government proclaimed neutrality and sought to confine hostilities to a defined zone, but it was in no position to prevent Japan from landing troops where it wished.

Historians have noted that the ultimatum gave Germany no realistic chance of compliance, and that its timing and language were designed to secure legal justification for war. Chinese officials later interpreted the phrase on restoration cautiously; Japanese statements in 1915 and 1919 about conditional restoration must be read against it.

Division I · Section 66.5

The Meaning of Participation: Alliance, Security, Expansion, and Opportunity

Historians have long debated how to weigh the motives behind Japan's decision. The evidence suggests that several motives coexisted and were held in different proportions by different actors. Katō Takaaki and his diplomats emphasized the value of the Anglo-Japanese partnership, Japan's standing as a great power, and the desirability of eliminating German power in the Far East, thereby preventing any future German revival and ending a potential strategic threat. The navy saw an opportunity to take Micronesian islands, which lay on the sea routes between Japan and the American Philippines, Guam, and Hawai'i, and which the navy regarded as strategically precious. Army leaders were interested in continental questions, and some officers and politicians regarded the war as a rare opportunity to settle the position of Japan in China. Elder statesmen such as Yamagata were wary of Katō's methods but shared an interest in making sure that Japan was among the winners.

British officials were ambivalent. London needed Japanese naval support and valued Japanese goodwill, yet it also distrusted Japanese intentions toward China and the Pacific. The British ambassador and Foreign Secretary Sir Edward Grey tried to hold Japan to a narrow role, but they could not prevent the larger one. Australian and New Zealand leaders were especially uneasy, and Australian Prime Minister William Hughes would later object forcefully to Japanese retention of the islands. American officials watched with suspicion. Chinese officials, for their part, understood that Japan's entry meant that the war would be fought on Chinese soil without Chinese consent. According to some accounts, Chinese leaders considered joining the Allies in 1914, in part to forestall Japanese claims, but Japan and Britain discouraged the idea, and China instead proclaimed neutrality.

The Japanese public's reaction was largely supportive. Newspapers praised the government's actions, commentators saw an opportunity for national advancement, and the fall of Qingdao in November would be celebrated with lantern processions. Some commentators, including liberals who generally favored parliamentary government, nonetheless worried that the government's China policy would alienate Chinese opinion. In general, public discussion of the war in Japan focused on opportunity and national prestige more than on the suffering that was beginning to be reported from Europe.

The most useful way to interpret Japanese participation is therefore to separate three questions that are often confused. Was Japan fulfilling an alliance obligation? In the strict legal sense, no: the alliance did not require entry into a war that did not involve attack on Britain's Asian possessions, although Japan acted at British request and within the alliance framework. Did Japan act purely out of opportunism? That claim also oversimplifies, since Japanese leaders did have genuine concerns about the postwar order and about Japan's relations with Britain. Did Japan's pursuit of additional advantages undermine its claim to be acting for the Allied cause? To the Chinese, and to many in the United States and the British dominions, it did. Japan's combination of legitimate alliance participation with the pursuit of additional regional advantages is the thread that runs through the rest of the chapter.

Division II · Section 66.6

The Campaign against Qingdao: Japanese and British Forces in Shandong

The siege of Qingdao was the clearest large-scale land operation that Japan undertook in the First World War. Its military geography shaped everything about it. The German leased territory occupied the south side of the entrance to Jiaozhou Bay on the southern coast of the Shandong peninsula. The city lay on a peninsula at the bay's mouth, backed by a ring of hills, including the Laoshan range to the east, which the Germans had fortified with a series of forts, redoubts, and entrenchments. The garrison consisted of about four thousand German marines and reservists, together with the crew of an Austro-Hungarian cruiser and some smaller naval vessels. The governor and commander, Captain Alfred Meyer-Waldeck, had little hope of relief. The German East Asia Squadron, under von Spee, had left for the Pacific before the war began and could not return.

The Japanese plan was to land well outside the leased territory, advance overland, and besiege the fortress. Command of the land forces was given to Lieutenant-General Kamio Mitsuomi, whose Independent 18th Division, with attached units, numbered about twenty-three thousand men. The Japanese Second Fleet, under Vice-Admiral Katō Sadakichi, blockaded the port from the sea. The first major landing took place on 2 September 1914 at Longkou, on the north coast of the Shandong peninsula, more than a hundred and fifty kilometers from Qingdao and well outside the leased territory. Japanese troops then advanced across Chinese territory in difficult weather. Heavy rains and flooding slowed the march and complicated supply. A British contingent of about 1,500 men (principally the 2nd Battalion of the South Wales Borderers and half of the 36th Sikhs, under Brigadier-General Nathaniel Barnardiston) joined the Japanese in late September, a token of Allied cooperation which also served the political purpose of presenting the operation as a joint one.

The legal and political significance of the route was substantial. China had proclaimed its neutrality and had attempted to limit military operations to a defined zone near the bay. Japanese forces went well beyond that zone, landing at Longkou, marching through the interior, and taking control of the Shandong Railway as far as Jinan. The Chinese government protested, but it lacked the means to resist. The occupation of the railway and the dispersal of Japanese troops through Chinese towns established a pattern that Chinese commentators would later cite: a belligerent had entered Chinese territory beyond the war zone and then claimed, as a consequence of victory, rights in that territory. For the people of Shandong, the campaign meant requisitions, billeting, the use of porters and laborers, and damage from flooding and military movement, though local records of these experiences are much less accessible than the official campaign histories.

By mid-October, Japanese forces were in position outside the German lines. The siege proceeded by classical methods, with trenches advanced toward the German forts under cover of artillery fire and naval bombardment. The main bombardment opened on 31 October, the birthday of the Taishō Emperor, and continued for a week. The Germans' defensive positions were destroyed one after another, their ammunition ran short, and on the night of 6–7 November Japanese infantry assaulted the remaining positions. Governor Meyer-Waldeck sought terms on 7 November and the garrison, numbering about 4,700 prisoners including naval personnel, surrendered. The fall of Qingdao ended German military power in China. The cost to the attackers was limited by the standards of the European war: Japanese losses amounted to several hundred killed and well over a thousand wounded, with smaller British losses, while German dead numbered in the low hundreds. Sources differ on exact figures, which should be treated as approximate.

Division II · Section 66.7

Siege Warfare, Artillery, Aviation, and the Experience of Combat

The siege of Qingdao was a modern operation in miniature, and military observers at the time saw it as a rehearsal for the techniques of the larger war. The attackers relied on heavy siege artillery, including large howitzers brought from Japan and positioned behind the lines, supplemented by naval gunfire from the Japanese fleet and from British ships. The Japanese navy included older battleships among the vessels that bombarded the German forts and harbor defenses. The German defenders, though short of heavy guns and ammunition, used coastal artillery, entrenchments, wire, and mines. Engineering was central to the offensive. Japanese sappers dug approach trenches, constructed roads and light railways for moving heavy guns, and handled the logistics of supplying an army across flooded country. These methods, which were developed during the siege of Port Arthur in 1904–1905, were refined at Qingdao.

Aviation made the campaign a pioneering moment for Japanese military flying. The army deployed a small provisional air unit with a handful of French-designed aircraft for reconnaissance and bombing, and the navy used the converted seaplane carrier Wakamiya, whose floatplanes conducted reconnaissance and dropped bombs on German targets and ships in Jiaozhou Bay. These raids are often described as among the first naval-launched air attacks in history, though they were small in scale. A German pilot, Gunther Plüschow, flew the garrison's last aircraft out of Qingdao on the eve of surrender, carrying documents. His later escape from captivity became a celebrated story in Germany. For the Japanese services, the experience encouraged investment in aviation, though in 1914 aircraft were still marginal weapons.

Medical services, communications, and the experience of ordinary soldiers are less well documented than operational narratives, but several features stand out. Japanese troops, fighting in the autumn rains and mud of Shandong, suffered from exposure and disease alongside combat injuries, and the Japanese Red Cross played an active role. The siege followed predictable rhythms: weeks of marching and digging, days of bombardment, a short and costly assault. For German defenders, the campaign meant isolation and the knowledge that surrender was almost certain; the final days were marked by the systematic destruction of installations to prevent capture. For the Chinese residents of Qingdao, who far outnumbered the German community, the siege meant disruption of work, flight from the city, and uncertainty about the future administration.

Some of the most striking historical consequences came from the treatment of prisoners. About 4,700 German and Austro-Hungarian prisoners were shipped to camps in Japan, where they were held for more than five years. Camp conditions varied, but at several camps, notably Bandō in Tokushima Prefecture, prisoners were allowed to organize workshops, sports, publications, and musical performances. A performance of Beethoven's Ninth Symphony by prisoners at Bandō in June 1918 is widely cited as the first complete performance of the work in Japan. Other prisoners later remained in Japan or influenced Japanese food culture, and bakers and butchers among them are credited with introducing or popularizing certain European breads, pastries, and sausages. These exchanges were incidental to the military outcome, but they are among the lasting social legacies of the campaign.

It is important not to exaggerate the scale. Qingdao involved tens of thousands of men over about two months, with Japanese fatalities in the hundreds and total killed and wounded exceeding a thousand. The Western Front in the same autumn was producing casualties in the hundreds of thousands. Qingdao's importance lay less in the scale of its casualties than in what it produced: an occupied territory and a railway, which gave Japan a physical position in Shandong that it then sought to convert into diplomatic rights.

Division II · Section 66.8

The Occupation of Germany's Pacific Islands

While the army besieged Qingdao, the Japanese navy moved into Micronesia. In early October 1914, naval squadrons took control of the principal German-held islands north of the equator. Within about two weeks, Japanese forces occupied Jaluit in the Marshall Islands, Yap and Palau, Pohnpei, and Chuuk (Truk) in the Carolines, and Saipan in the Marianas. German officials and traders were generally unable to resist, and the islands passed to Japanese naval control with little fighting. The German East Asia Squadron's wireless stations were destroyed or captured, and the islands' value as communications nodes and coaling points was transferred to Japan. Guam, held by the United States, was left untouched and became an American enclave surrounded by Japanese-held territory, an arrangement of considerable future significance.

The occupation was initially military, and the administering authority was the navy, which established a provisional defense force and civil administration at several centers. The inhabitants, including Chamorro and Carolinians in the Marianas, Palauans, Yapese, Chuukese, Pohnpeians, Kosraeans, and Marshallese, had not been consulted and experienced the change as another transfer of colonial authority. Japanese traders and some settlers had been present in parts of the islands before 1914, and the Japanese administration encouraged their expansion. The surviving administrative records and later accounts concentrate on Japanese and German actions, and the island populations' own perspectives are comparatively scarce in the sources, which mean that historians must take care not to treat their silence as consent.

Allied disagreements over the islands began immediately. The Australian and New Zealand forces occupied German possessions south of the equator, including Samoa, northeastern New Guinea, and Nauru, and an informal division of operations at the equator emerged. Britain had hoped to confine Japanese operations to the area north of the equator and to use the arrangement as a temporary measure, but Australia and New Zealand feared the long-term consequences of Japanese control of the islands and urged that Japanese occupation be limited. In 1917, however, Britain, anxious to secure Japanese naval help in the Mediterranean, gave a secret undertaking to support Japanese claims to the islands north of the equator at the peace conference, in exchange for Japanese support for British claims to the German islands south of it. France, Russia, and Italy gave similar undertakings in early 1917. These secret agreements, unknown to the public, would become central to Japan's position at Paris.

The navy's interest in the islands was strategic. They lay across the sea routes between Hawai'i, Guam, and the Philippines and the Asian mainland, and Japanese naval planners regarded control of them as a forward screen for the home islands. The United States, whose naval planners had long contemplated a Pacific war, recognized the same geography from the other direction, and the American Navy viewed Japanese occupation of Micronesia with growing alarm. Strategic questions would shape the legal form of the mandate settled in 1919–1920, discussed in Section 66.34.

Division II · Section 66.9

Japanese Naval Operations in the Pacific and Indian Oceans

The Imperial Japanese Navy's wider contribution to the war was less dramatic than a fleet battle but substantial. In the first months of the war, Japanese cruisers and battleships searched for the German East Asia Squadron and for German commerce raiders. Von Spee's squadron crossed the Pacific, defeated a British squadron at Coronel off Chile on 1 November 1914, and was destroyed by the Royal Navy off the Falkland Islands on 8 December. The light cruiser Emden, which had raided shipping across the Indian Ocean, was sunk by the Australian cruiser Sydney near the Cocos Islands on 9 November. Japanese ships, including the cruiser Ibuki, escorted the first Australian and New Zealand troop convoy across the Indian Ocean in the same period, and other Japanese cruisers patrolled the eastern Pacific and the coasts of the Americas in search of German vessels. The results of these operations were mixed, and Japanese naval power was never decisive against a major enemy fleet, but they helped to clear the Pacific and Indian Oceans of an organized German threat.

In 1915, Japanese forces played a smaller and little-known role in a colonial crisis. When Indian soldiers of the 5th Light Infantry mutinied at Singapore in February 1915, Japanese naval landing parties from visiting ships, together with Allied forces, helped to restore order. The episode shows both the extent of Japanese naval presence in Southeast Asian waters and the willingness of British officials to rely on Japan in matters touching their imperial security. It also reveals how the war was being understood in different ways. For the British, it showed the value of an ally in an Asian colonial emergency. For Indian nationalists and Chinese observers, Japan's role as a policing force for European empire in Asia was a troubling sign.

The Japanese navy also took on a long-term escort and patrol function. A special squadron based at Singapore, later called the First Special Squadron, patrolled sea lanes and escorted troop convoys between Australia and the Middle East. Japanese vessels guarded British and Allied shipping against submarine and raider attack. At the same time, Japanese industry supplied Allied needs: Japanese yards built merchant ships for Britain and destroyers for the French navy, and Japanese factories shipped rifles, ammunition, and other military supplies to Russia and to Britain. These supply contributions had major economic importance, as Sections 66.18 to 66.20 explain.

There were limits to what Japan would do. The Japanese government refused British requests to send major warships to the North Sea or the Western Front, and Japan did not send an army to Europe. Naval leaders feared losing capital ships and thought that the best use of the fleet was to preserve a strong position in the Pacific. They also resisted the extension of the war to areas that did not directly serve Japanese interests. Allied officials, including those in Britain, France, and later the United States, complained at times that Japan was doing less than it could. Japanese leaders replied that they were contributing in ways suited to their geography, and they made further contributions when the strategic situation changed, most visibly in the Mediterranean in 1917.

Division II · Section 66.10

Japanese Destroyers in the Mediterranean, 1917–1918

The most direct Japanese contribution to the European war was the Second Special Squadron. In February 1917, Germany declared unrestricted submarine warfare, and Allied shipping losses rose sharply. Britain urgently sought additional escort vessels and asked Japan to send warships to the Mediterranean, where U-boats were attacking the vital supply routes that carried troops and supplies between Europe, Egypt, and Salonika. After negotiations, Japan agreed to send a force of destroyers, though not capital ships, which Japanese leaders believed would be useless against submarines. Japan's agreement was coupled with diplomacy: in February and March 1917, Britain, France, Russia, and Italy gave the secret undertakings, noted above, supporting Japanese claims to German rights in Shandong and to the Pacific islands north of the equator. The naval contribution and the territorial settlement were therefore directly linked, though it should be added that Japanese leaders probably would have sent ships in any case once the British asked.

The squadron, under Rear-Admiral Satō Kōzō, left Singapore in March 1917 and reached Malta in April. It was initially built around the cruiser Akashi and eight destroyers, and it was reinforced during the year by a further flotilla of new destroyers, and over time by additional cruisers and smaller vessels. Altogether it comprised three cruisers and more than a dozen destroyers. Malta served as the base, and from there the squadron escorted troop transports and merchant ships through the central and eastern Mediterranean, notably on the routes between Marseille, Italy, Egypt, and Salonika. Japanese ships earned a reputation among British officers for the quality of their seamanship and the readiness with which they carried out dangerous escort work.

The work was unglamorous and hazardous. Japanese official histories record some 348 escort missions covering about 788 ships and, according to commonly cited figures, roughly 700,000 or more troops, a number which varies among sources. The squadron engaged submarines dozens of times without confirmed sinkings, but its escort work reduced the number of transports lost. In May 1917, when the troopship Transylvania was torpedoed south of Genoa, Japanese destroyers rescued many of the roughly three thousand people aboard, a rescue which British authorities praised. The squadron's worst single loss came on 11 June 1917, the destroyer Sakaki was torpedoed by an Austro-Hungarian submarine near Crete. Published naval histories differ between fifty-nine and sixty-eight deaths from the attack; the Japanese naval memorial in Malta commemorates sixty-eight war dead of the Second Special Squadron. These figures should be distinguished by source and counting category rather than presented as an uncontested single toll. Sakaki survived the attack and reached port. The memorial stands in Malta.

The strategic role of this operation should not be overstated or minimized. It did not change the course of the war, and Japan's casualties were small compared to those of the European powers. It did, however, show that Japan could project naval power across the world in support of the Allied cause, and it earned a measure of respect from Allied leaders, including Churchill, who praised the squadron's work. It also strengthened Japan's hand at the peace conference, though not decisively: the secret understandings of 1917 were honored by Britain at Paris, but American and Chinese resistance limited what Japan could win.

Division III · Section 66.11

Shandong after Qingdao: Occupation, Railways, and Chinese Sovereignty

After the fall of Qingdao, Japan did not leave Shandong. Japanese forces held the former leased territory under military administration and kept control of the Shandong Railway all the way to Jinan, together with the associated communications and mining assets. Japanese troops were stationed along the railway line, and Japanese officials, traders, and some settlers arrived in Qingdao to take advantage of the port and the city's commercial infrastructure. Within a short time Japan had in effect replaced Germany as the dominant foreign power in Shandong, but without the treaty basis that Germany had possessed. This situation was the immediate background to the Twenty-One Demands.

The legal questions were complicated, and Japanese and Chinese officials answered them differently. Japan argued that it had captured Germany's possessions by right of conquest and was entitled to hold them until a peace settlement. Chinese officials responded that the leased territory had been leased from China, that Germany's rights derived from a Chinese concession and could not be transferred by conquest, and that Japan's own ultimatum had promised eventual restoration. In international law, belligerent occupation of territory generally does not transfer sovereignty, which is acquired only by treaty or other lawful means. The Chinese government therefore treated Japanese control as a military occupation that should end with the war, while Japanese officials wanted Chinese acceptance of a permanent transfer of German rights. The distinction between military occupation and permanent sovereignty was the central question.

For Chinese authorities, Shandong was also emotionally and historically significant. It was the birthplace of Confucius and Mencius and a heavily populated province, and its coast and railway were strategically sensitive. A foreign power's control of the railway and the port threatened to cut the province, and potentially the northern Chinese plain, off from outside influence. Chinese opinion, as reflected in newspapers and later in student and merchant manifestos, treated the Shandong question as a measure of China's ability to survive as a sovereign state.

On the ground, the Japanese military administration encountered the usual frictions of occupation: disputes over taxes, policing, and trade, tension with local officials who had been loyal to Beijing, and competition with Chinese and other foreign merchants. The administration was gradually civilianized, but a substantial garrison remained along the railway. The persistence of troops after the original military objective had been achieved contributed to a growing Chinese conviction that Japan intended to stay. By the winter of 1914–1915, Japan had a strong position on the ground, a weak Chinese government that was dependent on foreign loans, and a Europe too preoccupied to intervene. Those were the conditions in which Katō Takaaki decided to present China with a comprehensive set of demands.

Division III · Section 66.12

The Twenty-One Demands of January 1915: The Five Groups Explained

On 18 January 1915, the Japanese minister in Beijing, Hioki Eki, presented Yuan Shikai personally with a list of demands. The manner of presentation was itself unusual and pointed. By going directly to the president, bypassing the foreign ministry, Hioki indicated that the matter was one of high policy and urgency, and that Japan expected to negotiate under conditions of secrecy. The document contained twenty-one articles, organized into five groups. Understanding each group is essential, because Chinese resistance, Allied reactions, and the final agreements all turned on which groups were being discussed.

Group 1 concerned Shandong. China would agree in advance to whatever arrangement Japan and Germany might make concerning German rights in the province, would not cede or lease any territory in Shandong or its coast and islands to any other power, would permit Japan to build a railway connecting Yantai or Longkou with the Shandong railway, and would open additional cities in Shandong to foreign trade. The first article was the most important: China was being asked to accept in advance the transfer of German rights to Japan, the very question that Chinese officials thought should be settled at the peace conference.

Group 2 concerned southern Manchuria and eastern Inner Mongolia, where Japan already had substantial rights as the successor to Russia. The Japanese asked that the leases of the Kwantung Territory (Port Arthur and Dalian) and of the South Manchuria and Andong–Mukden railways be extended to ninety-nine years. They also asked that Japanese subjects be permitted to lease or own land for commercial and agricultural purposes, to travel and reside in the region and engage in business, and to receive mining rights; that China obtain Japanese consent before granting railway or loan rights to other powers in the region; and that China consult Japan before engaging foreign political, financial, or military advisers there. The cumulative effect would have been to deepen Japan's legal position and to remove the territory from the reach of other powers' competition.

Group 3 concerned the Hanyeping Company, a large iron and coal enterprise in central China, centered at Hankou and including the ore of Daye and coal mines at Pingxiang. The Yawata Steel Works, Japan's government steelmaking enterprise, depended on Hanyeping iron. Japan asked that the company become a joint Sino-Japanese venture and that China not dispose of its assets or allow other nationals to operate them without Japanese consent. Group 4 consisted of a single article: China should not cede or lease any harbor, bay, or island along its coast to any third power, a provision aimed at preserving Japanese strategic position, particularly facing Fujian across from Taiwan.

Group 5 was the most intrusive, and it is the group that shaped how the demands were remembered. It included requests that China employ influential Japanese as political, financial, and military advisers; that Japanese hospitals, temples, and schools in the interior be granted land rights; that in certain important localities police administration be placed under joint Sino-Japanese control; that China purchase a substantial proportion (reported as half or more) of its munitions from Japan or establish a joint arsenal using Japanese technicians and materials; that Japan receive railway concessions connecting Wuchang with other parts of the Yangzi valley; that Japan be consulted on foreign loans for Fujian; and that Japanese be allowed to propagate Buddhism. Group 5 was presented as a set of "wishes" or requests, differently from the other groups, and Japan initially withheld its full contents from its Allies. Taken together, however, its provisions would have given Japan influence over Chinese policing, military supply, and advice at the center of the Chinese state. Chinese officials, and many foreign observers, considered that these provisions would have made China a Japanese protectorate in all but name.

Division III · Section 66.13

Yuan Shikai, Katō Takaaki, and the Diplomatic Struggle over China's Independence

The negotiations were an unequal contest between a strong, aggressive Japanese diplomacy and a Chinese government of limited power. Yuan Shikai, who had been president since 1912, headed a regime that controlled the Beiyang army and the Beijing bureaucracy but not the whole country. He had suppressed a rebellion in 1913, dissolved parliament in 1914, and ruled in an increasingly autocratic fashion, but he depended on foreign loans and could not resist a determined Japanese demand by force. His principal advantage was the opposition that the demands would arouse in other great powers, and his strategy was to delay, bargain, and publicize. Chinese negotiators included Foreign Minister Lu Zhengxiang and Vice Minister Cao Rulin, who would later be vilified as a pro-Japanese figure.

Katō Takaaki acted with unusual determination and an unusual degree of independence from the genrō, who complained that they had not been consulted. He believed that the war had given Japan an opportunity that would not recur, that China was unlikely to resist effectively, and that a comprehensive settlement was both achievable and necessary. Japanese troop reinforcements were sent to Shandong and Manchuria during the negotiations, a visible form of pressure. Formal conferences between Japanese and Chinese negotiators began in early February and continued through April, with some two dozen sessions, and Chinese officials resisted, wherever possible, by narrowing language, delaying replies, and seeking changes.

Chinese officials leaked the demands. By revealing Group 5 to foreign diplomats and to the press in February, they brought British, American, and other pressure to bear. The British government objected to Group 5 in particular, and Foreign Secretary Grey urged Japan to moderate its terms; Japan had told Britain at first only of the earlier groups. The United States, through Secretary of State William Jennings Bryan, sent a note on 13 March 1915 that acknowledged Japan's special position in Shandong, southern Manchuria, and eastern Inner Mongolia on the basis of territorial contiguity but objected to those items in Group 5 that threatened Chinese sovereignty and American treaty rights. A second note of 11 May 1915 declared that the United States would not recognize any agreement impairing American treaty rights, China's political or territorial integrity, or the Open Door policy. These notes are historically important because they show both the limits of American opposition (the United States was willing to concede a Japanese special position in some areas) and the origin of later American non-recognition policy.

Japanese domestic opinion was largely supportive in public, although the genrō, particularly Yamagata Aritomo, privately disapproved of Katō's methods, fearing that they would antagonize China, Britain, and the United States. The press generally backed the government. The army, which benefited from a demonstration of force and wanted to secure its Manchurian interests, was supportive. Katō's critics within the elite viewed his aggressive style as a diplomatic blunder, and when he resigned in August 1915, partly because of the controversy and partly because of political maneuvering, Ishii Kikujirō succeeded him as foreign minister. Chinese officials, for their part, had avoided total capitulation but at the cost of heavy concessions. Their struggle was not about whether to resist but about how much resistance was feasible.

Division III · Section 66.14

Ultimatums, Revisions, and the Agreements of May 1915

The negotiations reached their critical stage in April and May. On 26 April, Japan presented a revised proposal of twenty-four articles. It rearranged the groups, softened some of the Group 3 and Group 4 requests, and moved most of Group 5 out of the demands, while keeping the core provisions on Shandong and Manchuria. The Japanese presented the revision as a major concession, and Chinese negotiators responded with counterproposals that sought to limit the extension of Japanese rights, to reduce the provisions on land and residence in Manchuria, and to avoid any open declaration of Japan's special interests. Japan rejected most of these.

On 7 May, the Japanese government delivered an ultimatum, giving China forty-eight hours (until 6 p.m. on 9 May) to accept the revised terms, with Group 5 excluded except for the provisions concerning Fujian. Japanese troops had been reinforced in Shandong and Manchuria and warships were prepared. After consulting its advisers and weighing its situation, the Chinese government accepted, informing Japan on 8 and 9 May that it would agree to the demands as modified. The treaties and exchanges of notes were signed in Beijing on 25 May 1915. They included two treaties, one on Shandong and one on South Manchuria and eastern Inner Mongolia, together with a series of exchanges of notes covering the other matters. Chinese acceptance was given under the threat of force, a fact that Chinese officials and diplomats, and later Chinese historians, emphasized.

It is essential to distinguish the three stages. The original demands included Group 5 and the full text of the other groups. The revised proposal of 26 April dropped or deferred most of Group 5 and softened others. The agreements actually signed were narrower than the original demands but wider than the pre-war situation: Japan received extension of the Kwantung and railway leases to ninety-nine years, extensive rights of residence, land lease, and commercial activity in southern Manchuria, preferential treatment of its economic interests in eastern Inner Mongolia and Shandong, and Chinese acceptance in principle of whatever arrangements Japan and Germany might later make concerning German rights in Shandong. Japan did not obtain the advisers, joint police, or munitions provisions of Group 5. The matter ended with a partial Japanese diplomatic success and a long-term Chinese grievance.

The result also left a legal problem. Chinese diplomats later argued that the 1915 treaties and notes had been signed under duress and were therefore not binding, a position that they pressed at Paris and at Washington. Japanese diplomats replied that the agreements were legally concluded treaties, ratified by the Chinese president, and enforceable under international law. The dispute foreshadowed the Shandong argument of 1919, treated in Section 66.32.

Historical Debate

Were the Twenty-One Demands a Continuation of Unequal-Treaty Imperialism or a Qualitatively Greater Effort at Domination?

The question: Historians agree on the sequence of events but not on how to characterize the demands. Were they another episode in the long-running series of concessions extracted from China by the great powers since the 1840s, or did they mark a new stage in which a single power sought to control China's policy?

Interpretation A — Continuity with the unequal-treaty system: Some scholars emphasize that the great powers had repeatedly forced Chinese governments to grant leaseholds, railway rights, mining concessions, and spheres of influence. Germany had obtained Jiaozhou in 1898, Russia Port Arthur, Britain Weihaiwei, and France Guangzhouwan. Groups 1 to 4 asked for extensions and confirmations of rights of a familiar kind, and Japan was acting as the other powers had acted, taking advantage of a weak Chinese government and a Europe at war. On this view, Japan's methods, including the ultimatum, belonged to an established repertoire, and the Chinese reaction reflected the cumulative effect of many earlier humiliations.
Interpretation B — A qualitatively greater ambition: Other scholars argue that the combination of demands, especially Group 5, aimed at something different from earlier concessions: privileged advisers, joint control of police, and dependence on Japanese arms would have placed Chinese government functions under Japanese supervision. Japan had done much the same in Korea in 1904–1905, and some Chinese and foreign contemporaries drew the comparison. On this view the demands were an attempt to extend the Korean model to China, made possible by the temporary absence of the other powers, and the vigorous reactions of Britain and the United States were prompted by that wider implication.

Evidence: Both interpretations rely on the texts of the demands, the diplomatic correspondence of Japan, China, Britain, and the United States, the record of the negotiations, and later memoirs. They differ in how they interpret Group 5 (a bargaining position or a real objective), the intentions of Katō Takaaki, and how much the final agreements, as opposed to the original demands, should count.

Why it matters: If the demands were continuous with earlier practice, Japan's behavior should be understood as that of one imperial power among others. If they were qualitatively greater, they open the way toward a view of the war years as a stage in the development of Japanese hegemonic ambition in China that culminated in the 1930s. Students should note that the two interpretations are not necessarily exclusive: the demands may have been both conventional in method and expansive in aim. A historian must also avoid reading the 1930s back into 1915, as though later outcomes were inevitable.

Student task: Identify one provision in the demands that supports Interpretation A and one that supports Interpretation B. Then explain what additional evidence, such as internal Japanese cabinet records, would help to determine how seriously Japanese leaders took Group 5.

Division III · Section 66.15

Chinese Nationalism, Boycotts, and the Political Consequences of 1915

The Chinese response to the demands was immediate and strong. Within China, newspapers, merchant associations, student groups, and provincial assemblies protested the Japanese pressure and the Chinese government's acceptance. Chinese communities abroad, including students in Japan, sent telegrams and held meetings. A movement to boycott Japanese goods spread through the treaty ports and other cities, and in some commercial centers Japanese exports and shipping suffered noticeable losses in the months following May 1915. Day-to-day commercial effects were limited in the long run, because Chinese consumers depended on Japanese cotton goods and other products, but the boycott demonstrated the existence of an organized public opinion capable of political action.

Chinese commentators began to speak of 7 May and 9 May as days of national humiliation, and the phrase "remember the national humiliation" circulated in pamphlets, school texts, and public lectures. The memory of 1915 became part of a growing nationalist narrative in which foreign imperialism, and Japanese imperialism in particular, was linked to the weakness of the Chinese state. Many Chinese intellectuals concluded that China could not rely on the old political order and that deeper reform of politics and culture was needed. Chen Duxiu's journal New Youth, founded in 1915, helped create a wave of cultural criticism that became the New Culture Movement. The demands were one of several factors, but they are widely regarded by historians as an important catalyst of Chinese nationalism in the republican era.

The political consequences for Yuan Shikai were severe. His acceptance of the demands damaged his reputation as a defender of national interests, and his subsequent attempt to establish himself as emperor in December 1915 provoked armed rebellions in the provinces. Japan, which had earlier tolerated Yuan, now withheld support and joined other powers in advising him against monarchy, and some Japanese army and civilian figures aided anti-Yuan forces. Yuan abandoned the monarchy in March 1916 and died in June 1916. The Chinese state fractured into regional military regimes, a period often called the warlord era, and Japan's China policy had to adapt to the new fragmentation.

Within Japan, the effects were more muted but still significant. Public opinion largely approved of the results, but some critics argued that Katō's methods had damaged relations with China and Britain, and that Japan had made an enemy of a neighbor without securing real benefits. For the Japanese government, the lesson drawn by some officials was that coercive diplomacy was costly and that financial and political influence could achieve similar results at lower diplomatic cost. That view shaped the policy of the Terauchi government, examined next. For the Chinese public, by contrast, the lesson was that Japanese promises of friendship could not be trusted and that national mobilization was necessary.

Division III · Section 66.16

Influence through Finance: Terauchi, Duan Qirui, and the Nishihara Loans

After the death of Yuan Shikai, Japan's China policy changed in method if not in aim. The Terauchi Masatake cabinet, which took office in October 1916, pursued what it described as a policy of friendly cooperation with the Beijing government, in contrast to Katō's confrontational style. The principal figure in this approach was Nishihara Kamezō, a businessman with wide experience in Korea and a close associate of Terauchi, who traveled to China as Terauchi's private emissary. His principal Chinese counterpart was Duan Qirui, the premier and the most powerful general in the Beiyang government. Duan wanted money to build his army and consolidate his political position, and Japan wanted a cooperative government in Beijing.

Between early 1917 and September 1918, Japanese banks, led by the Bank of Chōsen, the Industrial Bank of Japan, and the Bank of Taiwan, extended a series of loans to the Chinese government, usually estimated at about 145 million yen in total. They were nominally for telegraph, railway, mining, and other development projects, but a large share of the money supported Duan's government and military. In return Japan obtained railway concessions, mining and forestry rights, and influence over Chinese telegraph and communications. Related agreements in 1918, including Sino-Japanese military cooperation agreements concluded in the spring against a possible Russian or German threat in Siberia and Manchuria, increased Japan's influence over Chinese military affairs. In September 1918, an exchange of notes on Shandong railways, in which a Chinese representative signified willingness to accept Japanese proposals for the future of the Shandong lines, would become a major element in the Japanese case at Paris, though Chinese diplomats later insisted that it had no legal force.

China also changed its status during this period. At Japan's encouragement and over considerable domestic opposition, Duan's government broke relations with Germany in March 1917 and declared war on Germany in August 1917. China sent no army to Europe, but around 140,000 Chinese laborers worked for the British and French behind the Allied lines, and Chinese leaders, including Wellington Koo, argued that China's participation entitled it to a place at the peace conference and to the restoration of German rights in Shandong. The Japanese government saw the Chinese declaration as useful insofar as it increased Beijing's dependence on Tokyo, but it was less happy when China insisted afterward that its status as an ally cancelled all German rights.

The policy had limited results. The loans did not buy lasting influence, since Duan's position was weak and the money was spent largely on military operations in China's civil conflicts. Chinese opinion regarded the loans as evidence that Duan was selling the country, and the Japanese policy was widely criticized by the Chinese press and public. Chinese students in Japan left in protest against the military agreements of 1918, and among them were future intellectuals and writers. Many loans were never repaid. In Japan, Hara Takashi and other critics argued that the government was throwing good money after bad, and the policy fell into disrepute after the Terauchi cabinet fell. Duan was himself defeated by rival warlords in 1920. The Nishihara Loans nevertheless show an important shift: Japan was learning to use its new financial strength as an instrument of influence in China.

Cause and Effect

Military Occupation, Political Pressure, Financial Diplomacy, and Chinese Nationalism

Long-term causes

The weakening of the Qing state after the nineteenth century, the unequal treaty system, Japanese interests in Manchuria and Korea acquired after 1895 and 1905, Japan's economic dependence on continental raw materials, and the rise of Chinese national consciousness since the 1890s.

Short-term causes

The European war's removal of competing powers, the weakness of Yuan Shikai's government, Katō Takaaki's desire for a comprehensive settlement, the presence of Japanese troops in Shandong, and the absence of effective international constraint in 1915.

Event or transformation

The Twenty-One Demands and the agreements of May 1915, followed after 1916 by a shift to loans, political sponsorship, and military agreements under Terauchi, Nishihara, and Duan Qirui.

Immediate effects

Chinese boycotts and "national humiliation" commemorations; American non-recognition; British distrust; collapse of Yuan's prestige; fragmentation of the Chinese state; and a Japanese turn toward financial influence.

Long-term consequences

The Shandong question at Paris, the May Fourth Movement, the deterioration of Sino-Japanese relations, and a pattern of Japanese involvement in Chinese domestic politics that continued through the 1920s.

Historical complexity

Japanese policy was not the product of one decision. Katō, Terauchi, the army, the navy, the Foreign Ministry, businessmen, and the genrō held different views. Chinese actors were also divided, and Chinese politicians sometimes sought Japanese support for their own ends. The Chinese nationalist reaction was caused by Japan's actions but also by wider changes within China.
Division IV · Section 66.17

Japan's Economy before the War: Industrial Capacity and International Dependence

To understand the wartime transformation, it is necessary to begin with the structure of the Japanese economy in 1914. Japan was an industrializing economy with a large agricultural base and a modern industrial sector concentrated in textiles. Cotton spinning, centered in the Osaka region, produced yarn for domestic use and export, especially to China and India. Raw silk, produced in the mountain districts of central Japan and reeled in mills that employed large numbers of young women, was the principal export, with the United States as the main buyer. Heavy industry existed but was smaller: the state-owned Yawata Steel Works, opened in 1901, shipbuilding yards at Nagasaki, Kobe, and Yokosuka, machine manufacturers, and mines for coal, copper, and other minerals. The great business houses, known later as zaibatsu, including Mitsui, Mitsubishi, Sumitomo, and Yasuda, dominated banking, trade, shipping, and mining (see Chapter 56).

Japan was heavily dependent on imports and foreign finance. It imported raw cotton from India and the United States, iron and steel, machinery, chemicals, and oil, and it exported silk, cotton goods, tea, coal, and manufactured products. For most of the twenty years before 1914, Japan ran a deficit in its merchandise trade, with the exceptions of a few years, and covered it through foreign borrowing. Much of the borrowing had been raised to pay for the Russo-Japanese War and for the postwar expansion of the empire, and by 1914 the country carried a large external debt, estimated by economic historians at around ¥1.9 billion in total public and private liabilities, with a large part owed abroad. The need to service this debt, together with continuing demand for imports, limited the government's room for maneuver and made Japan vulnerable to financial shocks.

The year 1913 and the early months of 1914 were difficult. A poor harvest in the northeast in 1913 caused hardship in rural Tōhoku, credit was tight, and the government had embarked on austerity measures to reduce its foreign obligations. Business conditions were weak. The outbreak of war in Europe in July and August 1914 initially made matters worse. Financial markets were disrupted, the gold standard was suspended or restricted across much of the world, exchange transactions became difficult, European shipping was thrown into confusion, and Japanese exporters feared the loss of markets. Raw silk prices collapsed as American demand wavered, and bankruptcies and stock exchange closures followed. The Bank of Japan stepped in with emergency credit. In 1914, Japan's exports, valued at about ¥591 million, were almost equal to its imports, at about ¥596 million, a pause before the boom.

Historians therefore warn against a simple narrative of instant windfall. Prosperity arrived with a lag, as it became clear that Allied demand and the disruption of European exports would open markets, and as Japanese firms learned to respond. The war would change the country's economic structure, but the change took place within a framework of existing industrial capacity, financial institutions, and dependence that conditioned how the opportunity could be used.

Division IV · Section 66.18

The Wartime Export Boom and the Expansion of Overseas Markets

From 1915 onward, Japanese exports rose steeply. The principal cause was the withdrawal of European suppliers from Asian and other markets. British textile producers, who had dominated the markets of India, Southeast Asia, and China, shifted their capacity to war production and suffered from shipping shortages, and Japanese cotton yarn and cloth moved into the gap. Japanese manufacturers also supplied the Dutch East Indies, the Straits Settlements, Australia, and other markets with a wide range of goods, including matches, toys, porcelain, glassware, and simple machinery. At the same time, American demand for Japanese silk, after a faltering start, revived and grew with American wartime prosperity, and Russia and Britain bought large quantities of arms and ammunition from Japanese factories. Japanese shipping companies, including Nippon Yūsen Kaisha and Osaka Shōsen Kaisha, took advantage of rising freight rates and the scarcity of European tonnage to expand services.

The geography of the boom reached beyond the Japanese home islands. Korea and Taiwan supplied rice, sugar, and raw materials, and Taiwanese and Korean sugar and rice output fed the home market under colonial arrangements that favored Japanese buyers. Sugar prices rose sharply during the war, bringing a short-lived boom to sugar-producing districts, including Okinawa, where the prefectural economy depended heavily on cane, before the collapse of prices in 1920. Hokkaido's farms, forests, and coal mines also benefited, and a wartime boom in beans, exported to Europe as food and feed, enriched some Tokachi growers and merchants who were called "bean millionaires." The evidence on how the Ainu communities fared is thin, but the Hokkaido Former Aborigines Protection Act of 1899 and the pressure of settlement had left many without secure land, and they were unlikely to share fully in the boom. The same pattern, in which the benefits of the war economy flowed unevenly across regions and groups, will recur through the next sections.

Japanese trading houses, including Mitsui Bussan and Mitsubishi Shōji, and newer firms such as Suzuki Shōten of Kobe, expanded their activities across Asia, opening branches, chartering ships, and speculating in commodities. Japanese cotton firms also began to invest in mills in China, where they could use cheaper labor and avoid tariffs, beginning a movement of Japanese capital into the Chinese industrial sector that would grow in the 1920s. The war thus changed Japan's commercial presence in Asia, and in some cases it made Japanese economic expansion a competitor with Chinese national industry, adding an economic dimension to the political tension discussed in Division III.

Division IV · Section 66.19

Shipbuilding, Steel, Chemicals, and the Expansion of Heavy Industry

The war accelerated the development of industries that Japan had previously imported. Shipbuilding was the clearest case. With the Allies suffering heavy losses to submarines and British yards committed to warships, freight rates rose to extraordinary levels and the demand for merchant ships grew far beyond supply. Japanese shipyards, notably those of Mitsubishi at Nagasaki, Kawasaki at Kobe, and Osaka Iron Works, expanded capacity, trained workers, and built ships for Japanese and foreign owners. Shipowners who had acquired vessels cheaply before the war earned large profits, and a group of newly rich shipping magnates, known as "ship narikin," became symbols of the boom. By 1919, Japan had become one of the world's major shipbuilding nations. The term narikin, from the game of shōgi in which a pawn is promoted, was applied more widely to those who became rich almost overnight during the war, and the word gradually acquired connotations of vulgarity and speculation.

Steel was a related story. Japanese steelmaking had been dominated by the state-owned Yawata Works, which relied on iron ore from China, including the Daye mines connected to the Hanyeping Company that was the subject of Group 3 of the Twenty-One Demands. The war increased demand for steel for ships, rails, and machinery. In 1917, the United States restricted steel exports as it prepared to enter the war, and Japan then faced a shortage of steel for its shipbuilders. In 1918, the two countries reached an arrangement under which Japanese shipbuilders supplied ships to the United States in return for American steel. The South Manchuria Railway Company and private firms built new ironworks in Manchuria and Japan, including the Anshan works founded in 1918, expanding the industrial basis for the later development of Manchuria.

Chemicals offer another example of import substitution. Before 1914, Japan had depended on Germany for many dyes, pharmaceuticals, and fertilizers, and the war cut those supplies. The government gave financial support to the establishment of domestic dyestuff and chemical firms, and Japanese companies expanded production of fertilizers, soda, and explosives. Electric power and electrical machinery also grew, as new hydroelectric plants supplied industrial districts. The result was a growing share of heavy and chemical industries in the industrial economy, though textiles remained the largest sector in employment and value for many years. It is commonly reported that around 1919 the value of Japanese industrial production first exceeded that of agriculture, a change that is not a precise date but marks a structural shift.

The expansion had limits and costs. Many of the new firms were thinly capitalized and dependent on temporary wartime demand. Coal and mining, in particular, expanded rapidly, with labor conditions that were harsh and dangerous, as Chapter 63 describes. When the war ended and European competitors returned, the weaker enterprises faced a crisis. The recession of 1920, the earthquake of 1923, and the banking crises of the 1920s (see Chapter 73) would reveal how much of the wartime expansion had been fragile. Still, the war accomplished a lasting shift in Japan's capacity: its industrial base, workforce, and technical training were permanently larger at the end of the war than at the beginning.

Division IV · Section 66.20

Banking, Profits, Trade Surpluses, and New Financial Power

The accumulated trade and payments surpluses transformed Japan's international financial position. According to a Hitotsubashi University study of the period, Japan's merchandise surplus for 1914 to 1918 amounted to about ¥1,375 million, and when invisible items such as shipping earnings and returns from investments are added, the balance of payments surplus for the five years came to more than ¥2,700 million. Gold flowed into the country through the Yokohama Specie Bank, which handled much of Japan's foreign exchange: about ¥94 million in 1916 and about ¥247 million in 1917, according to figures cited in the Encyclopedia of the First World War. Japanese leaders had long worried about gold reserves. The rapid accumulation of specie and foreign balances now meant that Japan could, in principle, repay a large share of its outstanding foreign debt, which stood at around ¥1.9 billion before the war. The financier Inoue Junnosuke suggested that the war economy had given Japan the capacity to liquidate all its outstanding debts, foreign and domestic.

Japan's financial status changed accordingly. From a net debtor that had borrowed heavily in London, Paris, and New York, it became a net creditor, buying British and French treasury bills and other Allied obligations, lending to Russia, and, as Section 66.16 describes, to China. The government suspended gold exports in September 1917, following the American embargo, to protect its stock of specie. The Bank of Japan's reserves expanded, and Japanese banks and trading firms extended credit overseas. This was a genuine historic reversal, though it was also a temporary and fragile one. The surplus partly reflected the abnormal conditions of war, and it eroded when imports revived after 1918.

Domestically, the boom enriched firms and individuals. Profits of cotton spinning, shipping, mining, and trading firms rose dramatically, and the established zaibatsu houses, with their banks and trading companies, were well placed to expand into shipbuilding, steel, and chemicals. Mitsui, Mitsubishi, Sumitomo, and Yasuda grew in size, scope, and political influence. New business groups, such as the Suzuki Shōten trading house, Kuhara Mining, and Kawasaki, also rose quickly, and some of them would later fail. Bank deposits increased, joint-stock companies were founded in large numbers, stock prices soared, and speculation became common. According to one widely used estimate, the number of people with very large incomes rose by more than 100 percent between 1915 and 1919, although the figure is an estimate and the precise numbers depend on how wealth is defined.

Banks and financial institutions were drawn into the speculative environment. Credit was easy, and many banks lent heavily against shares and commodities. The Bank of Japan's gold and foreign-exchange holdings expanded the money supply, contributing to the inflation described in the next section. These characteristics would have significant consequences. The same financial structure that facilitated profit in the war boom would amplify losses in the downturn after 1920, and the surviving weaknesses of the banking system would be exposed in the financial panics of the 1920s.

Division IV · Section 66.21

Inflation, Speculation, and the Unequal Distribution of Wartime Prosperity

The boom's monetary effects created inflation. Rising export earnings, expanding bank credit, and gold inflows increased the supply of money, while shortages of imported goods and rising demand for domestic products pushed prices upward. Price indices compiled at the time and since show that wholesale prices rose steadily from 1915 and accelerated after 1916; by 1918 they were roughly twice their prewar level, though the size of the increase differs by commodity and index. Speculation compounded the pressure: traders bought and held goods in expectation of further price increases, and merchants, hoarders, and speculators were widely suspected, sometimes correctly, of driving prices higher. In 1917 and 1918 speculation extended to rice, the staple food for most of the population.

Wages rose but not as fast as prices. Industrial workers in expanding industries, particularly skilled workers in shipbuilding, machinery, and metalworking, could win substantial pay increases because of labor shortages. Others, including unskilled laborers, textile workers, clerks, teachers, minor civil servants, and day laborers, saw their real incomes eroded. Historians' estimates, including those of Michael Lewis, suggest that real wages fell by perhaps 30 percent or more between 1914 and 1918 for many wage earners, and that salaried public servants lost about half their purchasing power. These are estimates, and the experience of different groups differed widely, but the broad pattern is clear: nominal wages lagged behind prices for most workers.

The relationship between prosperity and inequality varied by social position. Owners of shipyards, mills, banks, and trading firms profited. Landlords who received rents in kind benefited from rising rice prices. Small farmers who owned their land and sold rice or silk cocoons gained as well, though cocoon and silk prices fluctuated, and many farm households that bought food, fertilizer, and consumer goods experienced rising costs. Tenant farmers who paid rent in rice kept a smaller share of a crop whose price was rising. Urban wage earners and their families, who bought rice in the market and spent a large share of income on food, bore the full burden of rice price increases. Rice prices in particular spiked in mid-1918: according to Dojima rice exchange records, the price per koku rose from about ¥15 in January 1918 to over ¥20 in June, ¥30 by mid-July, and over ¥50 by 9 August, a price level more than three times higher than at the start of the year.

Several factors explain this rice spike. Expectations that the army would buy large quantities of rice for the Siberian expedition, announced in early August, encouraged speculators. Rice imports were constrained by shipping shortages, and domestic distribution was inefficient. The government's initial response was to treat the problem as one of speculation and distribution, not of supply, and its measures were late and ineffective. The details of the crisis and the riots that followed belong to Chapter 68. What matters here is the economic foundation: a country experiencing record export earnings and rising industrial profits was simultaneously experiencing a decline in the real purchasing power of many of its people. These two facts were not contradictory. They were different consequences of the same wartime economy.

Division V · Section 66.22

The Ōkuma and Terauchi Governments: Wartime Cabinet Politics

Japan's wartime politics took place within the constitutional framework established in 1889 and examined in Chapter 59. The emperor was sovereign, the cabinet advised him, the Diet had power over the budget and legislation, the army and navy had direct access to the throne, and the genrō, the surviving Meiji oligarchs, informally selected prime ministers. Within this structure, the war years saw competing claims of authority: from elected parties, from the services, and from the bureaucracy and the genrō. The sequence of two governments, the Ōkuma cabinet of 1914–1916 and the Terauchi cabinet of 1916–1918, illustrates the shifting balance.

The Ōkuma government was a party-based cabinet, with support from the Rikken Dōshikai, which won a large victory in the general election of March 1915. The election was marked by official interference and bribery allegations, which led to the resignation of Home Minister Ōura Kanetake. The government expanded the army by two divisions in 1915, a measure long sought by army leaders. It carried out the policy toward China discussed in Division III, and it presided over the beginning of the economic boom. Katō Takaaki resigned in August 1915 after the controversy over the Twenty-One Demands. By 1916 the government was losing the confidence of the genrō, who disliked its style and some of its conduct. Ōkuma resigned in October 1916 and recommended Katō as his successor, but Yamagata and the other elder statesmen chose instead Terauchi Masatake, the army field marshal and former governor-general of Korea.

The Terauchi government was a "transcendental cabinet" (chōzen naikaku), meaning a cabinet formed without party leadership, drawn largely from the bureaucracy and the military, in theory standing above party politics. Its formation led to the merger of several groups into the new party Kenseikai, led by Katō Takaaki, which opposed Terauchi. In the general election of April 1917, the Rikken Seiyūkai, led by Hara Takashi, emerged as the largest party. Terauchi sought to build a broad consensus on foreign policy and in 1917 established an Advisory Council on Foreign Relations, which included leaders of the major parties. Hara Takashi and Inukai Tsuyoshi took part, while Katō Takaaki declined, a decision which isolated his party. The council became a venue in which party leaders, bureaucrats, and military figures debated issues such as China policy and, later, the Siberian Intervention.

The relationship between wartime decision-making and constitutional institutions was complex. Foreign and military policy was shaped by a small group: the prime minister, the foreign minister, the service ministers and chiefs of staff, and the genrō. The Diet debated budgets and supplementary appropriations, and the press could criticize, but the Diet had little direct role in the decision to enter the war, the presentation of the Twenty-One Demands, or the dispatch of troops to Siberia. The parties sought to expand their influence by controlling the budget and bargaining over appointments, and the wartime experience strengthened the claim that foreign affairs, like domestic affairs, should be subject to party and parliamentary scrutiny. These arguments, which belong to Chapter 67, were beginning to gather force.

Division V · Section 66.23

Industrial Workers, Women, and the Expansion of Wartime Employment

The wartime boom created jobs. Factories, shipyards, mines, and warehouses recruited workers in large numbers, and the demand pulled people from the countryside into cities and industrial districts such as Osaka, Kobe, the Tokyo–Yokohama (Keihin) area, northern Kyushu, and the Inland Sea coast. Migration of young men and women from rural villages, especially those in poorer areas, accelerated. Employers competed for skilled workers by raising pay and offering bonuses, and workers with scarce skills, such as machinists and shipbuilders, changed jobs frequently to take advantage of the market. Chapter 63 describes the earlier history of industrial labor, and the account here concentrates on how the war changed its conditions.

Women were an essential part of this workforce. In the textile industry, which remained the largest employer of factory labor, a majority of workers were women, many of them young and from rural families who sent their daughters to the mills on fixed-term contracts. Women lived in company dormitories, worked long shifts, often with night work in spinning mills, and earned lower wages than men. The Factory Law of 1911, which restricted work hours for women and children and prohibited night work in some conditions, finally took effect in 1916 after a long delay, but exemptions and weak enforcement limited its effect. In coal mines and heavy industry, wartime demand increased the numbers of both male and female workers, and mine labor was dangerous and heavily regulated by labor contractors. In the colonies and in Japan, Korean laborers were present in small but growing numbers in some industries, an early sign of the labor migration that would grow in the following decade.

Wartime inflation changed workers' expectations. As prices rose faster than pay, workers' discontent grew, and strikes increased in number and scale. The number of labor disputes rose from a few dozen per year before the war to several hundred by 1918, according to commonly cited labor statistics, though precise figures vary with how disputes are counted. The Yūaikai (Friendly Society), founded in 1912 by Suzuki Bunji, grew from a small mutual aid society into a larger labor organization, and in the years during and just after the war the organization expanded to tens of thousands of members and began to demand wage increases and legal recognition of unions. Government and employers continued to regard collective action with suspicion, and the Public Order and Police Law of 1900 restricted union activity. The more developed Taishō labor movement is treated in Chapter 69, but its foundations were laid in the war years.

The war also brought changes in the nature of work. Large factories grew larger, new departments of engineering and management emerged, and some employers introduced company welfare schemes and training programs in the hope of securing a stable workforce. The proportion of workers in large factories increased, and a distinction developed between skilled workers in heavy industry and the more numerous unskilled and temporary workers. For women, the expansion of employment did not break the fundamental pattern of the textile labor system, in which young women worked for a few years before returning to rural life or marriage. The war demonstrated both the opportunities that industrial work could bring and the vulnerability of workers who lived with little protection against inflation or layoffs.

Division V · Section 66.24

Families, Rural Communities, and the Uneven Social Experience of War

About half of the Japanese population still lived in farm households in 1914, and the war affected rural communities in different ways. Landowning farmers who sold rice in rising markets prospered, as did sericulture households when silk prices recovered. Wealthy landlords, who collected rents in kind, benefited most. Tenant farmers, who made up a large share of the rural population, paid a high proportion of their crop as rent and found that rising prices for fertilizer, tools, and consumer goods offset any gain from higher rice prices. Small owner-farmers often had to buy food or farm inputs in the market and were exposed to inflation. The wartime boom also increased the gap between town and country in some regions, as industrial wages in cities rose faster than rural incomes, drawing young people away from villages and straining family labor.

Regional differences were considerable. In the Kansai and Inland Sea industrial belt, factories and shipyards supported rapid urban growth. In the silk-producing districts of Nagano, Yamanashi, and Gunma, prosperity followed the revival of American demand. In the northeast, Tōhoku, where agriculture was poorer and tenancy common, the benefits were fewer and the burden of high prices heavier. In Hokkaido, development of farmland, forests, and mines continued, and the bean boom enriched some farmers and merchants. In Okinawa, the sugar boom raised incomes for a time, but the prefecture's dependence on a single crop made it especially vulnerable to the price collapse that followed in 1920, with severe consequences in the 1920s. In Korea and Taiwan, which supplied rice and sugar to the Japanese market, colonial policies directed agricultural production toward imperial needs, and the benefits went disproportionately to Japanese companies and landlords.

Within households, women often managed the family economy. Daughters sent wages home from textile mills, wives took up supplementary work in sericulture, home industry, and small commerce, and mothers coped with rising food prices by adjusting what they cooked and bought. These contributions, though often invisible in official statistics, were central to household survival. Children's experience also differed: more children in cities attended school, which had become nearly universal at the elementary level, while rural children were often kept home to work. The rural–urban divide shaped conditions of health, nutrition, and mobility. The influenza pandemic of 1918–1919, which reached Japan in late 1918 and caused several hundred thousand deaths according to commonly cited estimates, arrived at the end of the period and struck crowded industrial and urban populations hard, though its full treatment belongs to a study of public health.

Division V · Section 66.25

War News, Nationalism, Censorship, and the Representation of Victory

Japan's wartime public sphere was shaped by a growing commercial press, new media technologies, and official efforts to guide opinion. Chapter 65 describes the development of newspapers, photography, and early cinema. This section examines how the war was represented and where its limits lay. Large newspapers such as the Ōsaka Asahi and the Tokyo Asahi, the Jiji Shinpō, and the Yomiuri provided extensive coverage, using telegraph reports from Europe, wire service material from Allied sources, and reports from their own correspondents. Photographs and illustrated supplements, picture postcards, and newsreels brought images of battle to urban audiences, and the fall of Qingdao in November 1914 was celebrated with lantern parades and public meetings in Tokyo and other cities.

Official efforts to shape the news were substantial. The Newspaper Law of 1909 allowed the Home Ministry to suspend or ban publications that threatened public order, and army and navy authorities restricted reporting on military matters. The government used official announcements, imperial rescripts, and ceremonies to link the war to loyalty and patriotism, and it encouraged patriotic associations and education campaigns. At the same time, public reporting of the war in Europe was selective: Japanese readers learned much about Allied victories and little about the human costs of the Western Front. The Japanese version of the war was a story of national achievement and of honorable alliance.

Dissent existed but was constrained. Some liberal journalists and academics, including the political scientist Yoshino Sakuzō, argued for a more democratic politics and greater attention to the people's welfare and to international cooperation, and some socialists and pacifists criticized the war and the policy toward China. They worked under police surveillance, and the memory of the High Treason Incident of 1910–1911, which had led to the execution of Kōtoku Shūsui and others, still weighed on the left. Critics of the Siberian Intervention and the government's handling of the rice crisis would face the most severe restrictions in 1918, as the next section shows.

A revealing case occurred in late summer 1918. After the Rice Riots, the government prohibited newspapers from reporting on them. The Ōsaka Asahi, one of Japan's most important newspapers, then published an article during a meeting of newspaper representatives protesting the ban, which used a classical Chinese phrase referring to a "white rainbow piercing the sun," an expression traditionally associated with rebellion or the assassination of rulers. Authorities seized on the phrase as evidence of subversive intent and prosecuted the paper. The "White Rainbow Incident" led to the resignation of prominent journalists, including the writer Hasegawa Nyozekan, and to a pledge by the newspaper to be more cautious, and it showed how press freedom was limited at a moment when the press had become a major force. It also illustrates the way in which the war's political tensions reached the newspapers, which were both beneficiaries of the war boom and its critics.

Division V · Section 66.26

The Crisis of 1918: Rising Rice Prices and the Transition to Hara Takashi

By the summer of 1918, the combination of inflation, speculation, a rice price surge, and anxiety about the Siberian dispatch had created conditions in which a local protest could ignite wider unrest. In the first days of August, women in fishing villages along Toyama Bay, notably at Uozu, gathered to prevent rice from being shipped out of the district and to demand that local merchants sell it at lower prices. The news spread. In the second week of August, disturbances broke out in Kyoto, Nagoya, Osaka, Kobe, and other cities, and continued in September with disturbances in coal mines. The riots, in which crowds attacked rice dealers, wealthy merchants, and sometimes police, were the largest popular disturbance in Japan since the Meiji Restoration. They involved participants from many social groups, including factory workers, fishermen's wives, day laborers, and members of outcast communities (Chapter 68 gives the sequence, regional spread, and policing in full).

For the purposes of this chapter, the main point is political. The Terauchi government responded with troops and police, price-lowering measures, and press restrictions, and it was widely blamed for failing to anticipate or manage the crisis. The Suzuki Shōten trading firm of Kobe, which was accused of rice speculation, was burned by a crowd. The combination of discredited leadership, the burden of the Siberian commitment, and the enormous disturbance made Terauchi's position untenable. He resigned on 21 September 1918, citing illness. Yamagata and the other genrō, recognizing that only a government with party support could manage the Diet and the public, recommended Hara Takashi, the president of the Rikken Seiyūkai.

Hara took office on 29 September 1918. He was widely described as a commoner prime minister, the first not to hold a noble title or to come from a domain oligarchy or the military, and his cabinet was the first in which, apart from the army, navy, and foreign ministers, most members belonged to a party. Hara's government included Tanaka Giichi as army minister, Katō Tomosaburō as navy minister, and Uchida Yasuya as foreign minister. Hara promised to continue the alliance and Japan's established foreign policy, but he also promised a more careful policy toward China and to lighten the burdens of the Siberian intervention, though he did not end it. The transition from the Terauchi cabinet to the Hara cabinet is a convenient place to end the domestic narrative of this chapter, since the fuller development of party government belongs to Chapter 67.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Economic Expansion and Political Consequences, 1914–1918

What Changed

  • Japan moved from chronic trade deficit and heavy foreign debt to export surplus, large specie holdings, and creditor status
  • Heavy industry, shipbuilding, chemicals, and electricity grew rapidly, and the labor force in factories, mines, and shipyards expanded
  • Inflation and rising rice prices created a mass economic grievance that crossed regional and occupational lines
  • The political balance shifted from the Ōkuma and Terauchi governments toward a party cabinet under Hara Takashi

What Remained Continuous

  • The Meiji constitutional framework, the autonomy of the services, and the informal role of the genrō in choosing prime ministers
  • The dominance of textiles in industrial employment and the concentration of wealth in the zaibatsu and landlord classes
  • Dependence on imported raw materials and on foreign markets, and the dependence of rural households on rice and silk prices
  • The colonial relationship in which Korea and Taiwan supplied food and raw materials to the metropole

Who Experienced Change Most Directly?

Industrialists, shipowners, traders, and skilled workers in expanding industries experienced the boom as rapid improvement. Urban wage earners, public servants, and tenant farmers experienced it chiefly as rising prices. Colonial populations in Korea and Taiwan experienced the economy's changing demands without any political voice.

Who Experienced Greater Continuity?

Landlords, large landholders, the established zaibatsu, and the senior political elite maintained their positions, though their relative power shifted. Rural tenants, in many districts, continued to live with the same rent obligations they had before the war.

Why the pattern matters: The wartime boom did not create a simple new Japan. It deepened existing divisions even as it enlarged national wealth, and the political crisis of 1918 grew from that combination.

Division VI · Section 66.27

The United States, Britain, and the Lansing–Ishii Agreement of 1917

The entry of the United States into the war on 6 April 1917 changed the diplomatic landscape. For the first time, Japan and the United States were on the same side in a major war, but they remained rivals in China and the Pacific. American policy toward China rested on the Open Door doctrine, the principle, announced in notes by Secretary of State John Hay in 1899 and 1900, that all nations should have equal commercial opportunity in China and that China's territorial and administrative integrity should be preserved. Japan accepted the Open Door in principle but argued that its geographical position and its investment in Manchuria gave it special interests. The Twenty-One Demands had tested the issue, and American officials, including Secretary of State Robert Lansing, who had replaced Bryan in 1915, sought a way to restrain Japanese ambitions without alienating a necessary ally.

In the summer of 1917, Japan sent a special mission headed by Viscount Ishii Kikujirō, a former foreign minister, to the United States. Its purpose was to explain Japanese policy, build understanding, and, if possible, obtain American recognition of Japan's position in China. Lansing and Ishii held a series of discussions, and on 2 November 1917 they exchanged notes. In them the two governments reaffirmed their commitment to the Open Door and to the independence and territorial integrity of China, and the United States acknowledged that territorial propinquity creates special relations between countries and that Japan therefore had special interests in China, particularly in the parts to which its possessions were contiguous. The notes also contained a mutual pledge that neither government would take advantage of the war to seek special rights that would abridge the rights of other states. A secret protocol to the same effect was kept confidential and revealed only later.

The agreement's meaning was contested from the start. Japanese officials and newspapers read the phrase about special interests as American recognition of Japan's leading position in China, especially in Manchuria and Shandong. American officials insisted that "special interests" meant economic interests arising from geography and that the reaffirmation of the Open Door and Chinese integrity controlled the meaning. The Chinese government protested that it was not bound by an agreement made without its participation and that no foreign government could acknowledge special interests in its territory. The agreement thus produced a diplomatic formula acceptable to both sides because they read it differently. It was later criticized in the United States as a concession to Japan, and the Washington Conference of 1921–1922 led to its formal cancellation in 1923.

The agreement also illustrates the wartime relationship between Japan and Britain. Britain had secretly promised in 1917 to support Japanese claims to Shandong and the Pacific islands, and it was anxious not to be seen as undermining American positions on China, while also wishing to retain Japanese naval cooperation. London therefore tried to avoid alienating either Washington or Tokyo. In 1918, the United States also proposed a new international financing consortium for China, in which Japan insisted on special treatment for its interests in Manchuria and Mongolia, a dispute that carried into 1919–1920. The Lansing–Ishii notes were a temporary truce, not a solution.

Division VI · Section 66.28

The Russian Revolutions and Japan's Strategic Uncertainty

Japan's relationship with Russia had been transformed since 1905. The two countries had moved from war to a series of agreements dividing Manchuria and Mongolia into spheres of influence (1907, 1910, 1912), and in July 1916 they signed a treaty of alliance-like character, which included secret provisions in which they promised to cooperate to prevent any third power from dominating China. During the war, Japan supplied the Russian army with rifles and ammunition and sold Russia ships. Japanese strategists regarded the Russian connection as a way to secure the northern frontier and Manchuria.

The collapse of the Russian state upset these calculations. The overthrow of the tsar in February (March, in the Western calendar) 1917 was followed by a weak Provisional Government, and then in November 1917 by the seizure of power by the Bolsheviks, who rapidly called for peace. The Soviet government concluded the Treaty of Brest-Litovsk with Germany in March 1918, taking Russia out of the war. Japanese observers worried about several possibilities: that Germany would gain control of Russian resources and of the Trans-Siberian Railway; that large stores of Allied war supplies at Vladivostok would fall into hostile hands; that revolutionary ideas would spread to Korea, Manchuria, and Japan; and that Russia's disintegration would create a power vacuum in the Russian Far East that other powers, notably the United States, might fill. Equally, some saw opportunity: the weakness of Russia might allow Japan to establish a dominant position in northern Manchuria and eastern Siberia.

Japanese opinion was divided. Terauchi, the army leadership (including Tanaka Giichi, then deputy chief of the General Staff), and Foreign Minister Motono Ichirō, and later his successor Gotō Shinpei, favored active intervention. Hara Takashi and others in the Advisory Council on Foreign Relations were more cautious, fearing that intervention would be costly and would damage Japan's relations with the United States, and they preferred to act only in concert with the Allies. The army was influenced by officers who had served in Siberia or Manchuria and by the presence of thousands of Japanese residents in Vladivostok and the Russian Far East. The navy sent ships to Vladivostok in January 1918, and the April 1918 killing of Japanese shopkeepers in Vladivostok led to the landing of Japanese and British marines on 5 April, an event that increased the pressure for a larger operation.

For Japan's northern territories, the stakes were also local. Karafuto, the southern half of Sakhalin acquired in 1905, was separated by a narrow strait from the northern half under Russian authority, and the fate of the Russian Far East bore on the security and resources of both parts of the island. The Ainu and other indigenous peoples of Sakhalin and the Amur region lived in a zone that was about to be fought over, though their own role in the story is poorly documented in the Japanese official records on which much of the narrative depends. The political uncertainty of 1917–1918 therefore connected Japan's strategic concerns about Manchuria, its colonial interests in Korea and Karafuto, and its ideological fear of revolution.

Division VI · Section 66.29

The Siberian Intervention of 1918: Allied Aims and Japanese Objectives

The Allied powers, led by Britain and France, pressed in 1918 for an intervention in Russia to reconstruct an eastern front against Germany, to protect Allied supply depots, and to support anti-Bolshevik forces. The Czechoslovak Legion became the central pretext. This force of some forty to fifty thousand former prisoners of war and others, who had fought for the Allied cause on the Russian side, was making its way eastward along the Trans-Siberian Railway to leave Russia by way of Vladivostok and rejoin the Allies in France. In May 1918, a dispute with local Soviet authorities led the Legion to rise in revolt, and it took control of much of the railway and several Siberian cities.

President Woodrow Wilson, who was reluctant about intervention, agreed in July 1918 to a limited operation. In an aide-mémoire dated 6 July, he proposed that the United States and Japan each send about seven thousand troops to Vladivostok, with the purpose of helping the Czechoslovaks and guarding military stores, and he stated that the action was not an intervention in Russian internal affairs. Japan was asked to take part under these limits. The Terauchi government agreed in principle but then expanded the plan. Japan announced in early August that it would send troops, and in the end it dispatched far more than seven thousand: some seventy thousand men, and by some accounts about 72,000, were deployed by November 1918. The troops were mostly drawn from the 12th Division and from other formations, under General Ōtani Kikuzō, who was designated commander of the Allied forces in the region. Japanese troops entered the Russian Far East through Vladivostok and from the Manchurian border, advancing along the railways toward Transbaikalia.

Japan's objectives were broader than the stated purpose. The army was interested in obtaining control over the Chinese Eastern Railway in northern Manchuria and the Siberian rail routes, in supporting anti-Bolshevik leaders such as Ataman Grigory Semyonov and Ivan Kalmykov who might accept Japanese influence, and in creating, if possible, a buffer state in Siberia. Japanese leaders also feared communism, and some saw the Russian Far East as a region of resources and settlement opportunity for Japanese traders and colonists. They also wanted to prevent other powers, especially the United States, from dominating the region. The Siberian intervention thus combined an Allied military and anti-revolutionary rationale with Japanese strategic and imperial aims. It cannot be explained by either one alone, since Allied concern for supplies and the Czechoslovaks, anti-Bolshevik fear, and Japanese ambition operated together.

The operation's costs became apparent quickly. Winter conditions, long supply lines, partisan warfare, and disease took a toll on Japanese forces. Historians' estimates of Japanese casualties for the whole intervention differ, but they run to the thousands, with disease and exposure accounting for many of the dead. The financial cost was very large, absorbing a substantial share of the army's budget. Domestic criticism grew, and the intervention became one of the most controversial aspects of Japanese policy in the immediate postwar years. The intervention's extension beyond 1918 and its eventual end in 1922 are bounded here by the chapter's chronology and are discussed only briefly in Section 66.30.

Division VI · Section 66.30

Allied Rivalries, Russian Communities, and the Limits of Wartime Cooperation

The Allies in Siberia did not act as a unified force. American officers and officials suspected that Japanese forces intended to take territory and to support Japanese-backed Russian leaders, and Americans sometimes complained about Japanese conduct and about the numbers deployed. The American expedition of some eight thousand men under General William S. Graves tried to avoid involvement in Russian politics, and the United States also sent a railway service group to help operate the Trans-Siberian line. Japanese officers, for their part, regarded the Americans as naive about conditions and as insufficiently committed to anti-Bolshevik goals. British, French, Italian, Canadian, and Chinese contingents were small, and their governments pursued their own priorities. The Czechoslovak Legion, which sought only to leave Russia, found itself entangled in Russian politics and dependent on Allied cooperation.

The Russian communities in the region experienced the intervention in very different ways. Cossack atamans backed by Japan, such as Semyonov and Kalmykov, ruled districts with a reputation for violence and extortion, and their relations with local peasants, Russian settlers, and Chinese and Korean residents were harsh. Partisan units of peasants, workers, and Bolsheviks fought against the interventionists and their allies, and Japanese troops conducted counterinsurgency operations that included reprisals against villages suspected of aiding partisans. Reports of such actions, including the March 1919 destruction of Ivanovka in the Amur region after fighting near Yufta, are documented in Russian and foreign accounts. The destruction of the village and killing of civilians are well attested, though differing accounts of responsibility for individual acts and the number of victims require careful source criticism. Korean and Chinese residents of the Russian Far East, some of whom supported anti-Japanese resistance, were also affected. The experience of civilians is much less visible in Japanese military records than the movements of units, and historians must rely on a variety of Russian, American, and other sources.

The intervention continued beyond the armistice of 11 November 1918. The original Allied purpose, to help the Czechoslovaks and keep Russia in the war against Germany, lost its main justification when Germany surrendered. The American government withdrew its troops in April 1920, and most other Allies left in 1919 and 1920, but Japan stayed. The Japanese government cited the need to protect Japanese residents, to prevent the spread of revolution, and to guard the border of Korea and Manchuria. After the killing of Japanese soldiers and civilians at Nikolayevsk on the Amur by partisans in 1920, Japan also occupied northern Sakhalin. Public and parliamentary criticism in Japan grew, and Japanese forces finally withdrew from the mainland in October 1922 and from northern Sakhalin in 1925. These later events lie outside the chronology of this chapter, but they are essential for understanding why historians regard the intervention as a failure of Japanese policy and a source of suspicion among both the Allies and the Soviet government.

Division VII · Section 66.31

Japan at the Paris Peace Conference: Saionji, Makino, and Great-Power Diplomacy

The Paris Peace Conference opened in January 1919, and Japan took part as one of the five principal Allied and Associated Powers, alongside the United States, Britain, France, and Italy. This status was itself a measure of change. Thirty years earlier, Japan had still been negotiating the revision of unequal treaties. Now its representatives sat in the Council of Ten, which directed the early work of the conference, and, although Japan did not sit in the smaller Council of Four that later made many of the most important decisions, its delegates were present when matters touching Japanese interests were discussed.

The Japanese delegation was headed by Saionji Kinmochi, the last of the genrō who still took an active role, who was elderly and frequently ill. Its working leaders were Makino Nobuaki, a former foreign minister, and Chinda Sutemi, ambassador in London, together with Matsui Keishirō and Ijūin Hikokichi, ambassadors to France and Italy. The delegation's principal objectives were clear. It wanted recognition of Japanese rights in Shandong, confirmation of Japanese possession of the Pacific islands north of the equator, and the inclusion of a racial equality principle in the League of Nations Covenant. It also wished to secure a position of full equality among the great powers. Japan's contribution to the conference's deliberations on European questions was limited, and Western observers sometimes described the Japanese delegates as reserved or silent on matters that did not concern them, a perception partly reflecting language barriers and partly reflecting a deliberate strategy of focusing on core interests.

The leading personalities of the conference were Woodrow Wilson, David Lloyd George, Georges Clemenceau, and Vittorio Orlando. Wilson's Fourteen Points and his proposal for a League of Nations framed much of the discussion, and Wilson's language of self-determination and the rights of peoples was heard throughout the world, including in Korea and China, where activists took it as promising an end to imperial domination. The Japanese government's attitude to this language was ambivalent. Japanese leaders wanted to participate in the new order, but they feared that principles of self-determination could be turned against their own empire, and, as the following sections show, the Shandong question and the racial equality proposal tested what the new order meant in practice.

In Japan itself, opinions about the peace and the League varied. Some liberals, including Yoshino Sakuzō, welcomed the League and the principle of international cooperation. Others, including the young aristocrat Konoe Fumimaro, who accompanied the delegation, argued in a widely read essay in late 1918 that Japan should reject what he saw as an Anglo-American peace that would freeze the existing distribution of wealth and power, and that Japan should demand a more equitable system including racial equality and access to resources. The government of Hara Takashi, which directed the delegation from Tokyo, was more pragmatic, and it sought to maintain good relations with Britain and the United States while securing Japan's specific goals.

Division VII · Section 66.32

The Shandong Settlement: Japanese Claims and Chinese Objections

The Shandong question was the most contested issue involving Japan at Paris. It was argued before the Council of Ten on 27 and 28 January 1919. The Japanese delegates, Makino and Chinda, claimed that Japan should receive the German rights in Shandong that it had captured in 1914. They based this claim on its military conquest, on the Sino-Japanese treaty and notes of 1915, on the exchange of notes of September 1918 in which the Chinese government had agreed to Japanese proposals regarding Shandong railways, and on the secret undertakings of 1917 made by Britain, France, Italy, and Russia. They also said that Japan intended eventually to return the leased territory to China, in line with its 1914 ultimatum, though they declined to commit to a date or conditions.

China sent a delegation that included Lu Zhengxiang, Wang Zhengting (C. T. Wang), and the young diplomat Wellington Koo, who made the principal presentation on Shandong. Koo and his colleagues argued that China, having declared war on Germany in 1917, had cancelled all treaties with Germany, so that German rights in Shandong had reverted to China; that the 1915 treaty and the 1918 exchange of notes had been signed under duress and were invalid; and that Shandong, the birthplace of Confucius and a densely populated province of great cultural importance, could not be cut away from China. They also noted that China had contributed laborers to the Allied cause. China's delegates stressed that the Chinese claim was based on the principle of national integrity that the conference's leaders said they supported.

The argument created a dilemma for the conference. Wilson was sympathetic to China but had to weigh the claims against other considerations. Britain and France had made secret promises to Japan in 1917 and felt bound by them. Japan's delegates made clear that they considered the issue vital and hinted that Japan might not join the League of Nations if its claims were rejected. The conference was also in crisis over other matters: the Italian delegation walked out in late April over the Fiume question, and Wilson worried about the integrity of the coalition and the League. On 30 April 1919, Wilson, Lloyd George, and Clemenceau agreed that German rights in Shandong would be transferred to Japan, not to China. The Japanese delegates gave an assurance, spoken rather than written into the treaty, that Japan intended to return full sovereignty to China and retain only the economic privileges Germany had held, including those of the railway.

The decision was incorporated in the Treaty of Versailles, in which Germany renounced its Shandong rights in favor of Japan (Articles 156 to 158). It was bitterly resented in China. For many Chinese, it seemed that the principles proclaimed by Wilson had been abandoned when they conflicted with great-power interests, and that China, a wartime ally, had been treated as a defeated state. Wilson himself was uncomfortable with the outcome, and he later recognized that it was a compromise made to preserve the League. The question was not finally resolved until 1922, when, at the Washington Conference and in a bilateral treaty, Japan agreed to return Shandong's leased territory and sell the railway to China. The Paris decision thus gave Japan a diplomatic victory that was short-lived and costly in Chinese goodwill.

Division VII · Section 66.33

The May Fourth Movement and the Chinese Refusal to Accept the Settlement

News of the Shandong decision reached Beijing in the first days of May 1919. Students, who had followed the conference closely and had hoped that Wilson's principles would vindicate China, were furious. On 4 May, several thousand students from Peking University and other colleges assembled at Tiananmen, issued manifestos protesting the settlement, and marched toward the Legation Quarter. They then went to the residence of the pro-Japanese official Cao Rulin, who as vice minister of foreign affairs had negotiated the 1915 agreements and the 1918 loans. The crowd burned the house and beat Zhang Zongxiang, the former minister to Japan who was present. Police arrested about thirty students. The incident gave the movement its name.

The protests spread. Students in other cities organized demonstrations and boycotts, and in early June, merchants and workers in Shanghai joined with strikes and shop closures in support of the students and in protest at their arrests, a significant expansion from student protest to wider urban participation. The government released the arrested students and, on 10 June, dismissed Cao Rulin and two other officials associated with Japan. Chinese public pressure then helped to persuade the Chinese delegation at Paris not to sign the Treaty of Versailles on 28 June 1919. China did not sign the treaty and made peace with Germany separately later. Boycotts of Japanese goods spread again, and anti-Japanese feeling became a standing element of Chinese nationalism.

The May Fourth Movement is generally treated in Chinese history as part of a broader intellectual and cultural ferment, the New Culture Movement, which included debate about language reform, Confucianism, democracy, science, and socialism. That history belongs to the study of China. For this chapter, the significance is the effect on Japan's regional standing. The movement showed that Japan's gains in China depended on a Chinese state whose legitimacy and capacity were increasingly tied to nationalist opposition to Japan, and it demonstrated that a new kind of Chinese public opinion had emerged, which Japanese diplomats and military officers had to take into account. It occurred, too, in the same spring as the March First Movement in Korea, a mass independence movement that Japanese authorities suppressed with considerable violence, and Chinese students drew inspiration from Korean protest, as Koreans had drawn inspiration from Wilson's language of self-determination.

Japanese press and government reaction was largely hostile to the Chinese protests. Newspapers portrayed the demonstrations as anti-Japanese agitation encouraged by Americans or by Chinese politicians, and the government worried about the safety of Japanese residents and merchants in China. Some Japanese observers, including a few liberals, recognized that the movement expressed a deep national feeling that could not be ignored and that Japan's China policy, based on cooperation with Duan Qirui and other warlords, was out of touch with Chinese opinion. The Hara government's response was cautious: it reaffirmed its promise to return Shandong's sovereignty, but it did not retreat from its position until the Washington Conference several years later.

Division VII · Section 66.34

The South Seas Mandate and Japan's Expansion in the Pacific

The third major Japanese aim at Paris concerned the Pacific islands. Japan held the former German islands north of the equator by military occupation since October 1914, and the 1917 secret agreements had given it British and other Allied backing. At Paris, however, the question arose of what legal form Japan's control would take. President Wilson and others opposed outright annexation of former German colonies by the victors, and the compromise was the mandate system, set out in Article 22 of the League of Nations Covenant. Under this system, former German and Ottoman territories were assigned to member states as mandatories, which governed them on behalf of the League with a duty to promote the well-being and development of their inhabitants.

The mandates were divided into three classes according to the supposed development of their peoples. Japan's islands were designated Class C mandates, the category covering territories considered best administered as integral parts of the mandatory's own territory, along with, for example, South West Africa. Japan's mandate over the Marianas (except Guam), Carolines, and Marshalls was confirmed by the League Council on 17 December 1920. The terms prohibited the construction of military or naval bases and fortifications and the military training of the inhabitants, required the mandatory to protect the rights of the islanders, and obliged it to report annually to the League's Permanent Mandates Commission. The Japanese authorities called the territory the South Seas Islands (Nan'yō Guntō); a civil administration, the South Seas Bureau (Nan'yō-chō), was established in 1922 with its headquarters at Koror in Palau.

The legal distinction between a mandate and formal annexation is important but not absolute. Japan did not hold sovereignty in the formal sense, and it accepted obligations to the League. In practice, as a Class C mandatary, it administered the islands as if they were part of its own territory, and the League's supervision was weak. The people of the islands, including Chamorro and Carolinians in the Marianas, Palauans, Yapese, Chuukese, Pohnpeians, Kosraeans, and Marshallese, were not consulted about the mandate. They experienced the arrival of a new colonial authority which promoted Japanese language education, introduced new economic enterprises such as sugar cultivation on Saipan, and, in the following years, encouraged large-scale immigration of Japanese settlers, many of them from Okinawa. Those developments, which changed the demography of parts of the islands, extend beyond the chapter's chronology, and later chapters will return to them. The surviving administrative records are largely Japanese, and the historians of Micronesia emphasize the need to recover islander perspectives from oral history and other sources.

Allied and American reactions to the mandate were mixed. The United States objected to the assignment of Yap to Japan, which in 1919–1921 became a controversy known as the Yap question, since the island was a major cable center, and the dispute was settled in an American–Japanese treaty in 1922 that guaranteed American cable rights. The prohibition of fortifications would become a point of contention in later decades, when American officials suspected that Japan was building military facilities in violation of the mandate. In 1919 and 1920, the mandate nevertheless confirmed Japan's expansion in the Pacific and gave a legal form, acceptable to the League, to what had begun as naval occupation.

Historical Vocabulary
League Mandate, International Responsibility, and Sovereignty

A mandate was a form of international trusteeship created by Article 22 of the Covenant of the League of Nations, under which a victorious power administered a former German or Ottoman territory on behalf of the League, with obligations to the inhabitants and to the League's Permanent Mandates Commission. It was designed as an alternative to outright annexation, reflecting Wilsonian reluctance to transfer colonies as spoils of war, though critics at the time and since have pointed out that it gave imperial rule a more acceptable legal form. Class C mandates, including Japan's, allowed the mandatory to administer the territory as an integral part of its own.

Sovereignty is the supreme legal authority of a state over its territory. In a mandate, sovereignty was formally ambiguous: the mandatory exercised extensive powers without being the sovereign, and jurists debated where sovereignty lay. The islanders themselves had no recognized sovereign voice. The concept of international responsibility meant that the mandatory was accountable to an international body for how it governed, an important novelty in international law, though enforcement was weak.

Division VII · Section 66.35

The Racial Equality Proposal: Japan and the Limits of the New World Order

Japan's fourth principal aim at Paris was the inclusion of a racial equality clause in the League of Nations Covenant. The proposal grew out of long-standing Japanese grievances over discrimination against Japanese immigrants in the United States, Canada, and Australia, and over the treatment of Japan by Western powers in general. The immigration restrictions of the Pacific coast and the White Australia policy were deeply resented, and Japanese leaders, who had worked since the Meiji period to win equal treatment with the West, were sensitive to any suggestion of racial inferiority. In February 1919, the Japanese delegation first proposed an amendment to the Covenant's article on religious freedom. Facing opposition, it revised the proposal into a milder statement for the Covenant's preamble, endorsing the principle of the equality of nations and the just treatment of their nationals.

Makino Nobuaki presented the proposal to the League of Nations Commission on 11 April 1919. According to National Diet Library research and other accounts, a majority of the commission members present voted in favor: eleven of the seventeen commission members present supported it, including representatives of France, Italy, and China, despite the Shandong conflict. The British delegation, led by Lord Robert Cecil, opposed it, largely because of objections from Australia, whose prime minister Hughes feared that it would threaten the White Australia policy. No formal tally of negative votes was recorded, so the balance of those present should not be described simply as an opposing bloc. President Wilson, chairing the commission, ruled that so important an amendment required unanimity, or at least no strong opposition, and he declared that it had not been adopted. Japan's delegates protested but did not press the matter further, and the Covenant was adopted without the clause.

The episode requires careful interpretation. It is true that a majority of the commission supported the Japanese proposal and that Wilson's procedural ruling prevented its adoption, but it would be inaccurate to say that all countries except Japan opposed racial equality. Support came from several European states and China, and the strongest opposition came from the British Empire delegation, largely because of dominion pressure, and from some others, with American domestic politics, especially on the Pacific coast and in the Senate, also a concern for Wilson. Nor was the Japanese proposal in its final form a demand for universal racial equality in the sense that later generations would understand it. It was a request for the equal treatment of the nationals of member states, and Japanese leaders themselves treated Shandong as a higher priority. The Japanese government did not make the racial clause a condition for entering the League.

The proposal also exposed a tension within Japanese policy. Japan demanded equality among great powers and the removal of discrimination against its emigrants, but it governed Korea, Taiwan, Karafuto, and now Micronesia in a colonial hierarchy that treated their inhabitants unequally. On 1 March 1919, as the delegates met in Paris, Koreans had begun the nationwide independence demonstrations known as the March First Movement, which the colonial authorities suppressed with arrests, shootings, and the destruction of villages. Japanese claims to racial equality in the international community did not extend to equality within the Japanese Empire, and Korean, Chinese, and other Asian activists noted the contradiction. The proposal's rejection was a source of lasting bitterness in Japan and of reflection about the limits of international acceptance, and it also helped strengthen arguments among some Japanese intellectuals and officers that Japan's destiny lay in leadership of Asia rather than in equal partnership with the West.

Division VII · Section 66.36

Japan in the League of Nations: Achievement, Contradiction, and Historical Legacy

The League of Nations came into being in January 1920, and Japan was one of its founding members and one of four permanent members of its Council, together with Britain, France, and Italy, since the United States, whose Senate rejected the Versailles Treaty, did not join. Japan's place on the Council was a significant diplomatic achievement and symbolized its acceptance among the leading powers. Japanese representatives took active parts in League work. The educator and writer Nitobe Inazō served as an Under-Secretary-General from 1920 to 1926, and the ethnologist Yanagita Kunio sat on the Permanent Mandates Commission in the early 1920s. Japan also participated in the International Labour Organization, created in 1919, though its labor legislation lagged behind the standards that organization promoted.

Japan's international position had improved greatly since 1914. It had territories in the Pacific confirmed by international mandate, a Shandong foothold supported by the peace treaty, a powerful navy that was now among the three largest in the world, an industrial economy substantially stronger than before, and financial reserves that made it a creditor. Japanese diplomats could argue that they had achieved the goal Japan had pursued since the Meiji Restoration: recognition as a great power. At the same time, the war had left Japan with new problems. The relationship with China was seriously damaged, American suspicion of Japanese intentions had grown, and Britain, whose alliance with Japan was increasingly questioned in the dominions and in Washington, was reconsidering it. At the Washington Conference of 1921–1922, the Anglo-Japanese Alliance was replaced by a Four-Power Treaty, naval limitation put Japan's fleet in a ratio below that of Britain and the United States, and Japan agreed to the Shandong settlement. These developments lie outside the chapter's chronology, but they show how the achievements of 1919 were quickly qualified.

Domestically, the war's economic consequences remained to be absorbed. The postwar boom of 1919 gave way to the crash of 1920, and the structural weaknesses of the financial system would persist through the 1920s. The Siberian Intervention continued to drain resources. The social movements encouraged by the war years, in labor, tenant farming, women's rights, and the Burakumin liberation movement, would flourish in the early 1920s (Chapters 67 to 69). The war thus produced a Japan that was stronger and more internationally prominent, but also more exposed to domestic conflict and more entangled in the affairs of its neighbors.

Historical Debate

Did the First World War Strengthen Japan's Long-Term International Position or Create Deeper Strategic Contradictions?

The question: Historians agree that Japan emerged from the war with greater power and status. They disagree about whether this strengthened Japan's long-term security or planted the seeds of later conflict.

Interpretation A — A net strengthening: Some scholars argue that the war gave Japan lasting advantages: a larger industrial base, creditor status, the Pacific islands, naval strength, a permanent seat on the League Council, and a firm place in the international system. Japan avoided the costs of the European war, and its leaders learned to operate through international institutions. On this view, Japanese diplomacy in the 1920s, including cooperation with Britain and the United States under the Washington system, was a rational use of the strengthened position, and the later crisis of the 1930s was a result of different factors, including the depression and domestic politics, rather than of the war.
Interpretation B — Strategic contradictions: Other scholars argue that Japan's wartime gains created problems that it could not resolve. The Shandong settlement and the Twenty-One Demands alienated Chinese nationalism and encouraged a hostile China. The Siberian Intervention cost money and lives and produced no gain. American and British suspicion of Japanese ambitions grew, and the alliance with Britain was eventually terminated. The war boom did not rest on sustainable foundations and it increased social tension. On this view, Japan acquired the appearance of power while committing itself to positions in China and the Pacific that required the use of force to defend, a pattern that shaped later decisions.

Evidence: Both interpretations use the same body of diplomatic, economic, and military evidence, together with the later history of the 1920s and 1930s. They differ in how much weight to give the war's long-term consequences compared with later events, and in how they define "strength." Historians such as Frederick Dickinson, who has emphasized Japan's wartime "national reinvention" and its engagement with new international ideas, give more weight to the possibility that Japanese leaders drew constructive lessons from the war, while others emphasize continuity in Japanese imperial ambitions.

Why it matters: If the war strengthened Japan, the later turn toward militarism requires explanations rooted in the 1920s and 1930s. If the war created contradictions, the origins of later conflict can be traced to 1914–1919. Students should note that these interpretations can be partly reconciled: Japan may have gained real strength while also acquiring commitments and enemies that limited its choices. They should also avoid teleology. Japan's later history was not determined by the events of 1919.

Student task: Choose one of Japan's wartime gains (the Pacific islands, creditor status, the League seat, or the Shandong settlement) and explain how it could be read as both an asset and a liability.

Chapter Review and Historical Thinking Checkpoint

This chapter has examined Japan's participation in the First World War as a military engagement, an imperial opportunity, an economic turning point, and a diplomatic transformation. Before moving on, students should be able to demonstrate understanding of the foundational insights of the chapter and to use them as tools of historical reasoning.

The core insights are these. Japan entered the war within the framework of the Anglo-Japanese Alliance but by a decision of its own leaders, who combined alliance participation with the pursuit of additional advantages in Shandong and the Pacific. Japanese forces conducted a significant land campaign at Qingdao, occupied German Micronesia, patrolled Pacific and Indian Ocean waters, and sent a naval squadron to the Mediterranean, but Japan did not experience the war's casualties or destruction. The Twenty-One Demands of 1915 extended Japan's rights in Manchuria and Shandong and caused a lasting Chinese nationalist reaction, and after 1916 Japan turned toward financial influence through the Nishihara Loans. The war transformed the Japanese economy, producing export growth, heavy-industry expansion, large trade and payments surpluses, and creditor status, but wartime profits were unevenly distributed and inflation eroded the living standards of many wage earners, leading to the Rice Riots of 1918 and the fall of the Terauchi government. The Siberian Intervention, originally justified by Allied needs, became a large and costly Japanese commitment that outlasted the war. At Paris, Japan obtained the award of German rights in Shandong, a mandate over the Pacific islands, and a permanent seat on the League Council, but it failed to win the racial equality clause and faced Chinese refusal to sign the treaty. The result was a stronger Japan with deeper tensions.

Students should be able to answer these questions in their own words: Why is it inaccurate to say that Britain ordered Japan into the war? How do the original demands, the revised proposal, and the final agreements of 1915 differ? Why do nominal trade statistics overstate real growth? Why did the Siberian Intervention continue after the armistice? What distinguishes a mandate from annexation? Why is it wrong to say that every country but Japan opposed racial equality at Paris? The review materials that follow, including the chronology check, source questions, and writing exercises, are designed to help in answering them.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that the First World War was, for Japan, simultaneously a military engagement, an imperial opportunity, an economic turning point, and a diplomatic transformation. Japan did not suffer the casualties or destruction of the European powers, but neither was it a mere bystander. It fought a major siege at Qingdao, occupied German Micronesia, patrolled the Pacific and Indian Oceans, and sent a destroyer force to the Mediterranean that escorted several hundred transports and lost sailors in the Aegean. The chapter's central claim is that these achievements brought greater international influence and industrial power, while also intensifying conflict over Chinese sovereignty, exposing contradictions between claims of equality among nations and imperial rule, and creating domestic economic strains that fed the political instability of the early Taishō period.

Japan's entry into the war in August 1914 was shaped by the Anglo-Japanese Alliance but was not required by it. Britain asked for limited help against German raiders, tried to narrow the request, and then accepted a Japanese declaration of war whose terms Katō Takaaki and the Ōkuma cabinet had defined for themselves. The ultimatum of 15 August demanded the German leased territory at Jiaozhou with the stated purpose of eventual restoration to China, language that left the future of Shandong open. The distinction between alliance participation and the pursuit of additional advantages is the thread that runs through the entire chapter.

The military campaigns of 1914 created the physical and legal facts on which later diplomacy rested. The landing at Longkou, the advance across Chinese territory, the seizure of the Shandong railway, and the capture of Qingdao established a Japanese presence in Shandong that China had not agreed to and that Japan sought to turn into treaty rights. The naval occupation of Micronesia did the same for the Pacific. The later Mediterranean operation, undertaken in 1917, was linked to secret Allied undertakings to support Japan's claims, so that naval service and territorial diplomacy were joined.

The Twenty-One Demands of January 1915 were the most consequential episode in Japan's wartime relations with China. Presented in five groups to Yuan Shikai, they sought to extend Japan's rights in Manchuria and Shandong and, in Group 5, to give Japan influence over Chinese advisers, police, and arms purchases. Chinese leaks and American and British objections led to revision, an ultimatum, and agreements in May 1915 that excluded most of Group 5 but confirmed extensive Japanese rights. Historians debate whether the demands were continuous with the older concession-seeking of the imperial powers or a qualitatively greater ambition. Whichever view is taken, the episode produced a durable Chinese nationalist memory and damaged Japan's reputation abroad.

After 1916, under Terauchi Masatake, Japan shifted from ultimatum to financial influence. The Nishihara Loans, extended mainly to the government of Duan Qirui, were intended to secure a friendly Beijing government and Japanese economic concessions, and they were accompanied by military agreements in 1918 and by the Chinese declaration of war on Germany. The policy yielded little lasting influence, was widely resented in China, and was criticized in Japan. It nevertheless reveals how Japan was learning to use its new financial strength as a tool of foreign policy.

The wartime economy transformed Japan's international financial position. Exports more than tripled in nominal terms between 1914 and 1918, though inflation accounts for part of the increase; trade and payments surpluses produced large inflows of gold and foreign balances, and Japan moved from heavy debtor to creditor. Shipbuilding, steel, chemicals, and electric power expanded, and import substitution replaced goods formerly supplied by Germany and Britain. Yet the evidence shows that the boom enriched shipowners, traders, and industrialists much more than it improved the lives of many wage earners, public servants, and tenant farmers, whose real incomes fell as prices rose, particularly the price of rice. The same economy produced both the new rich and the hungry.

The domestic political consequences were visible by 1918. The Ōkuma government, which had presided over the entry into the war and the Twenty-One Demands, gave way to Terauchi's non-party cabinet, which presided over the Nishihara Loans, the Siberian Intervention, and finally the Rice Riots of July to September 1918. The riots, the largest popular disturbance in Japan since the Restoration, brought down Terauchi and led to the formation of Hara Takashi's party cabinet. Chapters 67 to 69 examine the political and social developments that followed.

The Siberian Intervention illustrates the limits of wartime cooperation. Allied powers justified the operation as a rescue of the Czechoslovak Legion and protection of military supplies, and President Wilson proposed contributions of about seven thousand troops each from the United States and Japan. Japan sent roughly ten times that number, pursued its own strategic objectives in Manchuria and the Russian Far East, and remained after the Allies left, until 1922. Russian civilians experienced the intervention as foreign occupation and reprisal, and Japanese domestic opinion came to regard it as costly and unproductive.

At Paris in 1919, Japan was recognized as one of the principal powers. It secured the award of German rights in Shandong, a Class C mandate over the former German islands north of the equator, and a permanent seat on the League Council. It failed to win the racial equality clause, which a majority of the League commission supported but which President Wilson ruled had not been adopted, and its Shandong award provoked the May Fourth Movement and China's refusal to sign the Treaty of Versailles. The Paris settlement thus combined achievement and contradiction: Japan was recognized as an equal among powers, but its claims for equality abroad coexisted with imperial hierarchies at home, as Korea's March First Movement and the experience of Pacific islanders demonstrated.

The chapter has also kept several peoples in view who are often excluded from accounts of Japan's war: Chinese officials, merchants, and students; Micronesian islanders; Russian villagers; Korean and Taiwanese colonial subjects; Okinawan sugar growers; and the women and men who worked in mills, mines, and farms. Their experiences show that a "Japanese" history of the war must include those who lived under, resisted, or were excluded from the benefits of Japanese power. Japan's position in 1919 was stronger than in 1914, but it rested on commitments in China, Siberia, and the Pacific that would shape its choices in the decades that followed, though without making those later choices inevitable.

Key Terms

Anglo-Japanese Alliance
The treaty alliance between Britain and Japan, signed in 1902 and renewed in 1905 and 1911, which framed Japan's decision to enter the war without legally obliging it to do so.
Ōshū Taisen
The "Great European War," the Japanese name for the First World War in its early years, reflecting the sense of it as a European conflict.
Ultimatum of 15 August 1914
The Japanese demand that Germany withdraw its warships and surrender the Jiaozhou leased territory, with a vague promise of eventual restoration to China.
Leased territory
An area leased to a foreign power by a Chinese government under duress, with sovereignty nominally retained by China, as at Jiaozhou, Port Arthur, and Weihaiwei.
Sphere of influence
A region in which a foreign power claimed preferential economic and political rights, often without formal legal basis.
Jiaozhou (Kiaochow) Bay
The German leased territory on the Shandong coast whose port, Qingdao, Japan captured in 1914.
Shandong Railway
The railway from Qingdao to Jinan, built by Germany and seized by Japan in 1914; its control was a core issue in Sino-Japanese disputes.
Second Special Squadron
The Japanese naval force based at Malta from April 1917, which escorted Allied transports in the Mediterranean.
Twenty-One Demands
The five-group list of Japanese demands presented to China on 18 January 1915, revised in April and reduced in the agreements signed on 25 May 1915.
Group 5
The most intrusive section of the demands, seeking Japanese advisers, joint police, and arms purchases; withdrawn from the final agreements except for Fujian provisions.
Genrō
The surviving Meiji oligarchs who informally advised the emperor and chose prime ministers, whose influence declined in the Taishō period.
Open Door policy
The American principle of equal commercial access to China and respect for its territorial integrity.
Nishihara Loans
Japanese loans of about ¥145 million to the Duan Qirui government in 1917–1918, intended to secure influence in Beijing.
Narikin
A term for those who became rich suddenly during the war boom, drawn from the shōgi promotion of a pawn.
Creditor nation
A country whose overseas assets exceed its liabilities; Japan became one for the first time during the war.
Specie
Coin or bullion, especially gold; Japan's specie holdings rose sharply during the war.
Nominal and real values
Nominal figures are measured in current prices; real figures are adjusted for inflation. The distinction is essential for reading wartime statistics.
Chōzen naikaku
A "transcendental cabinet" formed without party leadership, as under Terauchi Masatake.
Yūaikai
The Friendly Society, founded in 1912 by Suzuki Bunji, a labor organization that grew rapidly during and after the war.
White Rainbow Incident
The 1918 prosecution of the Ōsaka Asahi over an article during the press protest against censorship of Rice Riot reporting.
Lansing–Ishii Agreement
The U.S.–Japan notes of 2 November 1917 acknowledging Japan's "special interests" in China while reaffirming the Open Door.
Czechoslovak Legion
A force of former prisoners and others in Russia whose stranded position helped justify Allied intervention in Siberia.
Siberian Intervention
The Allied and Japanese deployment to the Russian Far East and Siberia beginning in 1918; Japan's part lasted until 1922.
Treaty of Versailles, Articles 156–158
The provisions transferring Germany's Shandong rights to Japan, which China refused to accept by signing.
May Fourth Movement
The Chinese protest movement that began on 4 May 1919 against the Shandong settlement, spreading from students to merchants and workers.
Mandate
A League of Nations arrangement under which a power administered a former German or Ottoman territory with obligations to the inhabitants and to the League.
South Seas Mandate (Nan'yō Guntō)
The Japanese Class C mandate over the former German islands north of the equator, confirmed in 1920 and administered from Koror from 1922.
Racial equality proposal
The Japanese amendment, presented 11 April 1919, endorsing the equality of nations and just treatment of their nationals; supported by a majority but ruled not adopted.
League Council
The executive body of the League of Nations, on which Japan held a permanent seat with Britain, France, and Italy.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows a country achieving the status it had sought for half a century at the moment when the foundations of that status were being tested. Japan's participation in the war confirmed its place among the great powers, yet the same events revealed that recognition was conditional, that imperial gains generated opposition, and that prosperity could coexist with deprivation. The chapter's treatment of Shandong, the Twenty-One Demands, and the Paris settlement reveals how Japan's most visible diplomatic successes were also the sources of enduring conflict.

The chapter also reveals larger processes. The war accelerated Japan's transformation from a debtor, textile-exporting economy into a heavier industrial and financial power, and it intensified the social conflicts that the next chapters examine: the Rice Riots, the growth of labor and tenant movements, and the rise of party government. It also showed how imperial expansion and domestic politics were linked. Siberian intervention, Chinese policy, and the Pacific mandate were decisions made by a narrow circle of leaders, but their costs were borne by soldiers, taxpayers, Chinese and Russian civilians, and island peoples who had no voice in the decisions.

Several continuities survived beyond 1919. The pattern of using military occupation, treaty claims, and financial leverage to secure positions in China persisted, and so did Chinese nationalist resistance. The Pacific islands remained under Japanese administration until 1945, their inhabitants subject to developments that later chapters will describe. Korea and Taiwan continued to supply food and raw materials to the metropole, and the autonomy of the services within the constitutional order remained a structural feature. The growing public and parliamentary scrutiny of foreign policy that began in the war years would also shape the politics of the 1920s.

For the larger history of Japan, the First World War is a bridge between the empire-building of Chapters 60 to 62 and the crises of the 1920s and 1930s. It connected different regions of the archipelago and empire, from Hokkaido's bean fields and Okinawa's sugar cane to Osaka's mills and Kobe's shipyards, and it connected Japan to China, Russia, the United States, Britain, and the Pacific world. Students who understand it as a set of choices made under particular conditions, with real alternatives and unequal consequences, are well prepared to approach the politics, society, and culture of Taishō Japan in the chapters that follow.
