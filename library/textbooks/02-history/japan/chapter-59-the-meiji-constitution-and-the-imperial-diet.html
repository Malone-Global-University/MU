Part IX — Constitution, Industry, and Empire

The Meiji Constitution and the Imperial Diet

Japanese History: From Jōmon Japan to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

On 11 February 1889, in the imperial palace in Tokyo, the Meiji Emperor presented a written constitution to his prime minister. The same day, the education minister Mori Arinori was stabbed by an assailant outside his official residence; he died the next morning. Ceremony and violence arrived together, and the day introduced a constitutional order that would prove at once real and sharply limited.

The Constitution of the Empire of Japan, commonly called the Meiji Constitution, was not a fiction and not a democracy. It created an elected House of Representatives, a legally defined legislature, an independent judiciary, and a catalogue of subjects' rights. It also declared the emperor sovereign, left ministers answerable to the throne rather than to the elected chamber, restricted the franchise to a tiny fraction of the population, and preserved powers that elected representatives could not touch. This chapter studies how such a system was designed and how it behaved when the men who had built it met the men elected under it.

The central question is therefore not whether Meiji Japan became "constitutional," but what kind of constitutionalism emerged, who held power within it, and what happened when ministers and elected representatives disagreed. Three forms of power must be kept distinct throughout: the legal authority written into the constitutional text; the practical authority exercised by a small circle of senior statesmen and the bureaucracy; and the political influence that parties and elected representatives won through the budget, legislation, and public argument.

The chapter's principal period runs from the political crisis of 1881, when the government promised a national assembly, to the eve of the Sino-Japanese War in 1894. Three dates anchor it. The constitution was promulgated on 11 February 1889; the first House of Representatives election was held on 1 July 1890; and the first Diet session convened on 25 November 1890, with the formal imperial opening and the constitution's entry into force on 29 November. These events are separate and should not be collapsed into one.

The chapter also resists teleology. It is tempting to read the 1889 text as a blueprint that made later militarism or, alternatively, later party government inevitable. The evidence points elsewhere. The framework contained openings that elected politicians exploited and structures that could limit them, and early outcomes depended on personalities, fiscal facts, and decisions made under pressure in the first Diet sessions.

The evidence is unusually rich. Historians use the constitutional text and its official commentary, Privy Council deliberations, drafters' papers and memoranda, the Official Gazette, Diet proceedings, newspapers, police and prefectural reports on elections, and the memoirs and correspondence of participants. Each has a perspective: drafters' records show elite intentions, newspapers show partisan argument, and police files show the state's view of disorder. Very few sources record the voices of people who had no vote at all, including women, tenant farmers, and residents of Hokkaido and Okinawa, which is itself a historical fact.

This chapter follows the Freedom and People's Rights Movement (Chapter 57) and takes its demands as its immediate background, without retelling them. It prepares Chapters 60 through 62 on war and empire, and Chapters 67 through 74, which return to party government, suffrage, and the collapse of parliamentary restraint. Its subject is the machinery of the constitutional state in operation.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why the government in 1881 agreed to establish a constitution and assembly without accepting popular sovereignty.
  2. Trace the sequence from Itō Hirobumi's European mission through the Cabinet of 1885, the drafting of 1886–1888, the Privy Council, and promulgation in 1889.
  3. Analyze how the constitution defined imperial sovereignty and distinguish the emperor's formal position from the practical exercise of power.
  4. Distinguish constitutional office from informal authority, including the genrō, the bureaucracy, and the military command.
  5. Compare the functions and limits of the emperor, Cabinet, Privy Council, courts, and Diet.
  6. Assess the rights and duties of imperial subjects and the ways that statutes qualified them.
  7. Describe the House of Peers and the House of Representatives, including the original franchise and who was excluded.
  8. Explain how the Diet made laws and why budgetary authority, especially Articles 67 and 71, became the central arena of conflict.
  9. Interpret the first budget crisis and the violent election of 1892 as evidence of how institutional rules shaped political struggle.
  10. Evaluate competing interpretations of the Meiji constitutional order and avoid treating later outcomes as inevitable.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did Meiji leaders conclude that a constitution and an elected assembly were compatible with continued oligarchic leadership?
  2. Why did Itō Hirobumi and his colleagues choose continental European models over a British-style parliamentary system?
  3. In what sense was the emperor sovereign, and who actually exercised the powers the constitution assigned to him?
  4. How did the absence of ministerial responsibility to the Diet shape the political tactics available to the government and to parties?
  5. What did constitutional rights mean when most were guaranteed only "within the limits of law"?
  6. How did the restricted electorate shape the social and regional composition of the House of Representatives?
  7. Why did the budget become the decisive battleground, and how did the constitutional text structure that conflict?
  8. What does the 1892 election reveal about the relationship between holding elections and permitting elected opposition to succeed?
  9. Were the later crises of constitutional government predetermined by the 1889 framework, or did the framework permit several outcomes?

Historical Context

The Meiji government had promised from its beginning that deliberative assemblies would be established and public discussion encouraged (Chapter 53). In practice, the 1870s produced consultative and advisory bodies rather than representative government, among them the Genrōin, a legislative senate of appointed members created in 1875 and abolished in 1890, and elected prefectural assemblies from the late 1870s. After the destruction of the domains and the samurai order (Chapter 54), the central government had unprecedented administrative reach but a narrow base of political legitimacy, and former samurai, wealthy farmers, journalists, and urban activists demanded a voice.

Those demands crystallized in the Freedom and People's Rights Movement (Chapter 57). In October 1881, after a crisis in which Ōkuma Shigenobu was expelled from the government for advocating rapid parliamentary government and for criticizing a controversial sale of Hokkaido Colonization Office assets, an imperial rescript promised a national assembly in 1890. This chapter begins from that promise. The government had set the date and, critically, reserved to itself the authority to design the constitution.

Three further conditions framed the project. First, fiscal conditions after the deflationary policies of Matsukata Masayoshi (Chapter 56) left the state dependent on the land tax, the burden of which fell on landowning farmers, who were also the likeliest voters under any tax-based franchise. Second, the unequal treaties (Chapter 52) gave Meiji leaders a diplomatic motive: a recognizable constitutional and legal order was part of demonstrating that Japan merited revised treaties. Third, the leaders' own experience, in a decade that included the Satsuma Rebellion and rural unrest, made them wary of popular mobilization. Regions at the margins mattered too: Hokkaido, governed by a Hokkaido Agency from 1886 and not as a prefecture, and Okinawa Prefecture, established in 1879 after the annexation of the Ryukyu Kingdom, entered the constitutional order on distinctly unequal terms, as later sections show.

Division I — Constructing the Constitutional State, 1881–1889 · Section 59.1

From the Freedom and People's Rights Movement to the Promise of a Constitution

The crisis of 1881, treated in detail in Chapter 57, produced an unusual bargain. The government conceded that a national assembly would open in 1890, but it did so in the emperor's name and on its own schedule. The rescript of October 1881 settled the date; it did not settle the content. Ōkuma, who had proposed a British-style system with parties competing for office, was removed, and the leaders who remained, notably Itō Hirobumi, Iwakura Tomomi, and the bureaucrat Inoue Kowashi, were committed to a constitution in which the throne and its advisers retained initiative.

It is important to distinguish agreeing to a constitution from accepting popular sovereignty. Many Meiji leaders believed a constitution could strengthen the state: it could channel dissent into a legislature, satisfy foreign observers, and give stable form to the government's authority. Movement activists, by contrast, generally understood a constitution as a means of making government answerable to the people, and drafted their own proposals, discussed in Chapter 57. The next eight years were a period in which the government worked out its own answer largely in private, while repressing agitation through measures such as the Peace Preservation Ordinance of 1887, which expelled many activists from Tokyo.

Section 59.2

Itō Hirobumi, European Constitutional Models, and the Choice of Monarchy

From March 1882 to August 1883, Itō led a mission to study constitutions in Europe. In Berlin he heard lectures by the jurist Rudolf von Gneist, who emphasized the importance of an independent administration and local self-government under a strong monarchy, and by Albert Mosse, Gneist's student. In Vienna he studied with Lorenz von Stein, whose social and administrative theory argued that the monarch should stand above competing social interests. He also visited Britain. Itō's correspondence and later accounts indicate that he found in the German-speaking world a model that seemed compatible with a strong executive and with Japan's own claims about the imperial house.

The choice was not a simple imitation of Prussia. Meiji leaders also looked at Austria, Belgium, and other states, and the drafters adapted what they found to Japanese circumstances and to arguments about Japanese tradition. But the central decision was clear: a parliamentary system in which a cabinet depended on a majority of an elected chamber, the model Ōkuma favored, was rejected. Leaders feared that party cabinets would put the state at the mercy of factional competition, and they wanted the throne and its advisers to remain the source of executive authority.

After his return Itō headed a Constitutional Investigation Bureau (established in 1884 within the Imperial Household Ministry) and oversaw preparatory institutions. Foreign advisers were consulted, but the Japanese officials made the decisions. The process also illustrates the international dimension: Japan's rulers selected among foreign models to serve domestic purposes, and they hoped that a constitutional order would also support the long campaign to revise unequal treaties.

Historical Vocabulary
Constitutional monarchy and rikken seitai

Rikken seitai (立憲政体) means a "constitutional polity": a government whose powers are defined and limited by a written constitution. In English, "constitutional monarchy" can describe very different arrangements. In Britain, by the nineteenth century, ministers needed the confidence of the elected House of Commons. In Meiji Japan the emperor was sovereign, ministers advised him, and the elected chamber had defined but limited powers.

The term matters because Meiji Japan was constitutional in the sense that powers were codified and institutions had defined responsibilities, but not parliamentary in the sense of cabinet responsibility to an elected majority. Treating "constitutional" as automatically equal to "democratic" obscures what the 1889 system actually was.

Section 59.3

The Cabinet System of 1885 and the Reorganization of Executive Government

Before the constitution existed, Itō reorganized the executive. On 22 December 1885, the old Dajōkan system, inherited from the ancient-style court structure revived in 1868, was abolished and replaced by a Cabinet (naikaku) of ministers headed by a prime minister. Itō became the first prime minister. The new structure brought ministers of foreign affairs, home affairs, finance, army, navy, justice, education, and agriculture and commerce, and communications into a single body. Key posts went to men from Satsuma and Chōshū, which is why contemporaries and later historians speak of a "domain clique" (hanbatsu) government.

Court and government were deliberately separated. The Imperial Household Ministry sat outside the Cabinet, and a new post, the Lord Keeper of the Privy Seal (naidaijin), was created to serve as the emperor's principal court adviser, with Sanjō Sanetomi, formerly head of the Dajōkan, as its first holder. This separation allowed the imperial institution to be treated as above ordinary politics, an idea central to the later constitutional design. It also made the Cabinet the working executive, a body that would be defined in practice, as the constitution itself never mentions a cabinet or prime minister by name. In December 1889, new Cabinet Regulations redefined the prime minister's position as less commanding than in 1885, making each minister's direct line to the emperor more important and the premier closer to a first among equals.

Section 59.4

Drafting the Constitution: Inoue Kowashi, Hermann Roesler, and the Natsushima Draft

The text was drafted between 1886 and 1888 by a small group working in secret under Itō's direction: Inoue Kowashi, a bureaucrat with deep legal and historical learning; Itō Miyoji, Itō's secretary; and Kaneko Kentarō, a Harvard-educated lawyer. The German jurist Hermann Roesler, who had advised the Japanese government since the late 1870s, produced a draft and commentary in 1887, and Albert Mosse also advised. Many of Roesler's suggestions, including some that would have strengthened the executive and defined ministerial responsibility more clearly, were adopted in part, but the Japanese drafters rejected or modified others. In the summer of 1887 the principal group retreated to Natsushima, on the coast south of Yokohama, to revise the text, producing what is known as the Natsushima draft; further revisions continued into 1888.

The drafters negotiated several problems. How should sovereignty be stated? Inoue and others turned to ancient texts, including the Kojiki, to ground the emperor's position in a tradition of rule. These chronicles are important sources for what the drafters believed and chose to invoke, but under modern source criticism they are retrospective, partly mythological texts, not transparent records of early government. How much should the legislature control? The drafters gave the Diet the power to consent to laws and budgets but limited its reach through imperial prerogatives and protected spending. How should rights be guaranteed? They chose guarantees limited by law.

The drafting process was closed to the public. Unlike the private constitutional drafts produced by the movement (Chapter 57), the official text was never debated in public before promulgation. The main evidence for this stage comes from the drafters' own papers and later accounts, including memoirs and the manuscripts held in the National Diet Library; because these records come overwhelmingly from the drafters, they emphasize elite intentions and say little about popular responses.

Section 59.5

The Privy Council, Imperial Promulgation, and the Constitution of 1889

In April 1888 the Privy Council (Sūmitsuin) was established to review the draft, and Itō resigned as prime minister to become its president; Kuroda Kiyotaka succeeded him as head of the Cabinet. Deliberations ran from May 1888 into January 1889, with the emperor often attending. Records of the debates paraphrase Itō as arguing that a constitution's first purpose was to limit sovereign power and protect subjects' rights, while also arguing that the imperial house had to serve as the axis of the nation, since Japan lacked an established religion that could play the unifying role Christianity played in Europe. Both strands, limitation of power and its sacralization, marked the final text.

On 11 February 1889 the constitution was promulgated, the date chosen to coincide with the traditional anniversary of the legendary first emperor, Jimmu, a holiday known as Kigensetsu. The emperor first reported to his ancestors in the palace sanctuary and then handed the text to Kuroda in a ceremony captured in a commemorative woodblock print by Yōshū Chikanobu. The constitution was presented as a gift of the sovereign (a "bestowed constitution," kintei kenpō), not as an agreement between ruler and people. Other basic instruments were issued the same day: the Imperial Household Law, the Law of the Houses, the Election Law, the Law of Finance, and the House of Peers Ordinance. The Imperial Household Law was placed outside the Diet's reach, and in 1889 Itō's official Commentaries on the Constitution offered the authoritative interpretation. In the days after, Kuroda told prefectural governors that the government would stand above parties, a stance sometimes called "transcendental" cabinet government (chōzen naikaku).

Cause and Effect

The Making of the Meiji Constitutional Settlement

Long-term causes

The Charter Oath's promise of deliberation; the consolidation of central power; the unequal treaties; foreign models of constitutionalism; and decades of petitioning and organizing for representation.

Short-term causes

The 1881 crisis and rescript; Itō's mission; elite fear of party government; the desire to secure the throne's position and prepare Japan to negotiate treaty revision.

Event or transformation

Secret drafting, Privy Council review, and imperial promulgation of a constitution in 1889, followed by elections and a Diet in 1890.

Immediate effects

An elected chamber with real powers over legislation and the budget; a government that claimed to stand above parties; and a political arena that immediately became contested.

Long-term consequences

A durable constitutional framework that lasted until 1947, supported legal modernization, and permitted later party government, while also containing prerogatives and institutions that could limit it.

Historical complexity

The settlement was not one actor's design: popular pressure forced the promise, elites chose the form, foreign advisers shaped details, and later participants interpreted the text differently than its authors.
Division II — Emperor, Ministers, Bureaucracy, and the Constitutional Distribution of Power · Section 59.6

Imperial Sovereignty and the Constitutional Position of the Emperor

The constitution opens with a chapter on the emperor. Article 1 declares that Japan is to be ruled by a line of emperors unbroken for ages eternal; Article 3 makes the emperor sacred and inviolable; Article 4 states that he is the head of the empire, combining in himself the rights of sovereignty and exercising them according to the provisions of the constitution. (These are paraphrases; see the Primary Source Window below for guidance on verified texts.) The Article 1 claim is a statement of dynastic tradition, not a documented historical fact in the modern sense, and the idea of an uninterrupted line should be read as part of the ideology of kokutai, the national polity, rather than as evidence about early Japan.

The key phrase in Article 4 is "according to the provisions of the Constitution." The emperor's sovereignty was real but constitutionally framed: he did not govern alone and could act only through the institutions the document defined. The formal position and the practical exercise of power therefore diverged. In practice the Meiji Emperor read memorials, held audiences, attended the Privy Council and some high-level conferences, and sometimes exerted influence, but decisions were prepared and advised by statesmen. Later debates over whether the emperor was himself the sovereign or an organ of the state, notably Minobe Tatsukichi's "organ theory," grew out of ambiguity already present in the text.

Itō's official Commentaries on the Constitution (1889) explain how the drafters understood these provisions. They treat the throne as the source of the state's unity and of its legal order, and they ground that claim in the imperial line's supposed antiquity, citing early chronicles as tradition. This reasoning is a document of the 1880s: it shows what the authors wished the constitution to mean and how they used the past to justify present arrangements, but it cannot be read as an account of early Japanese kingship.

Three features of the arrangement shaped everyday politics. First, the doctrine of advice: because the emperor acted on the advice of ministers who bore responsibility, decisions could be presented as imperial while being prepared by others, and the emperor was in principle protected from blame. Second, the separation of palace and government (kyūchū and fuchū): the Imperial Household Ministry and the Lord Keeper of the Privy Seal stood outside the Cabinet, and the Imperial Household Law of 1889, which fixed succession in the male line and lay beyond the Diet's reach (Article 74), made the dynasty's internal affairs a matter for the court. Third, the emperor's actual involvement varied by occasion. He attended Privy Council deliberations on the constitution, received ministers' reports, and in 1893 issued the message that broke a budget deadlock (Section 59.23), yet he did not preside over ordinary cabinet business. The throne was therefore neither a ceremonial void nor a personal autocracy, and its political weight depended on the moment and on those who could claim to speak for it.

Historical Vocabulary
Imperial sovereignty and tennō taiken

Tennō taiken (天皇大権) refers to the imperial prerogatives: the powers the constitution reserved to the emperor, such as commanding the armed forces, concluding treaties, issuing certain ordinances, and convening or dissolving the lower house. These prerogatives were exercised on the advice of ministers and the military commanders, which is why they often functioned as the executive's powers.

"Sovereignty" itself requires care. Meiji sources used traditional vocabulary, and modern scholars debate how much of it reflected European legal doctrine and how much constructed tradition. Students should not equate the emperor's constitutional sovereignty with personal rule, nor treat the throne as a purely decorative institution.

Section 59.7

Imperial Prerogatives: Legislation, Appointments, Diplomacy, and Emergency Authority

The constitution gave the emperor, and through him his ministers, a wide set of powers. He sanctioned laws (Article 6), convoked, opened, closed, and prorogued the Diet and dissolved the House of Representatives (Article 7), appointed and dismissed civil and military officials (Article 10), declared war, made peace, and concluded treaties (Article 13), and could declare martial law (Article 14). Treaties did not require Diet approval, which kept foreign policy largely in executive hands, even though the budget gave the Diet indirect influence.

Two kinds of ordinances extended executive power. Under Article 9 the emperor could issue ordinances necessary for the administration of laws and the maintenance of order, though not to alter statutes. Under Article 8, when the Diet was not in session, he could issue emergency ordinances in urgent circumstances; these had to be submitted to the next Diet, and if it did not approve them, the government had to declare them invalid for the future. The dissolution power was especially important in the 1890s, because ministers used it repeatedly to try to change the political balance. Dissolution was constrained only by Article 45, which required the election of a new House within five months. These prerogatives made the executive powerful, but its ability to govern without the Diet was limited by the requirement that laws and budgets pass.

In practice the prerogatives were exercised through institutions that were themselves unequal. The Privy Council was consulted on emergency ordinances and important treaties, which meant that Itō and other senior figures could review executive acts outside the Diet. Treaty-making is a telling example. Because treaties required no Diet consent, negotiators such as the foreign ministers of the 1890s worked within the executive, yet the House could and did attack their policy through addresses, resolutions, and budget pressure. Treaty revision, a central national goal, was therefore both an executive prerogative and a constant subject of parliamentary agitation.

The ordinance powers raised a persistent question of boundaries between law and ordinance. Article 9 let the government issue ordinances to implement laws and maintain order, but not to change laws; Article 8 allowed emergency measures with a duty of later submission. Matters reserved to law, such as new taxes and the limits on subjects' rights, needed the Diet, while administrative detail could be handled by ordinance. The line was disputed repeatedly, and the dissolution power complicated it: a government that dissolved the House could carry on for months under existing laws and the previous budget, a pressure that opposition members understood well. Dissolution was thus a political weapon whose effectiveness depended on whether voters would return a different House, and 1892 showed how uncertain that was.

Section 59.8

Cabinet Ministers and the Problem of Political Responsibility

Article 55 is the hinge of the system. Ministers of state gave advice to the emperor and were responsible for it, and every law, imperial ordinance, and rescript concerning state affairs required a minister's countersignature. The text did not say that ministers must retain the confidence of the House of Representatives; it did not even mention the Cabinet or the prime minister. Ministers answered, constitutionally, to the throne. They could attend and speak in the Diet (Article 54) and could be questioned, but the House could not remove them. A hostile majority could resist through legislation and the budget, address the throne, and pass resolutions, but these did not compel resignation.

This is the sharpest difference between the Meiji system and parliamentary cabinet government. It also explains why 1890s politics centered on the budget: lacking the power to choose ministers, the House had to use the powers it did have. Ministers, for their part, could govern without a majority but not without negotiation, because laws and expanding budgets needed consent.

The workings of the Cabinet in these years followed from the absence of constitutional rules. Each minister headed a ministry and reported on its affairs, and under the 1889 Cabinet Regulations the prime minister could not dismiss colleagues. A cabinet therefore held together through personal relationships among a small group, chiefly the Satsuma and Chōshū leaders, and could be broken by the resignation of one member. The fall of cabinets in this period illustrates the point. Kuroda's cabinet resigned in October 1889 after Foreign Minister Ōkuma was maimed by a bomb thrown by an opponent of his treaty-revision negotiations. Yamagata's government gave way in May 1891 amid the first Diet's budget struggle and the Ōtsu crisis, and Matsukata's cabinet fell in August 1892 after the election scandal. In each case the decisive pressures came from the ruling circle, foreign affairs, and public criticism, not from a vote of no confidence.

Ministers dealt with the Diet through "government commissioners" (seifu iin), officials who attended to explain and defend bills, a practice that reinforced the sense of the executive as an administration addressing the representatives from outside. Members could put questions to the government, but there was no mechanism that required a satisfactory answer or compelled resignation. The consequence was a political style in which opposition members sought to make the government's position untenable through publicity, budget cuts, and addresses to the throne, while ministers sought to divide the opposition, appeal to the emperor, or dissolve the House.

Section 59.9

The Genrō, Central Bureaucracy, and the Unwritten Structure of Power

Much real authority lay outside the text. A small group of senior statesmen from the Restoration era, including Itō, Kuroda, Yamagata, Matsukata, Inoue Kaoru, Saigō Tsugumichi, and Ōyama Iwao, shaped appointments and policy through personal standing and their access to the emperor. They later came to be called the genrō. The practice of consulting them about the choice of prime ministers developed gradually through the 1890s and was consolidated later; the constitution does not mention them.

How did these actors interact in practice? Major decisions, including the choice of a prime minister, the response to a parliamentary crisis, and the line to take in negotiations abroad, were typically settled in conversations among the leading statesmen and then presented through formal channels: a cabinet decision, a Privy Council review, an imperial message. The genrō did not form a single bloc. Itō, with a base in the civil bureaucracy and the constitutional project, and Yamagata, whose influence rested on the army and the Home Ministry, differed on how far to accommodate parties, and Kuroda and Matsukata represented Satsuma interests. Their rivalries and compromises shaped each crisis, and the second Itō cabinet of 1892, filled with senior figures, shows an attempt to bring the whole circle into the executive when the Diet became difficult.

The bureaucracy's reach mattered for elections as well as administration. The Home Ministry supervised prefectural governors, who controlled police and local officials; this administrative chain gave the government the means of intervention that Section 59.22 describes. At the same time officials were not simply instruments. Examination-trained bureaucrats developed professional identities, ministries defended their budgets, and local notables who sat in assemblies and, later, in the House had their own interests. Constitutional government thus sat atop a dense administrative state whose habits changed more slowly than the formal institutions.

Historical Vocabulary
Genrō, Genrōin, and constitutional versus informal office

The Genrōin (元老院) was a formal legislative body of appointed members, created in 1875 and abolished in 1890. The genrō (元老, "elder statesmen") were a different thing: an informal group of senior leaders with no constitutional office. The similar names invite confusion, but one was an institution and the other a circle of individuals.

The distinction matters because it shows how a constitution can define offices while real influence flows through unwritten networks. Students should ask of any political decision whether it was made by a constitutional organ, an informal circle, or the bureaucracy.

Behind ministers stood a professionalizing bureaucracy. Higher civil service examinations introduced in 1887 and associated with Tokyo Imperial University created a path to career officials. Prefectural governors were appointed by the Home Ministry, and local government codes of 1888 and 1890, designed under Yamagata with advice from Albert Mosse, established municipal and prefectural bodies dominated by local notables while keeping them under central supervision. The bureaucracy gave the state continuity independent of the Diet, but bureaucrats and politicians were not always opposed; many officials later entered party politics.

Section 59.10

The Army, Navy, and Imperial Supreme Command

Article 11 gave the emperor supreme command of the army and navy, Article 12 determined their organization and peacetime strength, and Article 13 gave him the power to declare war. The principle of an independent command had precedents: the Army General Staff had been separated from the War Ministry in 1878, and the Imperial Rescript to Soldiers and Sailors of 1882, associated with Yamagata, told the armed forces to stay out of politics and obey the emperor directly. A separate Naval General Staff was created in 1893. Universal male conscription was extended and its exemptions narrowed by the Conscription Law revision of 1889.

The relationship between military leaders and the Diet in the 1890s must be understood on its own terms. Military leaders such as Yamagata were also senior political figures, so conflicts were often within the ruling circle. Requirements that service ministers be active-duty officers came later, from 1900, and the later "military autonomy" of the 1930s should not be projected back unchanged onto the early Diet. What the 1890s show is that the military budget and the claim to a separate command were already matters on which the Diet had to be managed.

Interpretations of the supreme command also matter. The official commentary and later practice treated command of the forces as a prerogative exercised without the ministerial advice that governed civil business, so that general staff officers, not Cabinet ministers, were the emperor's advisers on operations. How far this separation extended in the 1890s was less settled than later claims suggest. The army and navy ministers sat in the Cabinet and presented military budgets to the Diet; the army had Chōshū leadership and the navy a strong Satsuma tradition; and each service sought funds that the House of Representatives could cut. The naval expansion dispute of 1893 is the clearest example of the House using financial power against military plans, and its resolution by imperial message shows that military demands and parliamentary consent were being negotiated, not yet separated by a doctrine of autonomy.

For the population, the constitution's military clauses appeared mainly as conscription. The 1889 revision made service a general duty for men of age, abolished most remaining exemptions, and placed barracks life and regimental ties within the experience of rural families. Military service thus connected ordinary subjects to the emperor's command more directly than the franchise did, a fact that later chapters will connect to nationalism and mobilization.

Section 59.11

Courts, Judicial Independence, and Constitutional Legal Order

The constitution stated that judicature would be exercised by courts in the emperor's name according to law (Article 57), that judges could not be removed except through criminal conviction or disciplinary procedure (Article 58), and that trials would generally be public (Article 59). Article 61 sent claims against administrative authorities to a separate Administrative Court, established in 1890, so the ordinary courts could not review many administrative acts. The Court Organization Law of 1890 shaped the judiciary under a Court of Cassation (Daishin'in), and a Lawyers Law followed in 1893. Prosecutors were attached to the court system, and the Justice Ministry supervised administration, which limited independence in practice.

A famous test came in May 1891, in the Ōtsu Incident, when a policeman, Tsuda Sanzō, wounded the visiting Russian Tsarevich Nicholas. Government leaders feared diplomatic retaliation and pressed for the death penalty by applying provisions on crimes against the Japanese imperial house. Chief Justice Kojima Korekata and the judges instead held that those provisions applied only to the Japanese imperial family, and sentenced Tsuda to life imprisonment. Cabinet members resigned amid the crisis. The episode is often cited as evidence that the judiciary could resist executive pressure, though it was an exceptional case, and the broader Civil Code controversy of 1890–1892 showed that legal modernization was contested on other grounds. Access to courts also depended on cost, literacy, and distance, so formal legal protections were unequally available.

The judicial system that emerged from the Court Organization Law of 1890 had several tiers: local courts, district courts, courts of appeal, and the Court of Cassation at the top. Judges were selected through examinations and appointment, and constitutional tenure rules protected them from removal. Prosecutors, however, were part of the court structure, and the Ministry of Justice supervised judicial administration, so independence was real but incomplete. The Administrative Court heard disputes in which individuals complained of administrative acts, but its jurisdiction was limited to categories defined by statute, and it sat in Tokyo; many grievances against officials therefore never reached it.

The Ōtsu Incident of 1891 tested this structure. After Tsuda Sanzō's attack on the Tsarevich, ministers and senior leaders, anxious about relations with Russia, pressed for execution under provisions of the Criminal Code that concerned crimes against the imperial house. The Court of Cassation, convened for the case, concluded that those provisions protected only the Japanese imperial family and that Tsuda should be tried under the ordinary law on attempted murder. Chief Justice Kojima Korekata's court imposed life imprisonment. The decision was a public demonstration that judges could rule against the preferences of ministers, and several officials resigned in the aftermath. Historians generally regard the case as significant evidence for judicial independence, while noting its exceptional character.

The legal order also generated political controversy. The new Civil and Commercial Codes, drafted with the French adviser Gustave Boissonade and others, were promulgated in 1890 but met opposition from jurists and politicians who argued that they undermined family-centered values; in 1892 the Diet voted to postpone their implementation, and revised codes followed later in the decade. The episode shows the Diet acting on legislation of fundamental social importance and reveals that debates over law, household, and tradition were already intense. For most subjects, though, access to courts depended on cost, local lawyers, and literacy, and the legal profession, regulated by the 1893 Lawyers Law, was small and concentrated in cities.

Division III — Imperial Subjects, Elections, and the Powers of the Diet · Section 59.12

The Rights and Duties of Imperial Subjects

Chapter II of the constitution, "Rights and Duties of Subjects," addressed Japanese people not as citizens of a sovereign people but as shinmin, imperial subjects. Subject status was determined by law (Article 18), and equal eligibility for civil and military posts was guaranteed according to qualifications (Article 19). Subjects owed duties of military service and taxation (Articles 20 and 21). Rights included liberty of abode (Article 22), protection from arrest and trial except by law (Articles 23 and 24), inviolability of the home and of correspondence (Articles 25 and 26), security of property (Article 27), freedom of belief (Article 28), freedom of speech, publication, assembly, and association (Article 29), and the right to petition (Article 30).

The language of subjecthood was a deliberate choice. Official commentary treated rights as arising within the framework of imperial rule, not as natural rights the people held against it. Subject status also did not mean identical treatment. Burakumin had been formally freed from their status designations in 1871 but continued to face discrimination (Chapter 43). Ainu people in Hokkaido were subjects, registered in separate categories such as "former natives," and the later Hokkaido Former Aborigines Protection Act of 1899 is discussed in later chapters. Okinawans, annexed in 1879, were subjects of a prefecture with limited political incorporation. Colonial subjects of the later empire were not yet part of the picture (Chapter 60).

Specific guarantees illustrate how rights worked. Property (Article 27) was protected, but restrictions were permitted where law provided, and the state's taxation and public works powers were extensive. Freedom of belief (Article 28) offered protection to Buddhists, Christians, and others, but it was qualified by public order and subjects' duties, and the government's treatment of Shinto shrine rites as a non-religious national observance (Chapters 55 and 77) meant that obligations could be framed as civic rather than religious. Equality of eligibility for public office (Article 19) opened career paths to commoners through examinations, but it excluded women and depended on education, which was unevenly available.

The legal category of subject was also tied to household registration, which recorded status, residence, and family. Ainu in Hokkaido were registered in ways that marked them as a distinct group, and people formerly classed as outcastes could be identified through registers and local knowledge, so that equality in law coexisted with discrimination in practice. These facts mean that the constitution's rights chapter must be read against the social differences it did not abolish.

Section 59.13

Rights Within the Limits of Law: The Boundaries of Constitutional Freedom

Most guarantees were qualified. Freedom of religion (Article 28) applied within limits not prejudicial to peace and order and not antagonistic to subjects' duties; speech, assembly, and association (Article 29) were guaranteed "within the limits of law." Because the Diet and the executive defined those limits through statutes and ordinances, the effective scope of rights depended on legislation. The Newspaper Ordinance and the Peace Preservation Ordinance of 1887 already authorized censorship and expulsions; the Law on Assembly and Political Associations of 1890 restricted political organization and barred women, among others, from joining political associations and attending political meetings; the Publications Law of 1893 added further controls. The emergency provisions of Articles 8 and 31 also permitted suspension of some rights in specified circumstances.

The distance between recognition and exercise varied by social position. A landowning notable could often petition, publish, or run for office; a tenant farmer faced obstacles of literacy, time, and dependence; a woman was formally excluded from politics; a resident of Hokkaido or Okinawa lived outside the representative system. This is the context for understanding why the constitution could be genuinely significant, giving many people a legal framework and a public political arena, while still falling far short of equal political rights.

Concrete statutes show how limitation worked. The Newspaper Ordinance of 1887 required licensing and allowed the Home Minister to suspend or ban publications, and newspapers, including those allied with opposition parties, were repeatedly suspended in the 1890s. The Peace Preservation Ordinance of December 1887 authorized the expulsion from Tokyo of persons deemed dangerous to public order, and hundreds of activists were ordered to leave the capital. The Law on Assembly and Political Associations (1890) required notification of meetings, allowed police to supervise and break up gatherings, and excluded women, minors, soldiers, police, and teachers and students from political associations and meetings. The Publications Law of 1893 tightened controls on books and pamphlets. The Criminal Code's provisions on offences against the imperial house gave a further basis for prosecuting speech.

These measures did not destroy political life. Parties held meetings, newspapers argued vigorously, and elections were contested. But they meant that the exercise of rights depended on the goodwill of police and the Home Ministry, and those with money, local standing, and legal advice could use freedoms more safely than workers or tenants. The gap between constitutional recognition and effective freedom was widest for women, whose exclusion from political associations was written into statute, and for people in regions where local authorities regularly interpreted order broadly. For those reasons the rights chapter is evidence of both constitutional change and its limits.

Section 59.14

The House of Peers: Aristocracy, Imperial Appointment, and the Upper Chamber

The upper chamber was not an elected house. The Peerage Ordinance of 1884 had created five ranks (prince, marquis, count, viscount, baron) that incorporated former court nobles, former daimyō, and Restoration leaders. The House of Peers Ordinance of 1889, issued as an imperial ordinance, not a law passed by the Diet, determined membership. Members included adult princes of the imperial family; princes and marquises of full age; counts, viscounts, and barons elected by their peers for fixed terms; persons appointed by the emperor for distinguished service or learning; and representatives of the highest taxpayers, one chosen for each prefecture from among its largest taxpayers in land, industry, or commerce. The first House had roughly 250 members.

Because the ordinance was outside the Diet's control and the Peers had legislative powers equal to the Representatives' (except that the budget went first to the lower house), the upper chamber could block bills the lower house passed. Itō himself resigned the presidency of the Privy Council in 1890 to become the first president of the House of Peers, an indication of the chamber's importance for the government. Its composition made it a conservative counterweight, though peers did not always vote as a single bloc.

The ways members entered the chamber made it a body that the government could influence. Imperial princes and senior nobles sat by right on reaching the required age. The lower ranks of nobility (counts, viscounts, barons) chose a limited number of their own to represent them for fixed terms, so that most titled families had no seat. The emperor appointed members for meritorious service or learning, and these appointments, made on ministers' advice, brought in senior officials, scholars, and other figures who could be expected to support the state's priorities. The highest-taxpayer members, selected one per prefecture from among each prefecture's largest taxpayers, gave landed and commercial wealth a voice and tied the chamber to local elites. Because the House of Peers Ordinance could be changed only by imperial ordinance and not by the Diet, the elected chamber could not alter its composition.

Its legislative powers equalled the lower house's, except that budgets began in the House of Representatives. A bill could pass the lower house and die in the Peers. Because Itō himself presided over the Peers from October 1890, the chamber was closely linked to the government's senior leadership, although its members were not uniformly obedient and contributed their own criticism and amendments. For parties, the upper chamber was a standing obstacle; for the government, it was a reserve of support that made it less dependent on winning a majority in the elected house.

Section 59.15

The House of Representatives and the Restricted Electorate

The Election Law of 1889 defined who could vote and stand. Voters had to be men at least twenty-five years old who had been resident in their prefecture for a year and paid at least fifteen yen in direct national taxes (principally land tax, as well as income tax). Candidates had to be men at least thirty meeting the same tax threshold. Three hundred members were elected from 257 districts, 214 returning one member and 43 returning two. Voters wrote the candidate's name and their own name and affixed a seal, so the ballot was not secret. The qualified electorate was about 450,000, roughly 1.1 percent of the population, and most voters were landowners.

The excluded were therefore the great majority: all women; men under twenty-five; most tenant farmers, wage workers, and urban shopkeepers; and anyone below the tax threshold. Some further occupational groups, including active-duty military personnel, faced restrictions. Regional exclusions were also built in. Hokkaido and Okinawa had no seats in 1890; Hokkaido gained representation in 1904 and Okinawa in 1912. The tax threshold overrepresented landed rural areas relative to cities, and the system gave rural districts disproportionate weight in terms of seats per voter.

Because voters were few, elections were intensely local. With roughly 450,000 voters spread across 257 districts, a typical district had fewer than two thousand electors, and many members were elected with several hundred votes. Local officials compiled the lists of qualified taxpayers, and candidates relied on networks of landowners, village heads, and local organizers. The signed ballot meant that voters' choices could be known, and the practice of providing hospitality or favors to voters was widespread, so that money and social pressure mattered. Candidates also needed to meet the same tax qualification, which restricted the field to the propertied and to those who could draw on propertied allies.

The social effect was a House dominated by landowners and local notables with significant representation of former samurai, journalists, and lawyers, drawn from the same stratum that had supplied much of the movement's leadership. Urban merchants, industrial workers, and tenants had few direct representatives, and issues such as labor conditions and tenancy were largely absent from early debate. Regional unevenness added to this. The districts reflected prefectural populations in rural areas more than commercial cities, and Hokkaido and Okinawa, along with the Ainu and Okinawan people living in them, were outside the system until the early twentieth century, when representation was extended on restricted terms.

Section 59.16

How the Imperial Diet Made Laws

The constitution established a two-chamber Diet in which every law required the consent of both houses (Articles 33–37). Either chamber could consider government bills and could also initiate legislation (Article 38), though a bill rejected could not be reintroduced in the same session (Article 39). The Diet met annually for a three-month session (Articles 41 and 42), extendable and supplemented by extraordinary sessions. Members enjoyed protection for speech in the chamber (Article 52), and sittings were generally public (Article 48). The chambers could receive petitions and present addresses to the emperor (Articles 49 and 50). The Law of the Houses supplied detailed procedures for committees, readings, and the roles of the presidents and speaker. After passage, laws required the emperor's sanction (Article 6), and the emperor never refused it in this period.

The Diet could not amend the constitution on its own; amendment required an imperial order, with high quorum and majority requirements (Article 73). It had no authority over the Imperial Household Law (Article 74). Ministers could attend and speak, and members could question the government, though ministers were not constitutionally bound to answer fully. The Diet's real power was thus negative and procedural: it could withhold consent. For a government that wanted new laws or larger budgets, this power was considerable. The difference between legislative authority and imperial ordinance power defined the parameters of conflict.

The procedure of lawmaking gave the Diet powers of blocking and shaping. A bill was introduced in one house, referred to a committee, debated in the chamber, and voted, with the process repeating in the other house; changes made in one house had to be accepted by the other. Government bills made up the bulk of serious legislation, since ministries drafted most measures, but members could introduce their own, and either house could reject, amend, or let a bill lapse. The government could not enact a new law by ordinance in areas reserved to law, so a hostile Diet could stall measures the cabinet wanted, whether tax changes, military reforms, or administrative codes. Equally, the Diet could not pass a bill over the emperor's refusal of sanction or over the opposition of the other chamber.

The short, three-month sessions and the threat of dissolution constrained debate. Sessions could be cut short or ended in dissolution, as in December 1891 and again in 1893–1894, and sustained legislative work was difficult amid repeated confrontations. Members used additional tools: questions to ministers, petitions from constituents, resolutions, and addresses to the emperor criticizing government policy. These instruments did not bind the government, but they created a public record and a venue for argument that newspapers reported across the country. The Diet's real strength was therefore its power to refuse and negotiate, not to direct.

Section 59.17

Budgetary Authority, Taxation, and the Constitutional Limits on Finance

Chapter VI of the constitution made finance the Diet's strongest lever. New taxes required a law (Article 62), and the annual budget required Diet consent, with the House of Representatives considering it first (Articles 64 and 65). The imperial household's expenses were fixed by a civil list (Article 66). But Article 67 provided that expenditures based on the emperor's constitutional powers, expenditures arising from law, and legal obligations of the government could not be rejected or reduced without the government's concurrence. Article 70 allowed urgent financial measures by imperial ordinance when the Diet could not be convened, and Article 71 stipulated that if the Diet did not vote a budget, the government would carry out the previous year's budget.

Article 71 was intended as a safeguard against paralysis, but it also meant that a deadlock left spending frozen at the previous year's level. For a government planning military and administrative growth, that was a serious constraint. The land tax, still among the largest sources of revenue, was paid by many of the men who elected the House, and opposition politicians drew on that link with the slogan, in paraphrase, of economizing government spending and easing the people's burden (seihi setsugen, minryoku kyūyō). Article 67's categories were ambiguous, and how much of the budget counted as "fixed" became the first major constitutional dispute.

The budget process shows where leverage lay. The Cabinet, with the Finance Ministry in the lead, drew up the annual budget and presented it first to the House of Representatives, whose budget committee examined it item by item, proposing reductions or additions before the House voted and the Peers deliberated. Because the budget was a single large package of revenue and expenditure estimates, committees could target salary lines, administrative offices, or construction programs, and the government had to decide whether a proposed cut fell within the protected categories of Article 67.

Article 67 protected three classes of spending: expenditures grounded in the emperor's constitutional powers (for example, the organization of the administration and the armed forces), those arising by effect of law, and legal obligations of the government such as debt service. A broad reading treated most salaries, military establishments, and statutory programs as fixed, leaving little to reduce. A narrow reading confined the protection to items whose amount had been settled by prior law or contract, leaving room for the House to cut estimates that were discretionary. The official commentary leaned toward the government's reading, while opposition members read it narrowly, and the text offered no authoritative arbiter. The Diet could not obtain a ruling from the courts, which had no role in resolving such disputes, so the question was settled by political bargaining.

Article 71 shaped the stakes. If no budget passed, the previous year's budget continued, which meant no new programs and no growth, but also no shutdown of government. For a cabinet planning military or administrative expansion, that was costly; for an opposition that wanted to reduce spending, it was a less serious threat than it might have been. The land tax, which remained the principal tax and which the House's own electors paid, supplied the political motive for demands to economize, and the language of lightening the people's burdens became a central opposition theme in 1890–1892.

Division IV — The First Imperial Diet and the Struggle for Parliamentary Power, 1890–1892 · Section 59.18

The First General Election of 1890: Campaigns, Candidates, and Political Participation

The first election took place on 1 July 1890. Turnout among qualified voters was very high, in the neighborhood of 94 percent, a reflection of the small electorate's strong engagement and of local organizing. Campaigns relied on speeches, newspapers, and personal and local networks. The party landscape was still unsettled. The Rikken Kaishintō (Constitutional Reform Party) had been founded in 1882 and ran candidates in its own name, with Ōkuma Shigenobu as its leading figure. Candidates of the Liberal tradition, by contrast, stood under several separate organizations, among them Itagaki Taisuke's Tosa-based Aikoku Kōtō, the Daidō Club, and regional groups such as the Kyūshū Dōmeikai. These groups merged into the Rikken Jiyūtō (Constitutional Liberal Party) on 15 September 1890, after the election and before the Diet convened. Many candidates were landowners and local notables, a substantial number were former samurai, and others were journalists and lawyers. Reports of vote-buying and local pressure indicate that early elections were shaped by patronage as well as principle.

The results were a setback for the government's hopes of a friendly chamber. A commonly cited breakdown is about 130 seats for candidates of the Liberal groups that merged in September, 41 for the Kaishintō, 79 for the government-leaning Taiseikai, 5 for the National Liberal Party, and about 45 independents or others. Grouping the Liberal and Kaishintō candidates as the opposition "popular parties" (mintō) gives roughly 171 of 300 seats, against about 129 for the ritō (government-supporting groups) and independents; the figures are approximate and depend on how independents are classified. The result mattered less because parties were disciplined, which they were not, than because the electorate had returned a House inclined to scrutinize spending. Students should note that the organizations that sat in the first Diet were not identical to those that had fought the election.

Everyday Life and Politics

Politics Beyond the Ballot

Only about one person in a hundred could vote, but political life reached further. Newspapers and political speeches circulated beyond the electorate, public meetings drew listeners who could not vote, and local issues such as taxes, irrigation, roads, and schools were discussed in village and town networks. For qualified voters, voting meant going to a designated place and writing and sealing a ballot that others could in principle trace. Women, tenants, and laborers took part mainly through households, communities, and informal pressure, and the evidence for their views is far thinner than for candidates and officials.

Section 59.19

The Opening of the Imperial Diet and the New Parliamentary Arena

The first Diet convened on 25 November 1890 in a temporary wooden building in Tokyo's Uchisaiwaichō district, and the formal opening ceremony took place on 29 November, the day the constitution took effect. The House of Representatives elected Nakajima Nobuyuki, a Jiyūtō figure, as its first speaker, and Itō presided over the House of Peers. Ministers, led by Prime Minister Yamagata Aritomo, who had taken office in December 1889, faced a House in which opposition members had a majority. In January 1891 the temporary building burned down, a reminder of the improvised circumstances in which the new institution began, and a replacement was prepared for later sessions.

The opening mattered beyond the first session. A permanent national representative body now existed, with public debates, published proceedings, and a regular calendar. Even critics of the system had to use it. Its establishment also gave newspapers, local politicians, and interest groups a focus they had lacked.

The new arena had its own procedures and expectations. Members sat in a single chamber by seat assignments rather than party benches, committees examined bills and the budget, and the speaker managed debate. Itō became president of the House of Peers on 24 October 1890. At the opening, Prime Minister Yamagata Aritomo set out the government's priorities, and in a policy statement to the first Diet in December 1890 he argued that Japan must protect both a "line of sovereignty" (the national frontier) and a "line of interest" (adjoining areas, notably Korea, vital to its security), a rationale for military spending that links the budget debates to the foreign policy of Chapter 60. Opposition members, in turn, entered the Diet with the expectation that the House would reduce taxes and the costs of administration, and many saw the first session as a test of whether the constitution would give them real influence.

Section 59.20

The Popular Parties, Government Supporters, and Parliamentary Alliances

The popular parties had roots in the 1880s. The Jiyūtō (Liberal Party), founded in 1881, dissolved in 1884 under government pressure. The Kaishintō, founded in 1882 by Ōkuma and his allies, lost momentum after Ōkuma and others left it in 1884, and he later returned to politics, serving as foreign minister in the Kuroda cabinet until the bombing of October 1889. In 1886–1887 the Daidō Danketsu movement, led by former Liberal figures, attempted to unite opposition forces. When elections approached, those traditions reorganized in different ways: the Kaishintō's network continued, while Liberal-tradition candidates ran under separate banners and merged into the Rikken Jiyūtō on 15 September 1890 after the election, which Itagaki joined and led. The party's name was later shortened to Jiyūtō in 1891. This chronology matters: the unified Liberal party that faced the government in the first Diet did not exist as a single organization when voters went to the polls.

Parliamentary blocs formed in the same months. The mintō comprised the Liberals and the Kaishintō, who shared opposition to the cabinet but differed on policy and rivalry. The ritō included the Taiseikai, a group formed from members who claimed support for the government as non-partisan representatives of the "national" interest, and smaller groups. Boundaries were fluid, members shifted, and leaders could not command their followers' votes. These were parliamentary alliances more than disciplined modern mass parties.

Leaders such as Itagaki, Ōkuma, Kōno Hironaka, Hoshi Tōru, Ozaki Yukio, and Inukai Tsuyoshi worked within this system, balancing local constituencies, newspaper audiences, and bargaining with ministers. Their demand for lower government spending and attention to local interests suited their landowning base, and their ability to negotiate when government offered concessions would shape the early Diet's history.

  • Government control of the police, the press laws, and executive initiative

Who experienced change most directly? Politicians who won seats gained a new arena; who experienced greater continuity? Excluded groups, including women and the poor, remained outside formal politics. Why it matters: The shift explains why the early parties were both vigorous and fragmented.

Section 59.21

The First Budget Crisis: Yamagata Aritomo, Article 67, and Parliamentary Resistance

The budget for fiscal year 1891 became the first great test of the constitution. Finance Minister Matsukata Masayoshi presented estimates of about 83 million yen, and the House of Representatives, where the opposition held a majority, referred them to a budget committee. The committee proposed cuts of roughly 8 million yen, about a tenth of the total, aimed chiefly at administrative expenses, salaries, and the costs of government offices. The cuts reflected the opposition's central claim that the government was spending too much and that the land-tax burden on the people should be lightened.

The dispute turned immediately to Article 67. Ministers argued that large parts of the estimates, including salaries of officials appointed under the emperor's authority and costs arising from existing law, were protected: the Diet could not reduce them without the government's concurrence. Opposition members replied that the article covered only expenditures already fixed in a binding sense and that the House's duty to examine the budget would be hollow if every line were treated as protected. Each side could claim the text, and no court or other institution could rule between them, so the question became a matter of strength and bargaining. The government also had to weigh its alternatives: it could threaten dissolution, rely on Article 71 and carry out the previous year's budget, or negotiate. Yamagata's cabinet disliked the first two options, since a dissolution risked another opposition majority and Article 71 would freeze spending.

The crisis intensified in early 1891. The temporary Diet building burned down on 20 January and the work of the chambers was disrupted. Pressure mounted as the opposition held to its position and the government prepared contingencies. The impasse was broken by division within the opposition. According to the National Diet Library's documentary record, a group of 22 former Aikoku Kōtō members agreed to separate from the Liberal Party and support a compromise. Many were from Tosa, long a stronghold of popular-rights politics, and supporters of the compromise included Ueki Emori, a prominent theorist of the earlier movement. Ministers, among them Mutsu Munemitsu, were involved in the negotiations. Critics later accused the defectors of betraying the opposition, and rumors of improper inducements circulated; the surviving records establish the negotiations and the group's separation, but they do not settle every allegation about motives.

With the defectors' votes, the House passed a budget in early March with reductions smaller than the committee's: accounts give the negotiated cut as about 6.3 to 6.5 million yen, compared with the initial proposal of roughly 7.8 to 8 million. The distinction matters. The opposition could claim that it had forced real reductions and established that the House's consent was not a formality; the government could claim that it had preserved its essential programs and avoided both dissolution and reliance on Article 71. Neither side had won outright. The episode showed that the constitutional text produced bargaining rather than clear victory, that an elected chamber without power over ministers could still extract concessions through the budget, and that opposition unity was fragile. Yamagata resigned in May 1891 and Matsukata took over.

Historians use this crisis to illustrate how institutional rules shaped political struggle. The ambiguity of Article 67, the absence of an arbiter, the limited threat of Article 71, the government's reluctance to dissolve, and the opposition's internal divisions explain why the outcome was a compromise. Students should read it alongside the constitutional text in the Primary Source Window and ask how a different wording of Article 67 might have changed the contest.

Section 59.22

The Dissolution of 1891 and the Violent Election of 1892

Conflict returned in the second Diet. In December 1891 the House again voted large budget reductions. Navy Minister Kabayama Sukenori defended the government in a speech that praised the achievements of the Satsuma and Chōshū leaders, a statement that infuriated the opposition. On 25 December the government dissolved the House. The election of 15 February 1892 was conducted under heavy official intervention: Home Minister Shinagawa Yajirō directed prefectural governors and police to support pro-government candidates and hinder their opponents, while partisans on both sides mobilized hired toughs. Reports record roughly two dozen deaths and nearly four hundred injuries, with Kōchi and Saga among the worst-affected prefectures. Figures are approximate and drawn from contemporary reports.

The intervention failed to produce a government majority. The popular parties remained the larger bloc, though weakened, and public criticism of official interference was intense. Shinagawa resigned, and the Diet attacked the government for its conduct. A government-aligned association, the Kokumin Kyōkai, was organized under Saigō Tsugumichi and Shinagawa in an attempt to build a pro-government political base. The episode demonstrated both the state's willingness to bend election rules and its inability to secure the outcome it wanted. The Matsukata cabinet resigned in August 1892.

The election's mechanics show how the constitutional framework could be bent. Prefectural governors, appointed by the Home Ministry, directed police and local officials; police presence at polling, the arrest or harassment of opposition organizers, and pressure on voters and local notables served the government's candidates, while partisans on both sides mobilized hired men and clashes broke out in several prefectures. Because election law offered few effective protections and the ballot was signed, voters were vulnerable to pressure. Contemporary newspapers, prefectural and police reports, and parliamentary debate are the main sources; each reflects its writer's interests, and tallies of casualties differ, which is why the figures here are given as approximate.

The outcome mattered for constitutional practice. The government failed to produce a House that would support it; estimates of the opposition's strength range from roughly 130 to over 160 seats depending on how independents are classified, but it remained a substantial bloc and the cabinet's credibility suffered. The affair also exposed divisions within the ruling circle, since senior figures, including some genrō, criticized the interference as counterproductive and damaging to the constitution's reputation. Shinagawa left office in the spring, the new House attacked the government's conduct, and the Matsukata cabinet resigned in August. The lesson drawn by many leaders was that open coercion could not secure a reliable majority and that other methods, including party cooperation, would have to be considered.

Division V — Constitutional Practice, Contradictions, and Historical Significance · Section 59.23

From Parliamentary Deadlock to Negotiation, 1892–1894

In August 1892 Itō Hirobumi formed his second cabinet, staffed by senior statesmen and sometimes called the cabinet of the elder statesmen. The new government soon confronted the House over naval expansion. When the House cut funds for warship construction, a dispute that threatened another stalemate, an imperial message in February 1893 announced that the emperor would contribute part of his household funds annually for several years and that officials would contribute a portion of their salaries toward naval construction, and it urged harmony. The House then passed the budget. The episode shows that the throne could mediate when ministers could not command a majority, and that the government still needed the House's consent.

Conflict continued over foreign policy, especially treaty revision, on which hard-line critics attacked the cabinet as too accommodating, and over discipline within the House: Hoshi Tōru, a Jiyūtō leader who had served as speaker, was expelled in late 1893 amid corruption charges. Itō dissolved the House in December 1893 and again in June 1894, just before the outbreak of war with China, treated in Chapter 60. Behind the repeated dissolutions lay a gradual recognition by both sides: ministers found they could not govern effectively while ignoring elected representatives, and party leaders found that obstruction brought dissolution rather than power. This is the background to the cooperation between the government and parts of the parties in the later 1890s, treated in subsequent chapters.

The conflicts of 1892–1894 can be read as a negotiation over the terms of constitutional government. Itō's second cabinet, a government of senior statesmen, sought to combine authority with accommodation. It used the imperial household as a mediator in the naval dispute of 1893: according to the commonly cited account, the emperor pledged a yearly contribution from the court budget for six years and called on officials to give a portion of their salaries, framing military expansion as a matter of national duty. The opposition majority accepted the settlement, but the episode did not end the conflict; it showed that the throne could be used to resolve deadlock without removing the underlying disagreement.

Parties meanwhile divided over strategy. Some Liberal leaders, notably Hoshi Tōru, favored cooperating with the government in return for influence, while others and the Kaishintō sided with hard-line critics who attacked the cabinet's treaty-revision diplomacy and demanded strict enforcement of the existing treaties. The expulsion of Hoshi from the speakership in December 1893 over corruption charges and the dissolution that followed illustrate the volatility of these alignments. The election of March 1894 and the dissolution of June 1894 did not produce a durable majority for either side.

The pattern was one of mutual limitation. Ministers could dissolve the House but could not guarantee the result, and they could carry on under the previous budget but not expand programs. Parties could cut and criticize but could not choose ministers. Both sides accordingly developed incentives to bargain: the government by offering concessions such as lower land-tax pressure, local public works, and consultation, and the parties by seeking office or influence rather than permanent opposition. This is the background to later alliances between the government and party leaders, which later chapters examine, and it explains why the first four years are better described as a trial of the institutions than as a simple victory for either side.

Section 59.24

Constitutional Government without Popular Sovereignty: Achievements and Limits

By 1894 the constitutional system had real achievements. Japan had a written constitution, an independent judiciary with a recorded record of resisting pressure, a bicameral legislature that met regularly, published debates, and a lower house that had forced the government to bargain on the budget and had survived dissolutions and violent elections. The legal framework supported later codification and treaty revision. At the same time it had profound limits: sovereignty in the emperor, ministers not responsible to the Diet, an electorate of about one percent, the exclusion of women, uneven regional incorporation, an unelected upper house with equal legislative powers, protected expenditures and prerogatives, and a ruling circle that operated through informal channels.

Whether later crises were predetermined by the 1889 framework is a different question. The text allowed more than one trajectory. Its ambiguities, including the status of sovereignty, the scope of Article 67, and the absence of any rule about the Cabinet, were resolved by practice, personalities, and later struggles, from Minobe's interpretation to party cabinets in the 1920s and military claims to autonomy in the 1930s (Chapters 67 and 74).

Historical Debate

Did the Constitution Mainly Restrain Government Power or Preserve Oligarchic Authority?

The question: Was the Meiji Constitution primarily a framework that bound the state to law and gave an elected body real powers, or primarily a device by which a small elite preserved its dominance under a constitutional form?

Interpretation A — A constraining framework: Some historians, including Takii Kazuhiro in his study of Itō Hirobumi, place the constitution in a wider history of state-building, comparative constitutional thought, and encounters with the West, and emphasize that Itō and his colleagues intended to bind the monarchy and executive to rules, create institutions that could outlast individuals, and give the Diet genuine budget and legislative power. On this view the text created a constitutional state whose logic could, and later did, support greater party influence.
Interpretation B — A shield for oligarchic authority: Other historians, drawing on one strand of the long Marxist-influenced tradition of analysis of the "emperor system" (a body of interpretation, not a single uniform position), emphasize the sovereignty clause, the limits on rights, the unelected upper house, protected expenditures, and the informal power of the genrō and bureaucracy, arguing that the constitution preserved ruling-group authority.

Evidence: Both interpretations rely on the text, the drafters' papers, the Privy Council debates, and the record of the first Diets. A has the budget compromises, the Diet's survival, and judicial independence; B has restrictions on franchise and rights, ministerial responsibility to the throne, and the 1892 election. Many scholars hold overlapping positions, and the two are not mutually exclusive.

Why it matters: The answer shapes how later developments are understood, whether party government in the 1920s is seen as a natural extension or a departure, and how Japan's path toward the 1930s is explained.

Section 59.25

Chapter Review and Historical Thinking Checkpoint

This chapter has traced the making and early operation of the Meiji constitutional order. Students should be able to explain why the government chose a monarchy-centered European model, how the 1889 text distributed authority among the emperor, ministers, Privy Council, courts, and Diet, and why the budget became the principal arena of conflict. They should also be able to distinguish constitutional office from informal power, explain the restricted franchise and its exclusions, and describe how the first budget crisis and the election of 1892 showed institutional rules shaping political struggle.

The chapter's core caution is that constitutional arrangements created opportunities for elected representatives to gain influence but also preserved structures capable of limiting it. Neither a verdict of success nor of failure fits the evidence; the system should be understood as a negotiated, contested, and changing order.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how Japan acquired a constitution and a parliament between 1881 and 1894, and what kind of political order resulted. The government's promise of a national assembly in 1881 answered the Freedom and People's Rights Movement, but it also secured elite control over how the constitution would be designed. The distinction between agreeing to a constitution and accepting popular sovereignty remains the essential starting point.

Itō Hirobumi's European mission and the Cabinet reforms of 1885 prepared the ground. Itō and his colleagues, advised by German-speaking scholars, rejected a British-style system based on a cabinet responsible to an elected majority, and drafted a text in secret that placed sovereignty in the emperor and entrusted executive authority to ministers answerable to the throne. The Privy Council reviewed the draft, and the constitution was promulgated on 11 February 1889 as an imperial gift.

The constitution's institutions were real and distinct. The emperor held broad prerogatives exercised through advisers; the Cabinet administered government without being named in the text; the Privy Council advised; the courts exercised judicature under law, with an Administrative Court apart; and the Diet consented to laws and budgets. Beyond these institutions stood informal structures, including the genrō and a professional bureaucracy, and a military command with a claim to separate status.

Subjects' rights were guaranteed but qualified by law. Statutes on the press, assembly, and political association limited their exercise, and women were barred from political meetings and associations. The restricted electorate, about 450,000 voters or 1.1 percent of the population, favored landowners, left out Hokkaido and Okinawa, and gave the House of Representatives a social base unlike the population it formally represented. The House of Peers, composed of nobles, appointees, and selected taxpayers, had powers equal to the lower house over legislation.

The first Diet opened in November 1890 and quickly turned to the budget. The dispute over Article 67 in 1890–1891 ended in compromise and the defection of some opposition members. The dissolution of 1891 and the election of 1892, accompanied by official interference and violence, showed the state's readiness to manipulate elections and its failure to secure a compliant House.

From 1892 to 1894 the pattern shifted from confrontation toward negotiation. The imperial message of 1893 resolved the naval budget dispute, but repeated dissolutions continued until the eve of war with China. Both sides learned that the executive needed consent for laws and budgets, while parties learned that obstruction alone would not win office.

Regional and social dimensions remained visible throughout. Tosa and other former movement strongholds were central in the early opposition; Hokkaido and Okinawa were not represented; Ainu, Okinawans, and Burakumin were subjects without equal standing; women were excluded; and tenants and workers had little formal voice. The constitutional order also interacted with Japan's foreign relations, since Meiji leaders hoped legal modernization would help revise the unequal treaties.

Historians disagree about how to characterize the system. Some stress that it constrained government through rules and gave the Diet real leverage; others stress that it protected oligarchic authority. The evidence supports elements of both, and the framework permitted several later outcomes.

The chapter's lasting significance is that it created institutions that endured until 1947 and that shaped the arenas in which later struggles over suffrage, party government, military autonomy, and the meaning of sovereignty would be fought.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a state can adopt constitutional forms without adopting popular sovereignty, and how those forms can nonetheless create genuine political space. The Meiji Constitution gave Japan a legal framework, an elected assembly, and an arena in which opposition could operate, while preserving structures that limited what that opposition could achieve.

It also reveals processes that continued beyond 1894: bargaining over the budget, the rivalry between elected politicians and the ruling circle, the ambiguous place of the emperor, and unequal incorporation of regions and groups. These themes return in the chapters on party government, labor, suffrage, and the crisis of the 1930s, and again, in changed form, in the postwar constitution.

Finally, the chapter links the domestic and the international. Japanese leaders drew on European models, used constitutionalism in diplomacy, and then, as Chapter 60 shows, entered a war that changed the political balance. Students who understand the constitutional order as contested and open to several outcomes are prepared to study that history without treating it as inevitable.
