Part VII — Tokugawa Japan

Japan's Controlled International World

Japanese History: From Jōmon Japan to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

In Nagasaki harbor lay a small, fan-shaped artificial island, joined to the city by a single guarded bridge. A few European men lived and worked there under close supervision. Yet every year they delivered written reports on world affairs to the shogun's officials, and ships from Batavia carried Chinese silk and Southeast Asian goods into Japan. This was not a sealed country. It was a country that had decided who could enter, by which door, and under whose supervision.

The island was Dejima, and it is the best-known symbol of Tokugawa foreign relations. Its image has often been used to illustrate a Japan "closed" to the world, as if a single tiny aperture had admitted the only light. But Dejima was one point in a much larger structure. A short walk away stood a walled compound for Chinese merchants, whose junks arrived from Fujian, Zhejiang, and Southeast Asian ports. Across the Tsushima Strait, officials of the Sō family maintained a Japanese trading settlement near Busan. Every decade or so, a Korean embassy of several hundred people traveled the Inland Sea and the Tōkaidō to Edo. Envoys from the Ryukyu Kingdom, dressed in ways that emphasized their foreignness, processed through Japanese towns. In the north, the Matsumae domain traded with Ainu communities whose commercial networks reached the lower Amur and the Qing frontier.

This chapter argues that these relationships were not four isolated exceptions to a general policy of seclusion. Together they formed an organized, hierarchical, and changing system through which the Tokugawa shogunate regulated diplomacy, commerce, information, and security. The word usually applied to this system, sakoku ("closed country"), was not a term used by seventeenth-century officials. It was coined in 1801, in a Japanese translation of a European text, and it became a standard label only in the nineteenth century and after. Historians now treat the word cautiously because it captures one real feature of the period, tight restriction of European access and of Japanese travel abroad, while concealing much else.

The system was also unequal. Different partners received different treatment: the Dutch and Chinese were permitted to trade but not to conduct formal diplomacy; Korea and Ryukyu exchanged embassies; the Ainu were treated by Matsumae as a distinct people with whom trade, not ordinary subjecthood, was the relationship. Each channel had its own mediators, from Nagasaki's hereditary interpreters to the Sō family of Tsushima and the Shimazu of Satsuma. Each produced winners and losers. The system gave the shogunate prestige and security, brought merchants and domains profit, and supplied Japan with silk, medicines, books, and information. It also imposed heavy burdens on Ryukyuan peasants, subjected Ainu communities to growing economic dependency and exploitation, and left the Dutch and Chinese in tightly supervised enclaves.

Historians reconstruct this world from many kinds of evidence: the Dutch East India Company's factory diaries and annual Dutch Reports (Oranda fūsetsugaki) submitted to the bakufu; records of the Nagasaki magistrates; the archives of the Sō family; Korean travel accounts; Ryukyuan and Chinese investiture records; Matsumae documents; and, for the Ainu, a combination of Wajin (mainland Japanese) records, Russian and Chinese sources, archaeology, and oral tradition. These sources carry real biases. Most Ainu history in this period reaches us through the records of the people who traded with and exploited them, so students must read against the grain and ask whose voices are missing.

Chapter 48 builds on Chapter 37 (the arrival of the Portuguese and the early Christian mission) and Chapter 47 (Christian persecution, temple registration, and the Shimabara Rebellion). It does not retell them, but explains the international system that took shape alongside those religious policies. It anticipates Chapter 50, which examines Dutch learning (rangaku) and scholarship in depth, and Chapters 51 and 52, which treat the Perry expedition and the treaty crises. The chapter ends with a system that still functioned but was increasingly strained, which is the institutional background to the crises that follow.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why "sakoku" is an inadequate literal description of Tokugawa foreign relations, and when and how the term originated.
  2. Trace the movement from early-seventeenth-century overseas commerce to the regulatory edicts of the 1630s and identify the interests that shaped them.
  3. Distinguish diplomatic relations (Korea, Ryukyu) from commercial relations (the Dutch and Chinese) and from frontier exchange (Ezo and the Ainu).
  4. Describe how the bakufu, the Nagasaki administration, and the domains of Tsushima, Satsuma, and Matsumae each governed a distinct contact zone.
  5. Analyze the organization of trade at Nagasaki, including the roles of Dejima, the Chinese compound, hereditary interpreters, and the commodities exchanged.
  6. Assess Korea's role as an active diplomatic participant by interpreting the purpose, ceremony, and disputed meaning of the Joseon embassies.
  7. Evaluate how Ryukyu's dual relationships with Satsuma and Qing China shaped its autonomy, burdens, and strategies.
  8. Explain the political position of Matsumae and the transformation of Ainu-Wajin trade, including Shakushain's War and the 1789 Menashi–Kunashir uprising, while preserving Ainu historical agency.
  9. Connect Russian expansion in the North Pacific to bakufu and Matsumae policy before the 1850s.
  10. Interpret a primary source from a Korean embassy and identify its perspective and limitations.
  11. Evaluate competing historians' interpretations of the "isolation" question.
  12. Contextualize the system within wider East Asian maritime regulation, and recognize change within it from the seventeenth to the mid-nineteenth century.

Historical Context

Tokugawa foreign relations developed from the commercial and political turbulence of the late sixteenth century. Chapter 37 traced the arrival of Portuguese traders and Jesuit missionaries after the 1540s, along with the firearms and silk trade that tied Kyushu ports to Macau. Chapters 39 to 41 described the reunification of Japan under Oda Nobunaga, Toyotomi Hideyoshi, and Tokugawa Ieyasu. Hideyoshi's invasions of Korea (1592–1598) devastated the peninsula and broke the relations that had linked Japan, Joseon, and Ming China. The first generation of Tokugawa leaders therefore inherited a landscape of ruptured diplomacy, active Japanese merchants overseas, European competitors, and a new regime anxious about its legitimacy.

The wider East Asian setting mattered as much as domestic politics. Ming China had never fully accepted Japan as a diplomatic partner after the end of the tally trade in the mid-sixteenth century, and in the 1640s the Ming fell to the Manchu Qing. Qing rule, and the Zheng family's resistance to it from Taiwan and the southeast coast, convulsed maritime commerce for decades. Joseon Korea, recovering from the Japanese invasions and facing Manchu pressure in the north, had its own interests in stabilizing relations with Japan. Maritime regulation was common in the region: Ming and Qing authorities imposed coastal and shipping restrictions of their own, and Joseon restricted the movement of foreigners. The Tokugawa system should be understood as one regional variant of state control over sea trade, not a uniquely Japanese withdrawal.

Domestically, the shogunate was consolidating the bakuhan order described in Chapter 42. Daimyō governed their domains, but the bakufu reserved decisions on war, diplomacy, and the right to trade abroad. Coastal domains in Kyushu and the far south and north had long had their own overseas ties and military capacity. Tsushima depended on Korea for its livelihood, Satsuma had links to the Ryukyu Kingdom, and Matsumae lay at the edge of Ainu lands. A recurring theme of this chapter is that the Tokugawa did not eliminate such regional ties; it incorporated them into a hierarchy in which the shogun held ultimate authority over foreign relations and delegated specific responsibilities to particular houses. The reader should also keep in mind the religious developments of Chapter 47, because fear of Christianity was one important motive for controlling foreign contact.

Section 48.1

Beyond the Myth of Isolation: Tokugawa Japan in East Asia

The idea that Japan was sealed off from the world between the 1630s and the 1850s is one of the most durable images in popular history. It is not entirely false. Tokugawa authorities did forbid Japanese subjects from traveling abroad, prohibited the building of large oceangoing ships, outlawed Christianity, and expelled the Portuguese and Spanish, confining European commerce to the Dutch at Nagasaki. These were genuine and consequential restrictions. But the picture of an isolated country leaves out what continued: regular trade with China and Southeast Asia, diplomatic missions from Korea and Ryukyu, commercial and cultural exchange along the northern frontier, a steady flow of information about world events, and the movement of goods that shaped Japanese consumption, medicine, art, and learning.

The term sakoku itself has a specific history. In 1801 the Nagasaki interpreter Shizuki Tadao translated a section of The History of Japan, a work by the German physician Engelbert Kaempfer, who had served at the Dutch factory in 1690–1692. The section argued that Japan was justified in keeping its country closed to foreign intercourse. Shizuki rendered the title with a new word, "sakoku-ron" ("a discussion of the closed country"). The word became widely used after the 1850s, when Japanese and foreign commentators needed a name for the policy that Perry's expedition had challenged. Tokugawa officials themselves used other expressions, among them kaikin (sea prohibitions), and later conceptual distinctions between "countries of communication" (tsūshin no kuni, Korea and Ryukyu) and "countries of trade" (tsūshō no kuni, the Dutch and Chinese). Such language suggests that officials thought in terms of regulated categories of relationship, not of a single wall.

This chapter treats the Tokugawa system as an arrangement of several channels. A useful way to organize them is the model of four principal contact zones, developed by historians including Arano Yasunori and Ronald Toby: Nagasaki, where the Dutch and Chinese traded under bakufu supervision; Tsushima, where the Sō family managed relations with Korea; Satsuma, which supervised the Ryukyu Kingdom; and Matsumae, which controlled trade with the Ainu in Ezo. These zones were not identical in function. Two (Korea and Ryukyu) involved embassies and diplomatic ceremony, one (Nagasaki) involved commerce without formal diplomacy, and one (Ezo) involved a frontier in which the Tokugawa claimed only partial authority. Still, they were connected by the same logic: the shogun, not individual domains or merchants, defined Japan's external relationships, and the shogun's status was displayed and reinforced through them.

Seeing the system this way does not romanticize it. The Tokugawa order restricted Japanese mobility, sharply limited European access, and placed the Dutch and Chinese in enclaves. It also subordinated Ryukyu and Ainu communities to Japanese commercial and political interests in unequal and sometimes violent ways. The point is that these were policies of control, hierarchy, and selection, not of withdrawal.

Historical Vocabulary
Sakoku (鎖国)

Sakoku, "closed country" or "locked country," is the conventional label for the Tokugawa foreign policy of the seventeenth to mid-nineteenth century. The word was coined in 1801 by Shizuki Tadao in a translation of Kaempfer's work and was not a term used by the officials who devised the restrictions. It came into general use only in the nineteenth century, when the policy was being challenged and, later, repudiated by the Meiji government and its historians.

The term matters because it shapes expectations. If students begin with "closed country," they tend to look only for exceptions. This chapter instead begins with the question of how the shogunate organized its foreign relations and uses sakoku as a historiographical term to be tested rather than a description to be assumed. Many scholars now prefer expressions such as "maritime prohibitions" (kaikin) or "the regulated trade and diplomatic system," though debates continue (see the Historical Debate in Section 48.16).

Section 48.2

From Maritime Expansion to Regulated Foreign Relations

The restrictions of the 1630s followed a period of vigorous Japanese overseas activity. After Hideyoshi began issuing licenses for foreign voyages in the 1590s, the "red-seal" (shuin) ship system, continued by Tokugawa Ieyasu, authorized Japanese merchants, daimyō, and some foreign residents to trade in Southeast Asia. Historians count on the order of three hundred and fifty licensed voyages between the early 1600s and 1635, to destinations such as Siam, Cochinchina, Cambodia, Luzon, and Taiwan. Japanese communities (Nihonmachi) took shape in places such as Ayutthaya and Hoi An, and Japanese soldiers served in Southeast Asian armies. Exports included silver, copper, and weapons; imports included raw silk, deerskins, sugar, and sappanwood. Ieyasu also welcomed European traders beyond the Portuguese, receiving the Dutch and English, and the Englishman William Adams (Miura Anjin), who arrived on the Dutch ship Liefde in 1600, became an adviser to the shogun.

Between the 1610s and 1630s, the shogunate gradually narrowed this openness. A 1616 order confined European ship arrivals to Hirado and Nagasaki. Spain's trade was ended in the 1620s, and the English closed their Hirado factory in 1623 for commercial reasons. In 1633 the bakufu required, in addition to the red-seal license, a permit from the senior councillors (rōjū), and forbade Japanese who had lived abroad for five years or more from returning. In 1635 it prohibited all Japanese from leaving the country and banned oceangoing voyages, with death threatened for those who returned; the same year Chinese vessels were restricted to Nagasaki. The Portuguese were expelled in 1639, after the Shimabara Rebellion (Chapter 47) intensified fears of Christian networks, and the Dutch were moved in 1641 to the island built for the Portuguese.

Why did these changes occur? Historians emphasize a combination of factors rather than any single cause. Concern about Christianity, as Chapter 47 explained, gave the shogunate a security and ideological rationale. The bakufu also worried that powerful domains, especially in Kyushu, might build independent foreign connections or acquire military resources through overseas trade. Controlling the right to trade offered a way to channel profit to the shogunate and to approved merchants. The regime was also constructing a hierarchical political order in which foreign missions and the shogun's exclusive authority over foreign affairs enhanced its legitimacy at home. These motives overlapped and did not necessarily imply a desire to cut off all contact. The Ming–Qing transition, by contrast, should not be counted among the causes of the 1630s restrictions, since the decisive Ming collapse came in 1644, after the regulatory framework was largely in place. Its importance lay in what followed: once the system had taken shape, the Manchu conquest, the Zheng resistance, and continuing instability along the Chinese coast created new diplomatic and commercial challenges that Tokugawa authorities had to manage within it (Section 48.6).

The consequences were uneven. Japanese merchant communities abroad gradually disappeared, and Japanese seafarers lost a legitimate role in long-distance commerce. At the same time, trade did not vanish. It was concentrated in the hands of the shogunate's approved intermediaries and carried largely by foreign ships. The change was thus a reorganization of who could carry out Japan's overseas commerce and under what conditions, not an end to such commerce.

Section 48.3

The Bakufu, Domains, and the Governance of Foreign Relations

The Tokugawa system of foreign relations depended on a division of labor between the shogunate and particular domains. The bakufu, through its senior councillors (rōjū), decided who might trade, which embassies would be received, and what ceremonies governed them. It directly administered Nagasaki, where the shogun's magistrates supervised the Dutch and Chinese. For other contact zones it relied on specially placed daimyō houses: the Sō of Tsushima for Korea, the Shimazu of Satsuma for Ryukyu, and the Matsumae for Ezo. These houses carried out these responsibilities in part as military service (gun'yaku) to the shogun, and their special position gave them both privilege and obligation. They were not free agents; their relationships with foreign partners were bound by bakufu rules and could be revised by bakufu decision.

Students should distinguish several kinds of relationship. Formal diplomacy existed with Korea and Ryukyu: the first sent embassies for specific occasions, and the second sent congratulatory and thanksgiving missions to Edo. Commerce without diplomacy characterized relations with the Dutch and Chinese, who traded but did not exchange ambassadors with the shogun. Frontier exchange characterized Ezo, where the Matsumae handled trade with Ainu people who were not subjects of the shogun in the ordinary sense. Finally, mediated contact ran through all of these: the Sō for Korea, Satsuma for Ryukyu's China connection, Nagasaki interpreters for Dutch and Chinese communication, and Matsumae for Ainu goods and Santan (Amur) products. Almost every channel had a go-between.

Why did the shogunate not simply enter the Chinese tribute system, as the Ashikaga shoguns had done in the early fifteenth century? Tokugawa leaders had no interest in subordinating themselves to a Chinese emperor, and after the Manchu conquest they viewed Qing legitimacy with suspicion. At the same time, Japan did not formally refuse trade with China; it simply organized it as a merchant exchange outside the framework of tribute. The shogunate instead constructed a self-centered hierarchy in which it received Korean and Ryukyuan embassies and presented itself as a center of an ordered region. Arano Yasunori has called this a "Japan-centered" order in East Asia, parallel to, but not subordinate to, the Chinese order. Korea and Ryukyu understood their own relationships differently. Joseon saw its embassies as "neighborly relations" between equals, and Ryukyu answered to two powers at once. The shogunate and its partners therefore sometimes shared ceremonies while interpreting them differently, a theme developed in Sections 48.9 and 48.10.

This governance structure also controlled domestic politics. Daimyō who might have wished to trade independently were prevented from doing so, while the houses entrusted with foreign relations gained prestige and revenue. Because the right to manage foreign contact was delegated rather than abandoned, the shogun could remove or alter it. The same structure made the bakufu dependent on the loyalty and competence of its delegates, a vulnerability that became important when pressure from outside increased.

Section 48.4

Nagasaki: Japan's Regulated International Port

Nagasaki was a young city. It had developed in the late sixteenth century as a harbor for Portuguese trade and a center of Japanese Christianity, and it came under direct shogunal control (as bakufu land, tenryō) in the early Tokugawa period. Its administration was headed by the Nagasaki magistrates (bugyō), normally two, alternating residence between Nagasaki and Edo so that one was always in the city. They oversaw customs, security, justice, and relations with foreign merchants. Beneath them, town elders and ward officials, drawn from local merchant families, handled urban administration. Neighboring domains were drawn in as well: from 1641, Fukuoka and Saga alternated in providing the garrison that guarded the harbor, an obligation that tied two powerful Kyushu daimyō houses to the maintenance of the system.

Trade at Nagasaki followed procedures that left little room for improvisation. Arriving Dutch and Chinese ships were inspected, cargoes were appraised by designated officials and merchants, and prices were negotiated under bakufu supervision. In the late seventeenth century a central trading office (Nagasaki kaisho, established 1698) was created to handle accounts and oversee the sale of imported goods, and a portion of profits supported the city's population and its institutions. Merchants from Kyoto, Osaka, and elsewhere came to bid on goods. Nagasaki thus connected overseas trade to Japan's wider commercial system described in Chapter 45.

Interpreters were essential to this order. Hereditary families of Dutch interpreters (Oranda tsūji) and Chinese interpreters (tōtsūji) mediated communications, translated documents, and supervised foreign residents. They were not merely linguists; they were officials, brokers, and sources of information, and their social standing and incomes depended on the system. Interpreter families formed one pillar of the city's elite and later played a central role in the transmission of European learning (see Section 48.14).

Nagasaki also had a social life in which foreign and Japanese worlds touched. Chinese temples built by merchant communities, such as Kōfukuji and Sōfukuji, combined worship, community organization, and commerce. The pleasure quarter at Maruyama was one of the few places where Dutch and Chinese men met Japanese women, and courtesans were permitted to enter the foreign compounds. The city also remained a place of surveillance and ritual: as Chapter 47 explained, the annual treading on images (fumi-e) was a mark of the regime's religious policy. Nagasaki was therefore both a gateway and a checkpoint, and its prosperity depended on the shogunate's willingness to keep it open on strictly defined terms.

Section 48.5

The Dutch East India Company and Dejima

The Dutch relationship began in 1609, when the Dutch East India Company (VOC) received a shogunal trading permit and set up a factory at Hirado. Dutch interest lay in access to Japanese silver, to a market for goods from the VOC's Asian network, and to a base from which to compete with the Portuguese. The Dutch were Protestants who did not pursue missionary activity, and they emphasized this difference to the shogunate. In 1639, however, the bakufu objected to a warehouse at Hirado that bore a date in the Christian calendar, and the Dutch were ordered to demolish it. In 1641 they were ordered to move to Dejima, the island built between 1634 and 1636 by a group of Nagasaki investors to house the Portuguese. The VOC paid rent for the site.

Life at Dejima was regulated by detailed rules. The Europeans there, typically about a dozen at a time, were confined to the island, could not conduct Christian worship in public, and could leave only under permission and escort. The head of the factory (the opperhoofd, called kapitan in Japanese) was replaced annually. In the early period he traveled to Edo each year to pay respects to the shogun, a journey known as the hofreis; from 1790 the trip was made every four years. The Dutch brought gifts, observed prescribed ceremonies, and in return received continued access. They also submitted annual Dutch Reports (Oranda fūsetsugaki), written summaries of world news that supplied the shogunate with information on Europe, Asia, and the activities of other powers.

The Dutch were traders, not diplomats of an equal sovereign power. They had no treaty with the shogunate and held privileges that could be modified at will. The relationship was also lopsided in a second sense: Japan was only one outpost in the VOC's much larger Asian network, centered on Batavia, and Dejima was often more significant to the Japanese than to the company. Dutch trade carried Chinese raw silk and textiles, sugar, deerskins, spices, and other Southeast Asian products to Japan, and exported silver, then gold, and later in particular copper, along with lacquer and Arita-Imari porcelain, which the VOC sold in Asia and Europe. The company was dissolved in 1799, but the Dutch connection continued under the Dutch state, and the factory became a rare point of communication between Japan and Europe during the Napoleonic era.

Dejima was also a place of intellectual exchange, though a controlled one. Physicians attached to the factory, among them Kaempfer in 1690–1692, Carl Peter Thunberg in 1775–1776, and Philipp Franz von Siebold from 1823, taught Japanese students and collected information on Japanese natural history and society. The Dutch connection nonetheless remained, in the eyes of the bakufu, a commercial and informational relationship that it could supervise, restrict, or adjust as it chose. The deeper intellectual consequences belong to Chapter 50.

Section 48.6

Chinese Merchants, Maritime Networks, and Qing Relations

Chinese merchants were the largest foreign trading presence in Tokugawa Japan, even though the Dutch have received more attention in popular accounts. They came from Fujian, Zhejiang, Jiangnan, and from overseas Chinese communities in Southeast Asian ports such as Batavia, Siam, and Vietnam. Many of the ships were privately owned junks, and a given ship's cargo might include goods from several ports. After the Ming fell, the Qing government imposed a coastal evacuation and maritime ban in the 1660s to isolate the Zheng family's resistance, which operated from Taiwan under the leadership of Zheng Chenggong (Koxinga), whose mother was Japanese and who had been born at Hirado. When the Qing lifted its ban in 1684, the number of junks arriving in Nagasaki surged to nearly two hundred a year at the end of the 1680s.

The shogunate responded by tightening control. In 1688 it limited the number of Chinese ships to seventy per year, and in 1689 it established the Tōjin yashiki, a walled compound in which Chinese residents were required to live, accommodating on the order of two thousand people. In 1715 new regulations (the Shōtoku shinrei, associated with the scholar-statesman Arai Hakuseki) limited annual arrivals to thirty Chinese ships and two Dutch ships, with overall caps on the value of trade. Chinese captains also received licenses (shinpai) that regulated which ships might trade. The measures were responses to anxiety about the outflow of precious metals as well as to concerns about smuggling and security.

There was no formal diplomatic relationship between the Tokugawa and the Ming or Qing courts. After the end of the tally trade in the sixteenth century, and after Hideyoshi's invasion of Korea, neither side restored tribute relations. When the Ming collapsed and Zheng Zhilong's family sought military help from Japan in the 1640s and later, the bakufu declined to intervene. Qing authorities did not object to private trade, and the Tokugawa did not request a tributary relationship. Chinese commerce was accordingly treated as merchant business, a vital but formally non-diplomatic connection. The shogunate nonetheless collected extensive information from Chinese merchants about the Ming–Qing transition; its scholars compiled their reports in a work called Ka'i hentai ("Transformation from Civilized to Barbarian"), a title that reflects the Japanese perception of the Manchu conquest.

Chinese influence was cultural as well as economic. The monk Ingen (Yinyuan), who arrived at Nagasaki in 1654, founded the Ōbaku school of Zen, with its center at Manpukuji near Kyoto, and introduced Chinese Buddhist styles of ritual and architecture. Chinese painters such as Shen Nanpin, who resided in Nagasaki from 1731 to 1733, influenced Japanese artists. Chinese medicine, books, and artistic fashion circulated through Nagasaki and via Ryukyu. These contacts did not make Japan "Sinicized" in any simple sense; Japanese scholars, physicians, and artists selected and transformed what they received for their own purposes.

Everyday Life and Environment

Living in a Contact Zone

The international system was lived, not just administered. In Nagasaki, ordinary residents depended on foreign trade for work as porters, boatmen, warehouse keepers, brokers, and craftspeople; some shared in the profits distributed to the city. Dutch and Chinese residents lived under rules that limited movement and supervised contact, yet they also ate, prayed, gambled, and socialized in the city's quarters. Women of the Maruyama pleasure district had contact with foreign men, and children of such relationships, whom the authorities regarded with ambivalence, appear in the records.

The same was true at the other zones. Tsushima's population depended on the Korea trade because the island had little farmland. Ryukyuan villagers bore taxes shaped by Satsuma's demands and the costs of tribute missions. In Ezo, Ainu families participated in fishing, hunting, and exchange systems that determined their access to rice, iron tools, cloth, and sake. Such lives show that "foreign relations" were part of the everyday economy of coastal and frontier communities, far from the diplomatic ceremonies of Edo.

Section 48.7

Commodities, Silver, Copper, and the Economics of Regulated Trade

The goods that moved through Nagasaki and other contact zones changed over time. In the early seventeenth century Japan was among the world's major silver producers, and silver exports financed much of the import of Chinese raw silk and silk textiles. Silver was so significant that the bakufu began to restrict its export in 1668, after which gold and then copper took its place as principal exports, especially after the 1680s. Copper became especially important in the eighteenth century, much of it from mines such as Besshi. Other exports included lacquerware, camphor, and Arita-Imari porcelain, which found markets in Asia and Europe. By the eighteenth century, dried marine products such as sea cucumber, dried abalone, and shark fin, often called tawaramono ("bale goods"), became major exports to Qing China. These goods linked Nagasaki to the fisheries of Ezo and the northern sea routes, and later kelp from Ezo also reached China through Ryukyu.

Imports included Chinese raw silk and silk fabrics, sugar, medicines and medicinal herbs, books, deerskins, sappanwood, and other Southeast Asian products. Korean ginseng, brought through Tsushima, was a prized medicine. Trade at the contact zones therefore supplied luxury goods but also materials of practical importance, from textiles to pharmaceuticals. Japanese producers adapted. As Chapter 45 noted, domestic silk production in places such as Kiryū and the Nishijin district of Kyoto expanded partly to substitute for imports, and in the eighteenth century the bakufu encouraged domestic cultivation of ginseng and sugar to reduce dependency.

The bakufu's economic concerns were persistent. Officials worried about the outflow of gold, silver, and copper, and they responded with regulation: ceilings on trade values from 1685, the 1715 limits on ships and annual totals, and later revisions. Some historians have argued that these policies reflected a mercantilist impulse to protect the monetary base, a view associated with Arai Hakuseki, whose arguments about the drain of precious metals strongly influenced policy. Others stress that the regulations also protected the monopoly interests of bakufu-approved merchants and officials. The Tanuma Okitsugu administration (1767–1786), by contrast, showed that policy could change direction, encouraging exports of marine products and copper and considering the development of Ezo.

It is important not to overstate or understate the scale of this trade. International commerce was small relative to Japan's domestic economy, dominated as that economy was by agriculture and internal commerce, and it was far smaller than Japan's sixteenth- and early seventeenth-century overseas trade at its peak. But it was disproportionately significant for particular goods, such as silk, medicines, and sugar, and for the incomes of specific places, including Nagasaki, Tsushima, Satsuma, and Matsumae. The economics of controlled trade thus produced a system that mattered greatly to certain regions and groups while remaining a limited sector of the whole.

Section 48.8

Korea, Tsushima, and the Restoration of Diplomatic Relations

The war of 1592–1598 left Korea devastated and its memory bitter. Yet within a decade the Tokugawa regime and Joseon were negotiating. Ieyasu wanted to restore relations to end the diplomatic isolation of the Hideyoshi era and to secure the regime's standing, while the Sō family of Tsushima, whose domain had almost no arable land, depended on trade with Korea and had the strongest incentive to repair relations. Joseon had its own reasons: a need to secure its southern border while facing growing Manchu power in the north, the hope of recovering captives taken during the war, and the calculation that peaceful relations with Japan were safer than hostility. The result was a Korean mission in 1607, formally named an embassy "to reply to the letter and to bring back captives," which returned a considerable number of prisoners, and a trade arrangement in 1609 that set terms for Tsushima's commerce.

The Sō were the indispensable go-betweens. Reconciliation was achieved in part through diplomatic fabrication: Tsushima officials forged letters from Ieyasu and altered Korean replies in order to smooth negotiations, a deception that came to light in the 1630s. In the Yanagawa affair, the retainer Yanagawa Shigeoki, who had accused his lord of forgery, was himself exiled in 1635, but the shogun Iemitsu retained the Sō as the managers of Korean relations and reorganized the system, installing Zen monks from Kyoto to draft diplomatic correspondence. The affair is instructive because it shows how a go-between's interests could shape international diplomacy, and how the shogunate chose to keep a flawed but useful mediator.

Trade centered on the Japan House (Waegwan) at Busan, a walled settlement where several hundred Tsushima personnel lived and traded under Korean regulation (moved in 1678 to a larger site at Choryang). Joseon permitted Japanese residence only there; Japanese envoys were not allowed to travel to Seoul. Tsushima imported Korean ginseng, cotton, and rice, along with Chinese silk acquired in Korea, and exported copper, silver in earlier decades, and Southeast Asian goods such as sappanwood and pepper. The shogunate formally assigned the domain a very high nominal rank (equivalent to 100,000 koku), recognizing that its real wealth lay in trade rather than rice.

The relationship was asymmetrical in form. Joseon sent embassies to Japan, but the shogunate sent none to Seoul, and Korean officials limited Japanese access to Busan. Joseon policy toward Japan was called "neighborly relations" (gyorin), a term denoting relations between sovereign equals, as distinct from the hierarchical tribute relationship Korea had with China. Many Korean elites nonetheless regarded Japan as culturally inferior, and Japanese leaders regarded the Korean missions as evidence of the shogun's prestige. Understanding the Korean relationship requires seeing that both sides shaped it for their own purposes, and that the same ceremonies could be read in different ways.

Section 48.9

Korean Embassies and the Politics of Diplomatic Ceremony

Between 1607 and 1811 Joseon sent twelve embassies to Japan, most to congratulate a new shogun on his accession or to mark other major events. Each mission numbered between three hundred and five hundred people, including the ambassador and his deputies, scholars, physicians, painters, musicians, military escorts, interpreters, and servants. The party traveled from Seoul to Busan, crossed to Tsushima, and then moved by sea along the Inland Sea to Osaka before proceeding overland through Kyoto and along the Tōkaidō to Edo. At each stage, domains were required to provide ships, lodging, food, and security, an enormous cost for the hosts, and Tsushima officials escorted the embassy throughout.

The embassies were more than ceremonial exchanges. Japanese scholars, poets, physicians, and artists sought out Korean literati for conversations and brush-written exchanges. Crowds lined the roads and ports to watch the processions, and the embassies appear in paintings, scrolls, woodblock prints, and local festivals, some of which preserved the memory of the visits for generations. For Japanese Confucian scholars, the missions were a rare chance to engage with a culture they regarded as a major center of learning. For the shogunate, they were opportunities to display its authority before domestic audiences, and the bakufu presented the missions as demonstrations of the shogun's stature. Korean participants, for their part, saw the visits as the fulfillment of a policy of neighborly relations, as well as a chance to observe Japanese society firsthand. Their written accounts are now major historical sources.

The meaning of ceremony was contested. Joseon and Tokugawa officials used different titles and protocols, and subtle changes could signal claims about rank. In 1711 Arai Hakuseki, as the shogun's adviser, simplified costs and revised ceremonial practices, and proposed that Korean documents address the shogun as "King of Japan" (Nihon kokuō) rather than by the earlier title taikun, asserting a status equivalent to that of the Korean king. The change provoked Korean objections and was reversed by Yoshimune's government in 1719. Another conflict occurred in 1764, when a Korean interpreter was killed in Osaka by a member of the Tsushima escort, an incident that tested the diplomatic system at a human level. Finally, the cost of hosting the missions strained the shogunate. The last embassy, in 1811, was received at Tsushima rather than Edo, a compromise to reduce expense that signaled the system's changing priorities.

Behind the processions lay an elaborate machinery of mediation. After the Yanagawa affair, from the mid-1630s, diplomatic correspondence was drafted under Tsushima's supervision by Zen monks of the Kyoto Five Mountains (Gozan) temples, who served in rotation at the Iteian (以酊庵), a temple in the domain's castle town of Fuchū, and Tsushima scholars such as Amenomori Hōshū, who knew Korean well and escorted the 1711 and 1719 missions, acted as interpreters of both language and protocol. The embassies of 1636, 1643, and 1655 also went to Nikkō, but the visits differed. In 1636 the trip was primarily an excursion arranged at the shogunate's invitation; in 1643 the envoys took part in ceremonies at the Tōshōgū, the shrine of the dynasty's founder; and in 1655 ceremonies were held at both the Tōshōgū and the Taiyūin mausoleum. The visits thus tied the embassies to the cult of Ieyasu and his successors, though not identically each time. Costs fell on the bakufu, on the domains along the route, and ultimately on the villages that supplied labor, horses, and provisions, which is why reforms in 1711 and the 1811 compromise were driven as much by finance as by ceremony. Taken together, the embassies show how a single event could serve several audiences at once: Korean officials seeking stable relations, a shogunate seeking prestige, a domain whose livelihood depended on the connection, and townspeople and scholars along the road for whom the procession was a spectacle and an opportunity.

Historical Vocabulary
Tongsinsa (通信使; Japanese: Tsūshinshi)

Tongsinsa, usually translated "envoys of communication" or "communication embassies," were the Korean missions sent to Japan from 1607 to 1811 (the same characters are read tsūshinshi in Japanese). The name reflects the idea of "communication" (tsūshin) as a relationship between states that exchanged envoys, as opposed to simple "trade" (tsūshō).

The term matters because it highlights that Korea was an active diplomatic participant, not a visitor bringing tribute. Joseon regarded the missions as an expression of neighborly relations between sovereign states, whereas some Japanese commentators portrayed them as homage to the shogun. Both readings coexisted, and scholars examine the evidence for each.

Section 48.10

The Ryukyu Kingdom, Satsuma, and Dual Diplomatic Relationships

Chapters 32 and 33 described the rise of the Ryukyu Kingdom as a maritime trading state tied to Ming China, with deep connections to Japan, Korea, and Southeast Asia. In 1609, a Satsuma force of roughly three thousand men, with the approval of the Tokugawa shogunate, invaded the Ryukyu Islands. The motives included Satsuma's desire to profit from Ryukyu's trade and its financial needs after the Sekigahara settlement, as well as a dispute over Ryukyu's reluctance to acknowledge Tokugawa authority. The Ryukyuan defenses failed quickly. King Shō Nei was taken to Kagoshima and then to the shogun's court, and he was returned in 1611 after pledging to the Shimazu. Satsuma surveyed the kingdom, assessed its productivity for tax purposes, imposed regulations restricting Ryukyu's relations with outsiders, and annexed the Amami Islands to its own domain. A Satsuma magistrate was stationed in Naha to supervise.

Satsuma did not abolish the kingdom. The Shimazu and the bakufu needed Ryukyu to continue its relationship with the Chinese court. As a tributary state, Ryukyu sent regular missions to Beijing and Fuzhou and received Chinese investiture envoys who confirmed each new king, and the trade conducted under this framework supplied Chinese goods that Satsuma could obtain in no other way. The relationship required concealment: when Chinese envoys visited, Japanese officials withdrew from Naha, and Japanese-style practices were hidden. Ryukyu thus maintained a double identity, often described as "dual subordination": a tributary of China and a vassal state under Satsuma, with an internal government at Shuri that worked to balance both. Scholars debate how best to characterize this arrangement, since neither Chinese nor Japanese authorities understood it quite as the other did.

The shogunate also used Ryukyu for display. From 1634 to 1850, Ryukyuan missions traveled to Edo eighteen times, either to congratulate a new shogun or to thank the shogun for the investiture of a king. Their appearance was staged to emphasize foreignness, including Chinese-influenced dress and music. For Japanese audiences, the processions signaled that the shogun received envoys from distant lands. For the Ryukyuan court, the missions were obligations to be managed carefully, and they also provided opportunities to gather information and to represent the kingdom's distinct identity.

Ryukyuan society bore heavy costs. The kingdom paid a substantial tax burden to Satsuma and financed missions to both China and Japan. On the southern islands of Miyako and Yaeyama, a head tax introduced in the seventeenth century weighed heavily on villagers and remained in place until 1903. At the same time, Ryukyu's rulers and scholars used the pressures to build a distinctive culture and administrative tradition. Reform-minded leaders, including the regent Haneji Chōshū in the seventeenth century and the statesman Sai On in the eighteenth, promoted agricultural development and political stability. Ryukyu was neither a passive victim nor an autonomous independent state; it was a small kingdom exercising limited agency within a constrained position, a status that would be dramatically altered when Japan annexed it in the nineteenth century (see later chapters).

Section 48.11

Ezo, Matsumae, and the Northern Commercial Frontier

The northern frontier differed from the other three contact zones in an essential way: the people with whom Matsumae traded, the Ainu, lived on land that the Tokugawa did not administer as ordinary domain territory. The Matsumae family, descended from the Kakizaki who had held a foothold on the southern tip of Hokkaido since the fifteenth century, received recognition of its position from Hideyoshi in 1593. In a black-seal document dated January 27, 1604, which survives in the Hokkaido Museum, Tokugawa Ieyasu recognized Matsumae Yoshihiro's exclusive intermediary rights in trade with the Ainu, barring other Japanese from trading with them independently, while explicitly acknowledging Ainu freedom of movement and prohibiting certain abuses. The document is valuable because it separates a trade monopoly from jurisdiction over Ainu people. Historians nonetheless distinguish such legal provisions from their later enforcement, which, as Section 48.12 shows, often fell far short of the protections they stated. Matsumae was assigned a nominal rank of ten thousand koku, but since rice could not be grown in its territory, the domain's wealth rested wholly on commerce.

Geographically, Matsumae governed a small settled area (the Wajinchi) on the southern Oshima Peninsula. Beyond it lay the lands that Japanese called Ezochi, inhabited by Ainu communities whose territories extended across Hokkaido, Sakhalin, and the Kuril Islands. The Tokugawa regarded Ezo as part of Matsumae's field of responsibility but did not claim to govern it directly. In practice, the shogunate's authority over Ezo was indirect and varied across the period. This ambiguity shaped later conflicts, because by the late eighteenth century both Russia and Japan had to decide where Japan's boundaries lay.

Matsumae organized trade through a system of trading rights (akinaiba) assigned to retainers, who obtained a revenue by trading with Ainu communities in designated locations. Over time, as Japanese merchant interests expanded, the system gave way to contracting trading posts (basho) to merchants. Ōmi merchants and others entered the fisheries, producing herring, salmon, kelp, and other goods. Dried herring became a fertilizer for cotton and other crops in western Japan, and shipping routes along the Japan Sea coast (the kitamaebune) carried northern products to Osaka. Ezo thus became integrated into Japan's domestic commercial economy even as it remained politically distinct.

Matsumae's policy also reinforced the distinction between Ainu and Wajin. Wajin settlement in the Ezochi was discouraged, and Ainu movement to Honshu was limited. These rules protected Matsumae's monopoly but also treated Ainu as a separate people with whom relations were managed as external. This status was partly protective, because it limited direct incorporation, but it also left Ainu communities without legal recourse against exploitation by traders and officials, a problem that the next section examines.

Historical Vocabulary
Basho ukeoi (場所請負)

Basho ukeoi, the "trading-post contract system," was the arrangement under which Matsumae granted merchants the right to operate trading and fishing stations (basho) in Ezo, in exchange for fees. It developed from the earlier practice of assigning trading rights to Matsumae retainers and expanded in the eighteenth century.

The system matters because it shifted control of Ainu trade and labor toward merchant capital. Contractors sought to maximize output of fish and other products, and Ainu were increasingly drawn into wage-like or coerced labor under conditions that historians have documented as exploitative, with disruption of Ainu subsistence and communities. The system did not originate the tensions behind Shakushain's War, but it was central to the grievances behind the uprising of 1789.

Section 48.12

Ainu Communities, Trade, Resistance, and Changing Power

Ainu societies in this period were not unified under a single ruler. They were organized in local communities (kotan) and river-based groups, with leaders whose influence depended on kinship, reputation, and control of resources. Ainu economic life combined hunting, fishing, gathering, and, in some regions, small-scale cultivation, along with extensive trade. They exchanged salmon, kelp, furs, hawk and eagle feathers, and other products for rice, sake, iron tools, cloth, and lacquerware from Japan, and some Ainu groups maintained a separate trade on Sakhalin and the lower Amur. In this northern exchange, often called the Santan trade, Sakhalin Ainu obtained Chinese silks and glass beads from Qing officials and local peoples, and some of these goods reached Japan through Matsumae. Ainu communities were thus participants in an international trading world, not merely recipients of Japanese goods.

Relations with Matsumae involved ritualized exchange, including audiences at the Matsumae castle (uimam) where Ainu leaders presented gifts and received return gifts. These rituals carried different meanings for each side: Matsumae saw homage, while Ainu leaders understood relationships of reciprocity. Over the seventeenth century the terms of trade shifted. The rates at which Ainu goods were exchanged for rice and other items worsened, and competition among Ainu groups over resources intensified.

Shakushain's War (1669–1672) grew from these pressures. Shakushain was a leader of the Shibuchari community in the Hidaka region, whose conflict with a neighboring group, the Hae, over hunting and fishing grounds escalated in 1669. When the Hae leader was killed, Matsumae became involved, and Shakushain's forces attacked Japanese traders, gold miners, and ships along the coast, reportedly killing several hundred Wajin according to Japanese sources. Matsumae, supported by neighboring domains such as Tsugaru, mobilized forces, and Shakushain was killed at a peace banquet in late 1669, after which the fighting ended by 1672. The war reveals both the unity Ainu communities were capable of in a crisis and the divisions that Matsumae exploited. Afterward, Ainu were required to affirm their submission, and the terms of trade moved further in Matsumae's favor.

The eighteenth century intensified Ainu dependence. The contract-fishery system drew Ainu men into labor at the fisheries, often far from their families, and sources record abuse, coercion, sexual exploitation of Ainu women, and the spread of epidemic disease, which reduced Ainu populations in some regions. These conditions provoked the Menashi–Kunashir uprising of 1789 in eastern Ezo, in which Ainu killed about seventy Wajin traders and workers. Matsumae suppressed the rising with assistance from some Ainu leaders, notably Ikotoi and Tsukinoe, and thirty-seven Ainu were executed. The uprising was a response to exploitation under the contract system, and it also coincided with Russian activity in the Kurils, which raised new concerns in Edo.

Ainu historical agency must be emphasized. Ainu leaders negotiated, resisted, allied with Matsumae when it served them, maintained their own trade routes, and preserved their culture in the face of growing pressure. Their history is recorded in Wajin documents, Russian and Chinese sources, archaeological finds, and the oral epic tradition (yukar), which are not always consistent. Because the written record largely reflects the perspective of those who exploited Ainu labor, historians now read these sources critically and draw on Ainu oral history and recent Ainu scholarship. This chapter ends before the nineteenth-century colonization of Hokkaido, which later chapters examine as a transformation of an Ainu homeland.

Section 48.13

Russia, Sakhalin, the Kurils, and the Expansion of Northern Contact

In the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries Russian traders and officials pushed across Siberia to the Pacific, motivated by the fur trade, and by the early eighteenth century they had reached Kamchatka and begun moving south along the Kuril chain. They encountered Ainu communities, collected tribute in furs, and sought supplies and trade. By the late eighteenth century, Ainu leaders in the Kurils, including Tsukinoe, had contact with Russian traders, and reports reached Matsumae and Edo that foreigners were operating in lands Japan regarded as within its sphere. At the same time, the Qing state had extended its influence over the Amur region and incorporated some Sakhalin Ainu into a fur-tribute relationship, so Sakhalin lay at the intersection of three expanding powers.

The bakufu's response developed gradually. In the 1780s the Tanuma government sent survey expeditions to Ezo and the Kurils; among the explorers was Mogami Tokunai, who traveled the islands in 1785–1786. A 1783 treatise by Kudō Heisuke argued that Ezo should be developed and that trade with Russia might be advantageous, ideas that were debated though not adopted. After the Menashi–Kunashir uprising and the arrival of a Russian mission under Adam Laxman at Nemuro in 1792, which sought trade and returned shipwrecked Japanese including Daikokuya Kōdayū, the bakufu became more concerned with defense. Officials gave Laxman a permit to enter Nagasaki but refused to open trade elsewhere. In the following years Kondō Jūzō erected a marker at Etorofu in 1798, and Mamiya Rinzō explored Sakhalin in 1808–1809, determining that it was an island.

The bakufu took direct control of eastern Ezo in 1799 and extended its administration over all of Ezo by 1807, transferring Matsumae to a smaller territory on Honshu. Russian actions contributed to this shift: in 1804–1805 Nikolai Rezanov waited at Nagasaki for a response to his request for trade and was rejected, and in 1806–1807 Russian naval officers raided Japanese settlements on Sakhalin and the southern Kurils. In 1811 the Russian captain Vasily Golovnin was captured on Kunashir and held for two years, until negotiations in 1813 resolved the dispute. As tensions eased, the bakufu restored Matsumae to its domain in 1821. The episode revealed that the northern frontier was now an international boundary region, with implications for both Ainu communities and Japanese security. Its further development, including the border negotiations of the 1850s, belongs to Chapters 51 and 52.

Two further dimensions deserve emphasis. First, Ainu communities were not bystanders to Russian expansion. Kuril Ainu had been drawn into the Russian fur-tribute system from the early eighteenth century, and some, like Tsukinoe, traded with Russians while also dealing with Matsumae, using competing powers to improve their position. In 1778–1779 Russian agents, commonly identified as Ivan Antipin and Dmitry Shabalin and sent by the Siberian merchant Pavel Lebedev-Lastochkin, came to eastern Hokkaido seeking trade. This was a commercial venture, although Russian officials in Siberia supported aspects of such early ventures, and it was distinct from the official government mission later led by Adam Laxman in 1792. Matsumae put the visitors off and, according to the usual account, referred them to Nagasaki, an early example of the bakufu's practice of directing foreign requests into established channels. Second, direct administration changed Ainu life. Bakufu officials introduced policies aimed at making Ainu more like Wajin in language, dress, and custom (dōka), a first step toward the assimilation policies that later chapters discuss, while the new officials sometimes restrained the worst abuses of the contract fisheries and sometimes did not. Mamiya Rinzō's 1809 journey up the lower Amur to Qing frontier posts shows how the northern zone connected the Tokugawa world to the Qing frontier system as well as to Russia.

Section 48.14

Information, Interpreters, and Knowledge Across Borders

Information moved through the Tokugawa system in organized channels. The Dutch Reports (Oranda fūsetsugaki), submitted annually from 1641 to the mid-nineteenth century, summarized events in Europe and Asia as understood by the Dutch factory. Chinese ship captains were required to supply similar accounts, and the shogunate's scholars, notably the Hayashi family, compiled these reports, including the Ka'i hentai record of the Ming–Qing transition. Korean embassies, Ryukyuan missions, and Matsumae and Satsuma officials added further streams. The bakufu thus obtained a substantial stock of knowledge about the wider world, even though that knowledge was limited, selective, and controlled by officials.

Interpreters were the human infrastructure. The Dutch and Chinese interpreter families of Nagasaki translated documents, interrogated captains, and, in many cases, studied foreign languages and subjects beyond what their official duties required. Tsushima's officials mastered Korean, and Ryukyuan scholars were proficient in Chinese. In the north, Wajin and Ainu intermediaries negotiated trade across language barriers. These individuals were often socially modest, but they held positions of great practical influence because their knowledge could not easily be replaced.

Books and maps were also vehicles. Imported Chinese books, including works on medicine, history, and natural science, circulated widely. Christian-related books were banned from 1630, but in 1720 Tokugawa Yoshimune relaxed restrictions on Western books that did not concern Christianity, opening a path to the study of Dutch texts. Maps and geographic accounts improved with the Ezo expeditions, which produced more accurate charts of Hokkaido, Sakhalin, and the Kurils. Castaways who returned from abroad, such as Daikokuya Kōdayū, who spent years in Russia, provided firsthand testimony.

These channels helped create a body of foreign knowledge that Chapter 50 will examine as an intellectual movement, including rangaku. At this point the key observation is institutional: the shogunate treated knowledge about foreign countries as a matter of state, to be gathered, filtered, and, when necessary, withheld. Foreign information was neither suppressed entirely nor freely available. It flowed through approved intermediaries, and the officials who controlled it could shape what Japanese elites understood about the world.

The mechanics of this control are worth examining. The Dutch Reports were translated by the Nagasaki interpreters and forwarded to Edo, where they were read by a small circle of senior officials rather than published, and Chinese captains' reports (tōsen fūsetsugaki) were handled similarly. The annual journey to Edo supplemented these documents, since officials and physicians questioned the Dutch directly about medicine, astronomy, and geography. When events demanded, supplementary reports could be produced: after the Opium War began, special additional reports on the conflict reached the bakufu from 1840, and these helped shape the debates of the early 1840s. The limits were equally clear. The interpreters' linguistic skill was uneven, the Dutch reported what served the company's interests, and the shogunate decided who could see what. Information about Ryukyu and the north reached officials through Satsuma and Matsumae, whose own interests colored what they reported. Knowledge of the wider world was therefore plentiful but filtered, a condition that explains both the shogunate's relatively informed responses in the early nineteenth century and the gaps in its understanding.

Section 48.15

Stability and Strain in the International System

For more than a century after 1641 the system functioned with remarkable regularity. But it was not static. The bakufu adjusted trade ceilings in 1685, 1715, and later; it replaced the Korean embassy ceremonies under Arai Hakuseki and then partly reversed them; it altered Nagasaki administration; and in the eighteenth century it shifted between restriction and encouragement of exports. In the 1790s, as part of a broader program of retrenchment, the chief councillor Matsudaira Sadanobu reduced the frequency of Dutch missions to Edo and sought to lower the cost of Korean embassies, leading to the 1811 compromise at Tsushima. These changes show an administration continually adapting to fiscal pressure, to the depletion of copper supplies, and to shifting commercial conditions.

Strain also arose from maritime security. In the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries, Russian, British, and American ships increasingly appeared in Japanese waters, driven by whaling, the China trade, and exploration. In 1808 the British frigate Phaeton, hunting Dutch ships, entered Nagasaki harbor under a false flag, took hostages, and left after demanding supplies. The Nagasaki magistrate, Matsudaira Yasuhide, took his own life, and officials of the Saga domain, which had been responsible for the harbor guard, were disciplined. The episode exposed weaknesses in the coastal defenses on which the system depended.

The bakufu's response in the following decades was to harden its stance toward uninvited ships. In 1825 it issued an order to repel foreign vessels (ikokusen uchiharai-rei), instructing coastal authorities to fire on approaching ships. In 1837 an American merchant ship, the Morrison, which was attempting to return Japanese castaways and open contact, was fired on at Uraga and Kagoshima. But news of China's defeat in the Opium War (1839–1842), conveyed through Dutch and Chinese reports, prompted officials to reconsider. In 1842 the bakufu modified the policy, allowing foreign ships to be provided with firewood and water, a more cautious approach. The same news encouraged debate among domains and scholars about the need for coastal defense and reform.

Smuggling and informal exchange also complicated the picture. Some domains, notably Satsuma, were suspected of or engaged in trade beyond official limits, and sources mention illicit exchange (nukeni) at some ports. Fishermen and castaways encountered foreign ships, and the question of how to deal with shipwrecked foreigners raised recurring legal and diplomatic problems. These developments suggest that the system's edges were porous, and that officials were constantly negotiating with actors who sought to use the system for their own purposes. By the 1840s, however, the central problem was not leakage but the growing power and persistence of maritime states outside East Asia, a challenge that the Tokugawa structure was not designed to meet and that Chapters 51 and 52 will examine.

Cause and Effect

The Making of the Regulated International System

Long-term causes

Reunification under strong central authority; the legacy of Hideyoshi's war in Korea; the history of Japanese maritime commerce and of coastal domains' overseas ties; regional traditions of state control over sea trade in Ming China, Qing China, and Joseon Korea.

Short-term causes

Fear of Christianity and foreign networks (Chapter 47); competition among European traders; the Shimabara Rebellion; the desire to concentrate profit and authority in the shogunate; domain ambitions in Kyushu and the north.

Transformation

Between about 1609 and 1641 the shogunate created the channels described in this chapter: Nagasaki trade, the Tsushima–Korea and Satsuma–Ryukyu relationships, and the Matsumae–Ainu trade monopoly, while ending Japanese overseas travel and European missionary access.

Immediate effects

Concentration of foreign trade in Nagasaki; the end of the red-seal system; formal diplomatic exchanges with Korea and Ryukyu; confinement of Dutch and later Chinese merchants.

Long-term consequences

A durable, adaptable system that supplied goods and information, advantaged particular domains and merchants, burdened Ryukyuan and Ainu populations, and ran into limits when whalers, Russian expansion, and Western naval power altered maritime conditions.

Historical complexity: The system cannot be attributed to a single cause, such as anti-Christian policy, to a single decision, or to a shogunal blueprint carried out as planned. It emerged through responses to events, bargaining with domains and merchants, and gradual adaptation.

Section 48.16

Was Tokugawa Japan Isolated? A Historical Reassessment

The evidence assembled in this chapter allows a careful answer. Tokugawa Japan was not isolated in the sense of lacking trade, diplomacy, or information. It did restrict contact in significant ways: Japanese could not travel abroad, the Portuguese and Spanish were excluded, Christianity was banned, the Dutch and Chinese were confined to enclaves, and Western ships were limited to Nagasaki. It was also neither "open" in the modern sense of unrestricted flows of people, goods, and ideas. The most accurate description is that Japan maintained a regulated international order in which the shogunate selected partners, defined the terms of each relationship, and used those relationships to support its own legitimacy.

Placing Japan in its East Asian setting adds further perspective. Seen from this vantage, the Tokugawa system was one variant of a broader pattern of state-managed maritime commerce. The evidence also shows why neither extreme label survives scrutiny. Against "isolation" stand the records of Nagasaki trade, the Korean and Ryukyuan embassies, the Dutch and Chinese reports, the Santan and Russian contacts in the north, and the flow of goods such as silk, ginseng, sugar, and copper. Against "openness" stand the ban on Japanese travel, the confinement of the Dutch and Chinese, the exclusion of Catholic Europe, the limits on trade values and ship numbers, and the controls on information. The later nineteenth-century use of "sakoku" also served purposes of its own: for Meiji leaders it dramatized the old regime's backwardness, and for later writers it supplied a simple contrast between a sealed past and an open present. Reading the Tokugawa system on its own terms, as selective, unequal, hierarchical, and changing, lets students see both what the regime achieved and what it cost those at its margins, and it prepares them to read the crises of Chapters 51 and 52 as a test of an existing system, not as the first encounter of a hermit country with the world.

Historical Debate

Was Tokugawa Japan "Closed"? The Uses and Limits of Sakoku

The question: Does "sakoku" remain a useful concept for describing Tokugawa foreign relations, or does it obscure more than it reveals?

Interpretation A — Sakoku as a meaningful, if imperfect, description: Some scholars argue that the term captures real and consequential features: the near-total ban on Japanese overseas travel, the expulsion of the Portuguese and Spanish, the prohibition of Christianity, the strict confinement of the Dutch and Chinese, and the severe limits on foreign access to Japanese territory. From this perspective, to call these policies merely "regulation" understates how drastic they were relative to Japan's earlier maritime openness and to what neighboring states permitted, and it underestimates the real effects on Japanese contact with Europe.
Interpretation B — A regulated multi-channel system: Others, building on the work of Ronald Toby, Arano Yasunori, and later historians such as Gregory Smits and Robert Hellyer, emphasize that Japan maintained diplomatic, commercial, and informational channels with Korea, Ryukyu, China, the Dutch, and the Ainu, and that contemporary officials did not conceive their policy as closure. They argue that "sakoku" is an anachronistic, nineteenth-century label that misleads students, and that terms like "maritime prohibition" (kaikin) or "the Tokugawa foreign-relations system" better describe the evidence.

Evidence: Both positions use the same facts: the edicts of the 1630s, the Nagasaki trade records, the Korean and Ryukyuan embassies, Matsumae's trade with Ainu communities, and the Dutch Reports. They differ in what they treat as the defining feature, restriction or regulated connection, and in how they weigh Japan's relationship with the Western world against its relationships within East Asia. Most current scholarship combines the two views, accepting that restrictions were severe while rejecting the picture of isolation.

Why it matters: The label shapes broader narratives. If Japan is portrayed as closed until Perry, its modern transformation appears as a sudden opening imposed from outside. If the Tokugawa system is understood as a regulated international order with established channels of information and trade, the Meiji transformation appears more as a change within a long-standing engagement with the world than as an arrival from nowhere.

Multiple Perspectives

One System, Many Experiences

Note: these perspectives describe categories of experience reconstructed from historical evidence. The groups held very different degrees of power, and they are not equivalent.

Bakufu officials and Nagasaki magistrates: For senior officials, the system offered security, control of profit, and prestige, and it allowed the shogunate to display authority to domestic audiences while managing information about the outside world. Their concern was to keep the arrangement under control, especially as bullion outflow and coastal security became problems.
Dutch factory personnel at Dejima: The Dutch accepted confinement, ceremonial obligations, and limits on their activities as the price of access to Japanese silver, gold, and copper. For the VOC, Japan was one profitable post among many, and Dejima residents often experienced isolation and dependence on interpreters and on the goodwill of Japanese officials.
Chinese merchants at Nagasaki: Chinese traders, who had no state-to-state agreement protecting them, dealt with licensing, ceilings, and confinement after 1689, yet they were the largest foreign trading community. They built temples and community institutions, and their ships carried the goods and cultural influences that Japan most valued.
Korean envoys: Korean ambassadors and scholars regarded the missions as neighborly diplomacy and an opportunity to observe Japan, but often judged Japanese culture with assumptions of Confucian superiority. They resisted attempts to alter the protocol in ways that might diminish Joseon's standing.
Ryukyuan authorities and villagers: The Shuri court tried to preserve the kingdom's identity and autonomy by balancing demands from Satsuma and China, while peasants, especially on the southern islands, bore the burden of taxation and of missions they had no voice in.
Ainu communities: Ainu leaders sought favorable exchange, status, and security, and they resisted when exploitation became intolerable. As the contract-fishery system expanded, many Ainu experienced loss of autonomy, forced labor, disease, and violence, even as they remained actors with their own networks and strategies.
Continuity and Change Over Time

The International System, 1630s–1840s

What Changed

  • The commodity mix shifted from silver to gold and copper, then to marine products, as bullion concerns and domestic production reshaped trade
  • Chinese trade grew sharply after 1684, then was capped and confined
  • Korean embassy ceremonies were revised, reversed, and eventually relocated to Tsushima
  • The Ezo trading system moved from retainers' trading rights to merchant contracts, and then to direct bakufu administration during the Russian crisis
  • Foreign ships increasingly appeared in Japanese waters, and the bakufu's policy toward them shifted from repulsion to limited accommodation

What Remained Continuous

  • The principle that the shogunate defined Japan's foreign relations and delegated specific tasks to approved houses
  • Nagasaki as the sole site of European contact
  • The prohibition on Japanese overseas travel and the ban on Christianity
  • The dependence of Tsushima, Satsuma, and Matsumae on their contact-zone responsibilities

Who experienced change most directly: Ainu communities and Ryukyuan villagers faced the sharpest changes, as commercial and political pressure intensified. Nagasaki interpreters, merchants, and officials experienced continuity in routine but adaptation in detail. Why the pattern matters: It shows a system that was stable in structure but flexible in practice, which helps explain both its longevity and its vulnerability when external pressures outran its capacity to adapt.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that Tokugawa Japan did not withdraw from the world but reorganized its external relationships into a regulated, hierarchical, and changing system. The common label for that system, sakoku, was coined in 1801 and popularized only later. It identifies a real feature of the period, the severe restriction of Japanese travel and of European access, but it conceals the diplomatic, commercial, informational, and frontier relationships that continued throughout.

The system took shape between about 1609 and 1641 out of the overseas commerce of the red-seal era. Concern about Christianity, competition among European traders, the ambitions of coastal domains, and the shogunate's desire to monopolize foreign relations all contributed; the Ming–Qing transition of the 1640s then posed new problems for the system once it was largely in place. The edicts of the 1630s ended Japanese overseas voyaging and expelled the Portuguese, but they reorganized trade and diplomacy more than they ended them.

Authority over foreign relations was divided between the bakufu and specially placed houses. The shogunate administered Nagasaki directly, while the Sō of Tsushima, the Shimazu of Satsuma, and the Matsumae managed relations with Korea, Ryukyu, and the Ainu. Relationships fell into distinct categories: diplomacy with Korea and Ryukyu, commerce without diplomacy with the Dutch and Chinese, and frontier exchange with the Ainu. These were not four exceptions to isolation but components of a single order.

At Nagasaki the Dutch at Dejima and the Chinese in the Tōjin yashiki traded under the supervision of magistrates and hereditary interpreters. The Dutch provided annual world reports, Chinese merchants carried the largest volume of trade and significant cultural influence, and the commodities exchanged, from silver, gold, and copper to silk, sugar, medicines, and marine products, shaped both Japanese consumption and the bakufu's economic anxieties. The trade was small compared with the domestic economy but crucial for particular goods and places.

Korea was an active diplomatic participant. After the devastation of Hideyoshi's invasions, the two sides restored relations through the mediation of the Sō family, and twelve Joseon embassies traveled to Japan between 1607 and 1811. The embassies served the shogunate's claims to prestige, offered Korean elites an opportunity to observe Japan, and became arenas of disputes over titles and ceremony, in 1711, 1719, and 1764. The relocation of the last embassy to Tsushima in 1811 marked the strain of cost and changing priorities.

Ryukyu occupied a position of dual subordination. After Satsuma's 1609 invasion, the kingdom retained its monarchy and its tribute relationship with China, which Satsuma and the bakufu found useful, while paying heavily to Satsuma and sending missions to Edo. The arrangement preserved a distinct Ryukyuan polity but imposed burdens on its people, especially in the southern islands, and it laid the groundwork for the contested incorporation of Ryukyu in the nineteenth century.

On the northern frontier, Matsumae held a monopoly on trade with Ainu communities in lands the Tokugawa did not directly administer. Ainu people were integrated into wide trade networks, including the Santan trade, and had their own political structures. Over time the terms of exchange turned against them, as the shift to the contract-fishery system introduced coerced labor, abuse, and disease. Shakushain's War (1669–1672) and the Menashi–Kunashir uprising of 1789 were significant acts of Ainu resistance, and Ainu leaders remained agents who negotiated, allied, and resisted.

Russian expansion in the North Pacific transformed the northern frontier into an international boundary region. Surveys, Laxman's mission of 1792, Rezanov's rebuff in 1804–1805, the raids of 1806–1807, and the Golovnin affair led the bakufu to take direct control of Ezo from 1799 to 1821, and set the stage for the border questions that Chapters 51 and 52 will address.

Information moved through approved channels: Dutch Reports, Chinese merchants' accounts, embassy exchanges, interpreters, imported books, and expedition reports. The bakufu treated knowledge of the outside world as a matter of state, and the interpreter families of Nagasaki became essential brokers. After 1720 the relaxation of restrictions on non-Christian Western books opened paths for intellectual developments examined in Chapter 50.

The system proved stable but not static. Trade ceilings, ceremonial revisions, fiscal retrenchment, copper shortages, smuggling, the Phaeton incident of 1808, and the orders of 1825 and 1842 show continual adaptation. By the 1840s, news of the Opium War and the increasing presence of Western ships revealed that the system was not designed for an era of aggressive maritime powers. Neither absolute isolation nor open exchange describes the period. The Tokugawa international order was selective, unequal, and adaptable, and it distributed its benefits and burdens unevenly among officials, merchants, foreign partners, Ryukyuans, and Ainu.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has shown that one of the most familiar claims about Japanese history, that the country was sealed off for over two centuries, hides a more complex and more instructive reality. The Tokugawa shogunate did not end Japan's relationship with the world. It organized that relationship through channels it could supervise, and it used them to secure legitimacy, revenue, and information. Understanding those channels changes how students should read the Perry expedition and the Meiji transformation: as confrontations with a state that already had institutions, habits, and knowledge for dealing with foreign powers, even if those institutions proved inadequate to the new circumstances.

The chapter also connects regions of the archipelago that are often treated separately. Nagasaki, Tsushima, Satsuma, Ryukyu, Matsumae, and Ezo were not peripheries of a self-contained center but zones in which Japanese, Korean, Chinese, Dutch, Ryukyuan, Ainu, and Russian histories intersected. Ryukyuan and Ainu histories in particular belong within this chapter on their own terms. Their experiences of tribute, taxation, trade, labor, and resistance preceded their later incorporation into the modern Japanese state, and without them the history of the archipelago cannot be accurately told.

Continuities from this period reach well beyond it. Nagasaki's interpreter families and the information networks they sustained fed the intellectual currents of Chapter 50. The unequal treatment of the Ryukyu Kingdom and the Ainu prefigured the contested incorporation of Okinawa and Hokkaido discussed in later chapters, while the Korean embassies remain a point of reference in modern debates about Japan–Korea relations and historical memory. The belief that Japan was closed has itself shaped modern narratives, which is why a careful reading of the evidence matters.

Finally, the chapter demonstrates a method. A single label, even a useful one, can narrow the questions historians ask. By asking who regulated contact, who mediated it, who benefited, who paid, and how the system changed, students gain a fuller and more evidence-based picture of Tokugawa Japan, one that prepares them for the crises of Chapters 51 and 52 and for the many later chapters in which Japan's place in the wider world is again at issue.
