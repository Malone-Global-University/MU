Part VI — Sengoku Japan and Reunification
39

Oda Nobunaga and the Destruction of the Old Order

Japanese History: From Jōmon Japan to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

In the summer of 1582, a lord who had spent two decades breaking rival armies, shogunal authority, and fortified religious leagues died in a burning Kyoto temple, and his political order began to come apart within days.

Oda Nobunaga (1534–1582) is among the most familiar names in Japanese history, and the familiarity is a hazard. Popular tradition makes him the visionary who swept away a medieval world with guns and ruthlessness. This chapter treats him instead as a product of the Sengoku political world described in Chapters 35 through 38: regional territorial states, castle-centered administration, transformed warfare, maritime exchange, commercial networks, and elite cultural authority.

His distinctiveness lay in scale and combination. He used armies, castles, markets, alliances, and patronage more aggressively and over a larger geography than most rivals. In the process he destroyed or crippled institutions capable of acting independently of him: rival daimyō houses, the effective political role of the Ashikaga shogunate, and major fortified religious communities. The central concept of the chapter is the destruction of autonomous centers of power.

Those centers were not irrational "feudal obstacles." They were functioning institutions with constituencies, legitimacy, and histories. Nobunaga replaced one distribution of power with another, and the replacement was unfinished when he died in 1582. This chapter ends there; the completed settlement belongs to Chapter 40.

The evidence is uneven. Surviving orders and letters in Nobunaga's name are valuable, but the best-known narratives, such as Ōta Gyūichi's Shinchō kōki, were written by retainers, while Jesuit letters, court diaries, and temple records offer other viewpoints. The chapter asks throughout whose account is being read.

The geography matters. Nobunaga's career moved along a corridor of central Japan: Owari on Ise Bay, Mino and the mountain passes, the Ōmi shore of Lake Biwa, Kyoto and its court, and the Ōsaka Bay ports. Control of that corridor gave access to grain, markets, shipping, and the sources of legitimacy that Kyoto offered. Opponents lay on every side of it, and Nobunaga's changing bases mark his widening horizon.

The interpretive problem is whether to call him an innovator, an accelerator, or a destroyer of rival institutions. This chapter argues that he did the last most clearly: he removed centers able to act independently of him, but he had not yet replaced them with a settled order when he died. The chapter's central question, therefore, is how he transformed the possibilities of Sengoku politics and why that differs from completed reunification.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how Nobunaga rose from a fragmented Oda house in Owari.
  2. Assess the political significance of Okehazama without relying on legend.
  3. Trace Nobunaga's relationship with Ashikaga Yoshiaki and explain the meaning of 1573.
  4. Analyze why conquest was non-linear and met shifting coalitions.
  5. Distinguish Ikkō-ikki from Hongan-ji and explain why religious institutions held military-political power.
  6. Evaluate technological explanations for Nobunaga's success.
  7. Interpret Azuchi as a political, commercial, and symbolic center.
  8. Evaluate Nobunaga's market policies as selective and locally varied.
  9. Compare the Nobunaga regime with the Sengoku daimyō state.
  10. Distinguish Nobunaga's destruction of old centers of power from the completed reunification after 1582.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How did Nobunaga transform the political possibilities of Sengoku Japan?
  2. Why should his destruction of older power centers be distinguished from completed reunification?
  3. Why was his conquest not a linear march?
  4. Why could religious institutions function as political and military powers, and why did he attack some but not others?
  5. What does the evidence show about the limits of "guns explain everything"?
  6. How far was Nobunaga innovating, and how far intensifying existing practice?
  7. Whose voices do our sources privilege, and whose are missing?

Historical Context

By the 1550s the Ashikaga shogunate (Chapters 29 and 34) retained prestige but little coercive capacity, and power had passed to territorial lords such as the Imagawa, Takeda, Uesugi, and Hōjō (Chapter 35). Castles anchored regional government, and warfare had grown larger and more logistics-dependent (Chapter 36). Ports and markets tied regions to overseas trade (Chapters 31, 37), and aesthetic culture served as political currency (Chapter 38).

Alongside daimyō stood other autonomous powers: fortified temple complexes such as Enryaku-ji, the Jōdo Shinshū (Ikkō) networks centered on the Hongan-ji, self-governing towns such as Sakai, and village and league organizations. Nobunaga's career unfolded among them. Central Honshu, with Kyoto, the Ōmi corridor, and Ōsaka Bay, mattered because whoever controlled it commanded the court, the shogun, and the richest commercial zone.

Section 39.1

Owari and the Making of Oda Nobunaga

Owari was a prosperous, contested province near the Ise Bay trade zone. The Oda were not a single lordly house but several competing branches; Nobunaga's father Nobuhide headed a cadet Oda line subordinate within the Kiyosu Oda deputy-governor (shugodai) structure. After Nobuhide's death in 1551, Nobunaga had to establish control over his own household, retainers, and relatives, and he did so through a sequence of conflicts, including the death of his brother Nobuyuki, before becoming dominant in much of Owari by the early 1560s.

Owari's wealth lay in the Nōbi plain's rice, in ports and market towns around Ise Bay, and in shrine and temple communities such as Atsuta. The Oda were divided: one line held the provincial deputy governor's seat at Kiyosu, another was based at Iwakura, and Nobuhide's branch served as retainers of the Kiyosu line. In the conventional chronology, Nobunaga took Kiyosu in 1555, had his brother Nobuyuki killed in 1557, and captured Iwakura Castle in 1559; some sources differ by a year on these events. Nobunaga had broken the principal rival Oda branch at Iwakura by 1559 and become dominant in much of Owari, although resistance at Inuyama under Oda Nobukiyo continued until 1565.

Practical power in Owari did not follow formal office alone. It also operated through competing warrior households, retainers, castles, and local alliances. Nobunaga inherited retainers, castles, and income from his father's cadet line, but not undisputed sovereignty over the province: Kiyosu and Iwakura were rival Oda centers, and local warrior families could choose among patrons. Consolidation therefore meant defeating some relatives, absorbing others, and winning over or reassigning local families, a process that taught a pattern repeated later: military pressure combined with defections, redistribution of authority, and incorporation of former rivals.

Owari's resources made this possible. The Nōbi plain's rice and the Ise Bay ports and markets supplied provisions and revenue for repeated campaigning, and the province's position let armies move quickly along the bay and the plain. Geography is therefore best read as a causal explanation: the resources allowed an Oda lord to maintain soldiers and to reach dense commercial networks that could support sustained military mobilization.

Accounts describe unconventional dress and conduct in his youth. These come largely from later or retainer-authored narratives, and they may reflect reputation more than documented behavior. Historians therefore treat these anecdotes primarily as evidence for the construction of Nobunaga's reputation rather than as a sufficient explanation for his political rise.

Section 39.2

Okehazama and the Expansion of Oda Power

In 1560 Imagawa Yoshimoto, ruler of Suruga, Tōtōmi, and Mikawa, advanced toward Owari, and Nobunaga's forces surprised and killed him at Okehazama. The older "tiny army against overwhelming odds" story rests on later narratives; the Imagawa army's size, its dispersion, and the exact nature of the attack are debated. Its political importance is clearer. Yoshimoto's death broke Imagawa dominance and freed Matsudaira Motoyasu, later Tokugawa Ieyasu, to ally with Nobunaga in 1562.

The Imagawa ruled Suruga, Tōtōmi, and much of Mikawa through a developed territorial government, and they were tied by marriage and alliance to the Takeda and Hōjō. Historians debate whether Yoshimoto aimed at Kyoto or at securing the Owari border. The Imagawa lost their commander, and Mikawa vassals, including Matsudaira Motoyasu, gained room to act independently.

Nobunaga then turned to Mino, defeated the Saitō, and in 1567 took their base, renaming the castle town Gifu. He began using a seal bearing the phrase tenka fubu. The phrase's meaning is disputed: "tenka" could refer to the central region around Kyoto rather than the whole country, and it should not be read as a declared national program.

Mino fell through political as well as military pressure. Several Mino lords, including Inaba Ittetsu, Ujiie Bokuzen, and Andō Morinari, shifted to Nobunaga, weakening the Saitō before the 1567 assault on Inabayama. According to later tradition, the name Gifu was selected from names proposed by the Zen monk Takugen Sōon, and it may already have circulated among Zen monks; the renaming signaled a fresh start under a new lord. Around this time Nobunaga also arranged his sister Oichi's marriage to Azai Nagamasa of northern Ōmi, an alliance that secured the route toward Kyoto until Azai turned against him in 1570.

Taken together, these steps changed Nobunaga's strategic position. Okehazama removed immediate pressure, and the Tokugawa alliance made that relief durable. Mino extended his reach northward and westward, Gifu gave him a forward base, and the Azai connection helped secure northern Ōmi. Together these developments converted an Owari lord's regional position into access to the Kyoto political arena, which is why 1567–1568 stands as a period of rapid acceleration.

Section 39.3

Kyoto, Ashikaga Yoshiaki, and the Shogunate

In 1568 Nobunaga entered Kyoto escorting Ashikaga Yoshiaki, whose brother Yoshiteru had been killed in 1565, and Yoshiaki received the shogunal office. The arrangement served both: Yoshiaki gained armed backing, and Nobunaga gained legitimacy and access to the court. Tension grew as Nobunaga restricted Yoshiaki's independent diplomacy, and Yoshiaki corresponded with Nobunaga's enemies.

Yoshiteru's killers in 1565 were Miyoshi and Matsunaga forces, and after Yoshiaki appealed to several lords, only Nobunaga acted. The partnership initially divided labor: Nobunaga's army kept Kyoto and the shogun's office supplied authority. In 1570 Nobunaga presented Yoshiaki with additional articles requiring, among other things, that when the shogun sent written directives to provincial lords, Nobunaga be informed and an accompanying Nobunaga letter be attached. Yoshiaki later sent personal letters to the Takeda, Asakura, Hongan-ji, and other lords, helping to coordinate opposition.

In 1573 Nobunaga defeated and expelled him from Kyoto. This was not a constitutional abolition. Yoshiaki continued to claim shogunal authority from exile, notably under the protection of the Mōri, and no one formally replaced him. What 1573 ended was the shogunate's effective role in Kyoto politics. Nobunaga never took the title of shōgun. Yoshiaki first reached a settlement mediated by the court, then raised the Makishima fortress in the summer of 1573 and was driven out. The era name was changed to Tenshō in that year at Nobunaga's urging, an assertion of influence over court practice. Yoshiaki later lived under Mōri protection in Bingo and formally resigned only in 1588, so historians disagree over whether to date the end of the Muromachi shogunate to 1573, 1588, or to treat it as a gradual loss of function.

After 1573 Nobunaga governed Kyoto through a deputy, commonly identified as Murai Sadakatsu, who handled policing, litigation, and relations with the court on his behalf. This arrangement gave him continuous control of the capital without holding a shogunal office. It also meant that Kyoto residents, temples, and courtiers dealt with an Oda official, a change in daily politics that is easy to miss when the story is told only as the fate of one shogun.

Section 39.4

Enemies on Many Fronts

Nobunaga's rise was marked by reversals. In 1570 he narrowly extricated his army from an attack by the Asakura and Azai, then defeated them at Anegawa. In following years Asakura, Azai, Takeda, the Hongan-ji, and sometimes Yoshiaki acted in overlapping, uncoordinated opposition. Takeda Shingen's advance in 1572 defeated Tokugawa forces at Mikatagahara and threatened Nobunaga's position; Shingen's death in 1573 relieved the pressure. Alliances also shifted: Azai Nagamasa, married to Nobunaga's sister Oichi, turned against him, and Matsunaga Hisahide and others rebelled.

The sequence shows how fluid the opposition was. In spring 1570 Nobunaga attacked the Asakura in Echizen, and Azai Nagamasa's turn against him forced a hurried retreat from the north; his army then defeated the Asakura-Azai forces at Anegawa in the summer. In autumn the Hongan-ji called for resistance, and in winter the Asakura-Azai army took refuge on Mount Hiei until a settlement mediated through the court. Yoshiaki's letters reached Takeda Shingen, who advanced in 1572 and defeated Tokugawa forces at Mikatagahara. Uesugi Kenshin, at times allied with Nobunaga, confronted Oda forces in the Hokuriku in 1577; later accounts describe an Uesugi victory at Tedorigawa, although the battle's scale and course are difficult to reconstruct from the surviving evidence. Matsunaga Hisahide's revolt in 1577 and Araki Murashige's defection in 1578 showed that even allies and subordinates could break away. The Mōri entered the conflict through naval support for the Hongan-ji.

No permanent coalition formed because the opponents had different interests, distances, and timelines. Takeda's death in 1573 and Kenshin's in 1578 removed key leaders, while the Hongan-ji, Mōri, and exiled shogun acted from separate bases. Nobunaga's response was to delegate theater commands rather than fight every front himself.

The Takeda collapse of 1582 shows the same pattern. After Katsuyori lost support from kin and vassals, including Kiso Yoshimasa's defection, Oda forces led by Nobutada, with Tokugawa and Hōjō pressure from other directions, overran Takeda territory in a few weeks, and Katsuyori died in the mountains in March. A rival that had defeated Tokugawa forces in 1572 was ended by internal defections as much as by battlefield defeat.

Nobunaga prevailed less by a single decisive stroke than by outlasting a disorganized opposition, delegating regional commands to generals such as Shibata Katsuie, Hashiba Hideyoshi, and Akechi Mitsuhide, and exploiting rivals' divisions. In 1573 he destroyed the Asakura and Azai.

Section 39.5

Enryaku-ji, Ikkō-ikki, and Religious-Military Power

Enryaku-ji, the Tendai head temple on Mount Hiei, had for centuries combined ritual authority with estates, loan and brewing interests, links to Hiyoshi shrine, and armed followers who pressed court and warrior governments by force. Its location overlooking Kyoto and the Lake Biwa routes made its stance a strategic matter. The Hongan-ji, headquarters of Jōdo Shinshū, drew on a network of temples, lay confraternities, and local leagues. Ikkō-ikki had governed Kaga since the late fifteenth century, and the Hongan-ji's Ishiyama complex near Ōsaka Bay controlled water routes and a fortified town.

Large religious institutions held estates, markets, loan operations, fortified sites, and armed followers, and they sheltered Nobunaga's enemies. Nobunaga was not generally hostile to Buddhism: he patronized temples and received Jesuits favorably. His conflicts with Buddhist institutions cannot be reduced to generalized hostility toward Buddhism. Military strategy, political submission, institutional rivalry, and religious-symbolic authority all mattered, and he simultaneously patronized some temples while violently subordinating others.

In autumn 1571, after the Enryaku-ji complex on Mount Hiei had supported the Asakura and Azai, his forces burned its buildings and killed many inhabitants. Sources agree on the destruction but figures vary, and the numbers in retainer and Jesuit narratives should be treated cautiously. The attack removed a monastic power overlooking Kyoto and the Ōmi routes. Mitsuhide was entrusted with the Shiga/Sakamoto area and ordered to build Sakamoto Castle, which shows the attack served to reassign control of ground as well as to punish.

The Ikkō struggle was longer. The Hongan-ji's Ishiyama complex, a fortified temple-town in the Ōsaka area, resisted from 1570 until a negotiated surrender in 1580, supported at times by Mōri naval supply. In Ise-Nagashima Nobunaga's forces besieged and burned strongholds, with mass deaths in 1574, and in Echizen in 1575 he ordered large-scale killing of Ikkō followers. These campaigns were deliberate, concrete uses of force against communities that had governed themselves. Explanations centered on strategy, institutions, and religious politics are better supported than ones based on personal hatred of monks.

Nagashima in Ise, a Ikkō stronghold among river channels, defeated earlier Oda attacks, and Nobunaga's brother Nobuoki died there in 1570. The 1574 assault succeeded by combining naval blockade, encirclement, and fire. In Echizen, Ikkō forces had overthrown the Asakura-appointed officials in 1574, and Nobunaga's 1575 campaign reasserted Oda control through mass killing. At Ishiyama, Mōri-backed ships supplied the garrison; a Mōri fleet defeated Oda ships in 1576, and a rebuilt Oda fleet reportedly prevailed in 1578, after which the garrison's position weakened. In 1580, with court mediation, Kennyo agreed to leave, though resistance by some followers continued briefly and fire destroyed the complex.

The Hongan-ji abbot Kennyo called on followers to resist in 1570, and the war drew in Ikkō communities across Ōmi, Ise, Settsu, and Echizen, linking local leagues to a central institution without making them identical. Contemporary and later sources give widely varying death totals for Hiei, Nagashima, and Echizen, and many are rhetorical or hostile; this textbook therefore does not cite a single figure. Destruction also carried symbolic force, since burning a major temple publicly demonstrated submission to Nobunaga's authority. Students should note that most surviving narratives were written by his supporters or by outside observers, and Ikkō voices survive mainly through temple records and opponents' accounts.

Historical Vocabulary
Ikkō-ikki

An ikki was a league or compact: a collective political-military association that could be formed by warriors, villagers, or townspeople. Ikkō-ikki refers to leagues in which Jōdo Shinshū (Ikkō) adherents, often organized through Hongan-ji networks, acted collectively. They were not simply peasant rebellions, and not all Ikkō groups were identical: some governed provinces such as Kaga for decades, while others cooperated with or opposed the Hongan-ji leadership. The Hongan-ji gave religious and organizational structure; local leaders, temples, and militants shaped local action.

Section 39.6

War, Firearms, and the Limits of Technological Explanations

Chapter 36 explained Sengoku military practice; this section addresses its political use. At Nagashino in 1575 Oda and Tokugawa forces defeated Takeda Katsuyori's army before a fortified position. The popular image of disciplined volley fire has been questioned, since the main surviving source accounts are limited and later. What the campaign shows more reliably is Nobunaga's capacity to assemble manpower, supplies, firearms, field works, and allies in one place.

Command and delegation mattered. Nobunaga relied on household retainers and on generals given regional commands, who in turn raised troops, managed local relations, and requested supplies. Provisioning depended on castle towns, merchants, and transport routes. This allowed several armies to operate at once, which a single lord's personal following could not do.

The long siege of Ishiyama Hongan-ji (Section 39.5) illustrates how this worked. Rather than assault the complex directly, Oda forces built forts around it to cut access by land, while naval engagements decided whether supplies could arrive by sea. The campaign required commanders to hold positions for years, merchants and ports to provide shipping and materials, and the lord to maintain pressure on other fronts at the same time. Military capacity thus became political leverage over a religious center that could not be overrun quickly.

Technological explanations collapse several distinct things. Firearms were weapons, but effectiveness required organization: guns and powder had to be bought or made, soldiers trained and placed, field defenses built, and troops coordinated with other arms. Nobunaga did not monopolize firearms, so possession of the technology cannot by itself explain why some lords expanded and others did not. Campaign endurance mattered as much as battlefield lethality, since blockades, forts, fleets, and supply lines turned economic and administrative capacity into military power. Delegation was itself an organizing technique: Shibata, Hideyoshi, Mitsuhide, Takigawa, and others let separate campaigns proceed without Nobunaga present at each, a point that connects with the structure of the regime discussed in Section 39.9.

Nobunaga's advantages instead included wealth drawn from central Japan, access to materials through commercial networks, and a command structure of generals and household retainers. The Takeda were not finally destroyed until 1582, after internal defections, which underlines that politics mattered as much as weaponry.

Section 39.7

Castles, Roads, and Centers of Rule

Nobunaga moved his base from Kiyosu to Komaki, Gifu, and finally Azuchi on Lake Biwa, begun in 1576. Azuchi controlled water and land routes between the east, the Hokuriku, and Kyoto. Contemporary accounts, including Jesuit descriptions, describe a multi-storied keep with painted interiors, a castle town with settlers drawn from elsewhere, and an exceptional visual presence. Excavation and reconstruction studies have confirmed major stone-walled structures, though the interior details come mostly from narrative sources.

The sequence of bases shows a widening horizon. Kiyosu and Komaki served Owari, Gifu looked toward Mino and the east, and Azuchi, supervised by Niwa Nagahide, put Nobunaga beside Lake Biwa's shipping and the roads to Kyoto. Excavation has uncovered a broad stone-walled approach leading toward the hilltop, residential compounds, and a temple within the complex. The keep, which sources call a tenshu, was unusually tall and decorated, and visitors were deliberately shown it. Nobunaga moved in around 1579.

The town below was part of the design. Nobunaga encouraged settlement, and the 1577 ordinance gave newcomers incentives. Senior retainers kept residences on the slope approaching the keep, with sites traditionally identified as belonging to Hideyoshi and Maeda Toshiie, so moving through the space meant passing the households that made up the regime. Visitors, including court envoys and, in Jesuit accounts, missionaries, were received in a setting arranged to impress. A temple, Sōken-ji, stood within the complex, so Nobunaga's building program included Buddhist institutions that accepted his authority.

Students should distinguish what archaeology establishes (stonework, approaches, compound layout) from what narrative sources add (interior paintings and the keep's appearance, much of it from supporters and foreign visitors). The castle was burned soon after Nobunaga's death, and responsibility is unclear in the sources. Azuchi anticipated later practice in uniting castle, town, and commerce, but the Tokugawa system of fixed status quarters and obligatory residence of domain lords developed later and should not be read back into it.

Where regional castles in earlier chapters supported daimyō domains, Azuchi expressed a ruler claiming wider reach.

Section 39.8

Markets, Toll Barriers, Merchants, and Economic Policy

Nobunaga's regime issued orders opening markets, restricting some tolls, and managing merchants. A well-known 1577 order for the Azuchi castle town is typically read as granting rakuichi status. He also reduced or removed barriers on some routes, demanded a large levy from Sakai in 1568, and favored merchants who supplied his campaigns. These were selective, locally varied measures. Earlier daimyō such as the Rokkaku had used rakuichi policies, so he built on precedent.

The ordinance belongs to a longer sequence. The Rokkaku of southern Ōmi had authorized a rakuichi at Ishidera Shin'ichi near Kannonji Castle in 1549, an important precedent for Nobunaga's later policies. In the Azuchi order, the first article grants rakuichi conditions and exemptions from za privileges and exactions; as a grant it also invited merchants to settle, bringing population and revenue to a new town on the road and water routes beside Lake Biwa.

Nobunaga also dealt with roads and barriers. As he took territory, he ordered some toll barriers removed to ease movement of troops, goods, and revenue, though barriers that served him or his allies could remain, and many dues continued under other names. In 1569 he regulated the exchange of coin of differing quality (erizeni), a response to monetary disorder in the Kinai markets. Sakai was a self-governing port with merchant leaders. After entering Kyoto in 1568, Nobunaga demanded a large military levy of 20,000 kan from the city. By 1570 it had come under direct Oda control, and by the mid-1570s Matsui Yūkan served as an Oda administrator at Sakai. Nobunaga thus drew the wealthy port increasingly into his fiscal and political system while continuing to use its merchants, shipping, and commercial expertise.

These measures differ from modern liberalization. The ruler decided who received privileges, who paid, and which markets were favored. Merchants who supplied provisions, materials, or credit were protected, and others were taxed or restricted. Commercial policy therefore served military provisioning and political control as much as growth.

The old claim that he abolished guilds and created free markets everywhere is misleading. Some za kept privileges, and he also made use of established merchants. The aims were fiscal and political: easier movement, revenue, and loyalty from merchant communities.

Historical Vocabulary
Rakuichi-rakuza

Rakuichi ("free market") designated a market where some guild (za) monopolies and certain exactions were relaxed to encourage traders. Rakuza refers to loosening za privileges. The phrase did not mean modern free trade: the ruler still granted the privilege, set conditions, and benefited from the resulting commerce.

Primary Source Window

The 1577 Azuchi Castle-Town Ordinance (Azuchi Sangemachi nakaoki)

Creator: Oda Nobunaga's administration · Date: Tenshō 5 (1577) · Type: thirteen-article regulatory order for the Azuchi castle town, surviving in manuscript and catalogued by the National Diet Library

Context: Issued as Azuchi was being settled, the ordinance regulates conduct and commerce in the new town. Paraphrase (not a quotation): its first article establishes rakuichi conditions for the town, exempting traders from various za privileges, duties, and exactions. Students should consult a published edition for the remaining articles rather than rely on this summary.

Analysis: The order shows Nobunaga's regime directly shaping a commercial space as a grant from the ruler, which supports reading rakuichi as a local privilege rather than nationwide free trade. It is a prescriptive text: it shows what the regime wanted, not how well it was obeyed, and it says nothing about the experiences of merchants, residents, or displaced za members. Narrative sources such as Ōta Gyūichi's Shinchō kōki, a retainer's chronicle completed around 1610, should be read alongside it, and treated as a supporter's account.

Section 39.9

Authority, Court, Culture, and the Nobunaga Regime

Nobunaga cultivated the imperial court, supporting ceremonies and finances, and he accepted court ranks: he reached high office, including udaijin in 1577, but resigned from those posts in 1578. He never held the office of shōgun; his intentions regarding the highest court and warrior offices near the end of his life remain debated. He used gifts, spectacle, and collection of renowned tea objects, especially through Sakai connections, to display authority and reward followers (the aesthetics themselves belong to Chapter 38).

Nobunaga's court ranks rose steadily in the mid-1570s, culminating in the post of udaijin in 1577, and he supported court ceremonies and finances while the court, in turn, gave his regime recognized standing. The relationship was mutual dependence: the court needed a protector, and Nobunaga needed legitimacy that military success alone did not provide. After the defeat of the Takeda in 1582, sources describe court envoys raising the question of high office, often called the "three offices" problem (daijō-daijin, kanpaku, or shōgun). The sources are limited and their dating and interpretation are debated, so the episode cannot be used to prove that he intended to take a particular title.

In 1575 Nobunaga transferred the Oda family headship to Nobutada; by 1576 Nobutada was established at Gifu and administered portions of Mino and Owari. Nobunaga remained supreme commander, so the step signaled succession planning but left the heir's authority over senior commanders untested. Regional army commanders governed large areas: Shibata Katsuie in the Hokuriku, Hideyoshi against the Mōri, Mitsuhide in the Kinai and Tanba region, and Takigawa Kazumasu in Kōzuke and the eastern former Takeda territories in spring 1582. Land surveys and local grants (kenchi and enfeoffment) were used in conquered provinces, but practice varied and no uniform national system existed.

Four layers of authority should be distinguished. Court rank gave recognized standing; the Oda headship, held by Nobutada from 1575, defined the family; supreme command remained with Nobunaga; and territorial administration was exercised by commanders with their own retainers, alongside household guard units (umamawari) drawn from Nobunaga's followers. Unlike an ordinary Sengoku daimyō, who ruled one principal domain, Nobunaga directed several armies across many provinces. Yet "absolute monarchy" overstates a system that depended on the loyalty and discretion of commanders, lacked a settled legal order for all his territories, and failed to secure obedience when Mitsuhide acted in 1582.

Historical Debate

Was Nobunaga a Revolutionary?

The question: Did the scale, combination, and reach of practices that had Sengoku precedents amount to a qualitative political transformation, or was Nobunaga's regime an intensified version of the daimyō state?

Interpretation A — Transformation: Scholars emphasizing change point to the combined effect of Azuchi, control of Kyoto and the shogun's role, supra-regional command through delegated generals, and the elimination or subordination of Hiei, the Ikkō strongholds, and several major daimyō. No earlier lord had acted on this scale or across so many provinces at once, and the cumulative result altered what rivals could hope to achieve.
Interpretation B — Intensification: Others stress that castle towns, rakuichi grants (such as the Rokkaku's), firearms, land surveys, and merchant partnerships all existed in daimyō domains. On this view Nobunaga's regime remained personal and unfinished, dependent on loyal commanders, and lacked a settled succession, so it is better seen as an accelerated Sengoku state than as a break with it.

Evidence: For A: the size of Oda territories, Azuchi, the campaigns against religious centers, and the regional command system. For B: earlier market and castle-town grants, daimyō law, the reliance on delegated commanders, and the regime's collapse in 1582. Overall: the two positions use much of the same evidence but weigh scale and permanence differently.

Why it matters: The answer affects how historians periodize the shift from Sengoku territorial states toward late-sixteenth-century political concentration, and whether that shift is dated to the 1560s–1570s or to the settlements after 1582. Synthesis: Nobunaga's distinctiveness lay substantially in scale, combination, and consequence rather than in wholly new practices. Student task: identify one piece of evidence from this chapter for each position and one further source that would help decide between them.

Section 39.10

Honnō-ji and an Unfinished Political Transformation

In June 1582 Nobunaga stopped in Kyoto at Honnō-ji with few guards while his commanders were committed elsewhere, Hideyoshi against the Mōri. Akechi Mitsuhide turned his army on the temple, and Nobunaga died; his heir Nobutada also died at Nijō. Mitsuhide's motives are unknown and have been explained by grievance, ambition, and political calculation, none conclusively.

The regime was especially vulnerable because its senior commanders were dispersed across separate theaters. Hideyoshi was besieging Takamatsu castle in Bitchū against the Mōri, Shibata Katsuie was in the north facing the Uesugi, Takigawa Kazumasu was governing the newly conquered Takeda territories to the east, and Niwa Nagahide and Nobunaga's son Nobutaka were preparing a Shikoku expedition. Tokugawa Ieyasu was visiting the Kinai region. Mitsuhide had been ordered to reinforce Hideyoshi, so a substantial army was assembled near Kyoto under a commander Nobunaga trusted, while Nobutada was in the city with only a small force.

Nobutada's death created an immediate succession crisis. His young son Sanpōshi (later Hidenobu) would become the focus of the later Kiyosu settlement, while Nobunaga's adult sons Nobukatsu and Nobutaka and the senior Oda commanders were dispersed and pursued different interests. No authority existed that could automatically impose a settlement in the first days after Honnō-ji. Mitsuhide briefly seized the initiative in the Kyoto region, but he had no secure base of support. Hideyoshi's quick settlement with the Mōri and march back toward Kyoto, culminating in the battle of Yamazaki, belong to Chapter 40.

Nobunaga had destroyed centers capable of independent action, but he left no settled succession, no completed nationwide political settlement, and major unsubdued rivals. That unfinished inheritance sets up the struggle over the Oda succession in Chapter 40.

The sequence shows expansion in stages with long wars of attrition, and a regime expanding rapidly but without time to consolidate a stable nationwide political settlement.

Note the rhythm: eight years of regional expansion, then a decade in which the same campaigns, such as the Hongan-ji war, often stretched across years while new fronts opened. Acceleration in 1567–1568 came from Mino and Kyoto; delay after 1570 came from coalitions and fortified religious centers; consolidation after 1575 produced Azuchi, but only a few years before the break in 1582.

Multiple Perspectives

Nobunaga's Wars and Regime as Experienced by Different Groups

These sketches are reconstructed from the kinds of evidence each group left; they are not quotations or biographies.

An Oda retainer: Service brought land, command, and favor, and the regime rewarded results, as the elevation of regional commanders shows. It also meant long campaigns, transfers between fronts, and the risk of losing favor or of choosing the wrong side, as Matsunaga and Araki found. Retainer chronicles preserve this view in admiring form, so students should read them for what a loyal servant wished to record.
An Ikkō adherent: Temples, confraternities, and leagues organized worship and sometimes local self-government, so an attack on a stronghold threatened a community and its faith together. Siege, blockade, and burning meant hunger and death on a large scale, and survivors faced Oda rule or flight. Little survives from adherents themselves; temple records and opponents' accounts are the main sources.
A Kyoto courtier: The court gained a protector, funds for ceremony, and relief from the instability of the capital, but became dependent on a military lord who influenced its decisions, as the 1573 era-name change suggests. Court diaries record this ambivalence, and they reflect the concerns of the aristocracy, not those of other Kyoto residents.
A merchant: A Sakai or Azuchi merchant might gain a market privilege, protection on the roads, or work supplying an army. Others faced a levy, an Oda official over their city, or the loss of za privileges. The ordinance and city chronologies show what authorities required, not merchants' own calculations.
A village resident: Campaigns meant requisitions of grain, labor, and transport, the danger of fire, and uncertainty about which lord would collect dues next. Where fighting stayed distant, daily work may have changed little. Village records are sparse for these years, so this experience is inferred from regulations and from damage.
A Jesuit observer: Missionaries valued Nobunaga's tolerance and described Azuchi with interest, but wrote for European readers and for a mission seeking protection.
A defeated warrior: A defeated house might be destroyed, exiled, or absorbed, and some, like Asakura vassals, joined Oda ranks. Outcomes depended on timing and terms of surrender.

These groups did not have equal power or equal responsibility for violence; the aim is to show the range of consequences.

Continuity and Change Over Time

The Sengoku Daimyō State (Chapter 35) and the Nobunaga Regime

What changed? Territorial rule: Daimyō governed one region from a castle, whereas Nobunaga directed several regions through delegated commanders. Castles: Regional strongholds were joined by Azuchi, a political and symbolic center. Warfare: Large armies and fortifications continued, but coordination across fronts grew. Religious power: Many daimyō negotiated with temples; Nobunaga destroyed or subordinated the largest. Shogunate: Daimyō had used shogunal titles; Nobunaga ended the shogun's effective role without abolishing the office.

What remained continuous? Castles, retainer bands, assessment of land, market grants, and merchant partnerships all had Sengoku roots, and Nobunaga's regime still depended on personal loyalty. Villagers still paid dues, and most people's work was agricultural.

Who experienced change most directly? Defeated houses, the Ikkō communities, Mount Hiei, and Yoshiaki's circle lost their independence or their lives. Courtiers and merchants of Kyoto and Sakai faced new officials. Who experienced continuity? Many villagers in areas away from campaigns saw dues and routines persist under a new name. Why does the pattern matter? It shows a regime built on Sengoku institutions at a larger scale with fewer independent rivals, which explains both its power and the fragility revealed in 1582.

Cause and Effect

Fragmentation → Conquest → Concentration of Authority

Long-term causes

Decline of shogunal authority, regional states, commerce, and transformed warfare.

Short-term causes

Owari resources, Okehazama, the Tokugawa alliance, and the entry into Kyoto.

Event

Campaigns against daimyō, shogun, and religious leagues from 1560 to 1582.

Immediate effects

Large Oda territories, a new center at Azuchi, and no settled succession.

Interacting chains. A military chain (Okehazama, alliances, delegated commands) combined with a political chain (Yoshiaki's partnership and breach, court recognition), a religious-institutional chain (Hiei and Hongan-ji opposition), and a commercial chain (Sakai, Azuchi, roads and markets). Each chain depended on the others: the capture of Kyoto brought legitimacy and revenue, which paid for campaigns that removed rivals.

Long-term consequences: Nobunaga's conquests created territorial, military, and institutional conditions that Hideyoshi could exploit in further consolidation. Complexity: no single cause explains the outcome, and the result in 1582 was a concentration of authority around one household that was not yet secure.

Everyday Life and Social History

Living Through a Change of Lords

For many non-elite households in territories that changed hands, Nobunaga's rise appeared as a change in who demanded labor, dues, and obedience. A village near a campaign route might supply porters, grain, fodder, and timber, and a household could lose its harvest or home when a nearby stronghold was attacked. In Kyoto, residents dealt after 1573 with an Oda deputy for policing and disputes. In a castle town such as Azuchi, newcomers received market privileges and settled beside retainers' residences, while neighboring communities supplied materials and workers.

Merchants and carriers experienced the same changes as opportunity and obligation. Roads kept open for armies carried goods, but also demands for provisions, and market rules decided who could trade without paying older dues. The sources for these lives are thin: ordinances, chronicles, and temple records mostly describe rulers' aims rather than households' choices, so historians infer ordinary experience from what authorities regulated and from evidence of destruction and rebuilding.

Chapter Summary

Oda Nobunaga rose from a divided Oda house in Owari, a prosperous province on Ise Bay. By the early 1560s he had broken the main rival branch and become dominant in much of the province, though resistance continued until 1565. His career shows a lord using the resources of the Sengoku world, not a leader destined to unify the country.

Okehazama (1560) removed Imagawa Yoshimoto and the Imagawa threat, and the alliance with Tokugawa Ieyasu secured Nobunaga's east. Mino fell through military pressure and defections in 1567, and Gifu became his base.

In 1568 he escorted Ashikaga Yoshiaki to Kyoto and became the military backer of his shogunate. Their partnership turned into rivalry, and in 1573 Nobunaga expelled Yoshiaki without abolishing the office. Court ranks, a deputy in Kyoto, and support for the court gave the regime recognized standing, though Nobunaga never held the shogunate and his late aims remain debated.

His wars were fought on many fronts. Asakura, Azai, Takeda, the Hongan-ji, Mōri, Yoshiaki, and defecting subordinates formed shifting, uncoordinated opposition, and conquest was nonlinear. He prevailed by outlasting divided rivals and by delegating regional commands.

The great religious-military centers were the hardest obstacles. Enryaku-ji in 1571 and the long Ikkō and Ishiyama Hongan-ji wars, ending in 1580, involved extreme violence. Strategy, institutional rivalry, and the politics of submission explain it better than hostility to Buddhism, since Nobunaga also patronized some temples.

Command and supply, as much as firearms, sustained these campaigns: forts, fleets, ports, and merchants allowed several armies to operate at once. Azuchi, begun in 1576, gave the regime a residence, administrative seat, and symbol on Lake Biwa, though it should not be read as the later Tokugawa castle town.

Commercial policy served similar ends. Market grants such as the 1577 Azuchi ordinance, coin regulation, selective barrier removal, and relations with Sakai raised revenue and loyalty, building on earlier precedents and not amounting to free trade.

The regime was a distinct form: neither an ordinary daimyō state nor an absolute monarchy. Nobutada held the family headship from 1575, commanders governed large areas, and loyalty remained personal. This is why the debate over "revolution" is best settled by scale, combination, and consequence.

At Honnō-ji in 1582 the unfinished character of the order became plain. Nobunaga and Nobutada died, commanders were dispersed, and no settlement existed. He had transformed the problem of political power, but completing reunification fell to others, beginning with the struggle examined in Chapter 40.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter shows how a ruler working within Sengoku institutions altered what political power could mean. By breaking the shogunate's effective role, major daimyō houses, and fortified religious leagues, Nobunaga destroyed the independent centers that had made fragmentation durable.

The chapter also stresses continuity. Castles, retainer armies, market grants, and merchant partnerships all had Sengoku roots, and the regime's novelty lay in scale and combination. The old, rival centers were real institutions with their own communities, and their loss was felt unevenly across Owari, Ōmi, Ise, Echizen, and Kyoto.

The limits of the Oda order are equally important. It relied on delegated commanders and personal loyalty, and Honnō-ji showed how quickly it could fracture. Historians should avoid reading its end as evidence of failure or its achievements as inevitable preparation for later unity.

Chapter 40 takes up the question that remained: how Hideyoshi, building on the territorial, military, and institutional conditions Nobunaga had created, converted an unfinished conquest into a broader settlement, a result that was not predetermined in 1582.
