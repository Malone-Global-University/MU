Part VIII — Bakumatsu and the Meiji Revolution
54

Abolishing the Domains and the Samurai Order

Japanese History: From Jōmon Japan to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

On the fourteenth day of the seventh month of Meiji 4 (29 August 1871 in the Gregorian calendar), the former lords of Japan’s domains were summoned to Tokyo and told that their domains no longer existed. Within a few years the same government had ended hereditary samurai stipends, banned the public carrying of swords, and then fought a civil war against Saigō Takamori, one of the Restoration’s own architects.

This chapter studies how that transformation happened. The Restoration of 1868 changed who claimed sovereignty, but it did not by itself change who collected taxes, commanded soldiers or governed villages. For that the new government had to dismantle domains, build prefectures, create a conscript army, rework public finance and end the legal privileges of the warrior estate.

It also had to overcome resistance. Former samurai who rebelled in 1874, 1876 and 1877 were not simply opponents of modernization; their motives included economic loss, religious conviction, regional loyalty and political disagreement. Most former samurai did not rebel, and many became officials, officers, police and teachers in the new state.

The chapter treats institutions, social consequences and armed opposition together. Evidence comes from decrees, official registers, military records, newspapers, diaries and later memory. We should keep distinct the legal authority of the government and its practical capacity to rule, and avoid assuming that the outcome was inevitable.

The chapter follows Chapter 53 on the Boshin War and prepares for Chapter 55 on social reform and national identity, Chapter 56 on industry, and Chapter 57 on popular rights. Frontier regions such as Hokkaidō and Ryukyu appear because the same state-building reached them.

The sources reflect their makers’ positions. Decrees, army records and prefectural reports show the state’s aims and capacities; the petitions, diaries, newspapers, rebel proclamations and local memoirs show how those aims were received, resisted or adapted. A decree’s wording tells us what the government said it was doing, not what happened in a village, and a rebel’s proclamation tells us what he wished to be remembered for. The chapter keeps these kinds of evidence distinct.

Historical Context

Under the Tokugawa order (Chapter 42), the shogunate governed its own lands directly, while some 260 daimyō ruled domains (han) whose size was measured in kokudaka, the assessed rice yield. Domain power, shogunal authority and imperial legitimacy were distinct. Samurai, who were a small fraction of the population, served the shogun or a daimyō and received hereditary stipends, mostly in rice, and the domain was the unit of their loyalty. Daimyō obligations, including alternate attendance (sankin-kōtai), tied them to Edo, but each domain managed its own finances, courts and military organization.

The Restoration (Chapter 53) overthrew the shogunate in the name of imperial rule but left domains and samurai status intact. Many samurai were already administrators and clerks, and some domains, notably Satsuma and Chōshū, had adopted Western weapons and reforms before 1868. The new government therefore inherited an administrative patchwork, a population divided into status groups, and commitments to pay a large class of retainers.

Stipends were the core of the system. A samurai household received a hereditary stipend, usually in rice, set by rank, and for most domains these payments consumed a large share of revenue. Rank and income differed enormously, from the great retainers of large domains to low-ranking men who had to supplement their income with crafts or farming. Domains also differed in the proportion of samurai they supported: Satsuma is often said to have had an unusually high proportion, though estimates vary. This made the stipend not only an income but a mark of belonging, and its abolition touched both money and identity.

Evidence for this chapter includes decrees and official registers, military records, newspapers, memoirs and local documents. Most of them reflect the government’s perspective or that of literate former samurai, and the voices of commoners and women often appear indirectly.

Division I — From Domains to Prefectures

Section 54.1

Victory Without Centralization: The Meiji Government in 1868

The government that took power in 1868 was a coalition of court nobles and samurai from a few domains, chiefly Satsuma, Chōshū, Tosa and Hizen. It established a Council of State (Dajōkan) and in 1868 renamed Edo as Tokyō; the emperor moved there in 1869. It took over former Tokugawa lands as directly governed prefectures, including the major cities and ports of Kyoto, Osaka, Kanagawa and Nagasaki, though the administration of these lands was gradual. Yet about 260 domains still collected their own taxes, raised their own troops and employed their own retainers, and the government’s authority over them rested largely on the prestige of the emperor and on the cooperation of the powerful domains.

Three problems followed. First, the government lacked a secure revenue base. It relied on loans from merchant houses, on contributions from domains, and on inconvertible paper notes issued from 1868, which lost value. Second, it lacked an army of its own: it depended on domain contingents loyal to their lords, and these were needed in the fighting of the Boshin War (Chapter 53) and its aftermath. Third, it lacked administrators able to reach villages, and the former shogunal officials and domain clerks who knew local conditions served different masters.

The coalition itself was divided. Domain leaders who had cooperated against the shogunate did not necessarily agree on the shape of the new state, and the continuing existence of domains meant that each could still act on its own interests. Historians therefore read 1868–71 as a period in which the Restoration remained a program, with sovereignty claimed for the emperor but governing capacity dispersed.

Section 54.2

Hanseki Hōkan: The Return of Domain Registers, 1869

In early 1869 the lords of Satsuma, Chōshū, Tosa and Hizen petitioned to return their registers of land and population (hanseki) to the emperor. The step was organized by the leaders of the new government, who needed the leading domains to set an example, and other domains followed under pressure. In mid-1869 the court accepted the returns and appointed former daimyō as domain governors (chihanji). The new peerage, the kazoku, merged court nobles (kuge) with former daimyō, and the governors received a fixed portion of domain revenue (usually described as one tenth) as personal income, separating their finances from those of the domain.

The change was real but limited. In principle the land and people now belonged to the emperor, and governors acted as imperial appointees. In practice the same lords still governed, domain armies and officials remained, and retainers continued to be paid from local revenue. The government also issued instructions for domain reorganization, requiring domains to simplify ranks and report their finances, and these measures exposed how heavily indebted many were. Unrest in some domains, including among irregular troops in Chōshū after demobilization in 1869–70, showed that domain forces could not be assumed to obey either lords or the center.

The shift of legal sovereignty therefore came first, and governing capacity was to be transferred in a second step. That the major domains had volunteered made further change easier to propose, but it also meant that their leaders had accepted a principle whose logic pointed toward abolition.

Historical Vocabulary
Hanseki hōkan (版籍奉還)

The “return of the registers and lands” of 1869, by which daimyō formally surrendered their domains’ territory and population to the emperor.

It is easy to confuse with the 1871 abolition of the domains. Hanseki hōkan changed titles while the domains survived; haihan chiken ended them. Treating the two as one event hides how gradual centralization was.

Section 54.3

The Restoration Coalition and the Centralization of Political Power

Centralization was driven by a small group: Ōkubo Toshimichi and Saigō Takamori of Satsuma, Kido Takayoshi of Chōshū, and the court noble Iwakura Tomomi, working with Sanjō Sanetomi at the head of the Council of State (Dajōkan). Younger officials such as Yamagata Aritomo and Inoue Kaoru, both of Chōshū, did much of the practical planning. All agreed that the government needed troops and revenue it controlled directly, but they disagreed about timing and about how far existing privileges could be tolerated without provoking resistance.

The coalition’s own base was the difficulty. Satsuma and Chōshū leaders had to persuade their own lords and retainers to accept reform that would end the lords’ rule, and the Satsuma retired lord Shimazu Hisamitsu was hostile to the direction of change. In 1871 the leaders therefore brought Saigō, then still in Kagoshima, back to Tokyo as a councillor, and arranged for troops from Satsuma, Chōshū and Tosa to form an imperial guard (goshinpei) under the central government. Accounts of its size differ: about 8,000 men is a frequently cited figure for the troops assembled, while some accounts say about 10,000. The sources do not always make clear what the figure counts (men sent, men present, or authorized strength), and this chapter treats it as roughly eight to ten thousand. The guard still came from domains, but its command was now central, and that gave the leaders force that no single domain could match.

Securing the coalition took negotiation. Ōkubo visited Satsuma in 1870, and an imperial mission including Iwakura, Ōkubo, Yamagata Aritomo and others then negotiated with influential leaders in Satsuma and Chōshū, among them Shimazu Hisamitsu and Saigō. Kido Takayoshi’s influence in Chōshū mattered to the project, but the evidence used here does not establish that he took part in every stage of these visits. Saigō’s consent to come to Tokyo and bring troops was decisive. Court nobles such as Sanjō and Iwakura supplied legitimacy that the domain leaders lacked, while the domain men supplied force and administrative experience. This bargain meant that the central government was, in practice, dominated by men from a few domains, a feature that later critics called domain-clique rule (hanbatsu).

Section 54.4

Haihan Chiken: Abolishing the Domains in 1871

On the fourteenth day of the seventh month of Meiji 4 (Meiji 4/7/14, corresponding to 29 August 1871 in the Gregorian calendar), the emperor summoned the domain governors then in Tokyo to the palace and announced that the domains were abolished and replaced by prefectures. The governors were relieved of office and ordered to reside in Tokyo, and the center appointed prefectural officials who answered to the government, not to a local lord.

The decision was prepared in secret by a small group, including Ōkubo, Saigō, Kido, Yamagata and Inoue Kaoru, with Iwakura and Sanjō brought in at a late stage, and the new imperial guard was already in Tokyo. Several calculations made it possible. Many domains were deeply in debt, and the government promised to assume their debts and to keep paying retainers, which made abolition attractive to financially weak lords. The guard meant that no single domain could resist. Former lords were also offered peerage rank and income. Resistance was therefore limited, though not absent: Shimazu Hisamitsu is reported to have reacted with anger in Kagoshima, and some local officials and villagers were uncertain what the change meant for them.

Historians debate whether this shows that daimyō accepted reform or that they had little choice. Both elements were present: some lords supported centralization from conviction or financial necessity, while others recognized that opposition would fail. The reform also shifted the main political question from who ruled each domain to whether the government could actually administer the new prefectures.

The announcement itself was only the first step. Former lords were told to move to Tokyo, domain officials were dismissed or reassigned, and castles, arms and records were turned over to central officials, who had to find out what each domain owned and owed. Most former lords were not appointed to govern their own former territories. Some former retainers lost their domain posts, which meant that changes in employment followed quickly after the decree in some places and slowly in others.

Primary Source Window

The Imperial Decree Abolishing the Domains (1871)

Context: Issued by the Meiji government in the name of the emperor on Meiji 4/7/14 (29 August 1871) and read to the assembled governors. The Japan Center for Asian Historical Records (JACAR) holds the text as reference A15070001100. Source type: official decree.

Paraphrase (not a verbatim quotation, and not a verified translation): The decree recalls that the domains had already been returned to the emperor, but says that problems inherited from long-established practice persisted. It declares that, to unify the government’s orders, to secure the safety of the people, and to deal effectively with foreign powers, the domains are abolished and prefectures established.

Analysis: The document gives the state’s own justification: political unity, domestic order and standing among foreign powers, not conquest. It does not say what daimyō, domain officials or retainers thought, nor how it was received in villages. For those, historians use diaries, petitions and local records.

Section 54.5

Prefectural Consolidation and the End of Territorial Lordship

Immediately after abolition there were 3 urban prefectures (fu) and 302 rural prefectures (ken), mostly the old domains renamed, according to Japan’s National Archives. By November 1871 consolidation had reduced the number to 3 fu and 72 ken, and later changes produced the pattern of the late 1880s. New boundaries often cut across old domain lines, which weakened local loyalties, but they also made it hard to govern territories that were not contiguous.

Centralization also reached the frontiers. The government set up a Colonization Office in 1869 and renamed Ezo as Hokkaidō, beginning settlement that treated Ainu land as open for development (discussed further in later chapters). In 1872 it declared the Ryukyu Kingdom a “domain,” a step toward annexation as Okinawa Prefecture in 1879. These were extensions of state power over peoples with their own histories, not just administrative changes.

Governing the new prefectures depended on who occupied them. Governors came from outside, often from the leading domains, and prefectural offices were often set up in former domain castles or offices and staffed partly by former domain officials. Some prefectures covered territory with no shared history, so local tensions persisted. In 1872 (an imperial rescript of Meiji 5/9/14, preserved in the National Archives of Japan) the Meiji government designated Shō Tai, king of Ryukyu, head of a newly declared Ryukyu “domain” and enrolled him in the kazoku. The designation was Japan’s unilateral act: the kingdom continued to maintain its own relations with China, and its status remained unsettled until annexation in 1879. These changes tied the frontier to the new state without consulting the people affected.

Hokkaidō followed a different path from most of the old domain areas. Southern Hokkaidō had been the Matsumae domain, renamed Tate domain in 1869; the 1871 abolition converted it into Tate Prefecture, which was then merged into Aomori Prefecture and passed to the Colonization Office (Kaitakushi) in 1872. This was a limited domain history, however, and most of the island had never been a domain. There the state’s main instrument was the Colonization Office, whose development program was funded from 1871 and carried out from 1872 under Kuroda Kiyotaka, directing settlement and development from the center.

Ainu residents were incorporated into the commoner (heimin) classification under the new household registration system, not placed in a separate legal estate. In 1878 the Colonization Office standardized the term kyūdojin, “former natives,” for administrative situations in which officials wished to distinguish Ainu from other residents. The term is historical government language and carried discriminatory connotations; it is cited here as evidence of how the state classified Ainu, not as a description the textbook adopts. Later chapters return to the consequences for Ainu communities.

Division II — Building the Administrative, Fiscal, and Military State

Section 54.6

From Domain Administration to a National Bureaucracy

After 1871 the government reorganized the central administration, with a Council of State and ministries, and appointed prefectural governors from Tokyo, many of them men from Satsuma, Chōshū, Tosa and Hizen. The governors could not govern alone. Prefectural offices needed clerks, tax officers and police, and the government kept many former domain officials, who knew the land and people. In the countryside, registration districts were placed under local officials (kochō) who in many places were drawn from former village leaders, so that the new system relied on the people who had run the old one.

Information and communications were as important as appointments. The 1872 household registration (jinshin koseki) set out a single system for counting the population, which supported taxation and conscription. A postal service began in 1871, and telegraph lines extended from Tokyo, so that orders and reports moved faster. Policing was reorganized, and the Tokyo Metropolitan Police was established in 1874, drawing heavily on former samurai. The Home Ministry, created in 1873 under Ōkubo, took charge of local administration and policing.

Many officials were former samurai, so the new bureaucracy was built partly from the old elite. This helped it function, but it also meant that the interests of shizoku and the state were intertwined, a connection that makes the later revolts more complicated than a simple opposition of the old order to the new.

In practice, prefectural governors had many tasks: collecting land revenue, registering households, maintaining order and carrying out central orders. Responsibilities between ministries and prefectures were still being defined, and practice varied from place to place and with the ability and loyalties of individual governors. The state existed largely on paper in the form of registers, tax rolls and reports, so its reach depended on the quality of local record keeping, and on whether former domain clerks cooperated. Elected prefectural assemblies did not yet exist (they belong to Chapter 57), so local people had few formal means to influence decisions, and petitions, protest and negotiation with village officials remained their main recourse.

The Household Registration Law was promulgated in April 1871. It created registration districts (ku) with registrars (kochō) and deputies, and it was put into effect in 1872, when the first nationwide registers (jinshin koseki) were compiled. Local administrative offices were reorganized again in the following years, and arrangements differed among localities. The registers recorded every household by place, composition and status. These registers made it possible to find taxpayers and conscripts, and they fixed people’s classification in the new order. They also meant that the state’s reach depended on the accuracy of local clerks, and that people who wanted to avoid registration, taxation or service had to deal with officials they knew personally.

Section 54.7

Financing Centralization: Domain Debts, Revenue, and Public Obligations

The government inherited domain debts and the stipends of former retainers. In 1871 it announced arrangements to assume the debts of the domains. According to Japan’s Ministry of Finance, debts incurred before 1844 were repudiated, recognized debts of 1844–67 were converted into 50-year interest-free obligations, and recognized debts of 1868–72 were to be repaid over 25 years at 4 percent after a three-year grace period on principal. Stipends for kazoku and shizoku absorbed a very large share of expenditure in the early 1870s, a third or more by many estimates. Central power therefore depended on solving a financial problem: the state could not pay for an army, prefectural administration and its obligations from the revenue it had.

The land-tax reform of 1873 addressed the revenue problem. It established a fixed money tax assessed on land value, at 3 percent, paid by the owner of the land, replacing variable payments in rice that depended on the harvest. It gave the state predictable income and clarified property rights. Many peasants disliked paying in money and felt that the burden was heavy, and protests followed, including large ones in Mie and Ibaraki at the end of 1876. In 1877 the rate was lowered to 2.5 percent. Banking, industrial policy and the wider economic changes belong to Chapter 56.

Revenue collection thus tied central power to the villages. A state that could collect tax directly, without domain intermediaries, could also pay soldiers and officials, and so the fiscal reform and the military reform were parts of one process.

Fiscal reform was indispensable because central power could not rest on borrowing and paper money alone. Before 1873 land revenue was collected through a mixture of arrangements inherited from Tokugawa and domain practice, and much of it stayed at the local level. A uniform money tax gave the government predictable income and let it plan expenditure on the army, officials and stipends. It also moved the burden of the new state to cultivators, which explains the protests of 1876 and the rate reduction of 1877: the state’s fiscal strength was built from villages that bore its costs.

The rate reduction of January 1877 is commonly connected with the protests of late 1876 in Mie and Ibaraki, in which villagers attacked offices and records, though the government also had its own fiscal and political reasons. The episode shows the dependence of central power on rural taxpayers: the same tax that financed the army could provoke disorder, and the government had to adjust its demands to what villages would accept.

Section 54.8

From Domain Armies to Imperial Armed Forces

The Ministry of Military Affairs was established in 1869, and Ōmura Masujirō, its senior vice minister (Hyōbu Taifu) and the leading figure in early army reform, argued for an army of commoners and samurai alike; he was assassinated that year, and Yamagata Aritomo and others carried the program forward. In 1871 the government assembled the imperial guard from Satsuma, Chōshū and Tosa, and after abolition it took over domain arms and organized regional garrisons (chindai) at Tokyo, Osaka, Kumamoto (Chinzei) and in the north. In 1872 the Ministry of Military Affairs was divided into Army and Navy Ministries, the guard was reorganized as the Imperial Guard, and French advisers assisted army training.

Command was contested. Saigō, who held a senior army post, represented the loyalty of Satsuma troops to their own leaders, and the guard’s men came from specific domains. Yamagata, who became vice-minister and later minister, favored an army tied to the state and not to personal loyalty. The Kumamoto garrison, in the region where the 1876 and 1877 revolts occurred, became important in later events.

The government could dismantle regional power only by building coercive capacity that was its own. The existing guard sufficed to abolish the domains, but a lasting national army required a different source of men, and conscription was the means.

Officer recruitment relied heavily on former samurai, who had education and military backgrounds, while rank-and-file soldiers increasingly came from conscription. An army officer school was established in 1874, and the garrisons, reorganized in 1873 into a larger system of military districts, provided the framework for training and mobilization. This produced an army that was in some respects a continuation of samurai military culture and in others a break with it. Officers often came from shizoku families, but they commanded men drawn from all classes, and discipline rested on regulations and the state, not on hereditary loyalty.

Before 1877 many former samurai doubted whether peasant conscripts could fight, and the Satsuma-born guard and officers had reason to think of themselves as the army’s core. The experience of the Kumamoto garrison, which held the castle against Saigō’s force (54.18), answered some of these doubts, though the war also relied heavily on police units drawn from former samurai. The new army was therefore a mixture of conscripts, officers of samurai background and auxiliaries, and the balance among them was still being worked out.

Section 54.9

The Conscription Ordinance of 1873 and the End of the Samurai Military Monopoly

A proclamation of Meiji 5/11/28 (28 December 1872 in the Gregorian calendar, which Japan adopted on 1 January 1873) announced universal military obligation, and the conscription ordinance followed in January 1873. The proclamation stated the principle; the ordinance, and the registration and selection it required, were put into effect only in 1873. As the ordinance is usually summarized, men were examined at twenty, and since the army needed only a fraction of those eligible, selection was made by lot. Those chosen served three years in the regular army, followed by four years in the reserves. The principle that any man, not only a samurai, could be required to serve ended the warrior monopoly on arms.

The ordinance, however, contained substantial exemptions, which the National Archives of Japan documents. They covered household heads and heirs, only sons, officials, students at certain schools, men who failed physical standards, and men who paid 270 yen. These provisions favored wealthier and urban families, so the burden fell on poorer younger sons in the countryside. Many villagers opposed the measure and misunderstood the phrase “blood tax,” which rumor took literally. Disturbances (ketsueki ikki) broke out in several regions in 1873, and they often combined opposition to conscription with other grievances such as the new land tax and schooling fees. National obligation did not mean equal implementation.

The conscription system was applied through local officials, who compiled registers of eligible men and organized the draw by lot. Evasion by adoption, false registration or payment was common, and many men sought exemption. The earliest conscripts came mostly from the countryside, and shizoku formed a high proportion of the officer corps, so the army was not simply an army of commoners led by commoners. The 1873 disturbances in several regions were repressed, and the conscription system continued.

Division III — Dismantling the Samurai Order

Section 54.10

Daimyō, Kazoku, Shizoku, and the Reclassification of Hereditary Status

Former daimyō and court nobles became the kazoku, a peerage of roughly five hundred families, given titles and income and expected to reside in Tokyo. Former samurai were classified as shizoku, and lower-ranking retainers as sotsu, a category that was reorganized in 1872, with some people becoming shizoku and others commoners (heimin). The 1872 household registers recorded these three classifications. Soon after abolition, shizoku were permitted to take up farming and trade, and marriage between status groups was allowed, steps that ended the legal basis of the old estate order. The related emancipation edicts for eta and hinin are discussed in Chapter 55.

These labels did not erase advantage. Shizoku often had education, literacy and connections that helped in the new administration, and the label remained on registers for decades. Kazoku retained wealth and social prestige. A legal category and social advantage were different things, and governing and military privileges ended even though other advantages persisted.

The legal categories had different meanings. Kazoku was a rank carried by a family, with a title and a place in the new order, and it was no longer connected with ruling a territory. Shizoku and sotsu were classifications in the registers, descended from Tokugawa retainer categories, and the 1872 reorganization meant that many of the lowest-ranking retainers, whose positions had been precarious, were either elevated to shizoku or placed among commoners. Under the Tokugawa order, status (mibun) had been tied to occupation, household and service to a lord, and in the new system registration attached to the household while service and governing functions were removed. Rural retainers and those in small domains were classified unevenly, and the process itself had winners and losers. Hereditary rank, occupation and actual privilege therefore came apart: a person could be registered as shizoku and have no stipend, no office and no sword.

Section 54.11

The Stipend Problem: From Hereditary Payments to Government Bonds

Stipends for kazoku and shizoku were a major burden. In 1873–74 the government offered voluntary commutation, with payments partly in cash and partly in bonds, and research by Sergi Basco and John Tang reports that about one third of eligible recipients took part. In August 1876 it made commutation compulsory, replacing stipends with interest-bearing public bonds. The terms depended on the size of the original stipend: capitalization factors and interest rates differed across stipend levels, so recipients were not all treated alike. In the schedule reproduced by Basco and Tang from earlier work, a recipient of ¥70,000 or more a year received bonds worth five times the annual stipend at 5 percent interest, one with ¥100–150 received eleven times at 6 percent, and one with under ¥25 received fourteen times at 7 percent. In annual terms, these terms returned about 25 percent, about 66 percent and about 98 percent of the original stipend respectively (a calculation from the schedule, not a figure in the source).

Absolute resources and proportional losses must be separated. Higher-ranking recipients, including many kazoku, received much larger bonds in absolute terms, but the conversion cut a larger share of their annual income. Lower-ranking recipients lost proportionally less, but their bonds were small and could not support a household, so they had to sell them, take new work or depend on relatives. Inflation reduced the real value of fixed payments. For the state the reform ended an open-ended obligation, and for many shizoku it ended the income that had defined their status, which was one cause of the resentment behind the revolts of 1876.

The mechanics of commutation show how a financial measure became a social one. According to Basco and Tang, there were restrictions on the initial holding and redemption of the bonds, so recipients could not at once convert them into cash on demand. Within the rules, bonds could be transferred or pledged, and some holders sold them at a discount or invested in small businesses, many of which failed. Bonds could also serve as capital for banks, a subject for Chapter 56. For a household the arithmetic was simple: an annual payment that had supported a family was replaced with a bond whose interest could be much smaller, and which could be lost in a bad investment. The government’s purpose was to end a permanent charge, and the social consequence was to push thousands of households into the labor market within a few years, with very different resources depending on rank and region.

Shizoku was a registry status, not a profession, and by itself it did not signal wealth or poverty.

Section 54.12

Swords, Hairstyles, and the Visible End of Samurai Privilege

In 1871 an edict (danpatsurei) allowed men to cut the topknot (chonmage), and fashions changed gradually, first in cities and among officials. In March 1876 the sword-prohibition order (haitōrei) banned carrying swords in public except for uniformed soldiers and police, and others who were permitted. It was a legal restriction on public display, not a ban on swords or swordsmanship, which continued privately.

For many shizoku the sword had been the sign of status, and the order was understood as a symbolic loss, and for sword makers and others who depended on the market it meant economic loss as well. Samurai identity, however, was historical and changed over time, and the order marked the end of one legal privilege and not of a timeless warrior spirit. The later reinvention of martial traditions belongs to Chapter 58.

The changes came in stages. In 1870 commoners were allowed to take surnames, and in 1871 the topknot edict and a decree allowing, but not requiring, men to go without swords marked the first steps; the full prohibition followed in 1876. This gradual sequence meant that by 1876 many shizoku had already given up their swords, and the order removed the remaining exception. Resistance was strongest among those who tied swords to honor, as the Shinpūren revolt showed, and sword makers, polishers and fittings makers lost customers and turned to other work. Contemporary reactions ranged from relief among those who found swords burdensome to anger among those who saw the order as an insult, so the measure was both symbolic and economic.

Section 54.13

Former Samurai in a Changing Society: Work, Family, Mobility, and Dislocation

Shizoku took many paths. Some became officials, army officers, police, teachers or journalists, using education and connections. Others farmed, opened small shops, or failed in business; government programs to help shizoku find work, and settlement schemes in Hokkaidō, had mixed results, and many ventures by inexperienced former samurai in trade failed. The former Aizu retainers, defeated in 1868, were moved to the barren Shimokita region in the north, where they suffered severe hardship. Women of shizoku families took in sewing or weaving, taught, or entered new schools and factories. Others fell into poverty.

Most former samurai did not rebel, and many adapted or benefited. Not everyone in Tokugawa society had been a samurai, and commoners had their own view of the changes: they paid the land tax and bore conscription, and some resented the privileges that shizoku still held.

The Tomioka silk mill, set up by the government in 1872 in Gunma, recruited many of its first workers from shizoku families, partly because the government wanted respectable trainees who would then teach others. One was Wada Ei (sometimes read Eiko), the daughter of a former samurai of Matsushiro domain in Shinano, who entered the mill in April 1873 and began writing reminiscences in 1907, later published in 1931. Her account, written decades after the events, shows the practical meaning of status change for one shizoku daughter, though it comes from a single, literate and relatively favored participant, and it was written with hindsight. Industrial change as such belongs to Chapter 56.

Experience varied by region. In Satsuma, where samurai were numerous and local administration remained in their hands, adjustment was delayed and the pressure on leaders to act correspondingly strong. In Tōhoku, domains that had fought against the Restoration forces had lost land and rank, and their retainers, as the Aizu example shows, faced harsher conditions. In Hokkaidō, military-agricultural settlers (tondenhei) recruited from 1875 were drawn largely from shizoku, which linked the dislocation of former retainers to frontier colonization. These differences meant that “the shizoku” were not a single group with one experience.

Division IV — Political Division and Former-Samurai Resistance

Section 54.14

The Political Crisis of 1873: Saigō, Korea, and the Divided Government

The Iwakura Mission (1871–73), led by Iwakura Tomomi with Ōkubo, Kido and Itō Hirobumi, left for the United States and Europe, and the caretaker government under Sanjō and Saigō agreed not to make major new policy in its absence. The Korean question arose from Joseon’s refusal to accept Japanese diplomatic communications. The Meiji government had taken diplomatic responsibility away from the Sō domain of Tsushima, which had long mediated relations with Joseon, and was trying to replace those arrangements, including the Japan House (Waegwan) at Pusan, with direct relations between governments. No agreement had been reached. Korean officials objected to Japanese letters whose wording implied imperial superiority, and to Japan’s adoption of Western forms, and they refused to receive them. Some Japanese officials and many shizoku regarded the refusal as an insult.

In August 1873 the caretaker cabinet agreed in principle to send Saigō as envoy, and Sanjō held back the final report to the emperor until Iwakura’s return. Historians disagree about Saigō’s intentions. One view holds that he proposed to go in civilian dress, expecting to be killed, so that war would be justified; another that he sought a peaceful negotiation and the risk of violence was incidental; and others stress that his letters are ambiguous and that supporters and opponents read them in line with their own aims.

Iwakura returned in September, and he, Ōkubo and Kido argued that Japan should address domestic finances, treaty revision, frontier questions in Sakhalin and Taiwan, and administrative consolidation before any war. The issue was therefore not simply reform against conquest, since supporters of the mission included both reformers and conservatives, and opponents were not opposed to expansion as such. In mid-October the cabinet split, Sanjō fell ill under the strain, and Iwakura became acting head of the Council of State. He reported to the emperor, who endorsed the opponents’ position. Saigō, Itagaki Taisuke, Etō Shinpei, Gotō Shōjirō and Soejima Taneomi then resigned. The government was weakened but not defeated, and many shizoku who left it went to their home regions, where opposition could be organized.

Section 54.15

The Saga Rebellion of 1874

In February 1874 revolt broke out in Saga, where two groups of shizoku had grown restive. The Seikantō (Korea Expedition Party) supported the Korea invasion and was led by Etō Shinpei, the former Minister of Justice who had resigned in 1873. The Yūkoku-tō (Patriotic Party), led by Shima Yoshitake, was conservative and anti-foreign, and wanted to restore older ways. The groups’ aims were different, and they were united chiefly by hostility to the government and by local grievance.

The rebels attacked offices and seized Saga Castle, but the government sent troops under Ōkubo Toshimichi, who went to the region with wide powers. The rebels were defeated within weeks. Etō fled, was captured, and was executed in April 1874 after a summary trial, and Shima was also executed. The harshness of the punishment was meant to deter others.

The rebellion cannot be reduced to a reaction against the loss of stipends. It combined a policy dispute over Korea, local conservatism, personal ambition and regional rivalry, and it showed that the government could mobilize military force and use railways, telegraphs and ships quickly.

The rebels also looked for allies. Etō is reported to have gone to Kagoshima to ask Saigō for support, which was refused, and then to Tosa, where he was arrested; the attempt shows how isolated the Saga rebels were. The government’s response used troops from several garrisons and the telegraph to coordinate, and it was tied to Ōkubo’s personal presence, which made the suppression also a political demonstration of central authority.

Section 54.16

The Uprisings of 1876: Shinpūren, Akizuki, and Hagi

In October 1876, after the sword ban and compulsory commutation, three revolts broke out in quick succession. The Shinpūren (League of the Divine Wind) in Kumamoto, led by Ōtaguro Tomoo, rejected the sword ban, Western dress and other innovations on religious grounds. Its roughly 170 members, who relied on divination and refused firearms, attacked the Kumamoto garrison on the night of 24 October and killed the prefectural governor and the garrison commander, but were defeated within days.

Akizuki, in Fukuoka, rose on 27 October under Miyazaki Shanosuke and Imamura Hyakuhachirō, who had ties with the Shinpūren, and they were defeated within days. At Hagi on 28 October Maebara Issei, a Chōshū man who had held high office in the military administration and left the government in 1870, led a revolt against what he saw as the government’s mistaken policies, and he was captured and executed. The three differed in motive: religious revivalism at Kumamoto, regional shizoku resentment at Akizuki, and political opposition to the government’s direction at Hagi.

Participants did attempt to cooperate, and the Akizuki group sought contact with rebels elsewhere, including Hagi, but these efforts did not produce a sustained, unified campaign. The government could therefore deal with each revolt in turn, and the garrison system and telegraph allowed rapid response.

The constituencies of the revolts differed from those of Saga. In 1874 the leaders were former officials with national political ties and the issue was the Korea policy, while in 1876 the rebels were mostly local shizoku, motivated by religious revival, by resentment of the status reforms and by regional loyalty. In each case suppression was carried out by garrison troops, so the same institutions the rebels opposed, the conscript army and the telegraph, were the means of their defeat.

Division V — The Satsuma Rebellion and the Consolidation of the Meiji State

Section 54.17

Saigō Takamori, Kagoshima, and the Road to Rebellion in 1877

By the end of 1873 Saigō Takamori had left the government and returned to Kagoshima. He had resigned as councillor over the Korea question, and he gave up his army position in Tokyo, though he kept his Satsuma honors. His departure was followed by that of many Satsuma officers and policemen, who also went home. Satsuma was by this point unusual among prefectures. Its governor, Ōyama Tsunayoshi, was a local man, and former retainers filled many local posts. Central policies on land tax, registers and status were applied slowly or selectively, so Kagoshima stood partly outside the new administrative order. Historians therefore describe it as a prefecture that was formally integrated but substantially self-governing in practice.

In 1874 Saigō and his followers established the Shigakkō (private schools) in Kagoshima, with branch schools in the districts of the former domain. They taught military drill and Chinese classics, and enrolled several thousand young shizoku, many of whom came from low-ranking families. The schools were financed partly from Saigō’s own resources and partly through local administration, and their students formed a ready body of armed, organized men loyal to local leaders. Saigō himself spent much time hunting and in the hot springs, and the extent to which he directed the schools’ daily affairs is uncertain. The schools’ leaders, including Shinohara Kunimoto, Kirino Toshiaki and Murata Shinpachi, were important in shaping opinion.

The government watched Kagoshima with growing suspicion. In late 1876 it sent a group of police officers who were natives of Satsuma back to the region, officially on leave, and some of these men were later alleged to have been sent to spy or even to assassinate Saigō. A confession attributed to one of them circulated among the students and heightened tension; its accuracy and the government’s actual intentions remain debated. At the same time the revolts of Shinpūren, Akizuki and Hagi (54.16) had alarmed the government, which began to remove munitions from Kagoshima. In late January 1877 ships carried gunpowder and arms from the arsenals toward Osaka, and Shigakkō students raided the remaining arsenals and magazines.

Saigō was away hunting in the Ōsumi peninsula when the raids began. On learning of them, he returned to Kagoshima and, in early February, agreed to lead a march to Tokyo to “question” the government about the police affair. Interpretations of this decision vary. One view holds that Saigō sought to restrain his followers and then felt bound by their actions; another that he accepted that a clash with the government was inevitable and chose to share its risks. Whichever is correct, the leadership was collective: his followers, not Saigō alone, had set the rising in motion. The army set out in mid-February in snow, perhaps 12,000 or 13,000 strong at first, with more joining later. Not every Satsuma man joined; some, such as Saigō’s brother Tsugumichi and Ōkubo Toshimichi, remained with the government. The government declared the force in rebellion, deprived Saigō of his rank, and appointed Prince Arisugawa Taruhito as commander of the expedition.

The army was grouped in battalions under senior commanders, and volunteers from other parts of Kyushu joined it while some Satsuma men did not. Its recruitment and command relationship to the Shigakkō network is documented only in broad terms, and this chapter does not claim a precise one-to-one correspondence. Elsewhere, the Risshisha in Tosa divided: a faction considered armed action in support of the rising, while others rejected rebellion and pursued petitions for representative government (Chapter 57). The result was a rebellion centered on one region, with a strong local base and little national political coordination.

Section 54.18

The Satsuma Rebellion: Kumamoto, Tabaruzaka, and the Campaign of 1877

The rebels’ plan was to take Kumamoto Castle, the base of the regional garrison, and then move north. The garrison under Major General Tani Tateki numbered several thousand and contained conscripts, police and officers. Fires broke out in the castle in the days before the attack, and their cause is disputed (deliberate burning of stores or accident). Satsuma forces attacked on 22 February 1877, but the walls held, and the rebels settled into a siege instead of risking costly assaults. Because Satsuma had to keep part of its force around the castle while facing a government relief army, the siege divided its strength, and it also gave the government time to bring up reinforcements.

The government’s forces came by land and sea. Chartered steamships carried troops across the Inland Sea to Kyushu, and the telegraph linked Tokyo with the front, so orders and reports moved in hours. Rations and munitions were requisitioned in volume. The army mixed conscript regiments with new police battalions (battōtai) recruited from former samurai, some from areas such as Aizu that had fought against Satsuma and Chōshū in 1868, and for these men the war carried an element of local revenge. Because former samurai fought for the government, the war cannot be described as a conflict of shizoku against commoners.

The decisive fighting took place at Tabaruzaka, a ridge north of Kumamoto along the road a relief army from the north had to take. In early March the Satsuma main force took up defensive positions there, and government troops attacked repeatedly between about 3 and 20 March in some of the hardest fighting of the war. The Satsuma defenders used the ground, fought with rifles and some artillery, and made counterattacks, including night attacks and close combat with swords, which the government’s police swordsmen also practiced. The government answered with artillery and sustained rifle fire, and fresh troops. Satsuma’s limited stocks of ammunition were gradually exhausted, and on about 20 March its positions gave way. Both sides lost heavily.

The retreat opened the road toward Kumamoto, but the siege ended through a separate operation. A government force had landed by sea near Yatsushiro, south of the castle, in March and fought its way northward, and government troops entered Kumamoto Castle on about 14 April, ending the siege. The campaign shows why a simple contrast of swords and guns misleads. Satsuma had modern weapons but few supplies and no means of replacing losses, while the government had stocks, reinforcements and transport. The conscripts, whose reliability had been doubted, fought effectively.

Civilians experienced the campaign as requisition, destruction and displacement. Much of Kumamoto town burned, farmers were pressed into carrying supplies and digging, and the rebels issued their own paper notes, which lost value. Local shizoku divided: some joined Saigō, and others served the government, and the earlier suppression of the Shinpūren revolt in Kumamoto had weakened the local opposition. The sources are mostly official reports, military diaries and later memoirs, which emphasize commanders and battles; the voices of ordinary villagers are scarcer.

Section 54.19

Retreat, Shiroyama, and the Death of Saigō Takamori

After the failure at Kumamoto, Saigō’s army withdrew south and east, fighting rearguard actions around Hitoyoshi and then into the mountains of Hyūga (Miyazaki). It was pursued by government columns that gradually took the towns and ports, cutting access to supplies. In mid-August 1877, after a defeat near Nobeoka, Saigō dissolved the main army, reportedly burning his own uniform and papers, and led a few hundred men through the mountains. They reached Kagoshima in early September and occupied Shiroyama, the hill overlooking the city.

The government commanders surrounded the hill with tens of thousands of troops and warships in the bay, and offered terms that Saigō did not accept. In the early morning of 24 September 1877 they attacked. Almost all the defenders were killed within hours. Saigō was wounded, and according to the traditional account one of his officers, Beppu Shinsuke, helped him end his life; the details come from accounts written after the event and are not fully verified. Estimates of the dead for the whole campaign run into the thousands on each side, but they differ by source and by whether deaths from wounds and disease are counted, so this chapter does not give a single figure.

The immediate consequences were military and political. Organized armed opposition by former samurai ended. Kido Takayoshi had died in May 1877, before the war’s end, and Ōkubo became the dominant figure in the government. Rebels were tried and punished, many were imprisoned, and Satsuma lost its special position. Contemporary evidence for the campaign includes official war records, telegrams, newspaper dispatches and woodblock prints, which show how the war was reported to a national audience.

The government then dealt with those it had captured. Ōyama Tsunayoshi, governor of Kagoshima, was arrested during the campaign, convicted of supporting the rebellion, and executed in Nagasaki on 30 September 1877. A November 1877 American diplomatic report, drawing on Japanese newspapers, recorded 38,514 people tried in connection with the rising, of whom 35,918 were pardoned and 1,793 sentenced to imprisonment with hard labor. These are contemporary reported figures, not reconciled modern totals, but they suggest a policy that punished leaders severely and released most participants. Kagoshima lost its special administrative autonomy.

Section 54.20

After the Rebellion: The New State and the Legacy of the Samurai

The victory confirmed the central principle of the new order: that a conscript army, supported by a national treasury and a communications network, could defeat the most formidable armed former-samurai force in the country. It also had costs. The government spent about ¥41.56 million on suppression, according to records held by the Japan Center for Asian Historical Records (JACAR). That figure should be compared with the budget of the same year before any claim about its proportion is made. The government met part of the cost by issuing inconvertible paper money, which contributed to inflation and a fiscal crisis in the late 1870s that later reforms (Chapter 56) had to address. Peasants who benefited from higher rice prices and shizoku who held bonds were affected differently.

The government continued to depend on men of samurai background. Officers, police and officials in the war-winning army were largely shizoku, and the army was reorganized after 1877, with a General Staff Office created in 1878. Discontent also continued. In May 1878 Ōkubo Toshimichi was assassinated in Tokyo by former samurai from Ishikawa and neighboring areas, whose statement criticized the government’s policies and personal conduct. In August 1878 soldiers of the Imperial Guard artillery mutinied over pay and rewards. Meanwhile opposition took a political form, with Itagaki Taisuke and others in Tosa and elsewhere organizing associations that demanded an elected assembly (Chapter 57). The defeat of armed revolt did not end dissent; it shifted it toward petitions, newspapers and meetings.

Saigō’s memory changed over time. He died officially a rebel, but in 1889, at the time of the promulgation of the constitution, he was pardoned, and in 1898 a bronze statue of him was unveiled in Ueno Park in Tokyo. Later writers presented him variously as a loyal retainer, a defender of tradition, a critic of corruption or an exemplar of sincerity. These images tell us about later needs as much as about the man, and they should be separated from the evidence about his actual choices in 1873 and 1877.

Historical Debate

Why Did Some Former Samurai Rebel While Most Adapted?

The question: The reforms affected all shizoku, yet only a minority took up arms. What distinguished those who rebelled?

Interpretation A — Economic grievance: Loss of stipends and status pushed households toward revolt, especially in poorer regions and after the compulsory commutation of 1876. This reading finds support in the timing of the 1876 revolts and in rebel complaints about lost livelihood.
Interpretation B — Political and organizational factors: Leadership, existing networks and local institutions, such as Satsuma’s schools and the control that its elites kept over local government, mattered more than income alone. On this view, the Korea dispute of 1873 and the split among the leaders gave rebels both a cause and commanders.
Interpretation C — Ideology, honor and religion: Rebels such as the Shinpūren acted from religious conviction and a sense of insulted honor, which the sword ban and Western-style change seemed to violate. This reading emphasizes the diversity of motives rather than a single cause.

Evidence: Historians would compare participation with stipend level and region, read rebel proclamations and trial records, and examine local institutions. Each type of evidence has limits: trial records come from the government, and proclamations state what rebels wished to be known for. Why it matters: The answer determines whether the revolts are read as reaction against modernization, as conflict within the new state, or as a mixture varying by region. Student task: Choose one revolt and identify evidence that would favor each interpretation.

Most historians combine these factors, with different weights for each revolt.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From Warrior Government to National Military State

What changed? Hereditary rule, domain armies and stipends gave way to prefectures, conscription and bonds. What remained continuous? Former samurai staffed the officer corps and bureaucracy; local elites remained influential. Who experienced change most directly? Daimyō and low-ranking shizoku. Who experienced greater continuity? Many villagers, who still paid taxes to a distant authority. Why does the pattern matter? It shows that state-building transferred power and reused personnel.

Section 54.21

Chapter Review and Historical Thinking Checkpoint

The five divisions of this chapter together explain how the Restoration was consolidated. Division I shows the transfer of sovereignty and the abolition of the domains by leaders who combined political bargaining with the threat of force. Division II shows that this transfer meant little without bureaucrats, revenue and an army. Division III shows how hereditary privileges were ended and how that affected different households. Divisions IV and V show that the process split the Restoration coalition and met armed resistance that was defeated by the state it opposed.

Students should be able to distinguish the legal authority of the government, which came from decrees, from its practical governing power, which depended on tax collection, police, soldiers, information and the cooperation of local elites. They should also be able to compare the experiences of the kazoku, of high- and low-ranking shizoku, of conscripts and their families, and of people in Hokkaidō and Ryukyu. The questions at the end of the chapter ask them to use evidence to explain why centralization succeeded, why most former samurai did not rebel, and what the Satsuma Rebellion did and did not settle. Chapter 55 turns to the reshaping of schooling, religion and social classification that accompanied these changes.

Chapter Summary

The Restoration of 1868 produced a government but not yet a state. The domains held land, tax revenue and armies, and the coalition lacked a financial base, a national army and administrators who could reach villages. Sovereignty was claimed for the emperor, while governing capacity remained dispersed.

The return of the registers in 1869 shifted legal sovereignty but kept former lords as governors. The decisive step came on Meiji 4/7/14 (29 August 1871), when an imperial guard assembled in Tokyo and promises to assume debts and keep paying retainers allowed a small group of leaders to abolish the domains with little resistance. The government’s own decree justified the step by the need for unified orders, domestic security and standing among other nations.

Prefectures, ministries, registers, police and communications gave the new state its reach, but it relied on former domain officials, so the old elite helped to build the new bureaucracy. The land-tax reform of 1873 provided revenue, and the army was reorganized under Yamagata and others, so that fiscal and military reforms were parts of one process.

Conscription ended the samurai monopoly of arms in principle. Its exemptions, including the 270-yen payment, meant that the burden fell most heavily on poorer households, and rural riots showed that obligation was not accepted passively.

The compulsory commutation of stipends in 1876 and the sword prohibition ended the legal privileges of the warrior estate. The effects were uneven. High-ranking families held large bonds, while low-ranking households often fell into hardship, and some shizoku became officials, officers, police and teachers. Most did not rebel.

The Korea dispute of 1873 split the leadership, with disagreement over priorities and over Saigō’s intentions, and it sent former leaders back to their regions. The Saga revolt of 1874, the three revolts of 1876 and the Satsuma Rebellion of 1877 had different causes, including policy disagreement, religious conviction, economic loss and regional loyalty.

The government’s victory over Satsuma ended large armed former-samurai revolts but at great financial cost, with about ¥41.56 million spent on suppression. It confirmed that conscripts and police, supported by steamships and telegraphs, could defeat a determined force, though the government still depended on men of samurai background.

The chapter’s conclusion is that the Meiji state was built by transferring territory, fiscal obligations, military power and governing authority from hereditary institutions to a central state, and that this was achieved through negotiation, force and the reuse of existing personnel. Samurai ideals survived, reinterpreted, and Saigō became a figure of historical memory.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows that the Meiji state was built, not declared. Overthrowing the shogunate in 1868 did not by itself create a state that could tax villages, field an army or govern prefectures, and this required years of negotiation, administrative work and coercion.

It also shows how hereditary privileges were removed with unequal effects. A daimyō, a high-ranking shizoku, a low-ranking retainer, a village conscript and a commoner taxpayer experienced the same reforms very differently, and armed opposition arose from several causes, not from a single reaction against modernization. The government’s reliance on former samurai complicates the idea that old and new orders were simply opposed.

The same state-building reached the frontiers. Hokkaidō and Ryukyu were drawn into the new system on terms set in Tokyo, a process that later chapters examine from the perspectives of Ainu and Okinawan communities.

The chapter prepares for those that follow: Chapter 55 on schooling, religion and social classification, Chapter 56 on finance and industry, and Chapter 57 on popular rights, which took over from armed revolt as the main form of political opposition.
