The War in China

Japanese History: From Jōmon Japan to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

On the night of July 7, 1937, a Japanese infantry unit was conducting a night exercise near the Marco Polo Bridge (Lugouqiao), a stone bridge southwest of Beiping. Shots were fired in the dark, a soldier was briefly reported missing and soon returned, and within hours soldiers of both armies were exchanging fire. Eight years later, millions of people were dead, much of central and southern China had been devastated, and Japan's armies had still not produced a political victory.

The encounter at Lugouqiao was not the cause of the war that followed. It was the spark that landed in a region already thick with tension: a Japanese garrison stationed in North China under treaty rights dating from 1901, a Manchukuo created by Japanese arms in 1931–1932 and still pressing on China's northern provinces, a Chinese government unifying its territory and preparing for a confrontation it believed it could not postpone indefinitely, and a Japanese army whose officers had learned, since 1931, that insubordination in the field was often rewarded in Tokyo. Chapters 74 and 75 traced how military autonomy expanded inside the Japanese state. This chapter shows what that autonomy produced when it met a society of several hundred million people that did not collapse on schedule.

The chapter's central argument is that Japan's war in China became a conflict that Japan could neither decisively win nor easily end. Japanese forces captured Beiping, Tianjin, Shanghai, Nanjing, Wuhan, Guangzhou, and later much of the railway system from Manchuria to Indochina. But capturing cities did not destroy the Chinese government's capacity to resist, did not give Japan control of the countryside, did not eliminate guerrilla organizations, and did not produce a settlement acceptable to any Chinese authority with broad legitimacy. Japanese leaders had expected the war to be short. Instead it consumed a large share of the army's strength for eight years and drew Japan toward the confrontation with the United States and the European colonial powers examined in Chapter 78.

The chapter is also about Chinese history, and it treats Chinese actors as agents rather than as obstacles. The Nationalist government of Chiang Kai-shek chose to fight at Shanghai, traded space for time, relocated people and industry to the interior, and in June 1938 breached the Yellow River dikes with catastrophic consequences for civilians. Communist forces built rural base areas behind Japanese lines. Regional commanders cooperated and competed with Nanjing and Chongqing. Collaborationist regimes, led by figures such as Wang Jingwei, claimed Chinese national legitimacy under Japanese protection. Ordinary people farmed, fled, starved, paid taxes to several authorities, labored on roads and airfields, and sometimes joined or opposed armies. The United Front between Nationalists and Communists was a limited and unstable accommodation, not a merger, and its conflicts shaped China's postwar civil war.

The chapter does not soften what the war did to civilians. The Nanjing Massacre of December 1937, the bombing of Chongqing and other cities, the reprisals and village destruction of counterinsurgency campaigns, and the use of requisitioned and coerced labor are central to understanding the war, not episodes at its edges. Chapter 79 will treat forced labor, sexual slavery, and atrocity systematically and comparatively, and it will address the problem of memory. This chapter gives the atrocities of the China war the narrative attention they require while leaving that comparative treatment to Chapter 79. It also distinguishes carefully between what is documented, what is estimated within wide ranges, and what is politically contested; the uncertainty of numbers is not uncertainty about whether events occurred.

The evidence for this history is vast and uneven. Japanese official sources include the multi-volume war history compiled by the Defense Agency's war history office (Senshi sōsho), army and navy records digitized by the Japan Center for Asian Historical Records (JACAR), diaries and memoirs of officers and soldiers, and the Japanese press under censorship. Chinese sources include Nationalist military and government records preserved in Taiwan and in the Second Historical Archives in Nanjing, Chiang Kai-shek's diaries held at the Hoover Institution, Communist documents published selectively, local gazetteers, newspapers, and oral histories. Foreign evidence includes diplomatic correspondence, the reports and diaries of missionaries and journalists, and the official histories of the American campaigns in China, Burma, and India. Each category has a perspective. Students should ask of every source who created it, for what audience, and whose experience it omits.

The chapter covers the war from 1937 to 1945, concentrating most heavily on 1937–1941, and it ends with the Japanese surrender in China and the unresolved struggle between Nationalists and Communists that followed. It builds on Chapter 62's account of Japanese colonial rule in Korea, Chapter 74's account of the Manchurian Incident, and Chapter 75's account of Manchukuo and the transformation of the Japanese state. It stops short of the mobilization of Japanese society (Chapter 77), the war against the United States and European empires (Chapter 78), the systematic history of atrocity and forced labor (Chapter 79), the Japanese home front and atomic bombings (Chapter 80), and the surrender and repatriation narrative (Chapter 81).

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain the political and military conditions in North China and in Chinese national politics that preceded the outbreak of full-scale war in July 1937.
  2. Reconstruct the Marco Polo Bridge Incident, distinguishing what is known, what is uncertain, and what is established about Japanese responsibility for subsequent escalation.
  3. Trace the major campaigns of 1937–1938, from North China through Shanghai, Nanjing, Xuzhou, Wuhan, and Guangzhou, and explain why battlefield victories did not end the war.
  4. Describe the Nanjing Massacre on the basis of contemporary documentation and testimony, and distinguish uncertainty about casualty totals from the established fact of mass killing.
  5. Analyze Chinese strategy, including the Nationalist policy of prolonged resistance, the Yellow River breach of 1938, and the roles of regional commanders.
  6. Distinguish Japanese control of cities and rail lines from control of territory, and explain the strategic problem this posed.
  7. Compare the Nationalist, Communist, and collaborationist political projects and their claims to legitimacy.
  8. Assess the institutional causes and consequences of Japanese counterinsurgency violence against civilians.
  9. Evaluate the effects of bombing, displacement, inflation, and famine on Chinese civilian life, distinguishing Japanese military actions from Chinese administrative failures.
  10. Explain how the global war after 1941 changed the conditions of the war in China, and why Operation Ichi-Gō did not achieve decisive victory.
  11. Evaluate competing explanations for Japan's failure to secure a political victory in China.
  12. Evaluate primary sources for their creators, purposes, and limits.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How did a local clash near Beiping become a war lasting eight years, and who made the decisions that escalated it?
  2. Why did Chiang Kai-shek commit his best forces at Shanghai, and how did that decision change both sides' expectations?
  3. What does the Nanjing Massacre show about the Japanese army's organization, discipline, and treatment of prisoners and civilians, and how do historians reason from incomplete evidence?
  4. Why could Japan take Chinese cities but not Chinese resistance?
  5. In what ways did Nationalists, Communists, regional commanders, and collaborationist leaders differ in their strategies and their claims to represent China?
  6. How did violence against civilians arise from the structure of occupation and counterinsurgency, and what limits did evidence place on our knowledge?
  7. How did the world war beyond China alter the war within it, and how did Chinese resistance in turn affect the wider war?
  8. Why did Japan's military succeed operationally in 1944 yet fail to reach a political settlement, and what do competing historical interpretations emphasize?

Historical Context

Japan's position on the Asian continent in 1937 rested on a series of gains and claims. After the Russo-Japanese War (Chapter 61) Japan held the Kwantung Leased Territory and the South Manchuria Railway zone; after annexation in 1910 it ruled Korea (Chapter 62); and after the Manchurian Incident of 1931 (Chapter 74) the Kwantung Army created Manchukuo, a nominally independent state under Japanese control (Chapter 75). The Boxer Protocol of 1901 had allowed foreign powers to station troops along the railway between Beiping and the sea, and Japan maintained a China Garrison Army at Tianjin and in the Beiping area. Between 1933 and 1935 Japanese military agreements with local Chinese authorities, notably the Tanggu Truce and the He–Umezu understanding, pushed Chinese troops and Nationalist party organs out of much of Hebei, and Japanese sponsorship of autonomous regimes in eastern Hebei and Chahar raised the prospect that North China might be detached from Nanjing's control.

China's situation was equally unsettled. The Nationalist government in Nanjing, led by Chiang Kai-shek after the Northern Expedition of 1926–1928, controlled the lower Yangtze region and the economic heartland but depended on regional militarists whose cooperation was conditional, and it had spent the early 1930s fighting the Communists in Jiangxi and pursuing them during the Long March of 1934–1935. Chiang's policy of "internal pacification before external resistance" drew increasing criticism from students, intellectuals, and some of his own generals. The Chinese army was large but unevenly trained and equipped; a small group of divisions had received German training and equipment, while many units answered to regional commanders and lacked heavy weapons. China had a very small air force, little domestic heavy industry, and a railway and road network that was thin outside the east.

Japan's own decision-making structure contributed to the crisis. As Chapters 74 and 75 explained, the army had acquired practical independence from civilian oversight, and junior and mid-level officers in the field repeatedly acted without or against explicit instructions. Within the General Staff in Tokyo, officers such as Ishiwara Kanji, who had helped plan the Manchurian takeover, argued that Japan should avoid a major China war and prepare for confrontation with the Soviet Union, while others believed that a decisive blow would force Nanjing to accept Japanese terms. This disagreement mattered at the critical moment in July 1937. The Japanese government, led by Prime Minister Konoe Fumimaro since June 1937, had no unified strategy for China, and the army's operational bodies repeatedly outran the political leadership.

Finally, the international environment offered China and Japan different possibilities. The Soviet Union saw Japan as a strategic rival and was willing to supply China; Germany had both military advisers in China and a growing relationship with Japan, formalized in the Anti-Comintern Pact of 1936; the United States and Britain protested Japanese actions but were unwilling to risk war to stop them. The international concessions in Shanghai and Tianjin created zones in which Western and Japanese interests intersected and in which events would be observed by foreign journalists, diplomats, and missionaries whose reports became crucial evidence.

Section 76.1 · Division I — From the Marco Polo Bridge to the Fall of Nanjing, 1937

Japan and China on the Eve of Full-Scale War

By the winter of 1936 the question was not whether China and Japan would clash but where, when, and on whose terms. Japanese influence in North China rested on the garrison at Tianjin and Beiping, on the demilitarization of eastern Hebei after the 1933 Tanggu Truce, and on Japanese-sponsored local regimes. In November 1935 an East Hebei Anti-Communist Autonomous Council was proclaimed under Japanese protection, and the Nanjing government responded by creating the Hebei–Chahar Political Council as a buffer under General Song Zheyuan, whose 29th Army would be the force confronting the Japanese at Beiping in 1937. Smuggling of Japanese goods through eastern Hebei undermined Chinese customs revenue. In November 1936 forces from Manchukuo and Inner Mongolia attacked Suiyuan, and the defeat of that offensive by the forces of Fu Zuoyi at Bailingmiao was celebrated across China as the first clear Chinese military success against Japanese-backed forces in years.

The political landscape inside China also shifted. After the Long March, the Communist Party had established its base in northern Shaanxi, with Yan'an becoming its headquarters, and the Nationalist army was ordered to continue the campaign against it. Many commanders, particularly the Manchurian troops of Zhang Xueliang, whose homeland had been taken by Japan in 1931, were reluctant. On December 12, 1936, Zhang and Yang Hucheng, commander of Shaanxi troops, detained Chiang Kai-shek at Xi'an and demanded an end to civil war and a united resistance to Japan. Zhou Enlai of the Communist Party joined the negotiations. Chiang was released on December 25 and returned to Nanjing; Zhang, who accompanied him, was placed under confinement that lasted for decades. The terms of the understanding reached at Xi'an remain partly obscure and were reconstructed by later participants with differing emphases. What is clear is that military pressure on the Communists eased, that talks toward cooperation began, and that Chiang's government could no longer postpone preparation for war without political cost.

The Japanese leadership did not read these events uniformly. Some army officers regarded the Chinese turn toward unity as an opportunity to strike before China became stronger; others, concerned about the Soviet Union, wanted to avoid a quagmire. Japanese claims that its actions in China were necessary to restore order, suppress communism, and protect Japanese residents and interests should be treated as claims by an interested actor, not as independent evidence. China's internal divisions were real, but the pressures that tied Chinese factions together in 1937 were chiefly Japanese pressure itself. In the first half of 1937, Nanjing was continuing military reorganization with German advisers, strengthening defenses along the lower Yangtze, and seeking economic and diplomatic support abroad. Neither government had decided on a general war in June 1937. Both had positioned forces and commitments so that a local incident could spiral.

One reason a crisis could become a general war was the difference between the armed forces' views of political time. Chinese officials regarded additional months of preparation as valuable: training officers, improving river defenses, purchasing equipment, and negotiating among provincial commanders all promised to make resistance less desperate. Japanese officers who favored coercion drew the opposite inference. Delay could make any later intervention more expensive, while their army's privileges in the north seemed to them exposed to an increasingly self-assertive Chinese state. These were strategic judgments, not immutable laws of international relations. Chiang also had reason to fear that another concession would discredit his government among students, urban nationalists, and officers. The Xi'an settlement did not erase the civil conflict; it altered the domestic political costs of continued accommodation. Historians must therefore examine changing incentives on both sides rather than presume that the July incident mechanically caused an unavoidable war.

Section 76.2 · Division I

The Marco Polo Bridge Incident, July 7–8, 1937

Lugouqiao, the Marco Polo Bridge, spans the Yongding River about fifteen kilometers southwest of Beiping and lay beside the walled town of Wanping. The bridge mattered because it controlled the rail junction that connected Beiping with the south; control of it would allow the Japanese garrison to cut the city's communications. Japanese units from the China Garrison Army, a force of roughly six thousand men at the time, regularly held exercises in the area, and Chinese troops of the 29th Army were stationed nearby. On the night of July 7, a Japanese company was conducting maneuvers when shots were fired toward it from the direction of Chinese positions. One soldier did not appear at roll call and was soon back with his unit. The Japanese regimental commander, Colonel Mutaguchi Renya, authorized force, and brief fighting occurred near Wanping in the early hours of July 8.

Who fired the first shots has never been conclusively determined. Chinese, Japanese, and later Western historians have considered several possibilities: a stray or provocative shot by Chinese soldiers; a shot from a third party seeking to provoke a clash, a theory advanced in Chinese Nationalist and Communist accounts and by Japanese suspicions of Communist agents; or an accidental exchange in darkness. No archival evidence has shown that the Japanese army or the Kwantung Army planned the incident in advance. The question must be kept separate from the Mukden incident of September 1931 (Chapter 74), when Kwantung Army officers demonstrably staged an explosion on the South Manchuria Railway as a pretext for conquest. Marco Polo Bridge is not a documented repeat of that plot. But uncertainty about the first shots does not diminish responsibility for what followed. The question that matters historically is why a night skirmish became a war, and that answer lies in the decisions of July 8–30.

Local commanders reached an initial understanding on July 11, with the Japanese garrison and 29th Army leadership agreeing to withdraw, apologize, and control provocateurs. Japan's own decisions undermined the settlement. On the same day, the Konoe cabinet announced the dispatch of reinforcements from Korea and Manchuria while expressing willingness to settle locally. The General Staff was divided: Ishiwara Kanji, head of the operations section, urged restraint, while officers in the Army Ministry and the China Garrison Army argued that China needed to be taught a lesson and that reinforcement would force Nanjing to concede. On July 17, Chiang Kai-shek, speaking at Lushan, declared that China would resist if the limits of its endurance were reached and that once war began there would be no room for compromise. That statement is often read as the point at which Chiang committed to a national war. Skirmishes continued at Langfang (July 25–26) and the Guang'anmen gate of Beiping (July 26). On July 27 Japanese forces issued an ultimatum and on July 28 launched a general offensive. Beiping fell on July 29 and Tianjin shortly after.

In the same days, at Tongzhou east of Beiping, units of the East Hebei Japanese-sponsored regime mutinied and killed a large number of Japanese and Korean civilians, an event that Japanese media used to inflame public opinion. The Japanese government called the fighting the "North China Incident" and then, from September, the "China Incident" (Shina jihen), avoiding a declaration of war. The choice was practical: a declared war would have triggered American neutrality legislation and restricted access to American oil, scrap iron, and credit on which Japan depended.

The chain of decisions was longer than the first exchange of gunfire. Local Chinese and Japanese officers could and did negotiate tactical disengagement, yet central statements and new troops continually altered the bargaining position. Orders issued in Tokyo did not always match what officers on the scene intended to do, and Chinese commanders feared that accepting a local settlement would only license another demand. Each apparent pause thus became an opportunity for reinforcement, public accusation, or an ultimatum. Political leaders also lacked a common definition of success: protecting Japanese citizens, punishing particular Chinese units, and securing predominance across Hebei were very different objectives. The distinction matters because a limited protective operation could be represented to the home public while broader military aims developed on the ground. The language of an 'incident' obscured the extent to which the decision to continue offensive operations converted a local conflict into interstate war.

Section 76.3 · Division I

The Expansion of War in North China

With Beiping and Tianjin in Japanese hands by the end of July, the army had accomplished the stated purpose of its operation, yet fighting spread rather than stopped. In August Japanese forces moved against the Chahar–Suiyuan border: the Nankou Pass was forced and Zhangjiakou (Kalgan) fell in late August. In August the Japanese created the North China Area Army under General Terauchi Hisaichi and began a southward advance along the Beiping–Hankou (Pinghan) and Tianjin–Pukou (Jinpu) railways. The advance followed the railways because armies of this era depended on them for supply. Baoding fell in September, Shijiazhuang in October. Chinese commanders resisted with local forces and some central divisions, but they were outgunned in artillery, armor, and air power, and lacked the means to hold open ground.

The campaign in Shanxi shows the complexity of Chinese resistance. Yan Xishan, the long-time governor of Shanxi, commanded provincial forces and cooperated, uneasily, with the Communist Eighth Route Army, which had been formed in August 1937 from the Red Army units in the northwest. On September 25 a division of the Eighth Route Army ambushed Japanese supply and support units near Pingxingguan. The action was small by the standards of the war, but it was celebrated at the time and later as the first Chinese field success against the Japanese in the north, and the Communist leadership used it to build a reputation as active resisters. The larger Battle of Xinkou (Sept.–Nov.), fought mainly by Nationalist and Yan Xishan's forces, ended in Chinese withdrawal, and Taiyuan fell on November 8. The Communist forces then spread into the countryside of Shanxi, Hebei, and Shandong, a decision that would transform their position in the war (Section 76.12).

The political consequences of territorial loss were profound. In December 1937 Japan sponsored a "Provisional Government of the Republic of China" in Beiping, headed by Wang Kemin, and a Mongol autonomous regime in the northwest under Prince Demchugdongrub. These were the first of the occupation administrations that Japan hoped would replace or pressure the Nationalist government (Section 76.11). The Japanese high command also learned something important about the war it had entered. Operating in a vast territory with an army built for a short, decisive campaign, it discovered that each captured city required garrison troops, that rail lines were vulnerable, and that Chinese forces withdrew to fight another day. These problems would become central after Shanghai changed the scale of the war.

Civilian experience along the northern transport corridors reveals the costs that a campaign map conceals. Railway junctions were often surrounded by villages whose fields, animals, carts, and grain were essential to competing armies. A retreat could destroy bridges and stations needed for harvest trade; an advance could bring requisitioning, arrests of suspected guerrillas, or flight from artillery and bombing. The Chinese 29th Army was neither simply an instrument of the Nanjing government nor an independent national army: its regional officers had negotiated their own survival amid pressure from Tokyo and orders from Chiang. The defeat of such forces altered who could collect taxes and police towns long after front lines moved elsewhere. In Shanxi, Nationalist and Communist cooperation against Japan existed alongside disputes about territory, supplies, and recruitment. These tensions make it misleading to treat the North China campaign as a contest between two perfectly centralized armies.

Section 76.4 · Division I

The Battle of Shanghai and the Failure of a Quick Victory

Shanghai was China's largest commercial city, the home of the International Settlement and the French Concession, the center of Chinese banking and light industry, and the site of a Japanese naval landing force and a large Japanese civilian community. It lay within reach of the Nationalist capital at Nanjing, roughly three hundred kilometers up the Yangtze. When the north was already burning, Chiang Kai-shek decided to open a second front where Japanese naval and ground superiority would be less decisive, where his best German-trained divisions could be committed, and where foreign observers would see what was happening. Historians continue to debate Chiang's motivations: to draw Japan into a battle of attrition, to bring Western powers into the conflict, to protect the Yangtze heartland, or to demonstrate national resolve. The evidence suggests that several of these considerations coexisted.

Fighting began on August 9 when a Japanese naval officer was killed at Hongqiao airfield and escalated into open battle on August 13. On August 14 Chinese aircraft attempting to bomb the Japanese flagship and positions mistakenly hit the crowded International Settlement, killing large numbers of civilians near the Cathay and Palace hotels and the Great World entertainment center. The fighting that followed lasted three months. Japanese warships offshore and aircraft supported landings and advances, while Chinese troops, including some of the best divisions in the army, defended the northern suburbs in Dachang, Luodian, and Baoshan in intense urban and rural combat. The defense of the Sihang Warehouse by a Chinese battalion in late October, observed from the safety of the International Settlement across Suzhou Creek, became a widely reported symbol of resistance.

The Japanese, who had expected a rapid victory, sent reinforcements until some two hundred thousand troops were committed; a new Shanghai Expeditionary Army under General Matsui Iwane was formed. On November 5 the Japanese 10th Army landed at Hangzhou Bay south of Shanghai, threatening to encircle the Chinese defenders, who withdrew in the following days with heavy loss. Estimates of casualties vary widely: Chinese losses are often placed in the range of two hundred thousand or more, including a disproportionate share of Chiang's best-trained officers and soldiers, and Japanese losses on the order of forty thousand, but these are approximate and depend on the source. The Nationalist government announced on November 20 that it would move its seat to Chongqing. The International Settlement, though surrounded, remained under foreign control, and the "solitary island" (gudao) status of the settlement lasted until December 1941.

The battle's importance lies less in who held what ground than in what both sides learned. For Japan, Shanghai ended the idea that China would collapse after the first blows. For China, it cost enormous manpower but also demonstrated resolve and drew attention abroad, even if no Western power intervened militarily. A Nine-Power Treaty conference at Brussels in November 1937 condemned Japan's actions without action, and the German ambassador Oskar Trautmann carried Japanese peace terms to Chiang in November and December. The terms hardened as Japanese forces advanced, and by January 1938 Konoe publicly announced that Japan would no longer deal with the Nationalist government, a statement that removed a diplomatic exit and later embarrassed Tokyo. Japan had made a settlement more difficult by defining its goals in terms of eliminating the government with which it would need to negotiate.

Command and logistics made the fighting exceptionally expensive. Chinese defenses relied on improvised field fortifications and strongpoints amid dense neighborhoods, but the Japanese could combine heavy naval artillery, air observation, and repeated amphibious reinforcement. Chinese soldiers fighting in ruined streets could withstand assaults that would have been impossible in open fields, while exposed units suffered severe casualties when ordered to counterattack. Casualty figures often aggregate killed, wounded, and missing, which is why a large total cannot be treated as a death toll. The Chinese decision to remain engaged also had a political cost: the troops trained during the years before 1937 were among the few formations capable of carrying out modern combined-arms operations, and their losses could not quickly be replaced. The subsequent retreat toward Nanjing was therefore a consequence not only of the Japanese landing but of three months of attrition that had depleted Chinese command staffs and communications.

Section 76.5 · Division I

The Fall of Nanjing and the Nanjing Massacre

After Shanghai, Japanese forces pursued the retreating Chinese armies toward Nanjing, a decision made at the level of the field army and accepted in Tokyo. The army's operational orders extended the campaign step by step, and the speed of the advance outpaced supply, so that troops lived off the land, a practice that in China meant requisition and plunder. Chiang left Nanjing in mid-November, and the city's defense was entrusted to General Tang Shengzhi, who had little time to organize it. By early December Japanese columns, nominally under General Matsui Iwane but with Prince Asaka Yasuhiko commanding the Shanghai Expeditionary Army, converged on the city. Matsui had fallen ill, and many historians have debated how much responsibility lay with the officers who gave and implemented the orders. Tang Shengzhi fled across the Yangtze on December 12, and the Chinese defense collapsed in confusion, with thousands of soldiers killed or drowned while trying to cross the river at Xiaguan. Japanese troops entered the city on December 13.

What followed is among the best-documented atrocities of the twentieth century. Over six weeks, Japanese soldiers killed large numbers of Chinese civilians and disarmed soldiers in Nanjing and the surrounding area, raped many thousands of women, and looted and burned large parts of the city. Prisoners who had surrendered or been captured were taken in groups to the riverbank and to execution sites. A notice circulated in the Japanese army in August 1937, as documented by Japanese historians, had advised against treating Chinese captives under the usual rules for prisoners, and the army lacked any system for holding the tens of thousands of men who surrendered; many were killed. Killings occurred outside the walls and in surrounding counties, including along the Yangtze at Mufushan and Straw Shoe Gorge.

A small group of foreign residents stayed in the city and created the Nanjing International Safety Zone in the western quarter, a demilitarized area for civilian refugees, under the chairmanship of John Rabe, a German businessman with the Siemens company who was also a member of the Nazi Party. Together with American missionaries and professors, among them Minnie Vautrin of Ginling College, Lewis Smythe, M. Searle Bates, and the surgeon Robert Wilson, the committee documented the violence, protested to the Japanese embassy, and sheltered perhaps two hundred thousand or more people. Not all of those in the zone were safe: Japanese soldiers entered it repeatedly, and men suspected of being soldiers were taken out. Ginling College sheltered many thousands of women. John Magee, an American Episcopal missionary, filmed events and filmed survivors, producing visual evidence that was smuggled out of China. Journalists including F. Tillman Durdin of the New York Times and Archibald Steele of the Chicago Daily News reported the events before leaving the city.

The evidence for Nanjing is of several kinds that reinforce each other: diaries and letters of foreign residents; records of the Safety Zone Committee; contemporary survey work by Smythe in early 1938; photographs and film; Chinese survivor testimony, including the large collection of recorded testimonies maintained by the USC Shoah Foundation; burial records compiled by Chinese charitable organizations; Japanese soldiers' diaries and postwar recollections; and the judgments of the postwar tribunals, the International Military Tribunal for the Far East in Tokyo and the Nanjing War Crimes Tribunal, which convicted Matsui Iwane (executed 1948) and Tani Hisao (executed 1947) of responsibility. Prince Asaka was never prosecuted, having been shielded as a member of the imperial family (Chapter 83).

The number of victims remains subject to dispute among serious historians, and the variation is largely explained by definitions. Estimates depend on whether one counts only the walled city or the surrounding counties, whether one counts only civilians or also disarmed soldiers, how long a period is considered, and what categories of evidence, such as burial records, are accepted. The Tokyo tribunal reached a figure above 200,000, the Nanjing tribunal over 300,000, which became the official Chinese figure; Japanese scholars such as Hata Ikuhiko have proposed far lower counts, in the range of forty thousand with a stricter definition, while others, such as Kasahara Tokushi, have argued for well over one hundred thousand. These disagreements concern how many, not whether. Denial of the massacre, advanced by some political and commentator circles in Japan and elsewhere, is not supported by the documentary evidence, and professional historians in Japan, China, and the West do not treat it as an equally credible interpretation. Memory and textbook controversy concerning Nanjing belong to Chapter 79.

Nanjing also had effects on the course of the war. It did not bring about the capitulation that some Japanese officers had expected; Chiang's government had already moved and continued to fight. It damaged Japan's international reputation, particularly in the United States and Britain, where the reports of journalists and missionaries circulated widely, and it strengthened Chinese resolve even as it demonstrated the costs of occupation. Japanese leaders in Tokyo were aware, in outline, of reports of misconduct, yet there was no systematic effort to punish those responsible or change the conditions that had produced the violence. The army's lack of preparation for governing captured territory, its treatment of prisoners, its logistical dependence on plunder, and a culture in which brutality toward Chinese was tolerated all contributed to the pattern.

Section 76.6 · Division II — Chinese Resistance and the Campaigns of 1938

Chiang Kai-shek, the Nationalist Government, and the Strategy of Prolonged Resistance

After Shanghai and Nanjing, the question for Chiang Kai-shek was how to keep fighting while losing the coastal and Yangtze cities on which the government's revenue and prestige depended. The answer, adopted in practice rather than through a single announcement, was a strategy of prolonged resistance: trade space for time, avoid decisive battles that would destroy the army, impose costs on Japanese advance, and wait for international circumstances to change. The Nationalist government announced in November 1937 that it would move to Chongqing in Sichuan, protected by mountains and the Yangtze gorges. Through 1938 the military headquarters and much of the political apparatus operated at Wuhan, where leaders of several parties, including Communist representatives such as Zhou Enlai, worked in the same city for the last time during the war. After Wuhan fell, Chongqing became the wartime capital.

The relocation of people and industry was one of the war's most remarkable undertakings. Factories, arsenals, universities, hospitals, and government offices moved by river, rail, road, and on foot from the lower Yangtze to Sichuan, Yunnan, Guizhou, and Shaanxi. Equipment from hundreds of enterprises was dismantled and shipped upstream, and the shipping company of Lu Zuofu was prominent in moving people and machinery through the Three Gorges bottleneck at Yichang in the autumn of 1938. The scale of what survived should not be exaggerated: most industrial capacity remained on the coast and was lost, and the interior economy was agrarian and poorly connected. But the relocation gave the government a base of manufacture, however modest, and it brought millions of educated and skilled people into the interior, altering regional societies.

The strategy depended on political arrangements as well as geography. Chiang held the formal post of chairman of the Military Affairs Commission but did not command all Chinese armies directly. Regional commanders such as Li Zongren and Bai Chongxi of Guangxi, Yan Xishan of Shanxi, and the leaders of Yunnan, Sichuan, and the northwest contributed troops and kept their own power bases. Chiang sought to centralize command while avoiding open rupture. He also had to rely on foreign help: Soviet aircraft, pilots, and credits after the Sino-Soviet Non-Aggression Pact of August 1937, German advisers until Germany withdrew them in mid-1938 under pressure from its Japanese partner, and later American and British support. The Nationalist government's capacity was also limited by an inefficient tax system, a reliance on conscription that was often brutal and corrupt, and the loss of customs and coastal revenue. Chiang's critics, from Communists to American advisers, would later charge that he preserved forces for the postwar struggle with the Communists, a charge his defenders contested by pointing to the number of Chinese soldiers killed in the Nationalist army. The question is part of the larger debate discussed at the end of the chapter.

Chiang's strategy deserves to be understood on its own terms. Preserving the army and the government was not equivalent to passivity: it required decisions about where to fight, where to retreat, and how many men to sacrifice. Chinese forces fought major battles in 1938 not because they expected to win but because they needed to prevent Japan from reaching its objectives quickly and to show foreign powers that China could not be removed from the board.

Prolonged resistance demanded a fiscal state capable of operating away from the coastal economic centers it had previously taxed. Customs revenues, maritime shipping, and the industrial cities were disrupted by occupation and blockade, while the treasury still needed to pay soldiers, civil servants, refugees, and transport contractors. The government relied more heavily on borrowing, credit creation, and eventually inflationary finance. These measures kept institutions alive but transferred the costs of war to salary earners and consumers as prices rose. Provincial authorities sometimes controlled food, transport, or recruitment in ways that constrained central direction. Strategic withdrawal therefore preserved a political center but did not automatically preserve the resources that had supported it. The contrast between the military necessity of retreat and the civilian burden of financing an interior government is a central theme of China's wartime history.

Section 76.7 · Division II

The Xuzhou Campaign and the Chinese Victory at Tai'erzhuang

Xuzhou, in northern Jiangsu, was a railway junction where the Tianjin–Pukou line met the east–west Longhai railway. Japanese forces in North China and the Yangtze region were separated by the Yellow River and the lower Huai, and linking them required taking Xuzhou. In early 1938 the Japanese advanced from both north and south toward the junction, while the Chinese Fifth War Area under General Li Zongren, with forces drawn from Guangxi, Sichuan, and central armies, prepared to defend it. Li Zongren, a regional leader who cooperated with the central government after 1937, commanded a mixed force with varying levels of equipment and training.

At Tai'erzhuang, a town on the Grand Canal north of Xuzhou, the Japanese 10th Division under Isogai Rensuke advanced in March and found itself drawn into fighting house by house. Chinese forces under commanders including Sun Lianzhong and Tang Enbo, with units shifted by Li Zongren, counterattacked and encircled part of the Japanese force. After fighting from late March to early April, the Japanese withdrew from the town and Chinese forces claimed a major victory, celebrated across the country and abroad. Casualty figures are uncertain. Chinese claims of very large Japanese losses are widely regarded as exaggerated, while Japanese sources understate their setback; both sides lost heavily, and careful estimates place Japanese losses in the thousands.

Tai'erzhuang's significance requires careful assessment. It was a real tactical Chinese victory, achieved by tenacious defense and by well-timed counterattack, and it mattered for morale and diplomacy: it showed that Japanese forces could be defeated, that Chinese forces under unified direction could coordinate, and that the war would not be a walkover. But it did not reverse the invasion. Japan reinforced and concentrated forces around Xuzhou, and Li Zongren, facing encirclement, withdrew his armies in May in a successful operation that preserved most of his force. Xuzhou fell on May 19, and the Japanese forces from north and south linked up. The campaign illustrates a distinction central to this chapter: tactical victory is not strategic reversal. The Chinese retained their army but lost the junction; the Japanese gained their objective but at a high cost and without destroying Chinese forces.

The campaign illustrates why operational geography and command matter more than a simple scorecard of victories. The Japanese advancing from Shandong and the south did not always coordinate their schedules or supply arrangements, while Chinese commanders tried to exploit the gap. Li Zongren's Fifth War Area concentrated units from different regions and used local knowledge of canals, walled villages, and roads to isolate Japanese spearheads near Tai'erzhuang. The fighting was intense precisely because the attacking forces had advanced far enough to become vulnerable without achieving control of the entire district. Yet the Chinese success did not permanently reverse Japan's matériel advantage or restore the north–south rail connection. When the Japanese regrouped, Chinese units faced the choice of holding Xuzhou at the risk of encirclement or abandoning it to preserve an army for the next campaign. The retreat was a rational response to that larger imbalance, even as the earlier victory continued to matter politically.

Section 76.8 · Division II

The Yellow River Flood of 1938: Military Strategy and Civilian Catastrophe

After Xuzhou, the Japanese turned toward Zhengzhou, the junction of the Pinghan and Longhai railways, and Wuhan beyond. By early June the Japanese took Kaifeng, and Chinese commanders feared that Zhengzhou would fall and open the road to central China. At this moment the Nationalist leadership decided to break the dikes of the Yellow River. Earlier attempts at Zhaokou failed to produce the intended effect. In the first week of June the dikes were cut near Huayuankou, about twenty kilometers north of Zhengzhou, under the direction of military commanders and engineers acting on orders that, according to the historical record, came from Chiang Kai-shek.

The river, which flows above the surrounding plain for much of its lower course, burst through and spread across the Henan, Anhui, and Jiangsu plains, shifting into the channels of the Huai River system. The flood slowed the Japanese advance toward Zhengzhou and Wuhan by some weeks, and it drowned some Japanese units, though the military effect was limited and temporary. The human cost was immense. Estimates of deaths range from several hundred thousand up to nearly nine hundred thousand, and millions of people were displaced, with some accounts counting upwards of ten million affected. Villages, farmland, irrigation works, and crops were destroyed; large areas were turned into marsh or covered with silt, and the river did not return to its former course until the dikes were repaired after the war, in 1947. The ecological and social consequences, including salinization, disease, and the disruption of agriculture, extended for years and shaped the conditions in which the Henan famine of 1942–1943 occurred (Section 76.14).

The responsibility for the flood has been presented in different ways. At first the Nationalist government blamed Japanese bombing, and only later was the decision acknowledged. Chinese authorities did not warn the local population in advance, in part to preserve secrecy. The Japanese used the flood in propaganda, accusing Chiang of destroying his own people, and Nationalist censors suppressed discussion. Historians such as Micah Muscolino have used environmental and local records to reconstruct the event, its aftermath, and the way both the Nationalist and Japanese occupation authorities, and later the Communists, treated the flooded region. The episode must not be reduced either to a heroic act of resistance or to simple atrocity: it was a deliberate strategic decision by the Chinese government that imposed catastrophic costs on its own civilians, taken in a war the Japanese invasion had imposed. Both observations are necessary. The flood also shows how war operated through landscape and infrastructure, not only through battles.

The landscape did not return to its prewar condition when the first water receded. The breach diverted the Yellow River into much of the Huai River drainage, producing a shifting inundation zone across three provinces until engineers restored the old course in 1947. Villagers who survived initial drowning could lose fields to sediment or persistent waterlogging, be forced onto higher ground, or move repeatedly as embankments failed. Refugee registration, crop loss, deaths from disease, and deaths during subsequent famine were recorded through different administrative methods and timeframes. Micah Muscolino's historical reconstruction, drawing on a postwar damage-and-relief study, discusses more than 800,000 deaths over the wider 1938–1947 disaster, not deaths occurring on the day of the breach. That distinction is indispensable whenever a textbook presents a single casualty figure. Local communities also disputed where new dikes should run, since protecting one district could send floodwaters into another. Military engineering became an enduring contest over land, food, and survival.

Section 76.9 · Division II

The Battles for Wuhan and Guangzhou: The End of Rapid Conquest

Wuhan, the tri-city complex of Wuchang, Hankou, and Hanyang on the Yangtze, was the largest inland urban and industrial center and the political heart of Chinese resistance in 1938. Japan launched its largest offensive to date from the summer, advancing along the Yangtze and from the north along the Dabie mountains and Poyang Lake, with a force of some three hundred thousand troops supported by naval and air power, against Chinese armies numbering about a million in some accounts. The Chinese defense involved months of fighting in difficult terrain, including battles at Madang on the Yangtze and in the Dabie region, and used the summer heat, malaria, and floods as part of the defense. Japanese forces suffered heavy casualties, and Japan used chemical weapons in various operations, a practice documented in Japanese archival evidence by historians such as Yoshimi Yoshiaki and Matsuno Seiya.

In October the Japanese took Guangzhou (Canton) almost without resistance after landing at Bias Bay, cutting the main supply line from Hong Kong to the Nationalist government. Wuhan fell on October 25–27, as Chinese forces withdrew after the government had already largely evacuated. On leaving Changsha in November, Chinese authorities set fire to the city in a confused attempt at scorched earth; the fire, spread by miscommunication, killed many civilians and destroyed much of the city. Chiang ordered officials punished. The episode was another reminder that scorched-earth tactics could hurt civilians as much as the enemy.

The fall of Wuhan and Guangzhou marked a turning point. Japan had captured the main cities of eastern and central China and the major railways, yet China had not surrendered. The Japanese army had approached the limits of its offensive capacity in the existing theater: its logistics were stretched, its supply lines vulnerable, its manpower committed. In November 1938 Prime Minister Konoe announced a "New Order in East Asia," replacing earlier language of destroying Nationalist China with a vision of cooperation among Japan, Manchukuo, and China; in December he made a further statement that left the door open to a peace with a Chinese government willing to cooperate. These political shifts were intended to attract Chinese figures to Japan's side. The most important result was Wang Jingwei's departure from Chongqing in December (Section 76.11). The war thus moved from a sequence of large offensives to a prolonged struggle in which the Japanese held the cities and the Chinese held the interior and much of the countryside.

Wuhan's defense was not a single siege but a dispersed series of riverine and overland campaigns. Chinese troops held successive positions around the Yangtze approaches, the Dabie range, and the north shore of Poyang Lake while the Japanese assembled naval, air, and infantry forces to overcome them. In the fighting around Wanjialing, Chinese units succeeded in inflicting severe losses on a Japanese division, demonstrating the capacity for effective counterattack even as the broader defensive position became untenable. River transport, weather, and disease constrained operations on both sides; armies could not advance indefinitely merely by holding the nearest city. Chinese commanders also evaluated whether preserving units for the interior outweighed the symbolic value of urban defense. Japan's occupation of Guangzhou and Wuhan had major strategic consequences, but the survival of the Nationalist command and armies converted what Japanese planners had called victory into the beginning of another phase of war.

Section 76.10 · Division III — Occupation, Collaboration, Resistance, and Civilian Survival

Japanese Occupation: Cities, Railways, Resources, and the Limits of Control

Historians often describe Japanese control in China in terms of "points and lines": the army held points, the major cities and garrison towns, and lines, the railways and major river routes that connected them, but rarely the surfaces between. Outside these, in the countryside, power was exercised by a changing mix of Chinese guerrillas, militias, local elites, bandits, Communist organizers, and occasional Japanese punitive expeditions. The number of Japanese troops in China proper ranged in most years from roughly six hundred thousand to over eight hundred thousand, together with Manchukuo forces and collaborationist troops, a very large commitment that nevertheless could not garrison a country of this size. The army's organization into area armies and expeditionary armies, the China Expeditionary Army after 1939, and many smaller garrisons made coordination difficult, and rivalry between army and navy and between Tokyo and the field complicated policy.

The occupation authorities tried to build a system that would make the war pay for itself. Japanese state-backed companies, the North China Development Company and the Central China Promotion Company, established in 1938, coordinated investment in coal, iron, salt, cotton, transport, and electric power; Japanese textile firms resumed operations in Shanghai and Qingdao; and occupation-sponsored banks issued currency to displace the Nationalist fabi. The army requisitioned grain and labor and used military scrip. Scholars such as Parks Coble have studied how Chinese businessmen responded: some cooperated, some fled, some negotiated, and many adapted to occupation regimes that required licenses and permissions. The results disappointed Japanese planners. Resource extraction was hampered by guerrilla sabotage, damaged infrastructure, shortage of equipment, and a Chinese population with little reason to cooperate. Inflation spread in the occupied zones.

The experience of Chinese living under occupation varied by place and time. Cities such as Beiping, Shanghai, and Nanjing had gendarmes, checkpoints, and requirements to bow to sentries; black markets flourished; and educated people made hard choices about whether to teach in occupation-sponsored schools, work for the new administrations, or leave. The International Settlement in Shanghai offered a degree of protection until December 1941, when Japanese forces occupied it. In rural North China, villages could pay taxes to a Japanese-sponsored county government by day and to a Communist cadre by night. The word "control" thus requires care: Japanese forces could enter nearly anywhere they chose to send a column, but they could not remain everywhere, and this mismatch drove the escalation of violence described in Section 76.13.

Occupation operated through overlapping institutions rather than a single command that governed all Chinese territory uniformly. Japanese army headquarters, military police, consulates, development corporations, and Chinese local administrations possessed different claims to authority and competed over transport, tax revenues, labor, and supplies. A railway station might be secure only because a nearby garrison imposed curfews and searched travelers; the countryside a few kilometers away could be contested at night. The military's demand for grain and fodder raised costs for residents while disrupting the markets that occupation authorities said they wished to restore. An occupation regime could issue currency, sponsor schools, and appoint magistrates, yet still fail to secure the trust or obedience needed to govern without garrisons. Economically, the seizure of coalfields or factories did not guarantee usable output: mines required skilled labor, fuel, equipment, and access to transport routes vulnerable to sabotage.

Section 76.11 · Division III

Wang Jingwei, Collaborationist Governments, and the Politics of a Negotiated Peace

Japan tried several ways to turn military occupation into political settlement. It established regional regimes: the Provisional Government in Beiping (December 1937, under Wang Kemin), the Reformed Government in Nanjing (March 1938, under Liang Hongzhi), and Mengjiang in Inner Mongolia under Prince Demchugdongrub. These were weak, dependent on Japanese military advisers, and lacked prestige. What Japan needed was a figure with national standing who could claim to represent the Nationalist tradition and bargain with Tokyo. Wang Jingwei, long a rival of Chiang within the Nationalist Party and once Sun Yat-sen's close associate, seemed to fit.

Wang had become a leading advocate of peace within the Nationalist leadership. In December 1938 he left Chongqing for Hanoi and publicly endorsed negotiation with Japan on the basis of Konoe's declarations. An assassination attempt in Hanoi in March 1939 killed his aide instead. Wang moved to Shanghai, and in negotiations with Japanese representatives he sought terms that would preserve a degree of Chinese sovereignty. The terms he was offered, as revealed in January 1940 by two of his own negotiators who defected, were harsh and included Japanese troop stationing and economic privileges. Despite this, on March 30, 1940, the "Reorganized National Government" was formally established in Nanjing, using the Nationalist flag and claiming to be the legitimate continuation of Sun Yat-sen's Nationalism. Japan formally recognized it in a treaty of November 1940.

Wang's regime had real administrative functions and several hundred thousand troops at its peak, including many former Nationalist units, but its autonomy was limited, its finances depended on Japan and on the Central Reserve Bank notes issued from 1941, and its legitimacy was weak. It declared war on the United States and Britain in January 1943, when Japan returned some concessions nominally, and Wang died in Nagoya in November 1944 while undergoing medical treatment. The motives of collaborators varied: ideological conviction that peace and cooperation with Japan was better for China, anticommunism, opportunism, local self-protection, and coercion. Timothy Brook's study of collaboration shows how local elites in the Yangtze region negotiated with Japanese agents. After 1945 the Nationalist government prosecuted prominent collaborators as hanjian (traitors to the Chinese people), with trials that were uneven and often politically driven. The scholarly effort is to understand collaboration neither as simple betrayal nor as a legitimate alternative, but as a range of choices made under occupation.

The collaborationist experiment did not give Japan the political victory it sought. Chiang's government at Chongqing remained the internationally recognized government of China, supported by the United States and Britain. Japanese peace feelers to Chongqing continued, including contacts in 1940–1941 that foundered after the treaty with Wang's regime. Japan's investment in an alternative government made negotiation with Chiang harder, a problem that would recur.

Historical Vocabulary
Occupation, Collaboration, and Guerrilla War

Occupation in this war did not mean uniform territorial control. Japanese forces occupied cities and railways, while rural areas were contested or governed by Chinese authorities. Collaboration (in Chinese, hanjian is a charge of treason, not a neutral descriptor) covers a spectrum from political leaders of Japanese-sponsored regimes to clerks, merchants, policemen, and villagers who dealt with the occupiers for survival. Historians avoid using the word to imply a single motive. Guerrilla warfare refers to irregular operations by small units, militias, and local organizations that attacked supply lines, ambushed patrols, and relied on civilian support; it was practiced by Communist forces and also by Nationalist-affiliated units and local militias. Attrition refers to a strategy of wearing down an enemy through prolonged costs. Military control means the capacity to govern and extract resources reliably, not merely to move troops through an area.

These terms matter because the war in China cannot be described accurately as either a conventional war of fronts or a simple occupation. Japanese leaders spoke of the "China Incident," Chinese of the "War of Resistance," and later scholars of the Second Sino-Japanese War. The choice of name reflects differing views of the war's nature, and modern English categories such as "occupation" should be used with the understanding that the Japanese presence was partial and contested.

The language of collaboration needs to be used precisely. A senior official who voluntarily joined Wang's government, a policeman who remained at his post to avoid reprisals, and a merchant who sold supplies under military compulsion did not make equivalent choices. The degree of autonomy available to any official depended on Japanese military authorities, local social networks, access to money, and the capacity to protect families from violence. Wang argued that negotiation could preserve a Chinese political community and reduce suffering, but the concessions he accepted—especially regarding military presence and Manchukuo—undermined his claim to national representation. Some occupation institutions provided ordinary services, making the regime more than an empty façade, yet its dependence on Japan precluded an independent settlement. After 1945, accusations of collaboration often became instruments of political rivalry, and historians must examine actions and circumstances rather than repeat postwar labels uncritically.

Section 76.12 · Division III

The Second United Front, Communist Base Areas, and Guerrilla Resistance

The Second United Front, formalized in September 1937, was an agreement between the Nationalist government and the Communist Party to cooperate against Japan. In practice it meant that the Communists placed their armies nominally under the Nationalist military command, with the Red Army in the northwest reorganized as the Eighth Route Army under Zhu De, with Peng Dehuai as deputy commander, and the Communist forces in the south reorganized in the fall as the New Fourth Army under Ye Ting. The Communists kept their party organization, territory, and armies, and the Nationalists paid subsidies to the Eighth Route Army for a time. Both sides regarded the arrangement as tactical. The Communists wanted to expand while resisting Japan; the Nationalists wanted to use the Communists against Japan while limiting their growth. The front never became a merger.

Mao Zedong's strategy, laid out in lectures of 1938 on protracted war, argued that China would pass through stages: strategic defense, stalemate, and eventually counteroffensive, and that guerrilla warfare and mobilization of the peasantry would play a central role. In practice the Communists moved into the countryside of Shanxi, Hebei, Shandong, and elsewhere as Japanese main forces advanced down the railways, built local governments, organized militias, reduced rents in some areas, and recruited villagers. Base areas, such as the Shaan-Gan-Ning border region around Yan'an and the Jin-Cha-Ji and Taihang bases in the north, developed civil administration, taxation, and propaganda. Their growth was dramatic: the Party's membership, around forty thousand in 1937, grew to well over a million by 1945, and its armed forces to several hundred thousand regulars plus militia. Explanations differ. Chalmers Johnson famously argued that Communist success rested on peasant nationalism mobilized against the Japanese; later scholars, including Mark Selden and others, emphasized social revolution and land policy; and still others stress the role of coercion, organization, and Japanese violence in driving villagers toward the Party. These explanations are not mutually exclusive.

The largest Communist operation of the war was the Hundred Regiments Offensive, launched in August 1940 under Peng Dehuai, in which Communist forces attacked railways, bridges, and mines in North China across several provinces. It achieved considerable disruption and propaganda value, and historians commonly count it as the Communist forces' greatest military effort against Japan. It also provoked intense Japanese retaliation, discussed in Section 76.13, and in later years Mao's party leadership criticized aspects of the campaign. The relationship with the Nationalists also deteriorated. Nationalist forces blockaded Communist-held areas, and in January 1941, in the New Fourth Army Incident, Nationalist troops attacked units of the New Fourth Army moving through southern Anhui, killing or capturing many, including Ye Ting. The Nationalist government declared the army dissolved, while the Communists reorganized it. The incident exposed the fragility of the front, though neither side formally ended it, and it shaped American and Soviet perceptions.

It is important to distinguish the contributions and conflicts of the two movements. The Nationalist army fought the great majority of the large conventional battles and suffered the great majority of Chinese military casualties. The Communist forces fought fewer large battles but were the principal organized force in the Japanese rear, and their wartime growth positioned them for the postwar struggle. Each side later told the story of the war to its advantage: Nationalist histories emphasized their role in conventional resistance, Communist histories their leadership of a people's war. Modern scholars, including Hans van de Ven and Rana Mitter, treat both as important and critique each narrative.

The base areas were also experiments in governance. Communist organizers used village associations, taxation, rudimentary courts, political education, and military recruitment to sustain forces that could not rely on regular shipments of arms or money. Land-rent and interest reductions were generally easier to implement during the United Front than a wholesale redistribution that might alienate local property holders whose cooperation mattered. The capacity to extract food and manpower was therefore both the foundation of guerrilla endurance and a source of tension with peasants. Nationalist armies also maintained rear-area political systems and irregular networks; claims that only one Chinese movement resisted Japan flatten a complex military record. Conversely, official wartime Communist accounts frequently magnified the scope and success of some campaigns. Comparing battlefield records, local accounts, and later political narratives helps students understand both the actual fighting and the way wartime legitimacy was constructed.

Section 76.13 · Division III

Counterinsurgency, Collective Punishment, and the Violence of Occupation

Japanese forces could not hold the countryside, and guerrilla attacks on railways, outposts, and collaborators were frequent. From 1939 and especially after the Hundred Regiments Offensive, the army in North China under commanders such as Okamura Yasuji, who led the North China Area Army from 1941, adopted a program of pacification that combined military sweeps with attempts to isolate guerrillas from the population. Methods included the construction of blockhouses, trenches, and walls along railways and around areas; the concentration of villagers in controlled settlements; the registration and passes system for movement; destruction of crops and buildings in areas under Communist influence; and "mopping-up" (sōtō) campaigns by large forces.

In Chinese and much Western writing the most destructive practices are summarized as the "Three Alls" policy, meaning kill all, burn all, loot all. Japanese records use other terms, including "annihilation operations" (jinmetsu sakusen) and "pacification," and scholars debate whether a single formal policy existed under this name. The practices themselves are documented in Japanese and Chinese sources: villages burned in retaliation for attacks, suspected guerrillas and civilians killed, prisoners executed, grain and livestock seized, and men taken for labor. The violence was not random but was produced by several features of the Japanese army's situation: small garrisons surrounded by hostile territory, an intelligence system that could not distinguish guerrillas from villagers, a doctrine that emphasized offensive action, orders that gave local commanders latitude, the accumulated brutalization of long campaigns, discrimination against Chinese as inferiors, and a legal and disciplinary system that rarely punished atrocity. Mass operations in central Hebei in 1941–1942 and other regions cut Communist-controlled populations and territory in some areas and brought great civilian suffering. Chinese estimates of civilian dead in these campaigns are large and uncertain.

Other forms of violence accompanied these operations. Chemical weapons were used in various operations, and Japanese units, including special research units, conducted biological attacks in areas of central China in the early 1940s. These subjects, together with forced labor and the system of sexual slavery, are examined in Chapter 79. The point here is that violence against civilians in the China war was not an aberration confined to a few episodes but arose from the structure of the war itself, as Japanese forces sought to compensate for political failure with force. The consequence was a vicious circle: violence drove civilians toward guerrillas and made collaboration harder to sustain, while the guerrillas' persistence called forth further violence. Historians distinguish between orders from above, local initiative, and the permissive environment, and the evidence suggests that all three operated, with the high command both ordering harsh pacification and tolerating its excesses.

Japanese commanders described many operations as searches for guerrillas, protection of railway lines, or restoration of order. Their practices could nevertheless become indiscriminate when units treated villages as collectively responsible for attacks carried out nearby. Grain seizures and forced movement reduced a community's capacity to support resistance, but they also deprived civilians of food, shelter, and the ability to farm. Guarded settlements and so-called pacification campaigns sometimes separated rural families from land and livestock, requiring them to travel through checkpoints to work. The result was not an uncomplicated suppression of guerrilla warfare: displacement and coercion could increase resentment, drive people into alternative networks, and oblige an occupying army to patrol still more territory. Responsibility must be established through unit orders, incidents, testimonies, and command practices, while regional variation prevents attributing every recorded act to a single centrally issued policy at every date.

Section 76.14 · Division III

Refugees, Chongqing Bombing, Famine, and Civilian Life during the War

The war uprooted a vast part of the Chinese population. Scholars such as Diana Lary have estimated that tens of millions of people left their homes during the war, with some estimates ranging as high as eighty million or more, though counts are approximate and depend on definitions. Some fled temporarily and returned; others moved permanently to the interior or to the foreign concessions. Refugees traveled by boat, rail, cart, and on foot; they included students and professors moving universities, factory workers moving machinery, officials and families of the government, and ordinary villagers fleeing fighting. Many died of disease, hunger, exposure, and bombing along the way, and the arrival of large numbers of outsiders strained local resources and sometimes produced hostility between natives and newcomers.

Japanese air power turned cities into targets. The bombing of Shanghai, Nanjing, Guangzhou, and other cities began in 1937, and from 1938 to 1943 the Japanese army and navy air forces conducted a sustained bombing campaign against Chongqing. The raids of May 3–4, 1939 killed several thousand people and destroyed much of the city center. In June 1941, thousands sheltering in a large tunnel shelter were trapped during a long raid, and many suffocated, with commonly cited figures on the order of a thousand deaths. Chongqing's fog in winter limited raids, and the city's population, swelled by refugees to more than a million, built shelters in the rock, rebuilt, and carried on. The bombing aimed to break Chinese will to resist and to destroy government and industry; it did neither decisively. Japan's wartime messaging, preserved in JACAR records, presented the bombing as military necessity.

The wartime economy imposed hardship through inflation, shortages, and taxation. The Nationalist government financed the war largely by printing money, and prices in Free China rose to hundreds of times their prewar level by 1945. Salaried workers, teachers, and soldiers suffered most, while speculators profited. Conscription was widely abused, with men marched long distances under poor conditions, and grain requisitions strained farmers. The most terrible domestic disaster was the Henan famine of 1942–1943. Drought and locusts destroyed crops in 1942, in a province already affected by the Yellow River flood and fighting along the front. The government continued to collect taxes in grain and requisition for armies, relief was slow and inadequate, and Japanese forces and Chinese armies both drew on scarce food. Deaths are commonly estimated at between one and a half and three million, with millions more fleeing. Reporting by the Chinese journalist Zhang Gaofeng, suppressed by censors, and by the American correspondent Theodore White helped force the government to act. The famine involved natural disaster, military requisition, and government failure, and attributing it entirely to one cause would distort the record.

Yet people adapted. Women entered factories, schools, and relief organizations; children were enrolled in refugee schools; local charities, church groups, and foreign relief organizations fed and housed the displaced; peasants hid grain, paid multiple authorities, and negotiated survival. Chinese society during the war was not only a victim of events but the setting of continuous improvisation, and this resilience is part of why Chinese resistance continued.

Everyday Life and Environment

A household from the lower Yangtze might include a school teacher, his wife, their children, and an elderly parent. As Japanese forces advanced, the family sold or abandoned most of their belongings, took what they could carry, and traveled by river steamer to Wuhan, then, after Wuhan came under threat, to Yichang and up the Three Gorges toward Sichuan. Boat tickets were scarce and expensive, and at Yichang crowds waited for days. The family's savings were eroded by rising prices. The teacher might find work at a relocated school or university, where classes met in temples, huts, or caves and where students and staff shared hardship and poor diets.

In Chongqing the family would find crowded housing, rising rents, and regular air raids that sent them to shelters carved into the rock. Food prices would rise year after year. The wife might sew, teach, or take factory work; the children would attend a refugee school; and the elderly parent might not survive the journey. This experience suggests that the war touched the lives of educated urban families through displacement and economic decline, while rural families experienced it through conscription, requisition, and violence. The experiences of rural Henan households in 1942 or of villagers in Hebei under Japanese sweeps would be different and in many cases far more severe.

The famine in Henan makes visible the interaction of slow destruction and sudden crisis. Years of flooding and war had weakened reserves before drought and locust infestation damaged harvests in 1942. Both Japanese and Nationalist forces requisitioned grain, and the Nationalist government's military tax demands meant that food could continue leaving districts where villagers were already selling tools and clothing to survive. Rising prices, disrupted transport, and hoarding further separated those with cash from those without it. Specialist work by Micah Muscolino, discussed in the scholarly literature, places Henan famine mortality around 1.5–2 million, with additional millions fleeing; other published estimates use broader boundaries. A death estimate is not a census of bodies identified, and the mechanisms of hunger cannot be understood by assigning the whole catastrophe to rainfall alone. Relief, when available, was constrained by transport capacity, corruption, military priorities, and inconsistent information reaching officials.

Section 76.15 · Division IV — Prolonged Continental War and Japan's Failure to Defeat China, 1939–1945

The War of Attrition, 1939–1941: Campaigns, Blockades, and Failed Peace Strategies

After the fall of Wuhan the war changed in character. Large Japanese offensives were replaced by limited operations, blockade, bombing, and counterinsurgency, while the Chinese army, rebuilding, launched local counterattacks. Japan took Hainan Island in February 1939 and cut off Guangdong and Guangxi ports; it captured Nanchang in March 1939 and advanced toward Changsha in the First Battle of Changsha in September–October 1939, from which it withdrew. In late 1939 Japanese forces landed in Guangxi and took Nanning, but a Chinese counterattack at the Kunlun Pass in December 1939 and January 1940 inflicted a notable defeat. Chinese forces conducted a winter offensive in 1939–1940 that achieved limited gains and demonstrated that they could take the initiative, though at heavy cost. In 1940 the Japanese took Yichang, the gateway to the Three Gorges, in the spring, and General Zhang Zizhong was killed in the fighting.

The strategic situation shaped both sides' choices. Japan sought to close China's supply routes: it occupied northern French Indochina in September 1940 (with the agreement of the Vichy authorities) to cut the railway to Yunnan, and it pressed Britain to close the Burma Road, which Britain did for three months in the summer of 1940. At the same time, war in Europe diverted Western attention. Japan's 1939 border war with the Soviet Union at Nomonhan on the Manchurian–Mongolian frontier ended in a severe defeat for the Kwantung Army, and the Soviet–Japanese Neutrality Pact of April 1941 stabilized Japan's northern flank while reducing Soviet incentives to supply China. Japanese strategic planning increasingly turned to the south, where oil and resources lay, a shift that connected the China war to the origins of the Pacific War (Chapter 78).

Politically, Japanese leaders sought a settlement through various channels. Konoe Fumimaro resigned in January 1939, was followed by cabinets under Hiranuma, Abe, and Yonai, and returned in July 1940 for a second term, during which Japan signed the Tripartite Pact with Germany and Italy. Secret contacts with representatives of Chongqing, including the effort known as the Kiri operation in 1940–1941, failed to produce agreement. The Japanese demand for troop stationing, economic concessions, and recognition of Manchukuo, together with the recognition of Wang Jingwei's regime in November 1940, left little room for compromise. Chiang believed that time favored China as the international situation evolved. Japan's costs were substantial: more than half of its army's divisions were committed to China in these years, the economy was increasingly mobilized, and the war consumed resources that might have been used elsewhere. The point of the whole exercise, a decisive political result, remained out of reach.

After 1938 the war's center of gravity dispersed rather than disappeared. Major Japanese armies remained on the Yangtze approaches and at railway junctions, while Chinese armies defended positions around Changsha, the upper Yangtze, and the southwestern routes. The first battle for Changsha in 1939 and subsequent battles in 1941 illustrate a pattern of attacks, defensive withdrawals, counterattacks, and retreats in which taking a town did not necessarily confer sustainable control of its hinterland. Both armies also measured success politically: a Chinese ability to hold or retake a regional center could lift morale, while Japanese commanders sought operations that would justify continued sacrifice and secure vulnerable communications. Neither strategy removed the basic problem of governing a territory far larger than the forces available to police it.

Efforts to end the conflict diplomatically were repeatedly obstructed by incompatible demands. Japanese leaders required acceptance of Manchukuo, security arrangements in North China, and limits on Chinese independence that Chiang could scarcely endorse without destroying his political standing. Chinese officials hoped that international pressure, continued resistance, or a later change in the global balance might strengthen their position. The Konoe government's declarations shifted between publicly excluding Chiang from negotiation and suggesting a new Asian political order under Japanese influence. Covert contacts did occur, but an army invested in occupation and a Chinese government claiming national resistance could not easily find a settlement both could present as legitimate. The absence of agreement was therefore not simply a failure of communication; it reflected incompatible political goals reinforced by continuing military violence.

Section 76.16 · Division IV

China and the Global War: International Assistance and the Burma Road, 1941–1942

China's international support before 1941 came first from the Soviet Union, which provided aircraft, pilots, advisers, and credits from 1937 until about 1941, and then increasingly from the United States and Britain. The United States provided loans and, in 1941, extended Lend-Lease aid to China. The Burma Road, a highway of more than a thousand kilometers from Lashio in Burma to Kunming, built largely by hand by hundreds of thousands of Chinese laborers and opened in late 1938, became the principal route for supplies after the coast was blockaded. In 1941 the American Volunteer Group, known as the Flying Tigers, organized by Claire Chennault, began operations from Kunming and Burma; its first combat came in December 1941.

The Japanese attack on Pearl Harbor and on British and Dutch possessions in December 1941 (Chapter 78) transformed China's position. China declared war on Japan, Germany, and Italy formally on December 9, 1941, and became part of the Allied coalition, with Chiang named supreme commander of the China theater. In early 1942 Japan invaded Burma, took Rangoon in March and Lashio at the end of April, and cut the Burma Road. A Chinese Expeditionary Force entered Burma to assist the British but was driven back, with some units retreating into India and others crossing mountains into China at severe cost; General Joseph Stilwell, who was Chiang's chief of staff and commander of American forces in the theater, walked out with part of the force. With the road cut, supplies could reach China only by air over the Himalayas, the route known as the Hump.

This chapter treats the global war insofar as it affected China. Allied strategy gave priority to defeating Germany and, in the Pacific, to naval and island campaigns; China received limited resources, and relations between Chiang and American officers, especially Stilwell, were strained by disagreements over strategy, command, and the use of aid. Yet China's continued resistance tied down a very large number of Japanese troops, estimated at over a million in China and Manchuria combined, and that was strategically significant. In January 1943 the United States and Britain relinquished extraterritorial rights in China, a symbolic recognition of its status as an ally, and in November 1943 Chiang met Roosevelt and Churchill at Cairo, a meeting which produced a declaration on Japanese territorial losses. The Allies did not, however, supply China at the level that its needs, or its government's expectations, demanded.

The war's supply routes reveal the practical meaning of China becoming an Allied power. Soviet military assistance had provided aircraft, pilots, and matériel before the German invasion of the Soviet Union sharply altered Moscow's priorities. Supplies traveling through British Burma passed over mountain roads with limited carrying capacity, and their availability depended on the shifting political and military relationship between Britain, China, and Japan. Japan's 1942 conquest of Burma cut the established overland route, forcing Allied commanders to fly cargo from airfields in India over the Himalayan approaches commonly called the Hump. That air bridge was difficult and dangerous, limited by monsoon weather, aircraft performance, maintenance, and competing theater demands. A claim that 'aid reached China' needs to specify whether the cargo was ammunition, fuel, medicine, or aircraft and where it could be delivered from the western terminal.

Cooperation was contentious because Allied commanders had different definitions of victory. Chiang prioritized keeping his armies intact and protecting the wartime capital; Joseph Stilwell wanted a reformed, more effective Chinese army and, especially, an offensive through northern Burma; Claire Chennault advocated an air strategy supported by Chinese airfields. British leaders were concerned with Burma and India, while American strategic planners placed China within a much larger war against Japan. The tension between training soldiers, reopening communications, and supplying combat aircraft was not merely personal. It was a dispute over scarce planes, transport capacity, fuel, and political authority. Chinese resistance had continued for years before the United States entered the war, and Allied assistance strengthened it without transforming China's army into a single uniform force.

Section 76.17 · Division IV

The Continuing War, 1942–1943: Air Power, Supply, and Chinese Military Strains

After April 1942 the war in China was fought at lower intensity, but it remained deadly. The Doolittle Raid of April 18, 1942, in which American bombers launched from a carrier landed in China after striking Japan, provoked the Zhejiang–Jiangxi Campaign in May–September 1942, in which Japanese forces destroyed airfields and carried out reprisals against civilians in areas where the crews had been helped. A Republic of China Ministry of National Defense campaign summary reports approximately 250,000 Chinese soldiers and civilians killed during the campaign; that official combined-loss figure should not be presented as a verified count of civilians executed, and its underlying accounting requires scrutiny. In 1943 fighting centered on Hubei and Hunan: in May and June, Chinese forces defended the approaches to Chongqing in western Hubei, and in November–December the Battle of Changde saw a Chinese division under Yu Chengwan fight a prolonged defense of the city, which the Japanese took briefly and then abandoned.

American air power grew. Chennault's Fourteenth Air Force, formed in March 1943, struck Japanese shipping and bases, and supply over the Hump, which began in 1942 and expanded steadily, became the lifeline of the American and Chinese effort, though the tonnage in 1942–1943 remained modest. The Chinese army faced serious structural problems: poor pay and food for soldiers, disease, harsh conscription, corruption in procurement, an officer corps that often protected its own units, and dependence on limited supplies. Stilwell's frustration with Chiang's strategy and the quality of Chinese command led him to press for reorganization and for the training of Chinese divisions in India, and he argued for the use of Communist forces, positions that Chiang resisted. The Nationalist government in turn feared that American arms provided to local commanders or the Communists would undermine its authority. Inflation, famine in Henan, and war-weariness weakened the government's standing.

Chinese forces nonetheless continued to fight. They sustained heavy casualties in defensive actions, kept large Japanese forces tied down, and controlled much of the southwest and west. The point must be made explicitly, because wartime American commentary and later popular histories sometimes ignored it: China's continued fighting after 1941 was a significant component of Allied strategy, even if it did not produce spectacular victories. At the same time, the Nationalist government's weaknesses were real, and they provided the context for the crisis of 1944.

The 1943 fighting at Changde shows why the later war must not be dismissed as quiet. Japanese troops attacked the city in Hunan in November as part of a campaign along the central Yangtze approaches; Chinese defenders fought in and around the urban area, suffered grievous casualties, and counterattacked with reinforcements. Accounts differ on exact troop strengths and losses, but there is no doubt that the battle was extremely destructive and did not produce a permanent Japanese breakthrough. Civilians were caught between bombardment, forced evacuation, pillage, and disrupted food supply. The Chinese ability to continue such battles depended on the availability of local grain, replacements, and functioning roads, all of which were becoming more difficult to sustain. The war of attrition therefore persisted in recurrent high-intensity campaigns despite long periods without major changes to national front lines.

The 1942 Zhejiang–Jiangxi operation also reveals the interaction between global and local war. Japan targeted airfields associated with potential American use after the Doolittle Raid, while the broader campaign inflicted violence on communities that had little connection to the American raid. A figure of 250,000 Chinese soldiers and civilians killed appears in a Republic of China Ministry of National Defense summary of the campaign; it is a source-attributed wartime-loss claim, not a universally established count of civilians deliberately executed. Research should distinguish military deaths, atrocities, disease, and deaths caused by displacement, and note when an estimate comes from a particular government compilation. The campaign also demonstrates how punitive operations aimed at deterrence could devastate farming regions yet fail to stop the Allied air threat from developing elsewhere.

Section 76.18 · Division IV

Operation Ichi-Gō, 1944: Japan's Last Great Continental Offensive

By early 1944 Japan was losing the Pacific War, and American submarines were destroying its shipping. Japanese planners in Tokyo approved a large offensive in China with several purposes: to open an overland rail corridor from northern China to French Indochina, which would provide a land route if sea lanes were cut; to capture or destroy the American air bases in southern China from which bombers threatened Japan and shipping; and to damage the Chinese armies. China-based B-29 bombers began striking Japan in June 1944 from the more distant Chengdu area, where Chinese laborers had built large airfields; those western installations were distinct from the forward airfields targeted by Ichi-Gō. The offensive, known to the Japanese as Ichi-Gō ("Operation Number One") and to the Chinese as the Henan–Hunan–Guangxi campaign, was the largest Japanese operation of the entire war in China.

It began in April 1944 with a campaign through Henan to open the Pinghan railway. Roughly half a million Japanese troops, with tens of thousands of horses, large numbers of vehicles, artillery, and tanks, took part in the full operation. Chinese forces in Henan, under Tang Enbo and others, collapsed; Zhengzhou fell in April and Luoyang in May. Contemporary reports, including those of Theodore White, described Chinese soldiers abandoned by their commanders and in some cases attacked by local peasants angered by requisition and mistreatment, though the extent of such attacks is debated. In May the Japanese attacked in Hunan: Changsha fell in June, and the defense of Hengyang by General Fang Xianjue's 10th Army lasted forty-seven days before the city fell on August 8. In the autumn Japanese forces pushed into Guangxi, taking Guilin and Liuzhou in November and Nanning later that month, and in December linking up with Japanese forces advancing from Indochina. American airfields at Guilin, Liuzhou, and elsewhere were lost, and the Fourteenth Air Force had to withdraw to bases farther west.

The campaign was a stunning operational success for Japan: it captured large areas, several provincial capitals, and key airfields, and it opened the corridor. It was also a disaster for the Nationalist government's prestige, and it further strained relations with the United States. Stilwell's demand to be given direct command of all Chinese forces led to a break with Chiang, and Roosevelt recalled him in October 1944; General Albert Wedemeyer replaced him. Chinese forces, including divisions transferred from Burma by American air transport, stabilized the line in Guizhou after a Japanese advance toward Dushan in December caused panic in Chongqing. Chinese military casualties are often estimated in the hundreds of thousands, and millions of civilians were displaced again.

Yet Ichi-Gō did not produce decisive victory. The Chinese army was not destroyed and the Nationalist government remained in Chongqing. The railway corridor carried little use because American air power attacked it, supplies were short, and the war at sea made the connection with Southeast Asia largely irrelevant. American B-29 operations shifted to the Mariana Islands, which were closer to Japan, so the air base targets had less strategic importance than Japan had hoped. The offensive had also stretched Japanese forces in China, leaving them thin in areas where Communist forces expanded. The operation illustrates the theme of the chapter in its sharpest form: the greatest Japanese military success in China failed to deliver a political result.

The military plan was executed in successive geographically distinct phases, and their separation helps explain both Japanese success and strategic limits. In Henan the campaign sought to secure the north–south Peiping–Hankou railway; in Hunan the drive toward Changsha and Hengyang attacked Chinese defensive positions and forward airfields; farther south the invasion of Guangxi threatened Guilin and Liuzhou and sought links toward Indochina. Such resistance did not prevent the breakthrough, but it complicates any claim that Chinese armies simply evaporated. The extent of occupied territory increased at the same time that Japanese units were becoming dependent on exposed roads and railways and lacked the resources to exploit the conquest indefinitely.

A key distinction concerns aircraft and geography. U.S. Fourteenth Air Force aircraft operating from forward Chinese fields threatened Japanese shipping and regional bases, and those eastern and southern airfields were explicit targets. It also describes the operation's limits: neither Chongqing nor the Hump terminal at Kunming was an objective. Reaching a railway junction was thus an operational result, not evidence of the destruction of Chinese or Allied capacity to wage war.

Section 76.19 · Division IV

The War in China in 1945: Military Exhaustion, Chinese Resistance, and the End of Japanese Occupation

In 1945 the Japanese army in China remained large, more than a million men in China proper, but the strategic position had collapsed elsewhere. The Pacific War was moving toward the home islands, Burma had been largely retaken, and the overland road from India to China, the Ledo Road, opened in early 1945 after Chinese and American forces fought through northern Burma and Yunnan. Air transport over the Hump reached its peak tonnage in mid-1945. Japanese forces in China withdrew from parts of Guangxi in the spring, and Chinese forces retook Nanning on May 27, Liuzhou on June 29, and Guilin on July 27. In April–June 1945 Japanese forces attempted an offensive in western Hunan toward Zhijiang, an airbase area, and were defeated by Chinese forces supported by American air power, in what was the last major battle of the war in China. Japan's supply system, overstretched and attacked from the air, could not sustain the occupation indefinitely.

China survived as an Allied power and, at the war's end, became one of the five permanent members of the United Nations Security Council. Japan's surrender in August 1945, following the atomic bombings (Chapter 80) and the Soviet entry into war against Japan and invasion of Manchuria, ended the fighting; Okamura Yasuji, commander of the China Expeditionary Army, signed a formal surrender to Chinese representatives at Nanjing on September 9. The details of surrender and repatriation belong to Chapter 81. What is important here is that the defeat of Japan did not resolve the situation in China. Both the Nationalists and the Communists claimed the right to accept the surrender of Japanese forces; Chiang ordered Japanese units to surrender only to Nationalist authorities, and American transport moved Nationalist troops to key cities, while Communist forces expanded into the countryside and, with Soviet occupation, into parts of Manchuria. The Nationalist government prosecuted collaborators, but also in some cases used former collaborationist forces to hold territory against the Communists.

The Chongqing negotiations of August–October 1945 between Chiang and Mao produced an agreement on paper, but full-scale civil war resumed in 1946 and ended in 1949 with the Communist victory. China had won the war against Japan in the sense that Japan was expelled, but at a staggering cost. Estimates of total Chinese deaths, military and civilian, range widely, commonly from fourteen to twenty million, and tens of millions had been displaced. The economy, the government's legitimacy, and large parts of the country had been devastated. For Japan, the war cost some four hundred thousand military dead in China by Japanese government counts, left a legacy of atrocity and hostility, and stood among the causes of the broader catastrophe examined in the chapters that follow.

A major 1945 operation in western Hunan, often called the Zhijiang or West Hunan campaign, showed that Japanese units could still attack yet faced stronger Chinese resistance with growing air support. Nationalist formations defended approaches to the airfield region and, with American assistance, repelled the offensive during the spring. Counterattacks in Guangxi then recovered Nanning on May 27, Liuzhou on June 29, and Guilin on July 27 according to a Republic of China campaign chronology. These dates matter because the recoveries were achieved by Chinese forces before the final Japanese surrender; they should not be absorbed into a narrative implying that territorial restoration occurred only after the atomic bombings or Soviet entry. Recovery was incomplete, however, and much occupied territory remained under Japanese authority until the broader war ended.

The surrender produced an immediate administrative and security crisis. Nationalist commanders were designated to receive Japanese surrender in China, but troops and officials were widely dispersed and lacked transport to assume control everywhere at once. Japanese officers were in some places ordered to preserve order pending handover rather than disarm on the spot. Communist forces, rural militias, and regional authorities also attempted to obtain weapons, control rail junctions, and claim liberated districts. The conflict over who would administer recovered territory was therefore inseparable from the military end of the occupation. In autumn 1945 the sudden end of Japanese rule did not restore the economic and social conditions that had existed in 1937: families were displaced, transport networks damaged, currency unstable, and political rivalry sharpened.

Section 76.20 · Division V — Historical Interpretation and Chapter Review

The War in China: Conquest, Resistance, Occupation, and Historical Synthesis

The central question of this chapter is why Japan's military achieved major territorial and operational victories in China while failing to secure a decisive political victory. The narrative gives part of the answer. Japan's initial escalation was driven by local commanders, a divided high command, and expectations that a sharp blow would produce a quick settlement. When China did not capitulate, Japan lacked a political plan beyond further military pressure, and its demands hardened as its forces advanced, making negotiation harder. Chinese strategy, whatever its costs, kept the government and a substantial army alive: Chiang withdrew inland, relocated industry, accepted foreign aid, and used both conventional and irregular forces. The Communists built bases in the occupation's gaps. Japanese occupation could hold cities and railways but not the countryside, and its efforts to build a collaborationist alternative lacked legitimacy. Japanese violence, from Nanjing to the counterinsurgency campaigns, generated resistance and did not suppress it. And the war's expansion into a global conflict changed China's international position while exposing the Nationalist government's own weaknesses.

The chapter has also shown the war as a social catastrophe. Civilians experienced bombing, flooding, occupation, forced requisition, famine, and displacement. They were victims of Japanese violence and also of decisions taken by their own governments. They were also actors, who fled, worked, resisted, collaborated, bargained, and survived. This human history is not a sidebar to the military narrative but part of the explanation for its outcome.

Multiple Perspectives

Five Views of the War, c. 1940

Note: these perspectives summarize documented positions in general terms; they do not quote individuals and do not imply equal power or equal responsibility.

Nationalist leadership at Chongqing: Preserve the government and army, outlast Japan, secure foreign aid, and avoid being drawn into a settlement that would legitimize Japanese gains. The leadership also feared Communist expansion and sought to control the pace of its growth.
Communist leadership at Yan'an: Resist Japan through mobilization of the countryside, expand territory and party organization under the cover of the United Front, and prepare for the postwar contest. Communists saw the war as an opportunity to build a political base.
Japanese military leaders: Many believed that the war had to be concluded on terms preserving Japan's gains, regarded Chinese resistance as the product of Chongqing's stubbornness and foreign aid, and responded to guerrilla resistance with greater force. They had no consensus on how to end the war.
Collaborationist officials in Nanjing: Some argued that peace with Japan would save China from further destruction and that cooperation could win concessions; others sought personal advantage or protection. Their dependence on Japanese power constrained them, and many Chinese regarded them as traitors.
Civilians in occupied and unoccupied China: Their priorities were survival, food, safety, and family. They faced bombs, requisition, conscription, and the demands of several authorities, and their responses ranged from flight to accommodation to resistance. Their experiences differed greatly by place, class, and gender.
Cause and Effect

From Expectations of Quick Victory to Prolonged Continental War

Long-term causes

Japanese continental expansion since 1895; the army's autonomy and the precedent of Manchuria; Chinese nationalism and partial unification under Nanjing; Japanese treaty rights in North China; the weakness of civilian control; Soviet–Japanese rivalry.

Short-term causes

The Xi'an Incident and Chinese political consolidation; the Marco Polo Bridge clash; divided Japanese leadership; reinforcement decisions in July 1937; Chiang's decision to fight at Shanghai.

Event or transformation

A local incident becomes a general war; Japan takes cities and railways but cannot force a settlement; China adopts prolonged resistance.

Immediate effects

Fall of Nanjing and the massacre; the Yellow River flood; relocation to Chongqing; collaborationist regimes; Communist base areas; large civilian displacement.

Long-term consequences and historical complexity

Japan's commitment to China consumed army resources, shaped its search for resources in the south, and contributed to war with the United States; China's survival as an ally shaped the postwar order, while wartime devastation, Nationalist weakness, and Communist growth shaped the civil war. No single cause explains the outcome: Japanese overconfidence, Chinese geography and resolve, foreign aid, Soviet and American policy, and the structure of occupation all interacted.

The War in China, 1937–1945

What Changed

Who experienced change most directly? Chinese in the path of campaigns, refugees, and villagers in contested areas. Who experienced greater continuity? Historical Debate

Why Could Japan Not Defeat China Decisively?

The question: Japan won most major battles yet could not end the war on its terms. Was this due mainly to Japanese strategic failure or to Chinese capacity and resistance?

Interpretation A — Japanese strategic and structural failure: Some historians emphasize that Japan lacked clear political aims, escalated without a plan, divided its efforts between continental and southern strategies, relied on an army built for short campaigns, and fueled resistance through violence. Edward Drea and others have stressed the army's logistical limits and institutional weaknesses.
Interpretation B — Chinese resilience and agency: Others emphasize that China's strategy of prolonged resistance, relocation, regional cooperation, and international diplomacy, along with Communist mobilization and extraordinary civilian sacrifice, made Japanese victory impossible. Rana Mitter and Hans van de Ven have highlighted Chinese state-building and adaptation under war conditions.

Evidence: Both draw on Japanese military records, Chinese government and military documents, foreign reports, and local sources. Scholars note that the explanations are complementary: Japanese weaknesses created opportunities that Chinese strategy exploited, and Chinese resilience exposed Japanese limits. The balance of emphasis, and the weight given to Allied aid and the global war, remain contested.

Why it matters: The answer shapes how historians view Chinese agency, the legacy of Nationalist and Communist roles, and the relationship between the China war and Japan's decision to fight the United States.

Student task: Identify two pieces of evidence from this chapter supporting Interpretation A and two supporting B, and explain whether the interpretations can be combined.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced the war between Japan and China from 1937 to 1945 and argued that it became a conflict Japan could neither decisively win nor easily end. Japanese armies took the major cities, railways, and ports of eastern and central China and, in 1944, opened a rail corridor to Indochina. Yet they never destroyed the Chinese government's capacity to resist, never controlled the countryside, and never secured a political settlement accepted by a Chinese authority with broad legitimacy.

The war's origins lay in a long-developing situation: Japanese treaty rights and military influence in North China, the occupation of Manchuria, a divided Japanese leadership in which field commands outran Tokyo, and a Chinese government that had begun to unify and, after the Xi'an Incident, to cooperate with the Communists. The Marco Polo Bridge Incident of July 1937 began as a local clash. The uncertainty about who fired first is real, and it differs from the demonstrated staging of the Mukden Incident in 1931. What turned the clash into war was a series of decisions in July: reinforcements, ultimatums, and a general offensive.

The campaigns of 1937 changed the scale of the war. Japan advanced along the railways of North China and met an unexpectedly determined defense at Shanghai, where Chiang committed his best divisions. The battle cost China enormously but ended Japanese hopes of a quick victory. The advance on Nanjing produced the Nanjing Massacre, in which Japanese troops killed large numbers of civilians and disarmed soldiers and committed widespread rape and looting. The event is documented by contemporary eyewitnesses, tribunal findings, survivors, and Japanese sources; estimates of the dead vary widely with definitions, but the event itself is not in doubt.

In 1938 China adopted a strategy of prolonged resistance. The government relocated to Chongqing, industry and universities moved inland, and Chinese forces won a notable tactical victory at Tai'erzhuang. The Nationalist decision to breach the Yellow River dikes slowed the Japanese advance but caused catastrophic civilian losses. The fall of Wuhan and Guangzhou in October marked the end of rapid conquest, and the war entered a long phase of attrition.

Occupation proved less powerful than conquest. Japan held points and lines but not the countryside. It built collaborationist governments, culminating in Wang Jingwei's regime of 1940, which lacked legitimacy and autonomy. The Second United Front between Nationalists and Communists was a limited accommodation; Communist forces built rural base areas and grew dramatically, while the New Fourth Army Incident of 1941 showed the front's fragility. Japanese counterinsurgency, with its blockhouses, sweeps, village destruction, reprisals, and requisition, generated violence against civilians and, in many areas, more resistance.

Civilians bore much of the war's weight. Tens of millions were displaced, Chongqing and other cities were bombed, inflation eroded the living standards of soldiers and salaried people, and the Henan famine of 1942–1943 killed millions through the combined effects of drought, requisition, taxation, war, and government failure. Yet Chinese society adapted through relocation, relief, improvisation, and resistance.

The global war after 1941 changed China's position. China became a formal Allied belligerent, received limited American and British assistance, and sustained its resistance through the Hump air route after the Burma Road was cut. Tensions between Chiang and American officers, especially Stilwell, reflected differences over strategy and the weaknesses of the Nationalist state. China's continued fighting tied down a large part of Japan's army.

Operation Ichi-Gō in 1944, Japan's largest offensive of the war in China, captured large territory and airfields and badly damaged Nationalist prestige, but did not destroy Chinese forces or end the war. Its logistical and strategic purposes were overtaken by Japan's defeats in the Pacific. Japan's surrender in August 1945 ended the fighting but left the Nationalist–Communist conflict unresolved.

Historians explain Japan's failure by pointing both to Japanese strategic weaknesses, including unclear political aims, a divided command, and violence that fueled resistance, and to Chinese resilience, including prolonged resistance, relocation, Communist mobilization, foreign aid, and civilian sacrifice. The explanations complement one another. The chapter's larger lesson is that military success can fail to produce political victory when the aims of war are unclear and when the occupied society refuses to accept defeat.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because the war in China was the central military fact of Japan's history between 1937 and 1945 and the setting in which the later Pacific War took shape. The decisions examined here, to escalate in July 1937, to advance on Nanjing, to refuse negotiation with the Nationalist government, to sponsor collaborationist regimes, and to launch Ichi-Gō, show a state that repeatedly used force to compensate for the absence of political strategy. Chapter 77 turns to the mobilization of Japanese society that sustained this war, and Chapter 78 to the confrontation with the United States and the European empires that grew out of it.

The chapter also reveals how war reshaped China. The Nationalist government survived but was weakened by inflation, famine, conscription abuses, and military setbacks; the Communist Party emerged with armies, bases, and administrative experience; and tens of millions of Chinese lived through displacement and violence. These outcomes shaped the civil war and the founding of the People's Republic. Understanding the Chinese side of the war is therefore indispensable to understanding both countries' histories.

The war connected the Japanese archipelago to the wider world in ways that persisted. Korean, Taiwanese, and Japanese soldiers, laborers, and settlers were drawn into it; Soviet, American, British, and German policies intersected with it; and its memory, especially of Nanjing, remains contested in East Asia. Chapter 79 examines forced labor, sexual slavery, and atrocity systematically, and the later chapters on the postwar period return to the questions of responsibility and memory that this war raised.

Finally, the chapter offers a lesson in historical method. Estimates of the dead in Nanjing, the Yellow River flood, and the Henan famine differ widely, but disagreement about numbers is not disagreement about whether the events occurred. Sources written by observers, eyewitnesses, and commanders each illuminate part of the picture, and none shows all of it. Students who learn to ask who created a source, why, and what it omits are prepared for the remaining chapters of the war and its aftermath.
