Part VI — Sengoku Japan and Reunification
41

Tokugawa Ieyasu, Sekigahara, and the Founding of a New Order

Japanese History: From Jōmon Japan to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

When Toyotomi Hideyoshi died in the autumn of 1598, he left behind the most extensive territorial settlement the archipelago had seen in more than a century, and an heir who was about five years old. Within seventeen years, that heir, his mother, and the castle that symbolized his family's rule would be gone, and a different house would hold the title of shōgun and direct a new political order from Edo.

It is tempting to tell this story as a straight line: Hideyoshi dies, Tokugawa Ieyasu wins the Battle of Sekigahara in 1600, and the Tokugawa shogunate begins. Each element of that sentence contains something true, and the sentence as a whole is misleading. Hideyoshi's arrangements for his succession were real institutions, not empty gestures, and Ieyasu spent the first two years after his death working largely inside them. Sekigahara, fought on 21 October 1600 (Keichō 5/9/15), was a decisive battle, but it was one episode in a nationwide war whose theaters ran from the Mogami and Date lands of the northeast to Kyushu. It did not end the Toyotomi house, did not make Ieyasu shōgun, and did not by itself create the system of domains, obligations, and controls that Chapter 42 will examine. The Tokugawa order emerged through a sequence: a contested succession, a breakdown of coalition, a civil war, a vast redistribution of land, a bid for formal legitimacy, a deliberate transfer of office to a second generation, and finally the destruction of the surviving Toyotomi alternative at Osaka in 1614–1615.

This chapter therefore studies a political transition rather than a biography or a single battle. It asks how a settlement built around one man's personal authority, sustained by oaths, hostages, land surveys, and a precarious balance among senior lords, came apart after his death; how one of those lords converted the resulting crisis into supremacy; and why supremacy still had to be turned into legitimacy, dynastic continuity, and territorial security. It follows Chapters 39 and 40, which traced how Oda Nobunaga broke the old order and how Hideyoshi completed unification and extended it into an invasion of Korea. It ends where Chapter 42 begins, at the point when the Tokugawa had defeated every credible rival, and the question shifts from how they won to how the resulting system actually worked.

The chapter also resists the retrospective clarity that later Tokugawa historiography encouraged. The familiar labels "Eastern Army" and "Western Army" are modern shorthand rather than the names of two permanent blocs; many commanders on both sides had served together under Hideyoshi in Korea, held Toyotomi titles, and believed, or claimed to believe, that they were defending Hideyori's interests. The Tokugawa later had every reason to present 1600 as a righteous restoration of order and Ieyasu as its inevitable architect. Historians must read the winners' records, including the compilations of the Tokugawa house, alongside letters, pledges, diaries, domain records, and the accounts of foreign observers, and must remember that the men of 1599 did not know what the men of 1616 would know.

Regional and marginal histories remain visible in this period even though they are not its center. The war touched villages along the Nakasendō and Tōkaidō highways that fed, housed, and were plundered by armies; it drew in the lords of northeastern Honshu and of Kyushu, who fought their own local wars under the banner of the larger conflict; and it unfolded while the Matsumae house in southern Ezo (Hokkaido) was securing its position as the intermediary with Ainu communities and while the Shimazu of Satsuma, which would invade the Ryukyu Kingdom within a decade, were bargaining for their survival. The wider world intruded too: in April 1600, six months before Sekigahara, a storm-battered Dutch ship, the Liefde, reached Kyushu, and its pilot, the Englishman William Adams, soon became an adviser to Ieyasu.

The central argument of the chapter is that the Tokugawa settlement was constructed in stages and that each stage solved one problem while creating the next. Sekigahara destroyed the coalition that opposed Ieyasu but left the Toyotomi heir in Osaka. Redistribution of domains reshaped the map but left powerful lords in place. The shogunal title of 1603 supplied legitimacy but did not by itself guarantee that the office would pass peacefully. Osaka in 1615 removed the last rival dynastic center, and only then could the Tokugawa begin to treat the conflicts of the previous half-century as closed.

The evidence for this period is unusually rich and unusually partisan. It includes letters in the hands of Ieyasu, Ishida Mitsunari, and other commanders; pledges and oaths; domain genealogies; court and temple diaries from Kyoto; folding screens depicting the battle that were commissioned long after it; and reports sent home by Jesuit missionaries and later by the Dutch and English. Each type has strengths and distortions, and this chapter will repeatedly stop to ask who produced a source, when, and for whom.

By the end of the chapter, students should understand 1598–1615 neither as the simple triumph of a patient strategist nor as a purely accidental clash of ambitions, but as a period in which structures inherited from Hideyoshi, the interests of dozens of lords, the movements of armies across a mountainous and road-dependent landscape, and a series of contingent decisions together produced a durable settlement, one that would shape the lives of villagers, warriors, merchants, outcasts, and frontier peoples for more than two and a half centuries.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why Hideyoshi's arrangements for a child heir were fragile and how they shaped the political competition of 1598–1600.
  2. Trace the sequence of events from Hideyoshi's death through the breakdown of the senior lords' balance in 1599 to the outbreak of war in 1600.
  3. Analyze the composition and motives of the coalitions of 1600 and explain why "Eastern" and "Western" armies are retrospective labels.
  4. Describe the campaigns leading to Sekigahara and the geography of the battlefield, using the roads, castles, and terrain that structured them.
  5. Assess the role of defection, hesitation, and non-participation in the outcome of Sekigahara, distinguishing documented facts from later anecdote.
  6. Evaluate the nationwide dimension of the war, including fighting in the northeast, central Honshu, and Kyushu.
  7. Explain how confiscation, reduction, confirmation, and reward of domains after 1600 transformed the political geography of the archipelago.
  8. Distinguish military supremacy in 1600 from legitimate shogunal rule after 1603 and from dynastic stability after 1605.
  9. Interpret the Osaka campaigns of 1614–1615 as the resolution of an unresolved dual-center problem rather than as a pretext-driven accident.
  10. Compare Toyotomi and early Tokugawa methods of rule, identifying what the Tokugawa inherited and what they changed.
  11. Evaluate competing interpretations of Sekigahara as a Tokugawa conquest or a civil war within the Toyotomi order.
  12. Evaluate sources produced by victors, including later Tokugawa compilations, and identify perspectives they omit.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did Hideyoshi's death open a political crisis, and which features of his own settlement made a smooth succession unlikely?
  2. To what extent was Ieyasu acting within the Toyotomi order between 1598 and 1600, and when did that stop being a plausible description?
  3. Why did the coalition of senior lords break down, and how much of the breakdown reflected personal rivalry rather than structural conflicts of interest?
  4. Why did so many commanders take the side they did in 1600, and what does their behavior reveal about loyalty, risk, and self-interest in this society?
  5. How should historians weigh the battlefield events at Sekigahara against the preparatory diplomacy, communications, and regional wars that surrounded them?
  6. How did the redistribution of domains after 1600 convert a military victory into a durable political geography?
  7. Why did Ieyasu take the shogunal title in 1603 and relinquish it in 1605, and what did these acts accomplish that victory alone had not?
  8. Why did the Toyotomi house at Osaka remain a problem for the Tokugawa for fifteen years after Sekigahara?
  9. How have later Tokugawa and modern narratives shaped what students "know" about 1600, and how can historians correct for that?

Historical Context

Chapter 39 traced Oda Nobunaga's destruction of rival warlords, armed religious communities, and the Ashikaga shogunate's remnants; Chapter 40 followed Toyotomi Hideyoshi, who inherited Nobunaga's project after Nobunaga's death in 1582, defeated Shibata Katsuie, brought Tokugawa Ieyasu into a negotiated subordinate relationship, and later defeated the Shimazu and Hōjō, and gave unification an administrative form. Hideyoshi's land surveys measured domains in koku of rice-equivalent output, his sword hunt and status regulations began separating warriors from cultivators, and his control of daimyō families rested on required oaths, hostages kept at his castle towns, and the power to reassign domains. He took the court titles of regent (kanpaku) and later retired as Taikō, a title that denoted a retired regent and that students should not confuse with a modern office. The invasions of Korea (1592–1593 and 1597–1598) strained the finances and the personal relationships of the daimyō who fought them and left behind bitter divisions between military commanders and the administrators who supervised the campaigns.

The settlement Hideyoshi left was archipelago-wide but personally dependent. Many great lords possessed regional bases and claims that predated Hideyoshi, but their position within the new archipelago-wide hierarchy, and often the precise territories and assessments they held, had been confirmed, transferred, or reshaped by him. This was a real change from the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, when regional powers had built their own legitimacy. But it also meant that nearly every arrangement rested on the person of the Taikō, and that when he died, the question of who would supply the missing authority was open. Hideyoshi's own late actions show that he understood this: in his last years, after the birth of his son Hideyori in 1593, he removed his adopted heir Hidetsugu (who was forced to commit suicide in 1595) and arranged a council of senior lords and a group of commissioners to govern on the child's behalf. Those arrangements, as the sources describe them, were more a set of overlapping expectations and oaths than a codified constitution, and historians describe them with caution.

Geography shaped the crisis as much as politics did. Hideyoshi's capital region around Osaka, Fushimi, and Kyoto lay in the Kinai basin. Ieyasu, whom Hideyoshi had transferred in 1590 from his old lands in Mikawa and neighboring provinces to the Kantō plain, was based at Edo, a modest castle town at the head of a large bay in what would become the greatest city of the archipelago. The routes between east and west funneled through a small number of mountain passes and river crossings, above all the Tōkaidō along the coast and the Nakasendō through the interior, and those routes met near the Ōmi–Mino border, where Sekigahara lies. Armies of the period could not move across the landscape at will. They depended on roads, rice supply, castles, and the cooperation of local lords, and these constraints are visible in every campaign of 1600.

Finally, this chapter overlaps in time with developments treated in other chapters. Christianity, which Chapter 37 examined, still had followers among lords such as Konishi Yukinaga; Chapter 38's tea culture still structured elite interactions; and the maritime and frontier relationships examined in Chapter 48 were developing as the political order changed. The detailed institutions of Tokugawa rule, including the domain classifications, the imperial-court regulations, the alternate-attendance system, and the bakuhan structure itself, belong to Chapter 42 and are only previewed here.

Section 41.1

After Hideyoshi: A Child Heir and a Fragile Settlement

Hideyoshi died at Fushimi Castle in the eighth month of Keichō 3 (September 1598), after a decline of several months. The sources agree that he spent his final weeks worrying about Hideyori's future, and that he sought oaths from the leading lords, written pledges that they would serve the boy. The death itself was initially concealed from the wider public. The Japanese armies still in Korea had to be withdrawn without the enemy learning that their commander had died, and the withdrawal, completed by the end of 1598, was supervised by the senior lords then in the capital region. The war that had begun in 1592 thus ended without a negotiated settlement and without any conquest, leaving the lords who had fought it with heavy losses, unpaid costs, and unresolved quarrels.

Hideyoshi's arrangements are traditionally described as a council of five senior lords (Gotairō) and five commissioners (Gobugyō). The five lords usually named are Tokugawa Ieyasu, Maeda Toshiie, Mōri Terumoto, Ukita Hideie, and Uesugi Kagekatsu, though the membership shifted as individuals died or were reassigned. The commissioners, led by Ishida Mitsunari, were administrators who handled the day-to-day work of the Toyotomi government. These terms should be handled with care. They are convenient labels, and some are applied by later writers to bodies that Hideyoshi's contemporaries described more loosely. What the sources show is a set of overlapping expectations: the senior lords were to protect Hideyori and uphold Hideyoshi's decisions; the commissioners were to administer; Maeda Toshiie was to live in Osaka Castle with Hideyori while Ieyasu remained at Fushimi to oversee national affairs.

The problem was that these arrangements assigned responsibility without assigning clear precedence. Hideyoshi had not created an institution that could decide disputes among the senior lords, and no one possessed the authority to settle disagreements among them by command. Ieyasu was the most senior in status and resources: he held the Kantō lands estimated at roughly 2.5 million koku, the largest domain in the archipelago, and a seasoned retainer corps. But Mōri Terumoto and Uesugi Kagekatsu each held around 1.2 million koku, and Maeda Toshiie, though smaller in domain, had been Hideyoshi's closest associate and was entrusted with the heir. The Toyotomi house itself held perhaps two million koku in direct holdings, together with gold and silver mines and the commercial wealth of Osaka and Sakai. This was an unusual structure of power: a child sat at the center, with several lords, any one of whom could plausibly challenge the others.

Historians have often written as though Ieyasu's challenge was a foregone conclusion. It was not. Many lords had reasons to support the Toyotomi order in 1598, among them the lands, titles, and standing they owed to it. Others had reasons to distrust Ieyasu, who had himself been a rival of Hideyoshi before submitting in 1586, and whose relocation to the Kantō in 1590 had been both a reward and a means of removing him from his old base. The political question of 1598 was not whether the Toyotomi would survive, but who would hold the settlement together, and on whose terms.

Section 41.2

Tokugawa Ieyasu Inside the Toyotomi Order

Born in 1543 into a minor lord's family in Mikawa, Ieyasu had spent his youth as a hostage of the Imagawa and then built his power as an ally of Oda Nobunaga. After Nobunaga's death he consolidated lands in Mikawa, Tōtōmi, Suruga, Kai, and Shinano, fought Hideyoshi to a stalemate at Komaki–Nagakute in 1584, and then submitted in 1586, receiving Hideyoshi's half-sister and later his mother as hostages in a marriage-and-hostage settlement. His position within the Toyotomi order was thus that of a former rival who had become a principal ally. In 1590, after the fall of Odawara, Hideyoshi transferred him to the eight Kantō provinces, a vast but unconsolidated territory that Ieyasu governed from Edo. The move removed him from his hereditary lands, but it also gave him an enormous new base and the resources to build a castle town from relatively little.

By 1598 Ieyasu held the court rank of Inner Minister (Naidaijin) and had the loyalty of a seasoned corps of retainers, many of them from his Mikawa days. Ieyasu was not sent to fight on the Korean peninsula. Instead he served at Hizen Nagoya as one of the senior lords managing and supporting the invasion, while his core forces avoided the prolonged overseas campaigning borne by many western daimyō, a circumstance that preserved his army and treasury while other lords exhausted theirs. This relative fortune is among the structural advantages that historians point to when explaining his later success. Others include his seniority, his rapport with some of the Korean-campaign generals, and a reputation, among some contemporaries, for reliability in keeping his word.

What Ieyasu did between 1598 and 1600 is best understood as the exercise of leadership within the Toyotomi framework, with an increasingly open interest in shaping it. He used his position at Fushimi to receive petitions and issue decisions, he promoted marriage alliances, and he bestowed lands and titles, all actions that the Taikō's arrangements did not clearly authorize but that Ieyasu could present as managing affairs for Hideyori. It would be wrong to portray him in 1598 as an openly declared destroyer of the Toyotomi. His statements and those of his allies repeatedly claimed fidelity to Hideyori, and his opponents' principal charge in 1600 was that he had betrayed the Taikō's wishes. The historian's task is to ask when these claims ceased to be credible, and for whom.

Historical Vocabulary
Koku (石)

A koku was a unit of volume, roughly 180 liters, traditionally regarded as the amount of rice needed to feed one person for a year. Hideyoshi's land surveys turned it into the standard measure of the assessed productive capacity of a domain, and daimyō status, military obligations, and rewards were expressed in koku. A domain of 10,000 koku or more conventionally defined a daimyō.

The figure was a political and administrative construct as well as an economic one. It was assessed output, not actual harvest, and it could include non-rice products converted into rice-equivalent. When this chapter says that Mōri Terumoto held about 1.2 million koku, the number indicates his formal rank and obligations, not a precise statement of his income. Students should therefore treat koku figures as measures of status and standing, comparable across lords, rather than as exact economic data.

Section 41.3

Marriage, Oaths, Rivalries, and the Politics of Coalition

In the first months of 1599, the senior lords and commissioners accused Ieyasu of violating Hideyoshi's wishes by arranging marriages between his family and those of several other lords, among them Date Masamune, Fukushima Masanori, and Hachisuka Iemasa, without the consent of the council. The accusation, based on the assumption that Hideyoshi had restricted such alliances, shows that the senior lords regarded marriage as a political instrument. Ieyasu answered with a combination of denial and justification, and a confrontation was resolved when the parties exchanged pledges. Historians read the episode in two ways. For some it shows that Ieyasu was already quietly assembling a coalition; for others it demonstrates that the other lords were quite capable of checking him, and that Ieyasu's methods had to adapt. Both readings can be partly true.

Behind such episodes lay the deeper rift between two kinds of Toyotomi servants. The military commanders who had fought in Korea, among them Katō Kiyomasa, Fukushima Masanori, Kuroda Nagamasa, and others, felt that the commissioners, especially Ishida Mitsunari, had misrepresented their conduct to Hideyoshi, undervalued their sacrifices, and distributed rewards unevenly. Mitsunari had served Hideyoshi as an administrator, organizing logistics and land surveys, and had enemies precisely because of the authority such work conferred. It is an oversimplification to explain 1600 as a war of personal hatreds. But personal quarrels gave structural conflicts a human shape: administrators who spoke for the central government clashed with commanders whose power rested on their domains and armies.

The coalition politics of 1599 also involved the court, Kyoto and Osaka merchants, and the major temples, all of whom needed to know who would protect their interests. Ieyasu's skill lay partly in understanding that he did not need to defeat every lord. He needed to assemble enough support, and discourage enough opposition, that his rivals would be unable to unite. Ishida Mitsunari's strength lay in different assets: he understood the Toyotomi administration, knew the details of every domain's obligations, and was closely connected to the Osaka circle around Yodo-dono. But he lacked a large domain of his own (he held about 190,000 koku at Sawayama) and thus depended on the support of greater lords.

Section 41.4

The Death of Maeda Toshiie and the Collapse of the Balance

Maeda Toshiie, who held Kaga, Noto, and Etchū in the northwest and had been among Hideyoshi's oldest comrades, died in the spring of 1599 (late April). His death mattered because he alone among the senior lords had the standing to restrain Ieyasu and the personal bond with Hideyori to represent the heir's interests in Osaka. His heir, Maeda Toshinaga, inherited the domain but not the stature, and he soon found himself under pressure. Contemporary accounts report an alleged plot against Ieyasu in the autumn of 1599, involving Maeda Toshinaga and others, which Ieyasu used to demand submission; the Maeda responded by sending Toshiie's widow, Hoshun-in, as a hostage to Edo. The details of the supposed plot are uncertain and some historians doubt that it was ever more than a rumor, but the outcome is clear: the largest domain in the northwest accepted Tokugawa supremacy without a fight.

Toshiie's death also precipitated an attack on Ishida Mitsunari. Seven military commanders, among them Fukushima Masanori, Katō Kiyomasa, and Kuroda Nagamasa, are reported to have planned to kill or seize him, and Mitsunari took refuge in Fushimi, where Ieyasu was in residence. Ieyasu's mediation preserved Mitsunari's life but required him to retire from administrative duties and withdraw to his domain at Sawayama. This was a victory for the commanders and a demonstration of Ieyasu's ability to arbitrate. Some historians see in the episode Ieyasu's deliberate removal of a rival; others emphasize that it reflected genuine pressure from the commanders that Ieyasu managed rather than caused.

By the autumn of 1599, Ieyasu had moved from Fushimi into Osaka Castle, taking up residence in its western compound, where he could oversee Hideyori's household directly. He was now acting from the Toyotomi center itself, and the other lords had to decide whether they accepted him there. Some, like Mōri Terumoto, remained in Osaka and held back; others, like Uesugi Kagekatsu, returned to their domains and began strengthening them. The balance that Hideyoshi had intended, a set of lords each limiting the others, no longer existed, and what replaced it was a polarization in which every major lord had to consider where he stood.

Section 41.5

The Uesugi Crisis and the Opening of War

In 1598, shortly before his death, Hideyoshi had ordered Uesugi Kagekatsu to relocate from Echigo to Aizu in the northeast, an assignment that increased his domain to about 1.2 million koku and placed him as a counterweight to Ieyasu's Kantō. Kagekatsu returned to Aizu in 1599, and his chief retainer Naoe Kanetsugu began strengthening castles and roads. Reports reached Ieyasu that Uesugi was building up military forces. In early 1600 Ieyasu demanded that Kagekatsu come to Kyoto to explain himself. The reply, associated with Naoe Kanetsugu and known as the Naoe Letter, is reported to have been defiant; scholars debate the letter's authenticity and exact text, and the passage should be treated as a tradition rather than a verified quotation. What is clear is that Uesugi did not come, and that Ieyasu used the refusal to justify a military expedition.

Ieyasu left the capital region in the summer of 1600, leading an army northeast, and was soon at Edo and then on the road north. The decision carried risk. By leaving the Osaka–Kyoto region he gave his opponents an opportunity, and Ishida Mitsunari, who had been in retirement at Sawayama, began mobilizing allies. Whether Ieyasu intended this, deliberately luring his enemies into the open, or merely accepted the risk of the expedition is debated. The sources are largely written by his supporters, who had reason to credit him with foresight. A cautious reading is that Ieyasu was willing to gamble on his ability to win a war wherever it started, and that the Uesugi expedition, whatever its original purpose, had the effect of provoking his opponents to commit themselves.

In the weeks after Ieyasu's departure, his opponents in the west issued a series of accusations against him, circulated as thirteen articles under the names of Toyotomi commissioners, charging that he had violated Hideyoshi's last testament. They brought Mōri Terumoto into Osaka Castle as nominal commander and began seizing Tokugawa-aligned lords' families, who remained in Osaka as hostages. An attempt to take the family of Hosokawa Tadaoki ended with the death of his wife, Gracia (Hosokawa Tama), a Christian convert, who is reported to have died rather than be captured; the incident hardened the position of other lords and made hostage-taking from their families harder. Meanwhile Ieyasu's forces, at Oyama in Shimotsuke, held a council in which, according to later accounts, the lords who had marched with him chose to follow him rather than return to the Toyotomi capital. The accounts of that council are among the most tradition-laden in the story, and students should treat the dramatic details with caution.

Section 41.6

Building the Eastern and Western Coalitions

The familiar labels "Eastern Army" and "Western Army" are modern shorthand. The lords who opposed Ieyasu in 1600 did not form a standing alliance with a unified command, and Ieyasu's own coalition included men who had been among Hideyoshi's closest followers. Nor did the two groupings map neatly onto geography. Mōri Terumoto, nominal head of the anti-Ieyasu coalition, ruled western Honshu; Ukita Hideie held Bizen and Mimasaka; Ōtani Yoshitsugu, Konishi Yukinaga, and Ankokuji Ekei, a Buddhist monk who was also a political agent of the Mōri, joined them. But Shimazu Yoshihiro joined with a small force and with unclear commitment, and Kobayakawa Hideaki, who commanded a large army, had ambiguous loyalties from the start.

Ieyasu's coalition included Fukushima Masanori, Kuroda Nagamasa, Hosokawa Tadaoki, Ikeda Terumasa, Katō Kiyomasa, Yamauchi Kazutoyo, and others who had served Hideyoshi, along with his own Tokugawa retainers and allies such as Date Masamune and Mogami Yoshiaki in the northeast. Their decision to follow him reflected a mixture of motives: antagonism toward Ishida Mitsunari, expectation of reward, calculations of who was likely to win, and in some cases genuine belief that Ieyasu represented stability. Many presented the war as a defense of Hideyori against evil advisers, a claim that Ieyasu himself encouraged. This is an important reason to avoid describing the conflict as a "Tokugawa versus Toyotomi" war. In 1600 many on both sides saw themselves as acting on behalf of the Toyotomi house.

The coalitions were also looser than the label "army" suggests. A significant number of lords did not fight at all, or fought only nominally, or waited to see how events developed. The western coalition especially suffered from a lack of unified command: Terumoto remained in Osaka, and the generals in the field answered to Mitsunari, who lacked the stature to command greater lords. Historians note that Mitsunari's pledges of support from several lords, including some who later defected or stayed out of battle, were obtained by persuasion rather than loyalty, and could not be relied on under pressure.

Section 41.7

War Before Sekigahara: Fushimi, Gifu, and the Roads to Mino

The first major military event of the war took place at Fushimi Castle, the Toyotomi castle south of Kyoto, which Ieyasu had left under the command of his veteran retainer Torii Mototada. A western force of tens of thousands, including Kobayakawa Hideaki, Shimazu Yoshihiro, and Ukita Hideie, besieged it in the late summer of 1600. Torii's garrison, outnumbered many times over, held out for about ten days before the castle fell in early September, and Mototada died there. Later Tokugawa tradition made this a model of retainer loyalty, and it is true that the resistance delayed the western army and bought Ieyasu time. It also illustrates the way the war was conducted: through the capture of castles that controlled roads and rivers, not through open-field battles alone.

Elsewhere, western forces besieged Tanabe Castle, held by Hosokawa Fujitaka (Yūsai), a celebrated poet and an expert in classical learning. The siege of Tanabe was eventually ended in mid-October, by a decision that emanated from the imperial court and obliged the attackers to stop, an unusual instance of the court using its authority to protect a scholar of the courtly tradition, and a reminder that the imperial court was still a political presence even though it had no armies. In Ōmi, Kyōgoku Takatsugu held Ōtsu Castle on the shore of Lake Biwa until, by tradition, it fell on the very day of Sekigahara, tying down thousands of western troops who might otherwise have been at the main battle.

Meanwhile Ieyasu's eastern allies, led by Fukushima Masanori and Ikeda Terumasa, who had been sent ahead from the Tōkaidō, assembled at Kiyosu in Owari, and in late September they captured Gifu Castle, held by Oda Hidenobu, a grandson of Nobunaga who had joined the west. The fall of Gifu opened the road to the Mino plain and put the eastern lords at the gateway to Ōgaki, which was held by Mitsunari's allies. Ieyasu himself left Edo in early October, taking the Tōkaidō, and arrived in the area of Akasaka, a few kilometers from Sekigahara, on 20 October. His heir Hidetada was meanwhile leading a large Tokugawa force by the Nakasendō, a route that took him through Shinano and into conflict with the Sanada at Ueda.

These weeks show how much of the war depended on information, timing, and logistics. Communication by messenger took days; commanders did not always know the position of their allies; and an army that outran its supplies or took the wrong road might be destroyed without a battle. Historians reconstructing these weeks rely on letters sent between commanders, which survive in considerable numbers, and on later narratives that tend to compress and dramatize the sequence.

Section 41.8

Sekigahara: The Battlefield, 1600

Sekigahara lies in the mountains of Mino Province (modern Gifu Prefecture) at a point where the Nakasendō, the Hokkoku road leading north, and the Ise road leading south converge on a roughly bowl-shaped plain a few kilometers across. A gate (seki) on the Fuwa barrier had controlled this passage since antiquity, and the location mattered because whoever held the plain blocked the main road between eastern and western Japan. The western commanders had originally planned to defend farther east, around Ōgaki Castle, but, according to the sources, learned that Ieyasu was moving toward Sawayama and Osaka and shifted position to Sekigahara to block him. They arranged themselves on the surrounding heights, with Ishida Mitsunari at Sasaoyama, Konishi Yukinaga, Ukita Hideie, and Shimazu Yoshihiro on lower ground, Ōtani Yoshitsugu to the west, and a group of other commanders on the heights around Matsuoyama and Nangūyama. From these positions, the western army, on paper, enclosed the plain.

Contemporary estimates of the forces vary widely, and the numbers given in later accounts, often 70,000–80,000 for each side, include contingents that did not fight. A reasonable reading is that tens of thousands on each side were present on the field, but that several thousand western troops on the heights did not engage. Fighting began in the early morning of 21 October amid heavy fog, when Ii Naomasa, a leading Tokugawa retainer, and Matsudaira Tadayoshi, Ieyasu's fourth son, led a group forward past the vanguard of Fukushima Masanori, an act traditionally described as a violation of the order of precedence that was meant to ensure Fukushima's forces took the first position. The combat that followed was chiefly fought by foot soldiers armed with spears and arquebuses, supported by mounted samurai, in confused fighting among the paddies and field boundaries. Ukita Hideie's force engaged Fukushima's for hours, while Ōtani Yoshitsugu's smaller force fought on the western flank.

The decisive turn came in the middle of the day, when Kobayakawa Hideaki, a young lord who commanded around 15,000 troops on Matsuoyama and who had earlier been in contact with Ieyasu, led his army down the slope and attacked Ōtani Yoshitsugu's force. Several other lords near him, including Wakisaka Yasuharu and Ogawa Suketada, followed. Ōtani's force, already engaged in front, was crushed from the side, and Yoshitsugu, reportedly disfigured by illness, died on the field. As the western left flank collapsed, Konishi Yukinaga's and Ukita Hideie's armies broke, and Ishida Mitsunari's troops fled. By afternoon the battle was over.

Kobayakawa Hideaki's defection has been central to the popular story, which presents it as a "betrayal" that single-handedly decided the day. The evidence supports a more nuanced reading. Kobayakawa had been in contact with Tokugawa representatives before the battle and appears to have hesitated, and a widely repeated story that Ieyasu ordered gunfire directed at Matsuoyama to force Kobayakawa's decision is treated by the local museum interpretation as one theory rather than settled fact. It is also important to note that other commanders, including Mōri Hidemoto on Nangūyama, Kikkawa Hiroie, Chōsokabe Morichika, and Natsuka Masaie, stayed out of the fight. Kikkawa Hiroie had, according to the sources, negotiated with the Tokugawa side in advance, a connection that mattered for the later treatment of the Mōri. The western army lost not simply because of one defection but because it was a coalition in which many members were unwilling to commit and in which no single leader could compel them.

Shimazu Yoshihiro's men, who had not engaged with the rest of the army, escaped by a famous breakout through the Tokugawa lines toward the Ise road, at great cost: only a small fraction of his force survived. The retreat has become a legendary episode and illustrates both Shimazu's independence from the western command and the severe losses even among those who withdrew successfully. The victors pursued the fugitives. Ishida Mitsunari was captured near Mount Ibuki a few days later; Konishi Yukinaga, who as a Christian would not commit suicide, and Ankokuji Ekei were also captured. The three were executed in Kyoto in early November.

Section 41.9

A Nationwide War, Not Just One Battlefield

Sekigahara was decided in a single day, but the conflict of 1600 stretched across the archipelago, and its outcomes were determined as much by what happened elsewhere as by the main battle. In Shinano, Sanada Masayuki held Ueda Castle with a small garrison against Tokugawa Hidetada's much larger army in September–October. Masayuki's son Nobushige (Yukimura) fought alongside him. The siege did not capture the castle, and Hidetada, ordered by his father to proceed to Mino, arrived days after the battle. The delay of the Tokugawa main reserve is one of the reasons Ieyasu fought at Sekigahara with the forces at hand, and it became a long-lasting source of embarrassment for Hidetada and, according to later tradition, of resentment on the Sanada side. Masayuki was nonetheless spared execution, partly because his elder son Nobuyuki had fought for Ieyasu, and he and Nobushige were exiled to Mount Kudo in Kii.

In the northeast, the Uesugi, whose conflict with Ieyasu had begun the war, did not stay idle. Uesugi forces under Naoe Kanetsugu attacked Mogami Yoshiaki's lands in Dewa, while Date Masamune, who had committed to Ieyasu, campaigned against the Uesugi in the Shiroishi area. The fighting continued for weeks, and Uesugi forces withdrew only after news of Sekigahara reached them. In Kyushu, Kuroda Yoshitaka (Jōsui), a distinguished former adviser of Hideyoshi and father of Kuroda Nagamasa, raised an army in Buzen and advanced into Bungo, defeating the Ōtomo, who had been restored by the western side, while Katō Kiyomasa, on Ieyasu's side, attacked the territories of the Christian lord Konishi Yukinaga in southern Higo. The Shimazu of Satsuma, though Yoshihiro had been at Sekigahara, kept the main forces of the domain at home, and Kyushu became a theater in which local lords pursued their own territorial ambitions under the cover of larger loyalties.

The nationwide character of the war has several implications. First, it shows that the political divisions of 1600 ran through every region, and that local rivalries, the desire to expand holdings, and fear of neighbors shaped alignments as much as devotion to Ieyasu or the Toyotomi. Second, it reveals why the aftermath required such wide-ranging action: lords who had fought on the wrong side were distributed across the map, as were those who had sat on the fence. Third, it demonstrates that no single battle could have settled the matter; the decisive effect of Sekigahara was partly psychological, in that it ended the western coalition's will to continue and led most lords to submit before further fighting could occur. Mōri Terumoto, who had remained in Osaka Castle with a large army, withdrew within days, and by the end of the year the war was effectively over.

Frontier regions were affected in less direct but important ways. In southern Ezo, the Matsumae house, which had been confirmed by Hideyoshi as the authority over trade with the Ainu, cultivated Ieyasu's favor, and in 1604 it received a document from him that affirmed its trade privileges; this, and the long arc of Wajin–Ainu relations that followed, belongs to Chapter 48. In southern Kyushu, the Shimazu, having retained their domain, would within a decade invade the Ryukyu Kingdom, a story that also belongs to Chapter 48. The point here is that the Tokugawa settlement of 1600–1603 shaped the position of domains at the edges of the archipelago and that those domains in turn became instruments of Tokugawa policy toward Ainu communities and the Ryukyu Kingdom.

Section 41.10

Victory, Punishment, Reward, and the Redistribution of Japan

The most consequential effect of Sekigahara was not the defeat of an army but the reallocation of land that followed. In the weeks and months after the battle, Ieyasu, who held Osaka Castle by the end of the year, determined the fates of nearly every lord who had taken part, and many who had not. Scholars estimate that between eighty and ninety daimyō houses were eliminated, with their lands confiscated (kaieki), and that several more were reduced (genpō), with commonly cited totals for land taken from defeated lords exceeding six million koku (roughly eighty-eight houses, by one modern count). Estimates vary by source and counting method, but the scale is not in doubt: it was the largest single redistribution of land in Japanese history up to that point, larger than any of Hideyoshi's transfers.

The treatment of the principal defeated lords shows the logic of the settlement. Ishida Mitsunari, Konishi Yukinaga, and Ankokuji Ekei were executed. Ukita Hideie, who had fought hard at Sekigahara, was spared execution but exiled, eventually to the island of Hachijōjima. Chōsokabe Morichika of Tosa lost his domain and became a rōnin. The great houses of Mōri and Uesugi were handled differently, reflecting their size and the political cost of eliminating them. Mōri Terumoto, who had been the nominal head of the western coalition, was initially threatened with the confiscation of his entire domain, but after Kikkawa Hiroie's earlier contacts with the Tokugawa side were invoked, he was allowed to retain only the provinces of Suō and Nagato, a reduction from about 1.2 million koku to a fraction of that, which Mōri retainers would remember for generations as a grievance. Uesugi Kagekatsu was moved from Aizu to Yonezawa with about 300,000 koku, and Satake Yoshinobu, who had been ambiguous, was transferred to Akita and reduced by more than half. The Shimazu, remote and militarily formidable, survived through a drawn-out negotiation that ended with confirmation of their domain, an outcome that illustrates how distance and strength limited what Ieyasu could impose.

Rewards were as important as punishments. Fukushima Masanori received the large Aki and Bingo domain around Hiroshima; Kuroda Nagamasa received Chikuzen around Fukuoka; Ikeda Terumasa, Ieyasu's son-in-law, received Harima, including Himeji; Katō Kiyomasa expanded in Higo; Yamauchi Kazutoyo took over Tosa; Hosokawa Tadaoki received a domain in northern Kyushu; and Kobayakawa Hideaki, rewarded for his decisive role, was granted Bizen and Mimasaka, though he died only two years later. Ieyasu's own loyal retainers, such as Ii Naomasa, who received a domain around Sawayama and Hikone, were placed at strategic points near the capital and along the main roads. Maeda Toshinaga, who had submitted, was confirmed in a domain that was the largest in the archipelago apart from the Tokugawa holdings. The overall pattern was that the great lords who had supported Ieyasu received large domains, generally at a distance from the Tokugawa heartland, while Ieyasu's own vassals and relatives held smaller but strategically placed territories in central Honshu and along the Tōkaidō and Nakasendō, and the holdings of the Tokugawa house as a whole rose from about 2.5 million to roughly 4 million koku, including shogunal direct holdings (bakuryō or goryō, later commonly called tenryō) such as the mines, major cities, and ports that Ieyasu took over from defeated lords.

Historians later classified these lords into categories, fudai (those who had been Tokugawa vassals before Sekigahara) and tozama ("outside" lords who submitted afterward), a distinction examined in Chapter 42. The point here is that the original redistribution was less a preconceived plan than a series of decisions responding to the immediate circumstances: who had fought, who could be trusted, whom Ieyasu needed to reward, and whom he could not afford to antagonize. The effect, however, was systemic. By 1603, a considerable proportion of the archipelago's productive land was in the hands of lords who owed their holdings to Ieyasu's decisions, and who understood that the same hand could take them away.

The human costs of this transformation are visible in the consequences for the warriors and communities affected. Confiscation of a domain meant that the lord's retainers, who numbered in the hundreds or thousands, lost their positions, and many became rōnin. Estimates of the number of masterless warriors created by the confiscations of the early seventeenth century range into the hundreds of thousands over several decades, though the figures are uncertain and contested. Some found service with new lords; others became townsmen, farmers, or itinerants; and some, as discussed below, gathered at Osaka. Villages in confiscated domains faced new tax assessments, and the replacement of lords often meant new surveys and new demands.

Section 41.11

What Sekigahara Did—and Did Not—Settle

After Sekigahara Ieyasu was the most powerful man in the archipelago. But several features of the situation in 1601 show how far the settlement remained incomplete. Toyotomi Hideyori was alive and was installed in Osaka Castle, which Hideyoshi had designed as a fortress of unprecedented scale. The Toyotomi house kept substantial holdings, estimated at some 650,000 koku after the confiscations, along with the family treasury and the prestige of Hideyoshi's name. Many lords still held court titles and ranks that had been bestowed in Toyotomi times, and many saw Hideyori as their legitimate overlord, whatever their dealings with Ieyasu. The imperial court, which had granted Hideyoshi the titles of regent and Taikō, was an independent source of legitimacy and was cultivated by both sides. And the great lords whom Ieyasu had rewarded, including Fukushima, Katō, Kuroda, and Maeda, were not subordinates in the way Tokugawa vassals were. Some of them had served Hideyoshi for decades, and their loyalty to Ieyasu was in part a matter of calculation.

For this reason, the simplification "Ieyasu won Sekigahara and Japan became Tokugawa" distorts what happened. Sekigahara produced a Tokugawa supremacy, but not yet a Tokugawa system. That required further steps, some of which Ieyasu took immediately and others only over the following decade: the acquisition of the shogunal title, the establishment of a Tokugawa capital at Edo, the dynastic succession of 1605, the imposition of building and labor obligations on the lords, and ultimately the elimination of the Osaka alternative. Historians sometimes describe 1600–1615 as a period in which the Toyotomi and Tokugawa houses coexisted in an uneasy duality, in which each acknowledged the other's existence and neither could fully accommodate it. That formulation is useful but should not be pressed too far, since Ieyasu's resources were far greater and his stance toward the Toyotomi hardened over time.

Section 41.12

From Victor to Shogun: 1600–1605

In the third lunar month of Keichō 8 (spring 1603), Ieyasu was appointed seii taishōgun, "great general who subdues the barbarians," by Emperor Go-Yōzei. The Ashikaga shogunate had effectively collapsed when Nobunaga expelled Ashikaga Yoshiaki from Kyoto in 1573, although Yoshiaki did not formally relinquish the title until 1588. The title was not a monopoly of any family, and its prior holders had been Minamoto, Ashikaga, and others; Ieyasu cultivated a genealogy linking the Tokugawa to the prestigious Minamoto warrior lineage, reinforcing the historical legitimacy of his claim to the office; the documents involved are of uncertain reliability. The appointment gave him legitimacy of a kind that victory alone could not: court sanction for his position at the head of the warrior government, or bakufu. In effect, 1603 legitimized power that Sekigahara and the redistribution had already created. The title did not replace Ieyasu's earlier court ranks, and it did not make the emperor subordinate; the relationship between shogunate and court was complex and is examined in Chapter 42.

Ieyasu established his shogunal government at Edo, where he began extensive construction of the castle and the city around it, using labor and materials contributed by lords whose domains he had enlarged or confirmed. The requirement that even great lords supply resources to build the Tokugawa capital, an obligation that signaled hierarchy, was one of the means by which the Tokugawa turned economic strength into political authority. Edo, a modest settlement at the time of Ieyasu's arrival in 1590, began to grow into a major city, drawing carpenters, merchants, laborers, and the retainers of the lords who were obliged to maintain presence there. Kyoto remained the seat of the court, and Ieyasu maintained a presence there at Nijō Castle, which he constructed in 1601–1603, but the center of gravity of warrior government had moved to the Kantō.

In 1605, only two years after assuming the title, Ieyasu resigned it and arranged for his son Tokugawa Hidetada to be appointed shōgun. He did not retire from power; in 1607 he moved to Sunpu in Suruga, where as ōgosho ("retired shōgun") he continued to direct major decisions, including foreign relations, while Hidetada administered from Edo. The succession of 1605 was important because it demonstrated, to the lords and especially to the Toyotomi at Osaka, that the shogunal office would pass within the Tokugawa family, and that the Tokugawa did not regard the arrangement as a personal one tied to Ieyasu's life. It also tested the new system's mechanisms in a period when Ieyasu was still alive and able to correct mistakes. Contemporary observers, and the Toyotomi in Osaka, understood the message. As discussed in the next section, it contradicted the hope, which some held, that after Ieyasu's death the position might revert to Hideyori.

The period also saw early evidence of Ieyasu's interest in overseas relations. The arrival of the Liefde in 1600 brought William Adams and the Dutch pilot Jan Joosten to Japan, and Ieyasu used their knowledge of navigation and shipbuilding; Adams was eventually granted samurai status and a 250-koku estate at Hemi on the Miura Peninsula. Ieyasu also encouraged trade with Southeast Asia through licensed "red-seal" (shuin) ships, and negotiated with the Spanish in the Philippines and the Portuguese at Nagasaki. These contacts, and the controlled international world they helped create, are the subject of Chapter 48.

Historical Vocabulary
Seii taishōgun (征夷大将軍)

The title seii taishōgun, usually shortened to shōgun, originated in the eighth and ninth centuries as a temporary military commission for campaigns against the Emishi in northeastern Honshu. It became the title of the head of warrior government when Minamoto no Yoritomo received it in 1192, and it was later held by the Ashikaga. A bakufu ("tent government") was the military administration headed by a shōgun. In Ieyasu's circumstances, the title provided court sanction for his position at the head of the warrior government; the power of the emerging bakufu rested on the military and territorial settlement already being constructed.

In 1603 the title did not make Ieyasu a monarch. It gave him a formal commission from the court that other warriors could recognize. Its significance in this chapter lies in the contrast between military supremacy, which Ieyasu had by 1601, and sanctioned authority, which he acquired in 1603, and in the further step of 1605, when the title passed to Hidetada and became in practice hereditary.

Section 41.13

Two Centers of Power: Tokugawa Edo and Toyotomi Osaka

From 1603 to 1614, the archipelago had two centers: Edo, where the Tokugawa governed, and Osaka, where Hideyori, his mother Yodo-dono, and their circle of retainers lived in the castle that Hideyoshi had built. The relationship was formally one of reconciliation. In 1603, Ieyasu arranged the marriage of Hideyori to his granddaughter Senhime, daughter of Hidetada, and Hideyori was given a series of court ranks, rising to senior ministerial court ranks. In 1611, at Ieyasu's request, Hideyori, by then a young man, traveled to Kyoto and met Ieyasu at Nijō Castle. The surviving account records Ieyasu proposing reciprocal greetings, while Hideyori declined and performed the greeting to Ieyasu; the larger political meaning of the meeting nevertheless remained open to interpretation.

The underlying problem was that a political authority incompatible with the Tokugawa claim persisted. Osaka Castle was a huge fortress with a stock of gold and silver, and the Toyotomi continued to patronize temples and shrines, including the restoration of the Great Buddha at Hōkō-ji in Kyoto, a project that the Tokugawa may have encouraged in part to drain Toyotomi resources. The Toyotomi house had a large retinue, and the rōnin created by the confiscations of 1600 had a reason to see Osaka as a potential refuge. Many lords who had served Hideyoshi retained sentiment toward his heir, and the Tokugawa leadership was aware that the death of Ieyasu, who was in his seventies, might produce a crisis. In the years 1611–1614, several of the lords who had mediated between the two sides died, among them Katō Kiyomasa, Asano Nagamasa, and Ikeda Terumasa, as well as Asano Yukinaga and Maeda Toshinaga, and the remaining mediators lacked comparable standing.

Within the Osaka circle, tensions between the pro-accommodation and anti-Tokugawa factions made it difficult for Hideyori's government to act consistently. Yodo-dono was a powerful voice, and the position of her retainers, notably Ōno Harunaga, was shaped by their reading of Tokugawa intentions. The Tokugawa for their part exerted steady pressure: requiring that the Toyotomi contribute to construction projects, questioning the rank of its advisers, and indirectly, by restricting contact with other lords, isolating Osaka. Historians debate how much of this was deliberate strategy and how much the working-out of structural incompatibility, and the surviving sources, nearly all produced by or after the Tokugawa victory, make a definitive judgment difficult.

Section 41.14

The Osaka Winter Campaign, 1614–1615

The immediate trigger for the final confrontation was the completion in 1614 of the Hōkō-ji Great Buddha hall and its bell, whose inscription was composed by a prominent Zen monk, Seikan Bunei of Nanzen-ji. Tokugawa advisers, among them the monk Konchiin Sūden and the Confucian scholar Hayashi Razan, objected to phrases in the inscription as insulting to Ieyasu and favorable to the Toyotomi (see the Primary Source Window below). The Toyotomi sent explanations, and Katagiri Katsumoto, a vassal who had served both houses, tried to mediate. But the negotiations collapsed, and Katagiri left Osaka in late 1614, after being suspected of disloyalty. Historians generally agree that the bell dispute was a pretext rather than the cause: it gave the Tokugawa a justification for action, but the larger question was the existence of an independent Toyotomi center.

In the autumn of 1614, both sides prepared for war. Osaka sent out calls for supporters, and thousands of rōnin entered the castle, among them Sanada Nobushige (Yukimura), Gotō Matabei, Chōsokabe Morichika, Mōri Katsunaga, and Akashi Takenori, a Christian commander. The Toyotomi had expected, perhaps, that major lords who had once served Hideyoshi would join them, but none of any importance did; the lords' interests lay with the Tokugawa. The Tokugawa assembled a large force, usually estimated at around 200,000, against perhaps 100,000 within and around the castle, a figure that includes many of mixed quality.

The Tokugawa advance in the eleventh month (late November–December 1614) revealed the difficulty of taking Osaka. The castle, surrounded by moats and by the Yodo and Yamato rivers, was among the strongest in the archipelago. Sanada Nobushige built a semicircular outwork, the Sanada-maru, on the south side, and on the fourth day of the twelfth lunar month (early January 1615) his forces inflicted heavy losses on an attacking Tokugawa column. The Tokugawa responded by bombarding the castle with heavy guns, including cannons purchased from the Dutch and English, whose fire is reported to have terrified the inhabitants and to have reached the quarters of Yodo-dono. Shortly afterward, both sides agreed to a truce.

The terms of the peace are among the most debated points in the history of the campaign. The Tokugawa agreed to withdraw, the Toyotomi to tear down certain works and to retain the rōnin only in limited numbers, and the Toyotomi were to be left alone. But in the following weeks Tokugawa forces filled in the outer moat, and, according to most accounts, went on to fill in the inner moats as well, stripping Osaka Castle of much of its defensive capacity. Whether this was a violation of agreed terms or a legitimate interpretation of them is unclear, and later accounts differ. What is clear is that the Toyotomi were left with a castle that could no longer be held in the same way, and that the Tokugawa had accomplished through diplomacy what they had been unable to achieve by assault.

Section 41.15

The Osaka Summer Campaign and the End of the Toyotomi House, 1615

Peace did not last. In the spring of 1615, the Tokugawa demanded that Hideyori either leave Osaka for another domain or dismiss the rōnin from the castle. The Toyotomi side refused, and warfare resumed in the fourth month (May). Without its moats, Osaka could not be held for a siege, and the defenders attempted a series of sorties in the surrounding countryside. Gotō Matabei was killed in fighting at Dōmyōji in early May; other engagements at Yao and Wakae followed. On 3 June (Keichō 20/5/7), the final battle was fought south of the castle at Tennōji and Okayama. Sanada Nobushige led a determined charge toward Ieyasu's headquarters that, according to the accounts, came close enough to cause confusion in the Tokugawa lines before he was killed. The Tokugawa, much superior in numbers, pressed on, and the next day the castle fell. Fire consumed the keep. Hideyori and Yodo-dono died, in the accounts by suicide, within the burning castle.

The destruction of the Toyotomi was thorough. Hideyori's young son Kunimatsu was captured and executed. Hideyori's daughter, who was spared because she was Senhime's adoptive daughter, was placed in a convent. The Tokugawa also hunted down and executed leading rōnin. Contemporary accounts report that much of Osaka was devastated and that its inhabitants suffered violence and plunder, and later Tokugawa-era rebuilding transformed the castle site into a Tokugawa fortress. The era name was changed from Keichō to Genna ("original peace") in the seventh month of 1615, a symbolic declaration that the age of warfare had ended. The phrase Genna enbu (元和偃武, "the laying down of arms in Genna") came to symbolize the end of the prolonged age of large-scale civil war. Policies associated with the period, including the order that each domain keep only one castle, are discussed in Chapter 42.

Ieyasu died the following year, in the fourth month of Genna 2 (June 1616), at Sunpu, and was later deified as Tōshō Daigongen, with his principal shrine at Nikkō. By then, the Tokugawa had removed every plausible alternative to their own rule. There was no longer a living adult of the Toyotomi line, no great lord with a claim to the legacy of Hideyoshi, and no surviving dynastic and military center capable of rivaling Tokugawa rule as Toyotomi Osaka had done. The date 1615 is accordingly often used to mark the beginning of the Edo period in the stricter sense, since from this point onward the Tokugawa position was institutionally dominant and without a competing center.

Primary Source Window

The Hōkō-ji Bell Inscription and the Tokugawa Objection

Creator: Seikan Bunei, abbot of Nanzen-ji, composed the inscription for the bell of the Hōkō-ji Great Buddha hall; Tokugawa advisers objected to it · Date: 1614 · Type: Inscribed temple bell (physical artifact), with later accounts of the controversy in diaries and Tokugawa compilations

Context: The Toyotomi had undertaken the reconstruction of the Great Buddha hall at Hōkō-ji in Kyoto, a monument Hideyoshi had founded. When the bell was cast in 1614, the inscription became the subject of a dispute with the Tokugawa. The bell survives, and the inscription is visible on it today.

Brief excerpt and paraphrase (not a full translation): Two four-character phrases drew the objection: 国家安康 (kokka ankō, roughly "the state at peace and secure") and 君臣豊楽 (kunshin hōraku, roughly "lord and vassals in prosperity and joy"). Tokugawa advisers read the first as splitting the characters of Ieyasu's name (家康, with 安 placed between 家 and 康) and thus symbolically cutting him in two, and the second as hailing the Toyotomi (豊臣, whose first character appears in 豊楽) as the rulers of a prosperous age.

Analysis: The inscription is a physical object whose wording is not in dispute; what is disputed is the reading. Classical Chinese compositions were often constructed with elaborate wordplay, and it is possible that the monk, a learned man, intended some of the associations. But the Tokugawa side's reading was strained, and most historians treat the accusation as a pretext for action. The source illustrates how documents and artifacts could become instruments of politics, and how a dispute about literary meaning could serve as the public face of a decision made on other grounds. It also illustrates that the Toyotomi, in sponsoring a major Buddhist project in Kyoto, were exercising a form of patronage that rivaled Tokugawa legitimacy-building. The limits of the source are that it says nothing about the intentions of Hideyori or his advisers, and that the stories about the accusation come largely from Tokugawa-aligned writers.

Questions for students:

  1. What does the inscription say, and what reading did the Tokugawa advisers give it?
  2. Who created the source, and who interpreted it as offensive? What interests did each have?
  3. What can historians learn from this source about the relationship between the Toyotomi and Tokugawa houses and about political use of texts?
  4. What can historians not establish from the inscription alone about why war began in 1614?
Section 41.16

What Had Been Founded? From Reunification to Tokugawa Rule

By 1615 a political order existed that was both continuous with and different from the Toyotomi settlement. The Tokugawa inherited from Nobunaga and Hideyoshi the principle of territorial lordship based on assessed productivity, the centrality of the castle town, the power to reassign domains, the use of hostages, the separation of warriors from the land, and the premise that all lords stood in a hierarchy under a central authority. These features were not Tokugawa inventions, and the Tokugawa could not have established their rule without them.

What the Tokugawa changed was the character of the center. Hideyoshi's authority had been personal and, after his death, could not be transmitted. Ieyasu's system sought to make authority dynastic, held by a house rather than a man, and sanctioned by the court as a hereditary office. It redistributed territory to ensure that no coalition of lords could challenge the center, it eliminated the rival dynastic house, and it began to establish a capital at Edo. These were the foundations on which the mature structure described in Chapter 42 would be built. At the time of Ieyasu's death in 1616, much remained to be done: the regulations governing the lords, the court, and the temples had been only recently issued; the relationship between the Tokugawa and the great domains was untested over generations; and the long-term stability of the system was not assured. Chapter 42 begins from this point.

Historical Debate

Was Sekigahara a Tokugawa Conquest or a Civil War within the Toyotomi Order?

The question: Should the war of 1600 be understood as the beginning of Tokugawa conquest of Japan, or as a conflict among members of the Toyotomi political world, in which Ieyasu's later supremacy was not yet determined?

Interpretation A — A Tokugawa conquest: Historians who emphasize the long-term consequences point out that Ieyasu was the dominant lord by 1599, that he deliberately provoked his rivals, and that the redistribution after 1600 gave him control of much of the archipelago. In this view, Sekigahara was the decisive step in a process by which Ieyasu replaced the Toyotomi as the paramount power.
Interpretation B — A civil war within the Toyotomi order: Other historians stress that most participants on both sides were Toyotomi retainers who invoked loyalty to Hideyori, that Ieyasu held no shogunal title and claimed to act for the Toyotomi, and that the Toyotomi house survived for fifteen years. In this view, Sekigahara was a struggle over who would control the Toyotomi settlement, and the Tokugawa order emerged only gradually afterward.

Evidence: Both positions rely on the same documents: the pledges and letters of the participants, the content of the accusations of 1600, the structure of the redistribution, and the later events at Osaka. They differ in how much weight to give the participants' own language of loyalty and in how far they read the outcome back into the start. Much of the narrative evidence comes from Tokugawa-era compilations, which had reasons to present Ieyasu's rise as inevitable.

Why it matters: The question affects how students understand the nature of Tokugawa legitimacy, the motives of the lords, and the contingency of the outcome. The two interpretations are not mutually exclusive, and many scholars combine them.

Student task: Identify one piece of evidence from this chapter that supports each interpretation, and then explain what additional evidence would help to decide between them.

Multiple Perspectives

The Settlement of 1600: How Different Groups Experienced It

Note: these perspectives are reconstructions based on the documentary record, not quotations. The groups did not possess equal power or freedom of action.

Tokugawa retainers from Mikawa: Their service and sacrifices, such as those of Torii Mototada at Fushimi, were rewarded with positions of trust. The victory raised their status and gave them strategic holdings, though most received smaller domains than the great outside lords.
Great lords who supported Ieyasu: Men such as Fukushima Masanori and Kuroda Nagamasa gained substantial domains. But they now owed their holdings to a man who had been their equal, and many were placed at a distance from the center.
Defeated houses such as the Mōri and the Chōsokabe: The Mōri lost most of their lands and endured a long memory of reduction; the Chōsokabe lost all. Their retainers lost positions, and many became rōnin.
The Toyotomi household at Osaka: Hideyori, Yodo-dono, and their retainers retained the symbol of Hideyoshi's legacy and substantial wealth but lost the broader political base on which it had rested. They lived under growing pressure for fifteen years.
Villagers and townspeople along the roads: They supplied armies with food, labor, and transport, sometimes by compulsion; they suffered plundering where castles were attacked; and after confiscations they faced new lords, new surveys, and new tax demands.
Christian communities and missionaries: Konishi Yukinaga's execution, Gracia Hosokawa's death, and the commitment of Christian rōnin at Osaka illustrate that Christian lords were among the participants. Ieyasu's later policy toward Christianity is examined in Chapter 47.
Continuity and Change Over Time

From Toyotomi to Early Tokugawa Methods of Rule

What Changed

  • Authority moved from a personal, civil-regent model (Hideyoshi as kanpaku and Taikō) to a shogunal, dynastic one
  • The political center shifted from Osaka and Kyoto to Edo
  • The distribution of domains was reordered so that the largest holders were no longer those of Hideyoshi's creation
  • Succession was demonstrated to be hereditary within the Tokugawa house
  • The rival Toyotomi house was eliminated

What Stayed the Same

  • Koku-based assessment of domains and the land surveys behind it
  • Castle-town lordship and the separation of warriors from cultivators
  • The power to confiscate, reduce, and transfer domains
  • Hostages and oaths as instruments of control
  • Court titles as a source of legitimacy

Who experienced change most directly? The defeated lords and their retainers, villagers in domains that changed hands, and the Toyotomi household. Who experienced greater continuity? Villagers in domains left unchanged, and the court, which continued to supply titles. Why does the pattern matter? It shows that the Tokugawa built on the unification of Nobunaga and Hideyoshi rather than replacing it, and that the decisive change lay in the character of the center.

Cause and Effect

From Succession Crisis to the Tokugawa Settlement

Long-term causes

The personal basis of Hideyoshi's authority; the lack of institutions to resolve disputes among great lords; the rift between administrators and campaign commanders; the strain of the Korean wars; the accumulated resources of the Kantō lordship.

Short-term causes

Hideyoshi's death with a child heir; the marriage dispute of 1599; the death of Maeda Toshiie; Ieyasu's move into Osaka Castle; the Uesugi confrontation; Mitsunari's mobilization.

Event or transformation

A coalition war across the archipelago, decided at Sekigahara but not confined to it, followed by the redistribution of domains.

Immediate effects

Execution or exile of the western leaders; confiscation of more than six million koku by commonly cited totals; rewards to Ieyasu's allies; the creation of large numbers of rōnin.

Long-term consequences

The shogunate of 1603; the succession of 1605; Edo's rise; the destruction of the Toyotomi in 1615; and the foundation of the mature bakuhan order examined in Chapter 42.

Historical complexity

No single cause explains the outcome. Chance (Hidetada's delay, Kobayakawa's choice), structure (the personal basis of Toyotomi rule), and strategy (Ieyasu's coalition-building) all mattered.

Historical complexity: The outcome was not inevitable. A different course of events at Sekigahara, or a different decision by Mōri Terumoto, could have produced a prolonged war.

Everyday Life and Environment

War, Roads, and the Villages

For the villagers of Mino, Ōmi, and the other regions that armies crossed, the war was a matter of requisition and risk. Armies needed rice, horses, porters, and fodder, and they drew on the communities along the Tōkaidō and Nakasendō. Foot soldiers (ashigaru) were often recruited from the farming population, and many of the dead at Sekigahara and Osaka were men from such backgrounds whose names are not recorded. Villages sometimes sought protection by paying armies to spare them, and local records from the period, where they survive, show negotiated exemptions. Plunder was a regular feature of warfare, and the accounts of Osaka in 1615 describe attacks on townspeople and the seizure of captives.

The confiscations that followed meant new lords and sometimes new surveys, which in turn meant new tax assessments and obligations to supply labor for castle-building. In the rapidly growing Edo, laborers and craftsmen found work. Ieyasu's building projects, funded by contributions from the lords, drew migrants from across the archipelago, and the city began to take on the mixed, male-dominated character that would mark it for generations. For women of the warrior class, the war meant hostage-taking, as in the case of Hosokawa Gracia, and arranged marriages as instruments of alliance, such as that of Senhime to Hideyori.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that the Tokugawa order was not created at a single moment. It emerged between 1598 and 1615 through a sequence of stages: a contested succession, the breakdown of a coalition, a nationwide war, a massive redistribution of land, a bid for formal legitimacy, a dynastic succession, and a final war that eliminated the last rival center. Each stage solved one problem and created another, and none was inevitable.

Hideyoshi's settlement was archipelago-wide but personally dependent. The great lords held their domains by his grant, the Toyotomi house possessed wealth and prestige, and a council of senior lords and a body of commissioners were meant to preserve the arrangement for a child heir. But the arrangements lacked a mechanism for settling disputes among the lords, and the quarrels left by the Korean wars, especially between campaign commanders and administrators, gave those disputes a sharp edge.

Ieyasu's resources were exceptional: the largest domain, a disciplined retainer corps, and an army preserved from the Korean campaigns. Between 1598 and 1600 he acted inside the Toyotomi framework, through marriage alliances, arbitration, and residence in Osaka Castle, while steadily extending his influence. The death of Maeda Toshiie removed the principal counterweight, the attack on Ishida Mitsunari showed the depth of the commander-administrator rift, and the confrontation with Uesugi Kagekatsu became the occasion for war.

The coalitions of 1600 were not the stable "Eastern" and "Western" armies of later memory. Both included former Toyotomi retainers who claimed to defend Hideyori, and many lords hesitated, stayed out, or changed sides. The war was fought through sieges and marches across central Honshu, and in parallel campaigns in the northeast and Kyushu, which shows that the conflict was nationwide and that local rivalries shaped alignments.

At Sekigahara on 21 October 1600, the western coalition's lack of unified command and the unwillingness of several commanders to commit, together with the defection of Kobayakawa Hideaki, produced a collapse within a day. The popular story of a single betrayal oversimplifies the matter. The battle was decisive less because of the casualties than because it broke the will of the opposition and delivered the political initiative to Ieyasu.

The redistribution that followed was the true transformation. Some eighty to ninety houses were eliminated, others were reduced, and loyal allies and Tokugawa vassals were rewarded with domains placed to secure the roads and the capital region. This reshaped the political map, created large numbers of rōnin, and left villagers facing new lords and new demands.

Victory, however, was not legitimacy. Ieyasu's appointment as shōgun in 1603 supplied a formal commission from the court, and his resignation in favor of Hidetada in 1605 showed that the office would pass within the family. The construction of Edo, funded by contributions from the lords, turned economic strength into political hierarchy.

The persistence of the Toyotomi house at Osaka was the unresolved problem. For eleven years after 1603, the archipelago had two centers, and the death of mediating lords, the gathering of rōnin, and the bell inscription controversy led to war. The winter campaign of 1614 showed the strength of Osaka's defenses; the settlement that followed stripped them; and the summer campaign of 1615 ended in the fall of the castle and the deaths of Hideyori and Yodo-dono.

The Tokugawa inherited from Nobunaga and Hideyoshi the instruments of assessed domains, castle-town lordship, hostages, and confiscation. What they changed was the character of the center: from a personal authority to a hereditary house, from Osaka to Edo, and from a balance among great lords to a Tokugawa supremacy reinforced by territorial design. These changes also affected frontier regions: Matsumae and Satsuma, whose positions were secured in the Tokugawa settlement, would become the instruments of Tokugawa policy toward the Ainu and the Ryukyu Kingdom.

Students should remember that much of the evidence comes from the victors. The Tokugawa had reason to present the outcome as inevitable and to portray their opponents as disloyal. Careful historians read these sources against letters, pledges, domain records, and foreign accounts, and stress the contingency of the events. With the destruction of Osaka and the death of Ieyasu in 1616, the foundational political work was done; the question of how the system functioned belongs to Chapter 42.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it explains how an unstable, personally dependent unification became a durable political order. The result was not foreordained. It depended on structures inherited from Nobunaga and Hideyoshi, the choices of individual lords, the geography of roads and castles, and a series of decisions by Ieyasu that converted a crisis into supremacy and supremacy into dynasty.

It also reveals larger processes in Japanese history: the persistent tension between personal authority and institutional continuity, the importance of land assessment and domain assignment as instruments of rule, and the role of legitimacy, whether through court titles or hereditary succession, in making power durable. The dual-center problem of Edo and Osaka recalls earlier moments in which court and warrior government coexisted, and its resolution in 1615 shaped the political landscape of the next two and a half centuries.

The chapter connects regions and outside worlds. The war involved lords from the northeast to Kyushu. The Dutch ship Liefde arrived in the midst of it. The positions secured by Matsumae and Satsuma shaped later relations with Ainu communities and the Ryukyu Kingdom, which Chapter 48 examines, and Ieyasu's overseas policies foreshadow the controlled international world of the Tokugawa period.

Finally, the chapter invites caution about the stories that victors tell. Tokugawa narratives made the outcome look inevitable, and popular memory has preserved vivid anecdotes that the evidence does not fully support. The task of the historian is to recover contingency, to ask who is missing from the record, and to explain how a system that later seemed natural was in fact built. Chapter 42 turns from how the Tokugawa won to how their system worked.
