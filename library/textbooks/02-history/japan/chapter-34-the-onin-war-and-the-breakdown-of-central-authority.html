Part V — Ashikaga Japan and the Maritime World
34

The Ōnin War and the Breakdown of Central Authority

Japanese History: From Jōmon Japan to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

In the fifth month of 1467, armies encamped inside the capital of Kyoto — the same city that had for a century housed the imperial court, the Ashikaga shogunate, and the elegant cultural world of Zen temples, gardens, and Noh performance described in earlier chapters of this book. Within weeks, whole districts of the city were burning. Aristocratic mansions, temple compounds, and merchant streets that had stood for generations disappeared into ash. The fighting that broke out that year, and that would not fully subside for another decade, has come to be known as the Ōnin War, named for the era name — Ōnin — in use when it began.

It is tempting to tell the story of the Ōnin War as a simple morality tale: a weak or distracted shogun, a bitter dispute over who would succeed him, two rival strongmen who could not compromise, and a capital reduced to rubble as a result. Every one of those elements has some basis in fact. Ashikaga Yoshimasa, the eighth Ashikaga shogun, genuinely struggled to produce an heir, and he devoted extraordinary resources and personal attention to cultural patronage — the refined aesthetic world of tea, ink painting, and garden design examined in Chapter 30 — even as he struggled, with diminishing success, to manage increasingly difficult and interlocking political disputes among the great warrior houses. Hosokawa Katsumoto and Yamana Sōzen, two of the most powerful warrior power brokers in the shogunal system, genuinely led rival coalitions that came to blows in the streets of the capital. And Kyoto genuinely burned.

But if the chapter stopped there, it would badly misrepresent what actually happened, and it would misrepresent why the war mattered for the rest of Japanese history. The Ōnin War did not spring from a single family quarrel dropped onto an otherwise healthy, centralized government. It erupted from a set of problems that had been building within the Muromachi political order for decades, problems this textbook began to describe in Chapter 29: a shogunate that depended on the cooperation of a handful of enormously powerful shugo houses, a governing structure that had never fully resolved how authority was to be shared between Kyoto and the provinces, and repeated succession crises within the greatest of those houses that the shogunate could not reliably settle. Yoshimasa's personal succession problem was real, but it was only the spark that ignited kindling that had already accumulated.

Nor did the war produce an instant, dramatic transformation of Japanese political life. When the last encamped armies finally withdrew from Kyoto in 1477, the Ashikaga shogunate still existed. The emperor still reigned in the same city. The formal offices of the Muromachi government still had occupants. No single decisive battle had settled who would rule Japan, because the war had never really been a contest for that kind of victory. What had changed was something quieter and, in the long run, far more consequential: the shogunate's capacity to compel obedience from the provinces — always incomplete — had eroded further, while power in many regions had already begun shifting into the hands of local military families, deputies, and warrior leagues who no longer needed Kyoto's permission to act. The Ōnin War did not invent this shift. It exposed it, accelerated it, and made it visible to contemporaries and to later historians alike.

This chapter therefore asks students to hold two ideas together without collapsing one into the other. The first is that the Ōnin War was a genuine catastrophe — a decade of intermittent but often intense urban warfare that devastated much of Kyoto, displaced its residents, destroyed irreplaceable cultural property, and disrupted the political and economic life of the capital for a generation. The second is that the war's deeper historical significance lies less in the fighting itself than in what the fighting revealed and accelerated: the transition from a Muromachi order organized around negotiated cooperation between Kyoto and a handful of great shugo houses, toward the more openly decentralized, competitive, and locally grounded political world that later chapters will call Sengoku Japan.

The chapter's structure follows that argument. It begins by briefly recalling how the Muromachi political system worked and why, by the middle of the fifteenth century, that system had become difficult to hold together. It then traces the succession disputes — Yoshimasa's own, and the parallel and arguably more consequential disputes within the Hatakeyama and Shiba houses — that set rival coalitions on a collision course. It follows the outbreak of fighting in Kyoto in 1467, the decade of inconclusive warfare that followed, the deaths of the two principal coalition leaders in 1473, and the eventual winding down of the conflict in 1477. Finally, it turns to the chapter's central historical claim: that the most important outcome of the Ōnin War was not anything settled in Kyoto, but the accelerated shift of real political and military power into the provinces — a shift examined in its fuller, constructive form in Chapter 35's treatment of the Sengoku daimyō.

Students should resist two temptations while reading this chapter. The first is to treat 1467 as a bright line separating a stable "medieval" Japan from a chaotic "Warring States" Japan; the decentralization described here had earlier roots and continued to unfold unevenly for decades afterward. The second is to treat 1477 as the moment the Ashikaga shogunate "fell"; it did not, and it would continue to exist, with diminishing effective authority, for nearly a century more. The Ōnin War is best understood not as an ending or a beginning in itself, but as the loud, visible, destructive middle of a much longer story of political fragmentation — a story this chapter narrates and the next chapter continues.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how the negotiated, coalition-based structure of Muromachi government made the shogunate dependent on cooperation among powerful shugo houses.
  2. Describe the succession problem surrounding Ashikaga Yoshimasa and place it within the larger context of factional politics.
  3. Trace the parallel succession disputes within the Hatakeyama and Shiba houses and explain why the shogunate could not resolve them.
  4. Identify Hosokawa Katsumoto and Yamana Sōzen and characterize the loose, shifting nature of the coalitions they led.
  5. Trace the outbreak and course of fighting in Kyoto beginning in 1467, including its urban and destructive character.
  6. Analyze why a decade of warfare failed to produce a decisive military or political resolution.
  7. Assess the human and material costs of the war for Kyoto's residents, institutions, and economy.
  8. Explain the concept of gekokujō and evaluate its usefulness and its limits as a description of the period's social change.
  9. Distinguish the survival of Ashikaga institutions after 1477 from the erosion of their effective authority.
  10. Evaluate competing historical interpretations of whether the Ōnin War caused or merely accelerated the shift toward Sengoku decentralization.
  11. Connect the political breakdown examined here to the cultural and commercial world of Kyoto described in earlier chapters and to the new territorial order examined in the chapter that follows.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why was the Muromachi shogunate's authority always dependent on the cooperation of a small number of very powerful warrior houses?
  2. How did Ashikaga Yoshimasa's succession problem intersect with, rather than single-handedly cause, a broader factional crisis?
  3. Why were the Hatakeyama and Shiba succession disputes significant beyond the fortunes of those two houses?
  4. In what sense were Hosokawa Katsumoto and Yamana Sōzen leaders of coalitions rather than commanders of unified national armies?
  5. Why did a decade of warfare centered on Kyoto fail to produce a decisive resolution?
  6. How did the war affect ordinary residents of Kyoto — merchants, artisans, temple communities, and aristocratic households — differently from the warrior elites who led it?
  7. In what ways did the provinces move beyond Kyoto's effective control during and after the war, and how evenly did this happen across the archipelago?
  8. Why is it historically inaccurate to describe 1477 as the end of the Ashikaga shogunate?
  9. Did the Ōnin War cause the Sengoku political order, or did it primarily accelerate and expose transformations already underway?

Historical Context

Chapter 29 explained that the Ashikaga shogunate, founded by Ashikaga Takauji in the fourteenth century and consolidated under Ashikaga Yoshimitsu, never achieved the degree of direct administrative control over the provinces that the earlier Kamakura bakufu had approached in its own way, let alone the centralized bureaucratic ambitions of the ancient ritsuryō state examined in Part II. Muromachi government instead rested on a negotiated balance: the shogun in Kyoto held real authority and prestige, particularly at the height of Yoshimitsu's rule, but that authority was exercised largely through cooperation with a limited number of great shugo houses — including the Hosokawa, Yamana, Hatakeyama, Shiba, Ōuchi, and others — whose leaders occupied important positions in the Kyoto political order and held military and administrative power across multiple provinces; among them, the Hosokawa, Shiba, and Hatakeyama took turns supplying the shogunate's most senior administrative office, the kanrei, or shogunal deputy.

This arrangement had genuine strengths. It allowed the shogunate to project authority across a large territory without maintaining the kind of direct provincial bureaucracy the ancient state had once attempted. It also gave the great warrior houses a strong incentive to remain invested in the Kyoto-centered political order, since their standing depended in part on the offices, titles, and recognition the shogunate could confer. Chapters 30 and 31 have already shown some of the cultural and commercial consequences of this arrangement: a capital that hosted extraordinary artistic patronage, flourishing markets, and a genuinely cosmopolitan urban life, sustained in part by the wealth and prestige that shugo houses invested in Kyoto rather than exclusively in their home provinces.

The arrangement also had a structural weakness that this chapter's narrative will make vivid: because so much depended on cooperation among a small number of houses, a succession dispute within any one of those houses could not remain a private family matter. Rival claimants to headship of a great house typically sought backing from other powerful figures — including the shogun himself, or the kanrei, or a rival great house — in order to strengthen their claim. This meant that a succession dispute inside the Hatakeyama or Shiba house could become entangled with rivalries among the Hosokawa, Yamana, and the shogunal succession itself, since each side had an incentive to draw in as many powerful allies as possible. By the middle of the fifteenth century, several such disputes were unfolding simultaneously, creating a dense web of competing obligations and alliances that the shogunate proved unable to manage or resolve, as Yoshimasa's ability to arbitrate these increasingly interconnected disputes became progressively constrained.

It is important not to treat this fragility as evidence that Muromachi government had always been on the verge of collapse. For several decades under Yoshimitsu and his immediate successors, the coalition system functioned reasonably well, producing the political stability that allowed the cultural and commercial developments of Chapters 30 and 31 to flourish. The crisis examined in this chapter emerged from a specific convergence of succession problems in the 1450s and 1460s, compounded by economic strain, provincial unrest, and the fact that Yoshimasa's ability to arbitrate the increasingly interconnected disputes was becoming progressively constrained — not from an inherent, permanent impossibility of Ashikaga rule. Understanding the Ōnin War therefore requires understanding both the underlying structural vulnerability of the coalition system and the particular sequence of events that turned that vulnerability into open warfare.

Section 34.1

The Muromachi Political Order before 1467

As Chapter 29 explained, the Ashikaga shogunate governed Japan not through a centralized bureaucracy directly administering the provinces, but through a system of negotiated cooperation with a small circle of exceptionally powerful shugo houses. These houses, several of which held shugo appointments — provincial military-governor titles originally created to oversee local order and mobilize warriors — across multiple provinces simultaneously, relied on representatives called shugodai to administer day-to-day affairs in each of those provinces while the heads of the houses themselves often resided for long periods in Kyoto, close to the shogunate and the imperial court. This arrangement kept the greatest warrior families invested in the capital's political and cultural life, but it also meant that a great deal of actual provincial administration was delegated downward, to deputies and local officials who gained practical experience — and, over time, practical independence — in managing affairs largely on their own.

At the center of this system sat the office of kanrei, the shogunal deputy, traditionally rotated among three houses: the Hosokawa, the Shiba, and the Hatakeyama. Whichever house held the kanrei office at a given moment exercised enormous influence over shogunal decision-making, appointments, and the arbitration of disputes among other warrior families. This rotation was intended to prevent any single house from monopolizing power indefinitely, and for much of the fourteenth and early fifteenth centuries it worked reasonably well. But the system depended on each of the three kanrei houses itself remaining internally stable, with a clear and broadly accepted line of succession. When that internal stability broke down — as it did within both the Hatakeyama and Shiba houses during the 1450s and 1460s — the shogunate lost one of its most important tools for managing the wider coalition, since the very houses meant to help arbitrate disputes were themselves consumed by disputes of their own.

By the time Ashikaga Yoshimasa became shogun in 1449, this system had already been placed under considerable strain by decades of intermittent provincial unrest, peasant uprisings of the kind examined in later chapters' treatment of popular protest, and recurring tension between the shogunate's need for revenue and the great houses' interest in protecting their own territorial and financial autonomy. None of these pressures made large-scale civil war in Kyoto inevitable. But they meant that the coalition system Yoshimasa inherited was considerably less resilient than the one Yoshimitsu had presided over at the height of Muromachi power, and considerably less able to absorb the shock of multiple, simultaneous succession crises.

Section 34.2

Ashikaga Yoshimasa and the Problem of Succession

Ashikaga Yoshimasa is sometimes remembered, not without reason, as one of the great patrons of Muromachi cultural life — the sponsor of the aesthetic world of the Silver Pavilion and the refined tea, garden, and Noh culture examined in Chapter 30. It would be a mistake, however, to caricature Yoshimasa simply as a frivolous aesthete who abandoned politics for art while his government collapsed around him. Yoshimasa took an active role in shogunal politics for much of his tenure, attempted to mediate among rival factions on several occasions, and made real if ultimately unsuccessful efforts to manage the succession disputes convulsing the great warrior houses. His failures were failures of political judgment and capacity within an increasingly difficult situation, not simple negligence.

The succession problem most directly associated with Yoshimasa concerned his own household. For years, Yoshimasa and his wife, Hino Tomiko, had no son, and by the early 1460s Yoshimasa, still relatively young but evidently concerned about the succession, arranged in 1464 for his younger brother, Ashikaga Yoshimi, to leave religious life and become his designated heir. This was a conventional solution to a succession problem within a warrior or aristocratic household of the period, and it might have resolved matters cleanly had circumstances not changed.

They did change. In 1465, Tomiko gave birth to a son, the future Ashikaga Yoshihisa. This unexpected birth created exactly the kind of ambiguous, contested succession situation that the great warrior houses knew how to exploit for their own advantage. Yoshimi, as the already-designated heir, retained a strong claim; Yoshihisa, as Yoshimasa's biological son, represented an alternative and, to many, a more natural line of succession. Rather than remaining a private family matter, this ambiguity became entangled with the rival ambitions of the great warrior houses, since backing one candidate or the other offered a path to influence over the next shogun. Hosokawa Katsumoto initially aligned himself with Yoshimi, while Yamana Sōzen — at this stage, an ally of Tomiko's — initially supported the claims associated with the infant Yoshihisa's faction, though it is important to note that these alignments developed gradually, were shaped as much by the Hatakeyama and Shiba disputes discussed in the next section as by the shogunal succession alone, and would not remain fixed: as Section 34.4 explains, both the Yoshimi and Yoshihisa camps switched coalitions in 1468.

Students should therefore avoid two oversimplifications. The first is treating Yoshimasa's personal succession problem as the sole cause of the Ōnin War; it was one significant factor among several converging crises, and even without it, the Hatakeyama and Shiba disputes alone carried substantial potential for wider conflict. The second is treating Yoshimasa as simply indifferent to politics; his cultural patronage developed alongside, not instead of, years of genuine, if increasingly unsuccessful, political engagement with a crisis that ultimately exceeded his capacity — and perhaps any shogun's capacity — to resolve through negotiation alone. Much of the popular image of Yoshimasa and his wife Hino Tomiko as morally careless figures who let the capital burn while pursuing private pleasures descends from later narrative retellings of the war, discussed further in Section 34.6's historiographical note, and should be treated with the same caution this textbook applies to other retrospective literary traditions.

Section 34.3

Succession Struggles within the Great Warrior Houses

If the Ōnin War had been only about who would succeed Yoshimasa, it is unlikely to have escalated into a decade of warfare engulfing the capital. What transformed a shogunal succession question into a citywide catastrophe was the fact that two of the shogunate's own kanrei houses — the Hatakeyama and the Shiba — were simultaneously convulsed by their own unresolved succession disputes, disputes that the shogunate, weakened by the very same coalition dynamics described in Section 34.1, proved unable to settle authoritatively.

Within the Hatakeyama house, two named rival claimants to headship contested control for years by the 1460s: Hatakeyama Masanaga and Hatakeyama Yoshinari, each backed by different branches of the family and by different networks of retainers and allied warriors. Attempts by the shogunate to recognize one claimant over the other repeatedly failed to end the dispute, in part because the losing side could, and did, seek support from other powerful figures outside the house itself, including from Hosokawa Katsumoto and Yamana Sōzen, who each saw an opportunity to extend their own influence by backing a client claimant — Katsumoto generally supporting Masanaga's claim, and Sōzen backing Yoshinari's. A similar dynamic unfolded within the Shiba house, where two rival candidates for headship, Shiba Yoshitoshi and Shiba Yoshikado, likewise sought outside patronage rather than accepting the shogunate's attempted mediation, further entangling the Shiba succession with the same Hosokawa–Yamana rivalry already forming around the Hatakeyama dispute.

These parallel disputes mattered for several reasons beyond the fortunes of the two houses themselves. First, they demonstrated that the shogunate's capacity to arbitrate disputes among the great houses — one of its most important functions within the coalition system described in Section 34.1 — had become unreliable even before the Kyoto fighting began. Second, they created multiple, overlapping fault lines of alliance and rivalry that did not map neatly onto a single division between two sides; a family or faction might align with Katsumoto on the Hatakeyama question while harboring separate grievances connected to the Shiba dispute or the shogunal succession. Third, when fighting eventually broke out, it drew directly on the armed retainers already mobilized by Masanaga and Yoshinari, whose forces clashed at the Kami Goryō Shrine in Kyoto in the first month of 1467 — a confrontation that provided the immediate spark for the wider conflagration that followed a few months later.

Understanding this layered structure of grievance is essential to avoiding an oversimplified narrative in which two men, Katsumoto and Sōzen, simply decided to fight each other over who would be shogun. In reality, the war grew out of a dense, tangled set of separate but mutually reinforcing disputes, in which the shogunal succession was one important thread among several, and in which the great warrior houses' own internal fragility played at least as large a role as any single figure's ambition.

Section 34.4

Hosokawa Katsumoto and Yamana Sōzen

Hosokawa Katsumoto and Yamana Sōzen have often been presented, in simplified accounts, as the two commanders of the Ōnin War, each directing a unified national army against the other. This picture is misleading in an important way: neither man commanded anything resembling a centralized, disciplined national force. Each stood, instead, at the center of a coalition — a shifting network of allied houses, client factions, and retainer bands whose participation depended on calculations of self-interest, existing rivalries, family obligations, and the specific disputes discussed in Sections 34.2 and 34.3, rather than on unconditional loyalty to Katsumoto or Sōzen personally.

Katsumoto, as head of the Hosokawa house and holder of the kanrei office, occupied one of the most powerful positions available within the shogunal system, with extensive landholdings, marriage alliances, and client relationships across multiple provinces. Sōzen, head of the Yamana house, commanded a similarly extensive network, and the two men were connected by a complicated personal history that included past cooperation as well as growing rivalry; Sōzen was, in fact, Katsumoto's father-in-law, a reminder that the factional lines of the Ōnin War did not always follow the kind of clean, impersonal divisions a modern reader might expect, and that family ties could coexist with — and even intensify — bitter political rivalry.

As the Hatakeyama, Shiba, and shogunal succession disputes converged in the mid-1460s, Katsumoto and Sōzen each found themselves backing different sets of claimants across these overlapping conflicts, and each began gathering allied houses into what would become known, once fighting broke out, as the eastern army (associated with Katsumoto, initially concentrated in the eastern part of the city) and the western army (associated with Sōzen, initially concentrated in the west). These labels are useful shorthand, and this textbook will use them, but students should understand them as descriptions of shifting, loosely organized coalitions rather than as fixed, symmetrical, or permanently stable alliances. Houses changed sides over the course of the war; local rivalries sometimes mattered more to a given family's choice of side than any larger strategic calculation; and neither Katsumoto nor Sōzen possessed the kind of command authority that would have allowed him to simply order his coalition's members into or out of battle.

This coalition structure helps explain much of what follows in this chapter: why the fighting, once begun, proved so difficult to end through negotiation; why battles in Kyoto often failed to produce decisive results even when one side gained a temporary advantage; and why the deaths of Katsumoto and Sōzen in 1473, rather than ending the war outright, instead left their coalitions to continue fighting under new, often less authoritative leadership.

The fluidity of these coalitions is nowhere more visible than in the striking reversal of the shogunal succession alignments that occurred in 1468. At the outset of the war, Katsumoto's eastern coalition was understood to favor Ashikaga Yoshimi's claim to the succession, while Sōzen's western coalition was linked to the infant Yoshihisa and his mother, Hino Tomiko. By 1468, however, Yoshimi had broken with the eastern camp and taken refuge with Yamana Sōzen and the western army, while Katsumoto's eastern coalition shifted to back Yoshihisa's claim instead. Hino Tomiko's own political position moved with these currents rather than remaining fixed to a single camp throughout the war. This reversal should not be read as a minor footnote: it demonstrates concretely that the "eastern" and "western" labels described the shifting balance of allied interests at a given moment rather than fixed ideological or dynastic loyalties, and it further complicated any hope that resolving the shogunal succession alone could bring the war to a close.

Section 34.5

Kyoto Becomes a Battlefield, 1467

Open fighting associated with the Hatakeyama succession dispute broke out in Kyoto in the first month of 1467, as the forces of Hatakeyama Masanaga and Hatakeyama Yoshinari clashed at the Kami Goryō Shrine in the capital. Rather than remaining a contained, localized skirmish, this fighting drew in the wider networks of allied houses described in Section 34.4, and by the fifth month of 1467 the conflict had widened into large-scale warfare between the coalitions now identified as the eastern army, under Hosokawa Katsumoto, and the western army, under Yamana Sōzen. This escalation from localized succession violence to citywide war is conventionally treated as the true beginning of the Ōnin War.

The fighting that followed transformed Kyoto's urban landscape in ways that had few precedents in the city's history. Warrior forces fortified aristocratic mansions, temple compounds, and other substantial buildings as strongpoints, effectively turning entire neighborhoods into contested military positions. Trenches, barricades, and defensive works appeared amid the city's streets and residential districts. Fire, whether set deliberately to deny an opponent a defensible position or spreading accidentally from military encampments, repeatedly swept through densely built wooden neighborhoods, destroying homes, workshops, temples, and aristocratic residences alike. Contemporary observers described whole quarters of the city reduced to ash within the war's opening phases, and the destruction would continue, in uneven waves, for years afterward.

Unlike a set-piece battle fought on an open field, the fighting in Kyoto often took the form of extended, grinding engagements over specific fortified positions, interspersed with periods of relative calm during which both sides regrouped, sought new allies, or attempted negotiations that repeatedly failed to produce a lasting settlement. This pattern — intense but indecisive urban combat, punctuated by shifting alliances and failed truces — would characterize the war for years to come, and it is essential to understanding why a conflict fought in the heart of the world's most institutionally significant Japanese city could drag on for a full decade without producing a clear resolution.

Section 34.6

A War That Could Not Produce a Decision

Several structural features of the conflict help explain why years of warfare in Kyoto failed to produce a decisive outcome. First, as Section 34.4 emphasized, neither the eastern nor the western army was a unified command structure; each was a coalition of semi-independent houses whose members could, and did, shift allegiance, withdraw from active fighting, or pursue their own local objectives largely independent of their nominal coalition's overall strategy. This made it difficult for either Katsumoto or Sōzen to translate battlefield gains into a lasting political settlement, since a tactical victory over part of the opposing coalition did nothing to guarantee the compliance of the coalition's other members.

Second, many of the participating houses had strong incentives to avoid a truly decisive, existential confrontation. A great house that lost too much of its military strength in Kyoto risked exposing its home provinces to encroachment by rivals or by its own restive retainers — a vulnerability that, as Section 34.8 will discuss, became increasingly significant as the war dragged on and provincial authority grew more consequential relative to standing in the capital. This created a persistent incentive toward cautious, limited engagement rather than the kind of all-out commitment that might have produced a quicker resolution.

Third, logistical strain mounted steadily as the war continued. Sustaining large numbers of warriors and their retinues in and around Kyoto for years at a stretch required enormous quantities of food, supplies, and revenue, much of it extracted, often coercively, from the estates and communities of the surrounding region — placing additional strain on the same shōen estate system and provincial economy examined in earlier chapters, and giving local officials and cultivators further reason to look past Kyoto for effective protection and order.

The war's two principal coalition leaders did not live to see any resolution. Yamana Sōzen died in the third month of 1473, and Hosokawa Katsumoto died a few months later, in the fifth month of the same year. Their deaths removed the two figures most closely associated with the war's origins, but they did not end the fighting, since command of each coalition passed to successors — including, on the Hosokawa side, Katsumoto's son — who inherited the war's momentum without necessarily inheriting the same capacity, or perhaps the same personal stake, to negotiate its end. Later in 1473, Ashikaga Yoshihisa was formally installed as the ninth Ashikaga shogun, at last resolving the shogunal succession question that had been part of the war's original tangle of disputes. Yet this formal resolution illustrates precisely the chapter's central point about the limits of Kyoto politics during this period: settling who held the title of shogun did not, by itself, end a war whose deeper causes lay in the fragmented, coalition-based structure of Muromachi authority rather than in the identity of the shogun alone.

A note on dating and sources. This textbook follows the conventional chronology of the Ōnin War, dated from the outbreak of fighting in Kyoto in 1467 to the withdrawal of armies in 1477 — the dating used in most general histories and textbook treatments of the period. Students should be aware, however, that this Kyoto-centered narrative and its familiar 1467–1477 boundaries owe a considerable debt to later retrospective chronicles, above all the *Ōninki*, whose dramatic framing shaped how generations of readers, and later historians, understood the war's causes, cast of characters, and moral lessons. Recent scholarship, notably by the historian Thomas Conlan, has argued for a broader and differently bounded conflict — one traceable to fighting in western Japan as early as 1465 and continuing, in various theaters, into 1478 — and has assigned a more central role to regional figures such as Ōuchi Masahiro than the conventional Kyoto-focused narrative typically does. This textbook does not adopt that revised framework as its primary account, both because the conventional dating remains the standard reference point in most general and library-level histories and because a full account of the wider western-Japan theater lies beyond this chapter's scope. But students undertaking further research, particularly for the Research or Library Extension below, should understand that the neat 1467–1477 Kyoto narrative is itself a historiographical construction, shaped in part by a later literary chronicle, rather than a transparent and uncontested record of the war's actual extent.

Section 34.7

Kyoto's Destruction and the Human Cost of War

It would be a serious distortion of this chapter's subject to narrate the Ōnin War purely as a sequence of elite succession disputes and shifting alliances among warrior houses, without attending to the war's devastating effect on the ordinary residents, institutions, and physical fabric of Kyoto — the same city whose cultural and commercial vitality Chapters 30 and 31 described in some detail.

Fire was the single most destructive force at work in the war, and it did not discriminate between combatant and non-combatant property. Aristocratic residences that had stood for generations, temple compounds housing important religious communities and irreplaceable artistic and documentary treasures, and the closely packed wooden homes and workshops of ordinary merchants and artisans all burned, sometimes repeatedly, as control of particular districts changed hands. Court aristocrats, whose social and economic position already depended heavily on income from shōen estates increasingly difficult to collect amid provincial disorder, in some cases lost their Kyoto residences entirely and were reduced to seeking shelter with relatives, in temples, or, in a number of documented cases, by relocating to provincial domains where a sympathetic warrior patron could offer protection — a striking reversal of the capital's usual role as a magnet drawing provincial power toward Kyoto.

Merchants and artisans, whose commercial networks and guild organizations Chapter 31 described as a hallmark of Muromachi urban life, faced the destruction or disruption of workshops, warehouses, and trade routes into and out of the city, even as the prolonged presence of large armed forces created new, if unstable, demand for certain goods and services. Displacement was widespread: residents fled fighting in one district only to crowd into neighborhoods that might themselves become contested ground weeks or months later, and many of the capital's ordinary people spent years of the war in a state of repeated relocation, uncertain safety, and economic precarity.

Religious institutions occupied an ambiguous position throughout the conflict. Some temple compounds were fortified and defended by their own resident communities or by allied warrior forces, becoming military targets in the process; others served as places of refuge for displaced civilians and aristocrats, offering a degree of protection that increasingly fragile secular authority could not guarantee. The war's destruction of religious buildings, artworks, and documents represented a loss not only to the communities directly affected but to the broader cultural and historical record of the period, complicating the work of later historians attempting to reconstruct exactly what happened, when, and to whom.

The overall pattern of destruction was uneven rather than total. Some districts and institutions escaped largely unscathed, particularly those fortunate enough to lie outside the main zones of contested fighting, while others were destroyed multiple times over the course of the decade. This unevenness matters historically: it helps explain why Kyoto, though badly damaged, did not simply cease to function as a city or as a center of political and cultural life, even as its population, wealth, and institutional stability were profoundly shaken by a decade of intermittent warfare.

Section 34.8

The Provinces Move Beyond Kyoto's Control

While the great warrior houses' attention and military resources remained absorbed in Kyoto for years on end, developments in the provinces proceeded on their own trajectory, often with little reference to events in the capital. This section identifies the chapter's central conceptual claim: that the Ōnin War's most significant historical consequence was not anything decided in Kyoto's streets, but the accelerated shift of effective political and military authority away from the capital and toward provincial and local actors.

Several overlapping processes contributed to this shift. Shugodai — the deputies whom absentee shugo lords had long relied upon to manage their home provinces while the lords themselves attended to affairs in Kyoto — found themselves, during the war years, exercising an unusually high degree of independent authority, since their nominal superiors were often preoccupied with, or physically absent because of, the fighting in the capital. In some cases, these deputies used the opportunity to consolidate their own local power to such an extent that they became the effective rulers of their territories in practice, whatever their formal subordinate status remained on paper. Local warrior families, religious institutions, and organized leagues of cultivators and lesser warriors likewise found greater room to act autonomously, negotiating, resisting, or ignoring instructions from a Kyoto-based authority that was, for the time being, in no position to enforce compliance.

Historians and, in some cases, contemporaries of the period have used the term gekokujō — commonly rendered as "the low overcoming the high" — to describe this pattern of lower-ranking figures displacing or eclipsing their nominal superiors. The concept is genuinely useful for capturing an important dynamic of the period, and this textbook will use it, but students should apply it carefully rather than treating it as evidence of a sudden, universal social revolution. Gekokujō did not mean that hierarchy disappeared, that every deputy overthrew every lord, or that this pattern unfolded identically or simultaneously across every province of the archipelago. Some great houses retained substantial control over their home territories throughout and after the war; others fragmented severely; the pace and extent of change varied considerably by region, by the specific personalities and circumstances involved, and by how deeply a given house's leadership had been drawn into the Kyoto fighting.

What can be said with more confidence is that the war removed, for an extended period, much of the practical incentive and capacity for provincial actors to defer automatically to Kyoto-based authority. Great houses whose leaders spent years fighting in the capital, often at considerable cost in lives, resources, and prestige, frequently found on their eventual return — if they returned at all — that their home provinces had developed new centers of local power, new patterns of allegiance, and, in some cases, new rulers altogether. This is the environment from which the more openly territorial, self-governing daimyō examined in Chapter 35 would emerge: not created instantaneously by the Ōnin War, but given decisive room to develop by a decade in which the capital's great houses were, in a very real sense, looking the wrong way.

Section 34.9

The End of the War, 1477 — and the Failure to Restore the Old Order

By 1477, a full decade after the outbreak of large-scale fighting, the major armies that had occupied and fought over Kyoto for years began withdrawing from the capital, and the concentrated phase of urban warfare came to an end. This winding-down did not result from a decisive military victory by either coalition, nor from a comprehensive political settlement resolving the disputes that had originally driven the conflict. Instead, it reflected a gradual exhaustion of the will, resources, and coherence needed to continue fighting in Kyoto, combined with the practical reality that many participating houses now had more pressing reasons to attend to developments in their home provinces — the very shift in priorities that Section 34.8 identified as one of the war's most important consequences.

It is essential to state clearly what did not happen in 1477. The Ashikaga shogunate did not collapse or cease to exist. Ashikaga Yoshihisa remained shogun, and after his death the office would continue to pass to further Ashikaga successors for nearly a century afterward, down to the sixteenth-century events examined in Part VI. The imperial court continued to reside in Kyoto and continued to perform its ceremonial, religious, and symbolic functions. The formal offices of the Muromachi government — including the kanrei — continued to exist and to be filled. Any account of the Ōnin War that describes 1477 as "the end of the Ashikaga shogunate" or "the fall of Muromachi Japan" misrepresents the historical record and should be corrected.

What had changed, and changed durably, was the shogunate's practical capacity to compel the great provincial houses — and, increasingly, the lesser warrior families and local powers beneath them — to accept its authority when their own interests pointed elsewhere. The coalition system described in Section 34.1, already under strain before 1467, emerged from the war further weakened: many great houses had suffered military losses, financial exhaustion, and internal fragmentation during their years of involvement in the Kyoto fighting, while their home provinces had, in the meantime, developed new patterns of local power that were not easily reversed simply because the fighting in the capital had ended. The shogunate retained prestige, ceremonial authority, and, in many cases, genuine influence that later chapters will show being invoked and sought after by ambitious warrior leaders for decades to come. But its ability to act as the final, effective arbiter of disputes among the great warrior houses — the core function that had allowed the Muromachi system to hold together for much of the fourteenth and early fifteenth centuries — never fully recovered to its prewar level, even as the shogunate continued to command real, if diminished and increasingly contested, political influence in the decades that followed.

Section 34.10

From Muromachi Fragmentation toward Sengoku Japan

This chapter has argued that the Ōnin War should be understood not as a single catastrophic event that overturned an otherwise stable political order, but as the loud, destructive culmination of longer processes of factional conflict and provincial autonomy already at work within Muromachi Japan — processes the war then accelerated and rendered visible on an unmistakable scale. The war's origins lay in overlapping succession disputes within the shogunal house and within the Hatakeyama and Shiba houses, disputes the shogunate's coalition-based political structure proved unable to arbitrate. Its course was shaped by the loose, shifting nature of the eastern and western coalitions led by Hosokawa Katsumoto and Yamana Sōzen, which made a decisive military resolution difficult to achieve even after a decade of fighting. Its human cost fell heavily on the ordinary residents, merchants, artisans, and religious communities of Kyoto, whose city was repeatedly burned and whose lives were repeatedly disrupted by a conflict fought largely by and for the warrior elite. And its most significant long-term consequence was the accelerated transfer of effective political and military authority from a Kyoto-centered coalition system toward increasingly autonomous provincial actors — shugodai, local warrior families, religious institutions, and armed leagues — operating with diminishing reference to shogunal authority.

None of this happened instantaneously, uniformly, or completely by 1477. The Ashikaga shogunate persisted as an institution, the imperial court remained in place, and the transition toward the more openly decentralized, competitive political world that later chapters will call Sengoku Japan continued to unfold unevenly across different provinces over the following decades, shaped by local circumstances as much as by anything decided in Kyoto. Some regions saw relatively rapid consolidation of new territorial authority; others experienced continued fragmentation and conflict for much longer. The label "Sengoku," meaning roughly "Warring States" and borrowed from a much earlier period of Chinese history, is a useful periodization tool for describing this broader trend, but students should understand its beginning as a gradual, conventional marker rather than a precise date on which Japan suddenly became a different kind of political world.

What emerged from this environment — new kinds of territorial rulers governing consolidated domains through their own administrative structures, law codes, and military organizations, largely independent of shogunal oversight even while sometimes still seeking its titles and legitimation — is the subject to which Chapter 35 now turns. That chapter examines the Sengoku daimyō not as inheritors of a settled new order, but as the political actors who, over the following century, built lasting territorial power out of precisely the kind of provincial opportunity and central weakness this chapter has described.

Historical Vocabulary
Gekokujō

Gekokujō ("the low overcoming the high") describes the pattern, especially associated with the later fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, in which lower-ranking figures — deputies, retainers, local warrior families — displaced or eclipsed their nominal superiors in practical authority. The term captures something real about the Ōnin War's aftermath, but it should not be treated as describing a sudden, universal, or evenly paced social transformation. Its usefulness lies in naming a recognizable pattern, not in implying that hierarchy itself disappeared or that every province experienced the same kind of change at the same time.

Historical Vocabulary
Shugodai

A shugodai was a deputy who administered a province, or provincial affairs, on behalf of its shugo (provincial military governor), particularly when the shugo resided elsewhere or in Kyoto for extended periods. Because a single shugo might hold appointments across several provinces at once, shugodai were often essential to actually governing any one of them day to day. During the Ōnin War, many shugodai exercised unusually independent authority while their nominal superiors remained absorbed in the fighting in the capital, and some used this position to build lasting local power of their own — an important, though not universal, mechanism behind the provincial autonomy discussed in Section 34.8.

Contemporary Accounts of Kyoto's Destruction

Type: Composite textbook paraphrase drawn from the general themes of surviving aristocratic diaries and later chronicle literature describing wartime Kyoto, including the retrospective narrative chronicle known as the Ōninki; this is not a translation or presentation of any single identified document or passage. · Date of underlying material: diary entries composed during the war years, 1467–1477; the Ōninki chronicle itself was compiled later, drawing on and reshaping earlier material.

Context: Aristocratic diarists living in or near Kyoto during the Ōnin War recorded, often with evident shock, the destruction of familiar landmarks, the flight of neighbors and relatives, and the disruption of the ceremonial and cultural life that had defined the capital for generations. Much of the popular narrative of the war, however — including many of its most vivid scenes and its portrait of particular figures — reaches modern readers through the Ōninki, a later chronicle whose author shaped scattered events into a coherent, dramatic story. Historians such as Thomas Conlan have shown that the Ōninki is a constructed narrative with its own literary and moral purposes, not a transparent eyewitness transcript, and that its framing has strongly influenced later popular and even scholarly understanding of the war. This textbook therefore presents only a general paraphrase of recurring themes across this body of material, rather than treating any single passage as an unmediated firsthand account.

Paraphrase of a recurring theme across diary and chronicle material (not a translation or verbatim quotation of any single source): Writers describing the war repeatedly evoked a capital they scarcely recognized: familiar streets reduced to ash, once-grand residences gone, and a pervasive sense that the ordered world of court ceremony and settled residence had been overtaken by fire and armed men.

Analysis: This composite theme is useful for conveying the general emotional and material impact of the war on Kyoto's literate elite, but it should not be mistaken for a specific, verifiable eyewitness statement. It also says comparatively little about the experience of the city's merchants, artisans, and laborers, whose perspectives survive far less directly in the documentary record, and it is filtered through the retrospective, dramatized lens of chronicles like the Ōninki, compiled after the events they describe. Historians must supplement such material with other kinds of evidence — dated diary entries, property and temple records, and modern critical scholarship on how the war's narrative was later constructed — to reconstruct a fuller and more reliable picture of the war's impact across Kyoto's social spectrum.

Historical Debate

Did the Ōnin War Cause the Sengoku Order, or Accelerate It?

The question: Historians broadly agree that the decades following the Ōnin War saw the emergence of a more decentralized, territorially organized political world. They disagree, in emphasis if not always in substance, about how directly the war itself should be credited with producing that outcome.

Interpretation A — The war as a genuine turning point: Some historians emphasize that the scale and duration of the Ōnin War, and the concentrated damage it inflicted on the great houses that fought it, mark a real discontinuity: many kanrei-level houses never recovered their former standing, and the vacuum of authority the war created in Kyoto opened space for provincial actors that might not otherwise have emerged so quickly or so decisively.
Interpretation B — The war as an accelerant of existing trends: Other historians emphasize that provincial autonomy, weakening shogunal arbitration, and factional fragmentation were already well underway before 1467, as Chapters 29 and this chapter's own Historical Context have shown, and argue that the Ōnin War is best understood as a dramatic acceleration and exposure of trends already in motion rather than as their fundamental cause.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on the same underlying evidence — the coalition structure of Muromachi government, the pre-1467 succession disputes, and the postwar pattern of provincial consolidation — but weigh the war's independent causal contribution differently.

Why it matters: How historians answer this question shapes how they periodize the transition from "medieval" to "Sengoku" Japan, and how much explanatory weight they place on contingent events, such as a single unexpected royal birth, versus longer-term structural conditions.

Student task: Identify one piece of evidence from this chapter that supports each interpretation, and explain which standard of historical causation — a discrete triggering event, or a long-building structural condition — you find more persuasive for explaining the shift toward Sengoku Japan.

Multiple Perspectives

Living through the Ōnin War

A shogunal official in Kyoto: Watched the shogunate's traditional tools of arbitration fail repeatedly, as rival houses pursued their disputes through force rather than accepting mediated settlements, and struggled to maintain the appearance of central authority amid a war the shogunate could neither end nor decisively win.
A great warrior lord aligned with one of the coalitions: Balanced the pursuit of advantage in Kyoto against the risk that prolonged absence and military losses might weaken control over home provinces, weighing personal and family standing in the capital against long-term territorial security.
A lower-ranking retainer or shugodai: Found, amid the chaos and absence of close supervision from Kyoto-based superiors, expanded opportunities to exercise independent authority over local affairs — opportunities some seized permanently, in the pattern historians describe as gekokujō.
A Kyoto merchant or artisan: Faced repeated disruption of trade, destruction of workshops and homes, and the constant uncertainty of living amid shifting front lines, while adapting commercial activity as best as possible to a city under prolonged, intermittent siege from within.
A court aristocrat dependent on shōen income: Experienced both the direct destruction of Kyoto residences and the indirect erosion of income from provincial estates, whose administration became increasingly difficult to enforce as local authority slipped further from aristocratic or shogunal control.
A resident of a temple compound: Lived through the ambiguous role of religious institutions in the conflict — sometimes fortified and drawn into the fighting, sometimes serving as a refuge for displaced civilians and aristocrats seeking safety that secular authority could no longer reliably guarantee.
Continuity and Change Over Time

Institutions That Survived

What Changed

  • The shogunate's practical capacity to arbitrate disputes among great warrior houses was severely and durably weakened
  • Effective authority in many provinces shifted toward shugodai, local warrior families, and other actors operating with diminished reference to Kyoto
  • Much of Kyoto's urban fabric, including significant aristocratic and religious property, was destroyed or displaced
  • Several great houses suffered lasting internal fragmentation or loss of standing as a direct result of the war

What Stayed the Same

  • The Ashikaga shogunate continued to exist as an institution for nearly another century
  • The imperial court and its ceremonial functions continued in Kyoto
  • Formal offices such as the kanrei continued to be filled, even as their practical authority diminished
  • Kyoto remained a significant political, religious, and cultural center, despite severe damage and disruption

Why the pattern matters: Treating institutional survival and effective-authority decline as two separate, if related, developments prevents students from oversimplifying 1477 as either "nothing changed" or "everything collapsed."

Cause and Effect

From Succession Dispute to Civil War

Long-term causes

A coalition-based Muromachi political structure dependent on the internal stability of a handful of great warrior houses, combined with decades of provincial strain and unresolved tension between central and local authority.

Short-term causes

Overlapping and unresolved succession disputes within the shogunal, Hatakeyama, and Shiba houses during the 1450s and 1460s, converging with the rival ambitions of Hosokawa Katsumoto and Yamana Sōzen.

Effect

A decade of intermittent, destructive, and ultimately indecisive urban warfare centered on Kyoto, 1467–1477.

Long-term consequence

Accelerated erosion of shogunal arbitrating authority and durable shift of effective political and military power toward provincial actors, creating the conditions from which the Sengoku daimyō examined in Chapter 35 emerged.

Historical complexity: No single cause is sufficient to explain the war; structural weakness, contingent events such as Yoshihisa's birth, and the choices of individual leaders all interacted to produce the outcome historians observe.

Everyday Life and Environment

Living in a Capital at War

For the warrior elites whose disputes drove the Ōnin War, the conflict was largely a matter of alliance, strategy, and status. For the far larger number of ordinary people who lived in Kyoto throughout the war, daily life was reorganized around a very different set of concerns: the constant risk of fire spreading from a nearby fortified compound; the need to judge, sometimes on short notice, whether a given street or district was safe to remain in or ought to be abandoned; the disruption of markets and workshops on which household income depended; and the search for shelter, whether with relatives, in a temple compound, or in whatever housing remained standing in a less contested part of the city.

Displacement often meant not a single relocation but a repeated cycle of flight and return, as the front lines of the conflict shifted over months and years rather than settling into a stable division. Property abandoned during one phase of the fighting might be reoccupied by others, reclaimed later by its original residents if they survived and returned, or lost permanently to fire or seizure. Religious institutions that survived intact became especially important as informal centers of refuge, charity, and continuity amid the disorder, a role that reinforced their social significance even as some temple compounds were simultaneously drawn into the military conflict as fortified positions. Understanding this texture of ordinary experience is essential to appreciating that the Ōnin War was not simply a political event conducted at a remove from everyday life, but a sustained disaster that reshaped the daily circumstances of Kyoto's population for a full decade and beyond.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the Ōnin War of 1467–1477 not as an isolated catastrophe but as the destructive culmination of long-building weaknesses within the Muromachi political order first described in Chapter 29. The Ashikaga shogunate's authority had always depended on negotiated cooperation among a small number of powerful shugo houses, an arrangement that functioned well for much of the fourteenth and early fifteenth centuries but that proved fragile once several of those houses were themselves convulsed by internal succession disputes they could not resolve.

Ashikaga Yoshimasa's own succession problem — the initial designation of his brother Yoshimi as heir, followed by the unexpected birth of his son Yoshihisa — provided one important thread in this crisis, but it was not its sole cause. Parallel and arguably more consequential succession disputes within the Hatakeyama and Shiba houses demonstrated that the shogunate's capacity to arbitrate conflict among the great warrior families had already become unreliable before large-scale fighting began.

Hosokawa Katsumoto and Yamana Sōzen, the two figures most closely associated with the war, led not unified national armies but loose, shifting coalitions of allied houses whose participation depended on calculations of self-interest and existing rivalry. This coalition structure helps explain why fighting that broke out in Kyoto in 1467 continued, largely inconclusively, for a full decade, punctuated by shifting alliances, failed negotiations, and repeated destruction rather than by any single decisive battle.

The war inflicted severe and often indiscriminate damage on Kyoto's urban fabric, displacing aristocrats, merchants, artisans, and religious communities and destroying irreplaceable buildings, artworks, and documents. This human and material cost fell heavily on residents who had little direct stake in the succession disputes driving the conflict, and it disrupted the cultural and commercial vitality of Kyoto examined in earlier chapters.

The deaths of Yamana Sōzen and Hosokawa Katsumoto in 1473, and the formal succession of Ashikaga Yoshihisa as shogun that same year, resolved the shogunal question but did not end the wider war, which continued for four more years under new leadership on both sides. The eventual withdrawal of armies from Kyoto in 1477 ended the concentrated phase of urban fighting without producing a decisive victor or restoring the pre-1467 political balance.

Throughout and after the war, effective political and military authority shifted increasingly toward provincial actors — shugodai, local warrior families, religious institutions, and armed leagues — who exercised growing autonomy while the great houses' attention remained absorbed in Kyoto. This pattern, sometimes described using the term gekokujō, unfolded unevenly across different regions rather than as a single, uniform transformation.

Crucially, this chapter has emphasized that the Ashikaga shogunate did not collapse in 1477. The shogunate, the imperial court, and the formal offices of Muromachi government all persisted, in some cases for nearly another century. What changed durably was the shogunate's practical capacity to compel obedience and arbitrate disputes among the great provincial houses, a capacity that never fully recovered to its prewar level, even though the institution retained real legitimacy and continuing, if diminished, influence.

The chapter has also presented a genuine historiographical debate over whether the Ōnin War should be understood as a discrete cause of the Sengoku political order or as an accelerant of trends toward decentralization already underway. Both interpretations draw on the same underlying evidence, and students have been asked to weigh the relative explanatory power of contingent events versus long-building structural conditions.

Finally, the chapter has situated the Ōnin War as the hinge between Part V's treatment of Ashikaga Japan and the maritime world and Part VI's treatment of the Sengoku daimyō. The provincial opportunity and central weakness described here did not by themselves constitute a new political order; they created the conditions from which new kinds of territorial rulers, examined in the next chapter, would construct one.

Identification & Significance

Ashikaga Yoshimasa
Eighth Ashikaga shogun; his succession problem was one factor among several driving the Ōnin War; also a major cultural patron.
Ashikaga Yoshimi
Yoshimasa's brother and initial heir-designate, drawn into factional alignment after Yoshihisa's birth.
Ashikaga Yoshihisa
Yoshimasa's son; became ninth shogun in 1473, though his succession did not end the war.
Hosokawa Katsumoto
Kanrei and head of the Hosokawa house; leader of the eastern army; died 1473.
Yamana Sōzen
Head of the Yamana house; leader of the western army; died 1473; Katsumoto's father-in-law.
Hino Tomiko
Yoshimasa's wife and mother of Yoshihisa, an active political figure whose alignment shifted with the war's changing coalitions rather than remaining fixed to a single camp throughout.
Hatakeyama house
Great shugo house whose succession dispute, between Masanaga and Yoshinari, provided the immediate spark for the war's outbreak in 1467.
Hatakeyama Masanaga and Hatakeyama Yoshinari
Rival claimants to Hatakeyama headship whose clash at the Kami Goryō Shrine in 1467 ignited the wider war.
Shiba house
Great shugo house whose parallel succession dispute, between Yoshitoshi and Yoshikado, compounded the crisis.
Kyoto
Imperial capital and shogunal seat, transformed by the war into a repeatedly burned battleground while remaining a center of political and cultural life.
Kanrei office
The senior shogunal deputy position, rotated among the Hosokawa, Shiba, and Hatakeyama houses; its disputed occupancy was itself a source of tension.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has carried forward the political story begun in Chapter 29 to its most destructive and consequential episode within Ashikaga Japan: a decade of warfare that neither settled the disputes that caused it nor destroyed the institutions that hosted it, but that permanently altered the balance of power between Kyoto and the provinces. Understanding the Ōnin War requires holding together its immediate human catastrophe — a great capital repeatedly burned, its residents displaced, its cultural treasures destroyed — with its slower, structural significance as an accelerant of decentralization already underway.

The chapter's insistence that the shogunate survived 1477, even as its effective authority did not recover, matters for how students should read the century that follows. Sengoku Japan, examined beginning in Chapter 35, did not emerge from a political vacuum in which the old order had simply vanished. It emerged from a landscape in which old titles, old institutions, and old claims to legitimacy persisted alongside new, increasingly autonomous centers of provincial power — a landscape considerably more complicated, and more historically interesting, than a simple story of collapse and replacement.

The chapter has also reinforced a theme recurring throughout this textbook: that political history cannot be told responsibly without attention to the ordinary people who bear its costs. The merchants, artisans, temple communities, and displaced aristocrats of wartime Kyoto experienced the Ōnin War not as a chapter in a succession dispute but as a sustained disaster reshaping the basic conditions of daily life. Their experience belongs in the historical record alongside the maneuvers of Katsumoto, Sōzen, and the Ashikaga house.

Students who understand the Ōnin War as a hinge rather than a hinge-point catastrophe — a moment that exposed and accelerated long-building change rather than instantly creating a new political world — are well prepared to move forward into Chapter 35, where the textbook examines the new kinds of territorial rulers who would spend the following century building lasting power out of the opportunity this chapter has described.
