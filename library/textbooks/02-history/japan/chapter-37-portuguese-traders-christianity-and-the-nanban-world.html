Part VI — Sengoku Japan and Reunification
37

Portuguese Traders, Christianity, and the Nanban World

Japanese History: From Jōmon Japan to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

In the decades after the 1540s, ships built in a European style and crewed by Portuguese, Indians, Southeast Asians, Africans, and Chinese began to anchor each year off the coast of Kyushu. Japanese observers had a word for the newcomers who arrived from the southern sea: Nanban. What they did, and what Japanese people did with them, is the subject of this chapter.

According to the best-known Japanese account, a Chinese-owned vessel carrying Portuguese passengers was driven ashore at Tanegashima, an island south of Kyushu, in the early 1540s. The local lord, Tanegashima Tokitaka, is said to have acquired two firearms from the visitors. Because this story is so often retold, it is easy to imagine that Japan's modern encounter with the wider world began on that beach. This chapter argues the opposite. Sixteenth-century Japan was already linked by ships, merchants, pirates, monks, and diplomats to China, Korea, the Ryukyu Kingdom, and Southeast Asia. The Portuguese did not open a closed country. They entered, and eventually reshaped parts of, a maritime system that Japanese and Chinese merchants had long used.

What the Portuguese did bring was a new combination of things. They brought a commercial network stretching from Lisbon through the Indian Ocean to Goa, Malacca, and eventually Macao. They brought goods and technologies that were new or newly abundant in Japan. Most distinctively, they brought missionaries, above all the Jesuits, whose arrival in 1549 introduced Christianity to an archipelago whose religious landscape was already crowded with Buddhist institutions, shrine cults, mountain ascetics, and local devotions. These missionaries were dependent on Portuguese ships and on Japanese patrons, yet they were not the same thing as Portuguese merchants, and their interests often diverged.

Japanese people were not passive recipients of any of this. Kyushu daimyō competed to attract ships because port revenue and imported goods could change a domain's fortunes. Merchants in Hirado, Hakata, Sakai, and later Nagasaki calculated risks and profits. Some samurai, townspeople, and peasants became Christians for reasons that ranged from sincere conviction to social obligation, and Japanese catechists, translators, and patrons were indispensable to the growth of the church. Buddhist clerics argued against the foreigners, court and warlord authorities alternately tolerated and restricted them, and artists depicted the newcomers with curiosity and some ambivalence. The cultural world that resulted, later called Nanban, was a product of negotiation.

This chapter occupies a specific place in Part VI. Chapter 35 examined the regional states built by Sengoku daimyō, and Chapter 36 explained how those states transformed warfare, including the arquebus as a weapon. The present chapter treats the firearm only as one commodity within a much broader encounter; matchlock mechanics and battlefield tactics belong to Chapter 36. Tea and its aesthetics, including the politics of imported goods in tea practice, belong to Chapter 38. Oda Nobunaga's centralizing campaigns belong to Chapter 39, and he appears here only as one Sengoku ruler who engaged with missionaries and merchants. Chapters 31 through 33 supply the earlier history of Japanese commerce and of Ryukyu, which this chapter builds on without repeating.

The evidence for this encounter is unusually rich, but it is unevenly distributed and often one-sided. Jesuit letters, histories, and reports, written in Portuguese, Spanish, Italian, and Latin for superiors and donors in Europe, describe Japan in vivid detail and with clear purposes. Japanese sources, including domain records, diaries, temple documents, and later accounts such as the Tanegashima narrative, are sparser on these matters. Chinese records, Portuguese crown and trade documents, Japanese folding screens, printed books, and surviving objects fill in parts of the picture. Throughout the chapter, students should ask who is speaking, to whom, and what they wanted their readers to believe.

By the chapter's end, students should understand the Nanban world as a history of selective connection. Portuguese trade was profitable, Christianity acquired substantial communities, and European and Asian goods circulated in Kyushu and beyond. Yet rulers also grew anxious about autonomous religious loyalties, commercial dependence, and the links between missionaries and expanding Iberian empires. The chapter ends at that point of tension and looks forward, without narrating the full story of suppression, which belongs to later chapters.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how sixteenth-century Japan was already embedded in East Asian maritime networks before the Portuguese arrived.
  2. Trace Portuguese expansion from Goa and Malacca to Japanese waters and assess the conventional 1543 Tanegashima account as a source.
  3. Analyze the Portuguese-Chinese-Japanese trade triangle and the significance of silk and silver within it.
  4. Compare how Hirado, Bungo, Nagasaki, and other ports competed for trade and how commerce affected local political power.
  5. Describe the goals, methods, and misunderstandings of Francis Xavier and the early Jesuit mission.
  6. Distinguish individual religious conversion from daimyō policy, and evaluate multiple motives for conversion.
  7. Assess the roles of Ōmura Sumitada, Ōtomo Sōrin, Arima Harunobu, and the port of Nagasaki in regional politics.
  8. Interpret Buddhist, shrine, court, and Christian responses to religious competition, including conflict and accommodation.
  9. Evaluate Nanban material culture as selective adaptation and hybridization rather than wholesale Westernization.
  10. Distinguish Portuguese commercial interests from Jesuit missionary interests and explain why the two overlapped without being identical.
  11. Critique missionary sources as evidence, identifying the writer's purposes, audience, and blind spots.
  12. Contextualize the growing political anxiety about Christianity and trade as a prelude to later restrictions.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How did Portuguese trade and Jesuit Christianity enter Japan's existing maritime world, and why did rulers and communities respond to these connections in such different ways?
  2. In what sense was Japan "already connected" before 1543, and what was genuinely new about Portuguese contact?
  3. Why did the Portuguese position as intermediaries between Chinese and Japanese markets matter more than any single European commodity?
  4. How did access to overseas trade reshape the competition among Kyushu daimyō and port communities?
  5. To what extent were trade and mission connected, and why should they not be treated as a single project?
  6. Why is it misleading to explain Christian conversion in Japan through material self-interest alone?
  7. How did Christianity interact with an existing religious landscape of Buddhist, shrine, and local traditions?
  8. What does Nanban art and material culture reveal about Japanese selection, adaptation, and representation of foreigners?
  9. How reliable are missionary letters as evidence for Japanese society, and what voices are missing from them?

Historical Context

By the middle of the sixteenth century, the political order that had governed Japan for centuries had largely dissolved into regional competition. As earlier chapters explained, the Ashikaga shogunate's ability to exercise consistent nationwide authority weakened sharply after the Ōnin War (Chapter 34), although shogunal government continued to perform judicial and political functions, and Sengoku daimyō built territorial states with their own castles, taxation, law, and armies (Chapters 35 and 36). In western Japan, and especially in Kyushu, daimyō such as the Ōtomo, Shimazu, Ryūzōji, Ōmura, Arima, and Matsura competed with one another and with shifting alliances of retainers and local lords. Because no central authority consistently controlled foreign trade across Japan, each coastal lord could pursue his own relations with overseas merchants. This fragmentation is essential context for the Nanban encounter: it meant that the Portuguese encountered many Japans, not one.

Commercial and maritime life was also well developed. As Chapters 31 through 33 showed, ports such as Hakata, Sakai, Bōnotsu, and Hyōgo handled coastal and overseas shipping, and merchant organizations, money, and credit had grown through the medieval period. Japan had traded with Ming China through an official tally system that ended in the 1540s, with Korea through the Sō family of Tsushima, and with the Ryukyu Kingdom, whose ships linked Japan, China, and Southeast Asia. A less regulated world of Chinese merchants, Japanese shippers, and armed seaborne traders, often called wakō in Chinese and Korean sources, filled the gap left by Ming restrictions. Silver mining also expanded sharply in the sixteenth century, as new refining methods raised output in Japanese mines. Japan was becoming a significant source of a metal in great demand in China.

Religious life was equally dense. Temple-shrine complexes held land, armies, and markets; Zen, Pure Land, Nichiren, and Shingon communities competed for patrons; and mountain ascetics, local shrines, household rites, and healing cults shaped everyday devotion. Concepts of kami, buddhas, and ancestral spirits were interwoven rather than sharply separated. Christianity arrived into this setting, not into a religious void, and Japanese audiences evaluated it through the categories they already had. Students should keep in mind the peoples beyond Kyushu as well. Ainu communities in Ezo had their own trade links to the north and with the Matsumae; the Ryukyu Kingdom had its own relations with Europeans in Malacca, where Ryukyuan merchants were known to the Portuguese as "Gores." The Nanban world touched Japan from many directions, not only through Kyushu.

Section 37.1

Japan before the Portuguese: An Existing Maritime World

A textbook that begins Japan's international history in 1543 misrepresents the evidence. By 1500 Japanese ships, merchants, and envoys had travelled the waters of East Asia for centuries. The Ashikaga shogunate and powerful western families such as the Ōuchi had sent missions to Ming China under the tally system, in which official embassies carried tribute and traded under licence. Rivalry over these missions turned violent at Ningbo in 1523, when followers of the Ōuchi and Hosokawa clashed, and the Ming court's response was to restrict Japanese access. The last tally mission sailed in 1547. The end of the official system did not end the demand for trade; it moved commerce into less regulated channels.

Those channels were busy. Chinese merchants, many defying the Ming prohibition on private overseas trade, operated from the Fujian and Zhejiang coasts to Japanese ports. Some, such as the shipowner Wang Zhi, based themselves in the Gotō islands and at Hirado, where local lords welcomed their cargoes. Japanese and Chinese crews, sometimes mixed, also engaged in raiding and smuggling along the coast of China; the term wakō in Chinese sources covers a varied population, and modern scholars stress that many so-called Japanese pirates were Chinese or of mixed origin. Meanwhile the Sō family of Tsushima managed trade and diplomacy with Korea, and Ryukyuan ships, as Chapter 32 explained, carried goods between Naha, Fujian, Southeast Asia, and Japanese ports such as Hakata and Bōnotsu.

Japanese domestic developments also shaped the maritime economy. Silver production expanded after the introduction of improved refining technology in the 1530s, traditionally linked to a Hakata merchant and Korean technicians and first applied at the Iwami mines. China's monetary system increasingly depended on silver, and Japanese silver found a ready market. Japan in turn wanted Chinese raw silk, silk textiles, porcelain, medicines, and other goods. The structure of this demand would determine much of what the Portuguese could do in Japan. Their success came less from the novelty of their own products than from their ability to connect an existing Chinese supply with an existing Japanese demand at a time when official channels were closed.

Sources for this earlier world are fragmentary: Chinese official histories and coastal defence writings, Korean records, Ryukyuan diplomatic documents, Japanese merchant and temple records, and archaeological finds of Chinese ceramics at Japanese port sites. They are less vivid than Jesuit letters, and this contrast itself shapes the popular view. The Portuguese wrote a great deal about Japan because they were foreigners writing for European audiences; Japanese and Chinese merchants wrote little because nothing about their trade seemed remarkable. Students should therefore treat the abundance of European documentation as a feature of the sources, not as a measure of historical importance.

Section 37.2

The Portuguese Arrive

Portuguese expansion into Asia was driven by a mixture of crown, noble, commercial, and religious ambitions. After Vasco da Gama's voyage reached India in 1498, the Portuguese sought to control the pepper and spice trade by seizing or building fortified bases. They took Goa on the Indian coast in 1510 and Malacca, the great entrepôt of the strait between the Malay Peninsula and Sumatra, in 1511. From these bases, Portuguese and Luso-Asian traders, many operating privately rather than directly for the crown, moved along Southeast Asian and Chinese coasts. They relied heavily on local knowledge, pilots, languages, and partners. At Malacca they met Ryukyuan merchants, and they learned of Japan from Chinese and Southeast Asian informants before any Portuguese reached it. The Portuguese, in short, arrived in Japanese waters through Asian commercial circuits, not directly from Europe.

The conventional date for the first recorded Portuguese presence in Japan is 1543, on Tanegashima, an island off southern Kyushu. The principal Japanese source, the Teppōki ("Account of the Firearm"), was written by the Zen monk Nanpo Bunshi in the early seventeenth century, at the request of the Tanegashima family. It relates that a large ship with foreign passengers arrived; that a Chinese member of the company, identified in the text as Gohō, communicated with Nanpo by writing characters; and that the lord Tokitaka bought two guns and had local smiths study them. Portuguese writers of the 1550s and 1560s give different versions, and modern historians debate the exact year and circumstances. Because the Japanese text was composed decades after the event and partly to glorify a local family, it is best read as tradition with a probable factual core rather than as eyewitness documentation. One point in it deserves emphasis: the ship was Chinese, the interpreters were Chinese, and the Portuguese were passengers in a Chinese-run maritime system.

Later sixteenth-century Portuguese contact spread outward from this event along southern Kyushu. By about 1550 Portuguese ships were calling at Hirado, and soon afterwards at ports in Bungo and Satsuma. The first visitors were few, and their arrivals were irregular. Only after the Portuguese obtained a settlement at Macao in the 1550s, with Chinese local authorities' permission and under Chinese sovereignty, did a regular shipping route to Japan become practical. The Portuguese also encountered, as they moved north, Spanish competition; the Spanish established themselves at Manila in 1571, and Spanish ships began appearing in Japanese ports in the 1580s. Europeans in Japanese waters were never a single party, and Japanese use of the label Nanban (section 37.9 and the vocabulary box below) eventually covered Spaniards and Southeast Asians as well.

Firearms appear in this story only as one item. Portuguese guns reached Japan, and Japanese smiths, working from earlier models and Southeast Asian and Chinese techniques, began producing copies rapidly. The military and tactical significance of that development is the subject of Chapter 36, and students should turn there for manufacture, deployment, and battlefield impact. For present purposes, what matters is that the firearm story illustrates how Japanese actors, in this case a local lord with access to smiths and a reason to experiment, turned a foreign object to regional advantage. The same pattern, of Japanese actors adopting and adapting foreign offerings to their own circumstances, recurs throughout this chapter.

Section 37.3

The Nanban Trade

The trade that historians call the Nanban trade was above all a triangular exchange among China, Japan, and the Portuguese. After the end of the tally system and in the face of Ming bans on private overseas trade and, specifically, on Chinese voyages to Japan, direct commerce between the two largest economies in East Asia was legally obstructed. The Portuguese, who had no such prohibition against trading with both sides and who eventually had a base at Macao, became intermediaries. At the Canton fairs, held periodically, they purchased Chinese raw silk, silk textiles, and other goods. They carried these to Japan, where demand for silk was high among elites and for use in the textile industry, and where silver was plentiful. They sold their cargoes for Japanese silver and returned to Macao, where the silver financed further purchases in China. Chinese merchants continued to trade directly with Japan through unofficial channels, so the Portuguese did not monopolize silk; rather, they provided a reliable, large-scale, and relatively secure channel, particularly after the 1570s when a large annual ship sailed regularly from Macao to Nagasaki.

Silver was the crucial Japanese export. Output from Iwami and other mines grew through the sixteenth and early seventeenth centuries to a scale that, by some estimates, made Japan one of the world's leading silver producers, although the precise share of global output is uncertain and should be treated as approximate. The silver flowed mainly to China, where it was in great demand, and it helped to link Japan's domestic economy to a global circulation of the metal that also included Spanish American silver moving across the Pacific to Manila. Japan also exported copper, sulphur, swords, lacquerware, and folding screens, and Japanese merchants, like Chinese ones, profited from this commerce. The Portuguese, moreover, bought Japanese goods both for sale in China and for their own use, including fine lacquerware made to European tastes.

What the Portuguese carried besides silk is worth listing, but it should not overshadow the main exchange. They brought Indian and Southeast Asian textiles, hides, sappanwood for dye, tin, and lead and saltpetre, which mattered for gunpowder weapons; they brought medicines, glassware, clocks, and curiosities such as spectacles and mirrors, many of which were presented as gifts to rulers. The gift economy was an important part of the trade: a daimyō's favour could grant access to a port, and Jesuits and merchants alike brought presents to secure it. Food and plants also moved along these routes, though the chronology of introductions, such as tobacco and various crops, is complex and later than the period considered here. The term "Nanban trade" is thus a convenient label for a wider flow of Asian and European goods, and most of the cargo was Asian in origin.

Trade arrangements were managed by the Portuguese crown through a system in which the right to command the annual Japan voyage was granted as a privilege, and in which merchants, the captain-major, and, significantly, the Jesuits all took a share. From the later 1570s the Jesuits participated in the silk trade to help finance the mission, an arrangement that Valignano supported and that other orders and some officials criticized. It gave the mission a material base and exposed it to accusations that it was, in part, a commercial enterprise. The episode makes plain that Portuguese commercial interests and Jesuit religious interests overlapped in practice while remaining different in purpose. Merchants wanted profit, the Jesuits wanted souls and, in order to get them, revenue and the goodwill of daimyō who controlled ports.

Section 37.4

Ports, Merchants, and Maritime Competition

Because the Portuguese came in large ships that could call at only a few sheltered harbours, and because Kyushu daimyō controlled those harbours, port competition became a central feature of the Nanban era. A daimyō who secured a Portuguese ship gained customs revenue, access to silk, the goodwill of merchants who resold goods, and sometimes weapons and materials useful in war. He also attracted Chinese and Japanese merchants who wanted to deal with the ship, which multiplied the benefit. Conversely, a port that lost the ship lost trade, revenue, and standing. The history of the port towns, therefore, is also a history of rivalry among local lords, in which commercial and strategic calculation, and eventually religious policy, were intertwined.

Hirado, in the northwest, was the first major harbour. The Matsura family had long hosted Chinese merchants, and Portuguese ships began to call there around 1550. The relationship was not smooth. A violent quarrel in 1561, known to historians by the name of a local district, led to the deaths of several Portuguese, and the Portuguese thereafter looked for alternative harbours. In the Bungo region, Ōtomo Sōrin's domain had the port at Funai and its outer harbours, where Portuguese ships and Jesuits were welcomed from the early 1550s. In Satsuma, the Shimazu, who had received Xavier in 1549, were initially interested in trade but later grew more reserved toward the missionaries. Elsewhere, Sakai, the commercial city near Osaka Bay, participated in the trade as a hub for goods and for the manufacture of weapons. Hakata, long a centre of Sino-Japanese commerce, suffered repeatedly in regional wars and declined in relative importance for a time.

Ōmura Sumitada and Arima Harunobu, rulers of small domains in the Nagasaki and Shimabara areas, were the most active in offering their harbours. After the Hirado rupture, Sumitada opened the port of Yokoseura in 1562, but it was burned in an attack by opponents in 1563. He then tried Fukuda, and finally Nagasaki in 1570–1571, where he granted land and privileges to attract Portuguese ships. The Arima family, for their part, hosted ships at Kuchinotsu on the Shimabara peninsula in the late 1560s. These moves show smaller domains using harbours to gain resources and leverage against more powerful neighbours such as the Ryūzōji. The decision to accept Jesuit missionaries, and sometimes to convert, was part of this competition, because missionaries could influence where the ships anchored.

The merchants themselves, Japanese and Chinese, were central figures whose names are seldom recorded. Japanese merchants of Sakai and Hakata bought silk and sold silver, financed ships, and served as brokers and interpreters. Chinese traders remained active in Hirado and other ports and continued to supply silk directly. The Portuguese, who were a small presence in Japan itself, relied on local intermediaries for housing, credit, and cargo. The port town, in other words, was a multiethnic economic community, and its politics involved daimyō, merchant leaders, missionaries, and foreign captains. Nagasaki, which became the dominant harbour, exemplifies all of these relationships and receives a fuller treatment in sections 37.7 and the Everyday Life feature.

Section 37.5

Francis Xavier and the Jesuit Mission

The Society of Jesus was founded in 1540 and quickly became a missionary order. Francis Xavier, one of its founders, sailed to Portuguese Asia under the protection of the Portuguese crown, which held patronage rights over missions in its sphere (the padroado). In Malacca in 1547 he met a Japanese man named Anjirō, who had fled trouble at home, and who, after instruction at Goa, was baptized as Paulo de Santa Fé. Anjirō's accounts convinced Xavier that Japan would be receptive, and he and two Jesuit companions, Cosme de Torres and Juan Fernández, sailed with Anjirō, reaching Kagoshima in August 1549. The Shimazu lord Takahisa gave them permission to preach, but the mission made little progress, and Xavier soon moved on to Hirado, Yamaguchi, and Kyoto. He found the capital in disarray and left it without an audience. In Yamaguchi, under the Ōuchi, and later in Bungo, he received a more favourable reception. He left Japan in 1551 and died in 1552 off the coast of China, without ever establishing a large church.

Xavier's experience exposed the difficulties of the mission. Language was the first barrier; with Anjirō's help the Jesuits used Japanese terms for Christian concepts, and in the early months they used the Buddhist term Dainichi for God before realizing that it carried unintended connotations; thereafter they adopted the Latinized Deus. The episode shows how translation could produce misunderstanding on both sides: Japanese listeners initially heard Christianity as a variant of Buddhism, and the Jesuits were slow to grasp the doctrinal, institutional, and social differences. Xavier also discovered that formal status mattered: the ragged clothes of the first missionaries were a liability, and subsequent Jesuits adopted finer robes when dealing with elites. Xavier concluded that the support of the rulers was essential, and that cultural adaptation would be necessary.

Under Cosme de Torres and later Jesuits such as Gaspar Vilela, Luís Fróis, and Organtino Gnecchi-Soldo, the mission grew slowly through the 1550s and 1560s. Missionaries preached in streets and homes, established churches in Hirado, Funai, Yokoseura, and other places, held debates with Buddhist monks, and relied on Japanese helpers. Luís de Almeida, a Portuguese merchant who became a Jesuit, helped found a hospital at Funai in 1557 that offered medical care, an example of how charitable activity served as outreach. Japanese laymen such as Brother Lourenço, a former itinerant musician and storyteller, proved exceptional preachers and debaters. The mission's strength depended on such assistants, who knew the language and the culture in ways the foreigners could not, and on dōjuku (lay helpers trained at the mission).

The mission's character changed substantially under Alessandro Valignano, who came to Japan as Visitor, an inspecting superior, in 1579. He judged that the Jesuits were culturally insensitive and that the mission needed a Japanese clergy and a policy of accommodation: learn the language, respect etiquette, adapt customs where they did not conflict with doctrine, and train Japanese youth in seminaries. He established seminaries at Arima and Azuchi and a college at Funai in Bungo, organized the Tenshō embassy of 1582, in which four Japanese youths travelled to Europe and met the pope, and brought a printing press to Japan in 1590. The press produced devotional works, grammars, and a dictionary, and also romanized editions of Japanese classics such as the Heike monogatari. Valignano's policy was always a strategy of persuasion, not an endorsement of equality, and he doubted, for instance, whether Japanese priests should hold high office. Still, accommodation marked a real difference from the practices of missions elsewhere.

Students should treat the missionaries as historical actors with aims and limitations. They wrote prolifically to justify their work, to recruit and raise funds, and to inform superiors, so their letters emphasize successes, exaggerate some numbers, and depict Japanese society through a European and Catholic lens. They were frequently fond of the Japanese and impressed by their discipline and literacy, while also contemptuous of Buddhist clergy and of certain practices. They relied on, but did not always understand, the patronage relations through which their work proceeded. A fair reading acknowledges their courage, cultural effort, and role as mediators, while recognizing that they were part of a European expansion and subject to its assumptions.

Section 37.6

Conversion and Japanese Christianity

Estimates of the number of Japanese Christians vary, and all are approximate. Jesuit reports and later scholarly reconstructions suggest roughly a hundred thousand by the early 1580s and perhaps a few hundred thousand around 1600, concentrated in Kyushu and with smaller communities in the Kinai region and elsewhere. These figures imply that Christians formed a small minority of Japan's population, but a locally significant one. In some domains, particularly those whose rulers had been baptized, a majority of inhabitants were recorded as Christians, though the circumstances of these conversions differed greatly. The number of adherents is less important than the variety of reasons for which people joined.

Some converts were persuaded by the teaching itself: by the Christian message about salvation, by the moral discipline of the community, by the care of the sick, or by the appeal of a coherent doctrine offered in the framework of debate. Others joined through family or lordship; when a household head or a daimyō converted, dependants often followed, and religious choice was frequently communal, not individual. Some were drawn by the networks of trade and patronage that the mission offered, or by the status attached to association with powerful foreigners. Some found in the confraternities, lay organizations that performed charity and funerals, a form of community and mutual support. Others may have seen Christianity as an alternative in communities where local Buddhist institutions were in conflict or disrepute. The sources rarely allow historians to see the inner convictions of individuals, but they show that motives were various and that no single explanation suffices.

It is crucial to distinguish individual faith from daimyō policy. A daimyō's baptism was often strategic as well as devotional, and a lord's order that subjects convert or that temples be removed was a political act, whether or not he was personally sincere. The reports of mass conversions under Ōmura Sumitada and others, which come mainly from Jesuit sources, describe conversion as a consequence of the lord's command, and some subjects may have accepted baptism under pressure. At the same time, there is ample evidence that many Japanese Christians developed a deep commitment, maintained their faith when it became dangerous, and organized their own communities in ways that the missionaries did not control. The history of the later Kirishitan communities, treated in later chapters, depends on this earlier development.

Christianity in Japan was never merely imposed from outside. Japanese catechists preached and explained the doctrine; Japanese patrons provided land, buildings, and protection; Japanese translators shaped how concepts were rendered; and Japanese lay leaders ran confraternities, baptized the dying when no priest was available, and sustained worship. The Jesuits, who were always few in number, depended on such participants. Japanese Christians also interpreted their faith through their own culture, combining Christian teaching with existing ideas of ritual, community, and obligation. The result was a Japanese Christianity, with its own character, and not simply a transplanted European one.

Historical Vocabulary
Kirishitan

Kirishitan (from the Portuguese cristão, "Christian") is the Japanese term, written in various characters, for Japanese Christians of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries and for the Christianity they practised. Related words include bateren (from padre, the missionary priests) and ikon for images.

Historians use "Kirishitan" instead of simply "Christian" for this period to mark that this was a specifically Japanese historical formation, shaped by Japanese language, social organization, and, later, persecution and concealment, and not identical with contemporary European Catholicism. The term also helps to avoid anachronism: modern Japanese Christians belong to a different and later history. It matters to this chapter because it reminds students that Christianity in Japan was a Japanese religious community, led in important respects by Japanese people.

Section 37.7

Christian Daimyō, Nagasaki, and Regional Politics

The so-called Christian daimyō were a small group of Kyushu lords, and their conversions differ enough that generalizations are risky. Ōmura Sumitada, baptized in 1563 with the name Bartolomeu, was the first. His domain was small and surrounded by rivals, he had opened his harbours to the Portuguese, and the Jesuit sources report that he ordered the destruction of Buddhist temples and shrines and the conversion of his subjects. His conversion was probably a mixture of religious interest, the desire to secure Portuguese ships, and the need for allies against neighbours such as the Ryūzōji. Some of his retainers opposed him, and he faced an uprising early in his rule. Jesuit authors celebrated his piety; modern historians emphasize the political context of his choices.

Ōtomo Sōrin of Bungo was the most powerful of the Christian-sympathizing lords. He had received Xavier in 1551, and he supported the Jesuits for decades, allowing missionaries to preach and to build a church, hospital, and school in Funai. Sōrin had long-standing interests in Zen Buddhism and in trade, and his baptism in 1578 as Francisco came late in his career. The Ōtomo suffered a major defeat by the Shimazu at the Mimikawa in 1578, and the domain declined thereafter, which affected the mission's prospects in Bungo. Arima Harunobu, baptized in 1580 as Protásio, was a young lord in a domain where the Jesuits had a foothold and where trade through Kuchinotsu had brought wealth. He hosted the Arima seminary. The three, Ōmura, Ōtomo, and Arima, sponsored the Tenshō embassy, which symbolized the alliance between the mission and these Kyushu lords. Their case should be kept distinct from that of converts elsewhere, such as Takayama Ukon in the Kinai region, who was drawn to Christianity in the 1560s under different circumstances, through family and personal conviction.

Nagasaki is the best example of how trade, religion, and local politics converged. Before 1570 it was a small fishing and farming settlement in Ōmura's territory. As Yokoseura and Fukuda proved unreliable or vulnerable, Sumitada chose Nagasaki's deep, sheltered harbour and in 1570 and 1571 granted land for a town, with privileges for merchants and a church for the Jesuits. Portuguese ships came regularly, and the port grew rapidly. Its residents included Japanese merchants and craftsmen, many of them Christians who moved there from elsewhere, and Portuguese, Chinese, and other foreigners who stayed for the trading season. Nagasaki's prosperity made it desirable to its neighbours. The Ryūzōji threatened the Ōmura and Arima domains, and in 1580 Sumitada assigned Nagasaki and nearby Mogi to the Society of Jesus. The exact nature of this transfer has been debated. The Jesuits received administrative rights and, in part, revenue from port duties, and Nagasaki became in effect a self-managing community under Jesuit supervision. It was not a sovereign European colony: the Jesuits had no army, they remained dependent on the goodwill of Japanese rulers, and the land lay within a system of Japanese political authority. The grant should be understood as a defensive and commercial arrangement by a lord who hoped to secure his port by placing it under a protective religious order.

The transfer had both advantages and risks. It gave the Jesuits a base, an income, and a community, and it gave Sumitada a measure of protection. It also concentrated in one place the features that most alarmed outsiders: a port under missionary administration, controlled access to foreign ships, and a community of Christians. When a stronger ruler reached Kyushu, this concentration would make Nagasaki an object of suspicion, as section 37.10 explains. For the moment, the case of Nagasaki shows how a local daimyō could turn a combination of commerce and religion to his own purposes, at the risk of creating a community that no later ruler would tolerate as autonomous.

Section 37.8

Conflict, Accommodation, and Religious Competition

The Christian mission must not be understood as a one-sided triumph. It entered a religious landscape already full of powerful institutions, and relations ranged from cooperation to violence. In many places Japanese audiences treated the newcomers as another Buddhist sect: the first Jesuits' early use of Buddhist vocabulary encouraged this view, and some listeners assumed that "Tenjiku" (India) was the origin of both teachings. Once the differences became clear, in particular the Christian claim to exclusive truth and the rejection of rites for ancestors and kami, responses hardened. Buddhist clerics debated the Jesuits, sometimes respectfully and sometimes with hostility, and some argued that Christianity threatened social order by undermining loyalty to lord and household.

Opposition worked at several levels. At the court, the Buddhist and shrine establishment lobbied the imperial court and the authorities in Kyoto, and in 1565 an imperial order was issued against the missionaries, which forced the Jesuits to leave the capital for a time. A notable debate took place in 1569, in the presence of Oda Nobunaga, between the Jesuit Luís Fróis and the Nichiren monk Nichijō, which Fróis later described as a triumph of his own side; it is an example of a source whose account must be read with the author's purpose in mind. Nobunaga tolerated the missionaries for reasons of his own, which Chapter 39 explains, and the Jesuits established a church in Kyoto in the 1570s. In Kyushu, hostility could come from retainers and villagers who resented the destruction of their temples, from Buddhist monks who lost patrons, and from rival lords who saw the Christian lords as strategic competitors.

Christians also caused conflict. In domains where the daimyō converted, Jesuit and Japanese Christian sources describe the destruction of Buddhist temples and shrines, the burning of religious images, and pressure on residents to be baptized. Ōmura Sumitada is the best-known example, and the Jesuit Visitor Valignano himself later criticized some of the coercive conversions. Jesuit writers were also frequently scornful of Buddhist clergy, whom they described in terms shaped by their own polemical tradition. Such episodes made Christianity, in the eyes of many Japanese, a force that could disrupt local religious order and endanger the institutions on which communities relied. They also created the grounds for later charges that Christian lords abused their power.

At the same time there were forms of accommodation. The Jesuits' policy of adaptation, particularly under Valignano, encouraged them to adopt Japanese manners, to study Japanese literature, and to treat elite etiquette with care. Some Japanese religious figures engaged seriously with Christian teaching, and Japanese Christians sometimes retained elements of ordinary religious life, such as family and community obligations, that Jesuits regarded as problematic. The boundaries between traditions were porous in practice. Students should thus resist the image of a simple confrontation between "Christianity" and "Japanese religion," and should see instead a range of local encounters in which the balance of persuasion, coercion, tolerance, and hostility varied according to who held power and what they wanted.

Section 37.9

Nanban Culture and Material Exchange

The word Nanban was a Japanese label, adapted from Chinese usage, in which "southern barbarians" designated peoples to the south. Applied to Portuguese and later Spanish arrivals who came by the southern sea, it carried a flavour of the exotic and alien, without the uniformly negative meaning that a literal translation suggests. It was not a synonym for "European": it included Southeast Asians, Indians, and Africans in the ships' crews and was a Japanese way of categorizing newcomers, not a description they chose for themselves. The Nanban world, then, refers to the zone of contact in which these people, their goods, and their ideas met Japanese society, and to the culture that it generated.

Visual culture offers some of the best evidence. Nanban folding screens (Nanban byōbu), usually produced by artists of the Kanō and other workshops, depict the arrival of a large Portuguese ship, with its crew, merchants, and Jesuits, and the procession of foreigners through a Japanese port. The surviving screens mostly date from about 1600 and the early seventeenth century, somewhat after the period examined here, and they are therefore retrospective representations, not documents of the mid-sixteenth century. They show the newcomers in billowing trousers, hats, and capes, often with attendants of African and South Asian origin, and they render them with a mixture of curiosity and stylization. The screens reveal how Japanese viewers visualized foreigners and, because they were commissioned by elite patrons, what such patrons found interesting. They do not show what ordinary port residents thought. Lacquerware and other export goods were also produced in Japan for the Portuguese market, often in a style that mingled Japanese techniques, such as gold lacquer and mother-of-pearl inlay, with European forms of chest and casket.

Christian art formed another strand. Jesuits established painting workshops in the 1580s, where Japanese pupils trained under European and Japanese-European teachers to produce devotional images, using oil and other techniques. They also used music, including organ and choir, in liturgy and in the education of seminarians. Christian images and rosaries circulated among converts, and Jesuit accounts say that crosses and rosaries also became fashionable among non-Christians. Such reports suggest that exotic religious objects could be treated as ornaments, though the evidence for this practice comes from writers with their own agendas.

Printing, language, and knowledge also moved between cultures. The Jesuit press, introduced in 1590, printed in Latin, Portuguese, and romanized Japanese, and its products included a Japanese-Portuguese dictionary. These works are valuable sources for the spoken Japanese of the period. Portuguese words entered Japanese, among them pan (bread), tabako (tobacco), karuta (playing cards), botan (button), and biidoro (glass), and some, like kasutera (sponge cake), have become part of everyday vocabulary; the origins of a few other words, such as tempura, are debated. Clothing, including capes and breeches, and some foods were adopted to varying degrees. European medicine and surgery, introduced at the Funai hospital and elsewhere, attracted interest, and maps and globes carried by missionaries provoked curiosity about geography. Clocks and mechanical objects were given as gifts. In 1581 a man of African origin who had come with Valignano attracted attention in Kyoto and entered Oda Nobunaga's service for a short time; his case, known in later retellings by the name Yasuke, shows both that the Nanban world was multiethnic and that the surviving evidence is thin.

It is important not to overstate the impact. Most Japanese never met a Portuguese, and most of the cultural changes described here were concentrated in Kyushu, in port towns, and among elites. Japanese borrowed selectively, rejecting much, adapting some, and using other elements for their own ends. The exchange was not one of wholesale Westernization but of hybridization: Japanese artists painted Portuguese ships in Kanō style, Japanese craftsmen made lacquer for Portuguese chests, Japanese Christians incorporated the faith into local forms of community. The treatment of imported goods in elite tea practice is reserved for Chapter 38, and students should look there for how aesthetics and political status intersected with foreign objects.

Section 37.10

Opportunity, Anxiety, and the Limits of the Nanban Encounter

By the 1580s the Nanban encounter had produced a set of contradictory developments. Portuguese trade was highly profitable for many participants: daimyō collected duties, merchants made fortunes, and Japanese silver moved into the wider Asian economy. Christianity had built communities and institutions, and the Jesuits were well placed in Kyushu and the capital region. Japanese art, language, and material life had absorbed new elements. And yet the very features that made the encounter productive also generated anxiety in those who held or sought power.

One concern was religious autonomy. Christians owed spiritual loyalty to a priesthood linked to a distant pope and, in practice, to the Jesuit organization. Japanese lords accustomed to controlling religious institutions, as Sengoku rulers had sought to do with Buddhist temples, might see this as a rival source of authority. A second concern was commercial dependence: the Kyushu lords' reliance on the Macao ship gave the Portuguese and, through them, the Jesuits, some leverage, and Nagasaki under Jesuit administration seemed to be a place beyond ordinary control. A third was the connection between missionaries and the expansion of Iberian empires. The Portuguese and Spanish crowns were united under one ruler from 1580, the Spanish were established in Manila, and rumours circulated that missionaries served as the advance guard for conquest. Historians debate how seriously such fears reflected reality. The Jesuits themselves discussed, and in some cases rejected, proposals for military intervention, and the Iberian presence in Asia was in fact limited. But the rulers who worried about these possibilities were interpreting evidence available to them, including the example of Spanish conquests in the Philippines.

A fourth concern involved the treatment of Japanese people. Portuguese merchants purchased Japanese captives, some of them taken in Kyushu's wars, and shipped them as servants or slaves to Macao and elsewhere in Asia. Domestic slavery and the sale of war captives existed in Japan too, and Japanese traders took part in it, but the export of people troubled Japanese rulers and some Jesuits, who protested against it with limited effect. These concerns came into sharp focus in June 1587, when Toyotomi Hideyoshi, after his Kyushu campaign, issued two paired measures: a longer memorandum on the eighteenth day of the sixth month of Tenshō 15, followed the next day by a five-article edict ordering the missionaries to leave. Together they condemned coercive conversion, the destruction of shrines and temples, the alienation of land to the church, and the trade in Japanese captives; the expulsion edict also distinguished missionary activity from Portuguese trade, which was not prohibited. The expulsion was not fully enforced, and trade continued. The full history of Hideyoshi's policies, and of later Tokugawa prohibitions and the suppression of Christianity, belongs to Chapters 40 and 47 and later, and it would be a mistake to read the 1587 edict as the end of the Nanban world. It is better read as evidence that the rulers who were reuniting Japan would treat autonomous religious loyalty and uncontrolled maritime commerce as questions of state.

The limits of the encounter were therefore political. Japanese rulers had welcomed trade and, in some cases, missionaries, as long as these strengthened their own positions. As power began to concentrate, the terms of the welcome changed. The Nanban world was not destroyed by an abrupt rejection of foreign contact, nor did it end because Japanese culture was inherently hostile to outsiders. It was reshaped by the changing needs of governments that had, for the first time in a century, a prospect of controlling the whole archipelago. Students should carry this understanding into the next chapters, where aesthetics (Chapter 38), centralization (Chapter 39), and continental ambition (Chapter 40) continue the story.

Historical Vocabulary
Nanban

Nanban (南蛮, literally "southern barbarians") is a Japanese term derived from Chinese usage for peoples of the south. In the sixteenth century it was applied to the Portuguese and later the Spanish, together with the Southeast Asians, Indians, and Africans who sailed with them, because they arrived by sea from the south.

It is not a synonym for "European," and it was not a self-description. It was a Japanese category, and its connotation mixed curiosity, distance, and sometimes fascination rather than fixed contempt. Historians use it for the trade, art, and material culture of the period, and it matters here because it shows that Japanese people classified the newcomers in their own terms rather than simply receiving a European view of themselves.

Primary Source Window

Francis Xavier's Letter from Kagoshima

Creator: Francis Xavier, Jesuit missionary · Recipient: Jesuits in Goa · Date: 5 November 1549 · Place: Kagoshima · Type: Missionary letter, copied and circulated in Europe

Context: Xavier wrote soon after arriving in Japan, when he had been in the country a few months, had limited Japanese, and relied on Anjirō for interpretation. The letter was intended for Jesuit colleagues in Goa and Europe and was copied and circulated to encourage support for the mission. It is one of the earliest European descriptions of Japan written from experience there.

Paraphrase (not a verbatim quotation): Xavier reports that the Japanese are, in his judgment, among the most promising people yet encountered outside Christendom. He describes them as courteous, attentive to honour, and content with modest means, and he says that they value arms and respect the warrior class. He notes that many can read, that they are curious and willing to reason, and that Buddhist monks hold influence among them. He is hopeful about conversions but aware that language is a serious obstacle.

Analysis: The letter reveals how a European missionary, within months of arrival, framed Japan for an audience that needed to be persuaded to fund and staff the mission. Its positive portrait served a purpose: to show that the enterprise was promising. Xavier's impressions reflect the small part of Japan he had seen, primarily the warrior elite and the clergy, and the partial information he received from Anjirō and other informants. It says little about peasants, women, merchants, or Japanese Buddhist thought on its own terms. It also shows the limits of a first impression: Xavier's optimism about rapid conversion proved unrealistic, and his early error concerning the word for God, mentioned in section 37.5, shows the gap between his understanding and the situation. Historians use the letter with caution, as evidence of European perceptions and mission strategy, and not as transparent ethnography.

Questions for students:

  1. What does this source say about how Xavier viewed the Japanese?
  2. Who created it, for whom, and for what possible purpose?
  3. What can historians learn from it about the start of the Jesuit mission?
  4. What can historians not establish from this source alone, and whose voices are missing?
Historical Debate

Why Did Christianity Attract Converts in Sixteenth-Century Japan?

The question: Christianity grew rapidly in parts of Kyushu and acquired communities elsewhere, despite being a foreign and exclusive religion. Historians disagree about how to explain this.

Interpretation A — Religious persuasion and community: Some scholars emphasize that the Christian message, the moral discipline, charity, confraternities, and the skill of Japanese catechists attracted converts who found real meaning in it, and that the tenacity of later Kirishitan communities under persecution shows the depth of commitment.
Interpretation B — Patronage, politics, and commerce: Others stress that conversion often followed lordship, family obligation, and the benefits of access to trade and protection, that daimyō acted partly for strategic reasons, and that the speed of conversion in some domains reflects command as well as conviction.

Evidence: The evidence consists largely of Jesuit letters and histories, which are interested parties, together with limited Japanese documents, domain records, and later records of persecution. Historians also consider regional variation: conversion patterns in Ōmura's territory differ from those in Bungo or the Kinai area. Most recent scholarship treats the two interpretations as complementary. Additional factors include missionary adaptation, dissatisfaction with existing institutions in some locales, and the social uncertainties of the Sengoku period.

Why it matters: The answer affects how historians understand the Kirishitan communities, the role of Japanese agency, and the relation of religion to state power. It also guards against reducing conversion to material interest or, conversely, to spiritual triumph.

Student task: Choose two converts or communities described in this chapter and explain which motives seem most plausible for each, and what additional evidence would help you test your conclusion. No single motive explains conversion across different individuals and communities.

Multiple Perspectives

Continuity and Change Over Time

Maritime Exchange before and during the Nanban Period

What Changed

  • A new set of intermediaries, the Portuguese, joined the Sino-Japanese trade after official channels closed
  • Christianity became an organized presence, with missionaries, churches, schools, and lay communities
  • New goods, technologies, and images circulated, and new words entered the language
  • Nagasaki rose as a major port, shifting the commercial map of Kyushu

What Stayed the Same

  • The basic structure of Chinese supply and Japanese demand for silk, and Japanese silver for China
  • The central role of Chinese merchants and Asian shipping and pilots
  • The importance of local lords in controlling ports and deciding access
  • The religious landscape of Buddhist, shrine, and local traditions, which continued to shape most people's lives

Who experienced change most directly: Port residents, Kyushu elites, and Christian converts. Who experienced greater continuity: Most farmers, villagers, and inhabitants of regions far from the coast. Why the pattern matters: Seeing both change and continuity prevents the error of treating the Portuguese as a turning point for all of Japan.

Cause and Effect

Trade and Mission in Kyushu

Long-term causes

Japan's existing maritime connections, growth of silver production, Ming restrictions on trade, Portuguese expansion in Asia, and political fragmentation in Sengoku Japan.

Short-term causes

The end of the tally trade in 1547, the settlement at Macao, daimyō competition for ships, Xavier's arrival in 1549, and local conflicts such as those at Hirado and Yokoseura.

Event or transformation

The growth of the Nanban trade and the Jesuit mission in Kyushu, culminating in the development of Nagasaki.

Immediate effects

Increased revenue and imported goods in host domains, the spread of Christian communities, and new forms of cultural exchange.

Long-term consequences

A distinctive Nanban culture, a durable Kirishitan community, and heightened anxiety among rulers about religious autonomy and trade, which shaped later policy.

Historical complexity

Trade did not mechanically cause conversion, nor did the mission simply follow commerce. The two were linked through patronage and shared logistics, yet they pursued different goals, and Japanese actors made their own choices.
Everyday Life and Environment

Living in Nagasaki

Nagasaki was a planned port town with a deep harbour and streets laid out for merchants, and its life followed the rhythm of the trading season. When the annual ship from Macao arrived, the town filled with crew, Chinese and Japanese buyers, brokers, carriers, and porters. Goods had to be unloaded, weighed, priced, stored, and resold, and money and silver changed hands through merchants' credit. Between seasons, the town's residents worked as fishermen, farmers, artisans, and shopkeepers, and some Portuguese and other foreigners lived there for extended periods.

Religious life shaped the town. Many residents were Christians, and Jesuit sources describe churches, processions, confraternities that cared for the poor and buried the dead, and feast days. Women took part in religious life as members of confraternities and as household managers; children received instruction in prayers and, in some cases, in reading. The evidence for this comes mainly from missionary accounts, which emphasize piety, and historians must also consider the labourers, sailors, enslaved people, and non-Christian residents who left fewer traces. The vulnerability of the town to fire, disease, and the political decisions of distant rulers reminded its inhabitants that prosperity depended on circumstances they could not control.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that Portuguese contact after the 1540s did not pull an isolated Japan into an unknown world. Sixteenth-century Japan was already embedded in East Asian maritime networks that linked it to China, Korea, Ryukyu, and Southeast Asia. Official tally trade with Ming China had ended in 1547, unofficial Chinese and Japanese commerce was active, and the growth of Japanese silver production created a product in great demand abroad.

The Portuguese entered this system from their bases at Goa, Malacca, and later Macao, travelling on Asian routes and relying on Asian partners. The conventional story of Tanegashima in 1543 rests on a Japanese text written decades later and on a Chinese ship, and it is best understood as a tradition with a probable core. The firearm was one item among many, and its military significance belongs to Chapter 36.

The central economic pattern was a triangular exchange in which the Portuguese carried Chinese silk to Japan and Japanese silver to China. This role as intermediaries mattered more than any catalogue of European goods, and it gave the Portuguese a position in Japanese ports that they had not created. Kyushu daimyō competed for ships, which linked port revenue to regional power and shaped the rise of Hirado, Bungo, and especially Nagasaki.

The Jesuits, who arrived in 1549 with Francis Xavier, were distinct from the merchants and depended on Japanese patrons, catechists, and lay helpers. Xavier's early mistakes over language, and Valignano's later policy of accommodation, show the mission learning through error. Missionary sources are rich but partisan, and students must read them for their purposes and omissions.

Conversion was driven by varied motives, including religious persuasion, family and lordship, community, and politics. A daimyō's baptism should be distinguished from individual faith, and conversion should never be reduced to a bargain for guns. Japanese catechists and communities were essential to Christianity's spread, and the resulting Kirishitan tradition was a Japanese formation.

The Christian daimyō Ōmura Sumitada, Ōtomo Sōrin, and Arima Harunobu acted in different circumstances and for different reasons. Nagasaki illustrates the convergence of trade, religion, and defence: the 1580 assignment of the port to the Jesuits was a local arrangement within Japanese authority and not a European colony, but it created a concentration of autonomous influence that later rulers would find troubling.

Christianity entered a crowded religious landscape, and relations ranged from debate and accommodation to hostility and coercion, including temple destruction in some Christian domains and court and Buddhist efforts to exclude the missionaries. The encounter was not a triumphalist narrative but a contested negotiation.

Nanban culture, seen in folding screens, export lacquer, Christian art, printing, vocabulary, medicine, and material objects, reflects selective adaptation and hybridization rather than wholesale Westernization. The surviving evidence, such as screens from about 1600, often reflects elite perspectives and later representation.

By the 1580s the encounter was both profitable and unsettling. Concerns about religious loyalty, commercial dependence, links to Iberian empires, and the trade in Japanese captives led Toyotomi Hideyoshi to issue an edict against the missionaries in 1587. The chapter closes at that point, noting that the full story of restriction belongs to later chapters.

These developments matter because they show Japan's regional states responding to new connections in different ways, prefiguring debates about foreign contact that would recur. They also show that the Nanban world, in its cultural and commercial complexity, was a Japanese as well as a Portuguese creation.

Key Terms

Nanban
A Japanese term, derived from Chinese usage, for the "southern barbarians," applied to Portuguese, Spanish, and their Asian and African associates arriving by sea.
Kirishitan
The Japanese rendering of "Christian" for Japanese Christians of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries and their faith.
Bateren
A Japanese rendering of the Portuguese padre, used for missionary priests.
Society of Jesus
The Catholic order, founded 1540, whose members conducted the mission in Japan from 1549.
Padroado
The Portuguese crown's patronage rights over missions and church appointments in its overseas sphere.
Estado da Índia
The Portuguese maritime empire in Asia, headquartered at Goa.
Carrack (nau)
A large ocean-going Portuguese merchant ship used on the Macao–Japan voyage.
Kurofune
"Black ships," a Japanese term for large foreign vessels, later also applied to nineteenth-century Western ships.
Wakō
A term in Chinese and Korean sources for maritime raiders and smugglers, who were of mixed Japanese, Chinese, and other origins.
Tally trade
The system of official licensed trade between Japan and Ming China, ended in 1547.
Raw silk
Unwoven silk thread, the principal Chinese commodity carried to Japan.
Tanegashima
An island south of Kyushu, traditionally the site of the first recorded Portuguese landing.
Teppōki
The early seventeenth-century Japanese account of the Tanegashima arrival, written by the monk Nanpo Bunshi.
Catechist
A lay teacher of Christian doctrine, in Japan often a Japanese convert.
Dōjuku
Japanese lay assistants trained at the mission to help with preaching and instruction.
Accommodation
The Jesuit policy, associated with Valignano, of adapting to local customs and language where doctrine allowed.
Visitor
A Jesuit inspecting superior with authority to reorganize a mission.
Confraternity
A lay association for devotion, charity, and burial, important in Japanese Christian communities.
Seminary
A school for training clergy and lay assistants, such as those at Arima and Azuchi.
Tenshō embassy
The 1582 mission of four Japanese youths sent to Europe under Jesuit and daimyō sponsorship.
Nanban byōbu
Folding screens depicting the arrival of Portuguese ships and foreigners, mostly dating from c. 1600 and after.
Ningbo incident
The 1523 violent clash between rival Japanese missions that led to tighter Ming restrictions.
Port duties
Revenues collected by a lord from ships and goods at a harbour, a central incentive in port competition.
Syncretism
The blending of religious ideas and practices from different traditions.
Dainichi / Deus
The Buddhist cosmic buddha first used by the Jesuits for God, and the Latin term later adopted to avoid confusion.
Macao
The Portuguese settlement near Canton, from the 1550s, base of the Japan voyage.
Nagasaki
The port developed from 1570–1571, centre of the Macao trade and of Christianity in Japan.
Edict of 1587
Hideyoshi's June 1587 order for the missionaries to leave Japan, issued a day after a longer memorandum on conversion and the captive trade; it did not ban Portuguese trade and was not fully enforced.

Major People and Places

Identification & Significance

Francis Xavier
Jesuit who began the Japan mission in 1549; his letters shaped European views of Japan.
Anjirō (Yajirō)
Japanese convert who guided Xavier and interpreted for the first mission.
Cosme de Torres
Jesuit who led the mission after Xavier until 1570.
Alessandro Valignano
Visitor who promoted accommodation, seminaries, and printing.
Luís Fróis
Jesuit historian of the mission; a key but partisan source.
Brother Lourenço
Japanese Jesuit brother and preacher, an example of Japanese leadership in the mission.
Ōmura Sumitada
First Christian daimyō; opened Nagasaki and assigned it to the Jesuits in 1580.
Ōtomo Sōrin
Lord of Bungo and patron of the Jesuits; baptized in 1578.
Arima Harunobu
Lord who hosted a seminary and traded through Kuchinotsu; baptized in 1580.
Matsura family of Hirado
Hosts of the first regular Portuguese ships and Chinese traders.
Tanegashima Tokitaka
Local lord associated in tradition with the acquisition of firearms.
Nanpo Bunshi
Monk who wrote the Teppōki, the principal Japanese account of Tanegashima.
Toyotomi Hideyoshi
Ruler who issued the 1587 edict against the missionaries; treated fully in Chapter 40.
Goa, Malacca, Macao
Portuguese bases linking India, Southeast Asia, and China to Japan.
Hirado, Funai, Kagoshima, Hakata, Sakai
Major ports and commercial cities of the Nanban era.
Nagasaki
Port town that became the centre of the Macao trade and of Japanese Christianity.
Iwami silver mines
The principal source of Japanese silver exported to China.

MGU Library Research Project

Mapping a Port in the Nanban World

This project asks you to study one sixteenth-century port and to reconstruct its commercial, religious, and political life from library sources.

  1. Choose a port. Select Hirado, Funai, Nagasaki, Hakata, Sakai, or another port discussed in the chapter.
  2. Build a timeline. Identify at least eight dated developments in the port's history between about 1540 and 1590, noting which dates are secure and which are traditional.
  3. Map the connections. Produce a map showing the port's links to other Japanese ports, China, Ryukyu, Southeast Asia, and Portuguese bases, and mark the goods that moved along each route.
  4. Compare sources. Find at least two sources, for example a missionary account and a Japanese or Chinese record or an object such as a screen or a piece of lacquer, and compare what each reveals and omits.
  5. Write your findings. In a 4–6 page essay or multimedia presentation, explain how trade, religion, and local politics interacted in your port, using language such as "the evidence suggests" where the record is thin.

Library resources to use: Scholarly histories of the Portuguese in Asia and of Christianity in Japan, translated Jesuit documents, museum catalogues of Nanban art, and the MGU History Library's reference materials (see also Appendix A, Glossary of Regional and Historical Terminology).

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it corrects a familiar picture. The arrival of Europeans is often told as a moment when a closed Japan met the West. The evidence shows something different: a Japan already at the centre of busy maritime exchange, in which new participants found a place, and in which Japanese rulers, merchants, converts, artists, and clerics acted on their own interests and convictions. The Nanban world was made in Japanese ports as much as on Portuguese ships.

The chapter also reveals how Sengoku political fragmentation shaped foreign relations. Because no central authority consistently controlled the coast, Kyushu daimyō negotiated individually, and the results varied from port to port and lord to lord. This diversity is a reminder that Japan's relations with the world were shaped by regional politics and not by a single national policy, and that the later assertion of central control over maritime trade and religion marked a real change in how such relations were governed.

The encounter left durable legacies. Kirishitan communities, the vocabulary and material culture of Nanban exchange, the commercial prominence of Nagasaki, and the memory of a moment of cosmopolitan contact all outlasted the period. The same developments sharpened questions about religion, loyalty, and commerce that rulers would address in the following decades. The next chapters turn to tea and the politics of aesthetics (Chapter 38) and to Oda Nobunaga's campaigns of centralization (Chapter 39), in which some of the resources and tensions described here would play a part.

Finally, the chapter shows the value of reading sources critically. The Jesuit letters that make the Nanban era so vivid were written for European audiences with specific purposes, and the Japanese voices that would balance them are often missing or survive only in fragments. Students who learn to ask who is speaking, to whom, and with what aim are better prepared to interpret every later period in this textbook, and to see that an encounter between societies is never the story of one side alone.
