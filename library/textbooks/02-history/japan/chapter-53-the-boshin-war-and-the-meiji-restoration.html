Part VIII — Bakumatsu and the Meiji Revolution
53

The Boshin War and the Meiji Restoration

Japanese History: From Jōmon Japan to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

On January 3, 1868, troops from several domains took the gates of the Kyoto palace and a new government declared the shogunate abolished. A declaration was not yet a government. It took civil war, negotiation, improvised offices, and eighteen months to turn the claim into an operating state.

This chapter traces the overthrow of the Tokugawa shogunate, the restoration of imperial rule, the Boshin War (January 1868–June 1869), and the creation of a new central government. Its central question is how competing proposals for preserving or reorganizing Tokugawa authority turned into civil war, and how the victorious coalition turned imperial legitimacy into an operating government.

The outcome was contingent. Yoshinobu returned governing authority in 1867 expecting to keep great influence; the war began because rival parties disagreed about what that meant. Domains changed sides after Toba–Fushimi, commanders chose negotiation over assault at Edo, and northern domains resisted what they saw as an illegitimate seizure of power. Defeated domains were historical actors with their own claims to lawful government.

The war was fought by domain armies and experienced by farmers, townspeople, women, and refugees. It ended in Ezo, the homeland of the Ainu, where a Tokugawa fleet formed a provisional regime. Historians rely on court and domain records, military reports, diaries, newspapers, battle prints, diplomatic correspondence, and the Charter Oath, each reflecting its author’s perspective; the victors’ narratives shaped later memory.

The chapter follows Chapter 52 (treaty ports, loyalist violence, the crisis to 1866) and leads to Chapter 54, which examines the abolition of the domains and the samurai order.

Regional complexity runs through the story. Kyoto and Edo were the political centers, but the war was settled in Echigo, Aizu, and Ezo, and the costs fell on farmers, townspeople, and refugees as well as samurai. Satsuma, Chōshū, Tosa, and Saga supplied the core of the winning armies; Sendai, Yonezawa, Nagaoka, and Aizu led resistance; and Tokugawa retainers carried the war north by sea. Treating the conflict as a clash between Satsuma–Chōshū modernizers and a decaying shogunate hides these differences.

Ezo, the homeland of the Ainu, enters as the last theater of the war. The chapter treats Ainu communities as people whose land became the stage for others’ conflict, not as bystanders to a purely Japanese story, and it notes that the Kaitakushi and the name Hokkaidō followed within months.

Historical Context

By the mid-1860s the bakuhan system (Chapter 42) was strained by the treaty regime, military vulnerability, inflation, and domain rivalry (Chapters 51–52). The failure of the 1866 expedition against Chōshū exposed the shogunate’s limits. Yet the Tokugawa were not obsolete: they imported arms, built a navy, and employed French advisers. The crisis was one of authority as much as technology.

The court had gained prominence as a source of legitimacy since 1853 but had no army, tax system, or administration. Whoever acted in the emperor’s name depended on domain troops and revenues, which shaped the new government’s weakness after 1868.

A note on dates: this chapter gives Gregorian dates, with Japanese lunisolar equivalents where useful. The calendars do not correspond month for month, so traditional dates (such as Keiō 4/5/15) must be converted, not simply relabeled.

Geography gave the war several distinct theaters. The Kyoto–Osaka corridor, where the Toba and Fushimi roads converge, was the arena of the opening battle and the center of court politics. Edo and the Kantō plain held the Tokugawa’s own lands and administrative machinery, so control of the city was both a military and a political prize. North of the Kantō, the Echigo corridor along the Shinano river led from the Sea of Japan coast toward Aizu, and the mountain passes of the northeast, such as Bonari and the Shirakawa barrier, controlled access to Aizu’s basin. Ezo lay across the Tsugaru Strait, a distant frontier of Matsumae rule, Wajin fishing and trade, and Ainu communities.

The domains were not uniform. Satsuma and Chōshū were led by reform-minded samurai who had overridden more cautious elders; Tosa was divided between supporters of the shogun and of the loyalists; Sendai had factions for and against mediation. Many small domains had no firm political line and followed their neighbors. Understanding the war requires attention to these internal divisions as well as to the great domains.

Section 53.1

The Tokugawa Shogunate at the Breaking Point

The Second Chōshū Expedition of summer 1866 was meant to restore the shogunate’s authority over the domain that had defied it. It did the opposite. Satsuma refused to send troops, having concluded a secret understanding with Chōshū in March 1866, and Chōshū’s rifle-armed forces, reorganized under commanders such as Ōmura Masujirō, defeated shogunal and allied armies on several fronts, including at Ōshima and Kokura. The shogun Iemochi died at Osaka Castle on August 29, 1866, and a truce was arranged through the Tokugawa retainer Katsu Kaishū. A government that could not discipline one domain had lost the presumption that it could command all of them.

Tokugawa Yoshinobu, from the Hitotsubashi branch and the choice of many reformers, took over the house in autumn 1866 and became shogun on January 10, 1867. Emperor Kōmei, whose hostility to the treaties had made him an obstacle to every settlement, died on January 30, 1867, probably of smallpox; rumors of poisoning circulated then and later, but no firm evidence supports them. The fourteen-year-old Mutsuhito acceded in early 1867. A boy emperor could not himself direct politics, so the court became a field in which nobles and domain leaders competed to speak in his name.

Yoshinobu was no passive heir to a collapsing regime. With French advisers, a military mission that arrived in 1867, and the minister Oguri Tadamasa’s fiscal and administrative plans, he reorganized shogunal offices into ministries, expanded modern infantry and artillery, and used his command of the navy. In mid-1867 he secured imperial consent to open the port of Hyōgo, a diplomatic success that deflated critics who had argued that the court would never accept the treaties. Meanwhile, high rice prices and distress fed riots in town and country, and in the autumn of 1867 crowds in Kyoto, Osaka, and elsewhere danced in the streets to chants associated with the ‘ee ja nai ka’ movement. Historians debate whether this was religious exuberance, anxiety, or political protest, but it shows a society unsettled by the question of who governed.

Section 53.2

Competing Plans for Japan’s Political Future

By mid-1867 three main programs were in play. Yoshinobu’s was to turn the shogunate into a modern central government, using ministries, a council of daimyō, and perhaps a deliberative assembly, with the Tokugawa still at its head. The summer’s ‘four lords’ conference’ of Shimazu Hisamitsu, Yamauchi Yōdō, Matsudaira Shungaku, and Date Munenari failed to impose a settlement on him, in part because he outmaneuvered them over Hyōgo.

A second plan came from Tosa. A program traditionally attributed to Sakamoto Ryōma and Gotō Shōjirō in June 1867, the ‘shipboard plan’ (Senchū hassaku), imagined a return of authority to the court and a two-house assembly of nobles and samurai. The original June text does not survive and its authenticity is debated; Ryōma’s surviving November 1867 Shin seifu kōryō hassaku (eight-point program for a new government) is a separate document. Yamauchi Yōdō submitted a petition along these lines to Yoshinobu in October. The Tosa plan sought to avoid war by letting the shogun remain a major participant, and it appealed to those who wished to avoid a Satsuma–Chōshū victory.

The third program was the military option of Saigō Takamori, Ōkubo Toshimichi, and Iwakura Tomomi, a court noble who had been in disgrace and was now restored to influence. Satsuma agreed with Chōshū and Aki (Geishū) in autumn 1867 to move troops to Kyoto, and these leaders sought imperial authority to move against the shogun. Court factions pulled in opposing directions: the regent Nijō Nariyuki and others remained wary of Satsuma and Chōshū, while reformist nobles pressed for change. On November 8–9, 1867, a document known as the secret rescript to overthrow the shogunate (tōbaku no mitchoku) was issued to Satsuma and Chōshū, but it lacked normal formalities, and its legitimacy remains debated. ‘Restoration’ thus meant different things: a ceremonial court under a Tokugawa council, an assembly of lords, or a new government without Tokugawa leadership.

Section 53.3

The Return of Political Authority: Taisei Hōkan

Yoshinobu consulted representatives of about forty domains at Nijō Castle in Kyoto on November 8, 1867, and submitted his petition to the court on November 9; the court accepted it the next day. He returned the ‘great political authority’ (taisei) that the Tokugawa had held by delegation from the court. In practical terms he was giving the court responsibility for an administration it was not equipped to run.

His calculation was strategic. The petition undercut the Satsuma–Chōshū case for armed action, since the shogun had submitted. It placed the burden on the court to create a new structure, and Yoshinobu expected that the lords he had consulted would convene in Kyoto under imperial summons and look to him. He remained the largest landholder in Japan, with an estimated four million koku, a navy, and a trained army concentrated near Osaka. The court asked him to continue handling diplomacy and routine business until a new council met; few lords arrived.

The taisei hōkan thus was not a surrender. It was a bid to keep power by changing its form. It also reveals why war followed: the Satsuma–Chōshū leaders and their court allies knew that unless the Tokugawa were stripped of land and position, the old balance would persist under new names.

Historical Vocabulary
Taisei hōkan (大政奉還)

The “return of great political authority” by Tokugawa Yoshinobu in 1867. It is not the same as the Restoration declaration (ōsei fukkō) of 1868. The first transferred formal governing responsibility to the court while leaving Tokugawa wealth and influence largely intact; the second abolished the shogunate and set up a government without Tokugawa leadership. Treating the two as one event hides why war followed.

Section 53.4

The Restoration of Imperial Rule, January 1868

On January 3, 1868 (Keiō 3/12/9), troops from Satsuma, Owari, Echizen, Geishū, and Tosa took control of the palace gates in Kyoto. The regent and pro-Tokugawa nobles were excluded, and the young emperor’s government declared the restoration of imperial rule. The shogunate, the regency, and the older court offices were abolished. In their place came provisional posts: a sōsai (supreme president) held by Prince Arisugawa Taruhito, gijō (councillors) from the court and domain lords, and san’yo (advisers) drawn from samurai officials such as Saigō, Ōkubo, and Gotō Shōjirō. The declaration was a coup in procedure and a revolution in claim.

The same evening, a conference in the Kogosho of the palace debated what to do with Yoshinobu. Iwakura and the Satsuma side demanded that he give up his court rank and return his lands (jikan nōchi). Yamauchi Yōdō and Matsudaira Shungaku objected that excluding him from the new government was unjust. A compromise was reached in the following days, softening the demands into a partial surrender of lands and a contribution from the domains. Yoshinobu left Nijō Castle for Osaka on January 6.

The January declaration created a government in name; it did not create revenues, officials, or obedience. The new leaders held Kyoto and the court, but the Tokugawa still held Osaka and Edo and the great majority of domains had not committed. Whether the declaration would become a government depended on what followed.

Section 53.5

From Political Confrontation to Civil War

In Osaka, Yoshinobu’s advisers and the foreign representatives he received presented him as the injured party. Tokugawa retainers, including those of Aizu and Kuwana, pressed him to act. In Edo, agitators sponsored by Satsuma had spent weeks raiding merchant houses and provoking shogunal police; on January 19, 1868 (Keiō 3/12/25) Shōnai troops and other shogunate-aligned forces attacked and burned the Satsuma residence at Mita. News reached Osaka within days.

Yoshinobu then authorized a march to Kyoto to present a memorial demanding the punishment of Satsuma’s leaders. His army of perhaps fifteen thousand outnumbered the roughly five thousand troops of Satsuma and Chōshū in the Kyoto area. He apparently believed that a show of strength would force the court to restore him. The Satsuma and Chōshū leaders treated the march as an attack on the court. Both sides were willing to use force, and the decision on each side was made by officials who could have chosen negotiation. The war began not because it was inevitable, but because each side saw the other’s moves as a challenge it could not accept.

Section 53.6

The Battle of Toba–Fushimi

The battle began on the afternoon of January 27, 1868 when the Tokugawa vanguard on the Toba road, advancing in column, met a Satsuma checkpoint and was fired on by artillery. Fighting spread to Fushimi, where Aizu troops and the Shinsengumi attacked Chōshū positions around the magistrate’s office. The Tokugawa forces were numerous and had modern rifles but advanced on narrow roads with poor coordination and little unified command. The imperial side had fewer men but better-prepared positions, artillery, and units trained in volley fire.

On January 28–29 (sources differ on the day) the court named Ninnaji-no-miya Yoshiaki (later known as Komatsu-no-miya Akihito) commander of a punitive army and issued the imperial brocade banner, an emblem used to mark forces acting in the court’s name. Its appearance changed the battlefield politically: troops who had been fighting a faction now faced forces carrying the emperor’s mark. On January 29–31 domains close to Osaka wavered. Yodo, whose lord had served as a shogunal senior councillor, closed its gates to retreating Tokugawa troops, and Tsu turned its guns on them. On the night of January 30 (Keiō 4/1/6), Yoshinobu left Osaka by ship for Edo with a few close associates, abandoning his army. Casualty figures vary by source and remain uncertain; the political consequences were far greater than the losses.

Historical Evidence

Documents and Images of Toba–Fushimi

A domain report. The National Museum of Japanese History holds a communication on the battle from the Akita domain’s Kyoto residence to authorities at home, dated Keiō 4/1/5. It shows how a domain with a stake in the outcome gathered and passed on news within days, from an administrative standpoint rather than a battlefield one.

A diary. The National Diet Library preserves the diary of Katsu Kaishū, whose entry of February 4, 1868 records him trying to learn what had happened from men returning from the fighting. It illustrates incomplete information and the gap between eyewitness and secondhand accounts.

A retrospective print. Tsukioka Yoshitoshi’s 1874 three-panel print of the fighting at Shimotoba, in the collection of the Edo-Tokyo Museum, was made six years after the battle for a commercial audience. It shows what the battle was imagined to look like, not what happened, and its drama cannot be treated as documentation.

Section 53.7

Imperial Legitimacy and the Collapse of Shogunal Resistance

After Toba–Fushimi the court declared Yoshinobu an enemy of the throne (chōteki) in early February and made Prince Arisugawa Taruhito commander of an eastern campaign. Three armies advanced along the Tōkaidō, Tōsandō, and Hokurikudō roads, with Saigō a staff officer under the supreme commander (tōsei daisōtoku-shita sanbō). Many domains along the way, including Hikone and Ōgaki, which had long been Tokugawa strongholds, submitted rather than fight. Their lords calculated risk, lineage, and the loyalties of their own retainers. The new government, lacking tax revenue, borrowed from merchant houses such as Mitsui and Ono in Kyoto and Osaka.

Legitimacy had limits. The Sekihōtai, an irregular unit led by Sagara Sōzō, marched ahead promising halved land taxes in the name of the new government. When the government could not afford to honor the promise, it disavowed the unit, and Sagara was executed in March 1868. The episode shows that village support depended on material concerns and that the new authorities were wary of promises they could not keep. In Edo, Yoshinobu entered confinement at Kan’eiji temple on March 5, 1868 (Keiō 4/2/12), pledged obedience, and dismissed Oguri Tadamasa, who had urged resistance. The decision to submit was his own, and it left the Tokugawa military to decide whether to follow.

Section 53.8

The Negotiated Surrender of Edo

Yoshinobu entrusted Tokugawa affairs to Katsu Kaishū, who held the post of army commissioner (rikugun sōsai) and was well placed to negotiate; he did not control the whole Tokugawa navy, much of which answered to Enomoto Takeaki. On April 1 he had sent Yamaoka Tesshū, a bakufu retainer, through imperial lines to Suruga to meet Saigō, who set out conditions that included Yoshinobu’s confinement, the surrender of Edo Castle, weapons, and ships, and the punishment of those who had led resistance. Katsu and Saigō met on April 5 at the Satsuma residence at Takanawa and on April 6 (Keiō 4/3/13–14) at the Satsuma storehouse residence in Tamachi, with other officials present. An assault on Edo had been scheduled for April 7.

The negotiators agreed to a settlement in which Yoshinobu would be confined at Mito instead of being punished more severely, the castle would be handed over, and the Tokugawa house would continue. The ships would be surrendered, though Katsu delayed, and in the end Enomoto took part of the fleet north. Contributing factors included Katsu’s skill, pleas by Tokugawa household women, among them Kazunomiya, the widow of Iemochi, and Tenshōin, Atsuhime, and the concern of the British minister Harry Parkes that an attack on a city of over a million would endanger foreign residents and trade. How much weight Parkes’s view carried with Saigō is debated. Saigō and other commanders could have continued the war; their choice to negotiate reflected their need to avoid a prolonged struggle and the risk of destroying the city that they wished to govern.

Yoshinobu left for Mito on May 3. Edo Castle was surrendered on May 3, 1868 (Keiō 4/4/11), and the Tokugawa house was later assigned a reduced domain in Shizuoka under the child Tokugawa Kamenosuke (Iesato). Edo’s population did not experience a siege, but many retainers faced loss of position and income, and those who refused the settlement chose to fight.

Section 53.9

The Shōgitai and the Battle of Ueno

The Shōgitai was formed in February 1868 by Hitotsubashi and other Tokugawa retainers, among them Shibusawa Seiichirō (Kisaku) and Amano Hachirō, to guard Yoshinobu. Seiichirō’s cousin Shibusawa Eiichi was in Europe with a Tokugawa delegation and took no part. Seiichirō left the Shōgitai before Ueno and organized a separate force, the Shinbugun. When Yoshinobu left, hundreds remained at the Kan’eiji temple at Ueno, where they sheltered the temple’s prince-abbot. Many were loyalists who regarded the surrender as dishonorable; others had nowhere to go. Their presence challenged the new government’s authority in the very city it had just taken.

The imperial side placed Ōmura Masujirō in command. On July 4, 1868 (Keiō 4/5/15), forces from Satsuma, Chōshū, and other domains attacked the position in a single day. Saga’s Armstrong artillery, firing from the Hongō side across the pond, was decisive against the Shōgitai’s defenses at the temple’s Black Gate. The temple burned, and the survivors fled north, many to join resistance in Aizu and elsewhere. The prince-abbot escaped as well, and northern leaders later sheltered him, which raised questions about rival claims to legitimacy.

Ueno shows that the surrender of Edo Castle was a negotiated settlement among the top leaders and did not end loyalty to the Tokugawa among retainers. It also demonstrated that the imperial side could use modern weaponry decisively, and that the war’s center of gravity would now shift north.

Section 53.10

The Northern Domains and the Ōuetsu Reppan Dōmei

After Edo’s surrender the government treated Aizu and Shōnai as enemies, the first for its record as Kyoto Protector and the second for its part in the attack on the Satsuma residence. It sent Kujō Michitaka as commander of a northern pacification force, with Chōshū and Satsuma staff officers, and ordered Sendai and Yonezawa, the leading northern domains, to attack Aizu. Both were reluctant. They had ties of marriage and long association with Aizu, and many of their samurai doubted that Satsuma and Chōshū acted for the emperor rather than for themselves. Aizu offered apologies through them, but the government demanded surrender and Katamori’s punishment.

The alliance did not begin with a single event. Representatives of northern domains met several times at Shiroishi in May and June 1868 (around May 15 and in the intercalary fourth month, including a gathering of about twenty-five domains around June 13), drafting petitions for Aizu’s pardon and negotiating the alliance before it was formalized. On June 10 Sera Shūzō, a Chōshū staff officer, was killed at Fukushima by Sendai and Fukushima samurai, which hardened both sides. On June 22 twenty-five northern domains signed the agreement establishing the Ōu Reppan Dōmei, and the Echigo domains then joined to form the Ōuetsu alliance. Its documents sought peace for Aizu and Shōnai and criticized the government’s leaders for pursuing private ends.

The alliance was weak. It lacked a central command, funds, and a shared aim, since some members wanted mediation and others wanted to defeat the government. Several joined under pressure from stronger neighbors. On August 21, 1868 (Keiō 4/7/4), after the killing of Sendai emissaries, Akita broke with the alliance and attacked its former allies. Separately, after Ueno the prince-abbot of Kan’eiji escaped north, and some leaders later sheltered him as a figurehead; how far this amounted to a rival court is debated and should not be confused with the alliance’s original purpose. Northern resistance reflected a real dispute over who held legitimate authority, which victory would settle by force.

The coalition’s members had unequal weight and conflicting aims. Sendai, the Date house with some 620,000 koku, was the largest member and the nominal leader, but its leadership was divided between those who wanted mediation and those who wanted to resist the new government. Yonezawa’s Uesugi, smaller but close to Aizu, pressed for a negotiated peace. Shōnai and Aizu, the two domains the government had named as enemies, were fighting for survival. Smaller domains, many of them on the borders of Aizu, joined to avoid being the first target. These differences meant that the alliance could not coordinate strategy: Aizu fought along its southern frontier, Echigo domains defended river lines, and Sendai’s forces were held back for mediation.

Military pressure exposed the weaknesses. The southern gateway to Aizu at Shirakawa was contested for months, and an imperial force landed on the Iwaki coast in the summer to open a second front. As defections such as Akita’s multiplied and the Echigo front weakened, Yonezawa submitted its surrender document on October 18 (Keiō 4/9/3), according to the National Diet Library, although some regional chronologies place its capitulation between October 16 and 19, and Sendai on October 30 (9/15). Aizu, increasingly isolated, surrendered on November 6 (9/22). Regional chronologies date Shōnai’s capitulation to November 8 (9/24), and the Sakai family’s historical organization dates its formal surrender and apology to November 10 (9/26); these may describe different stages. The alliance thus failed because its members could not agree on what they were fighting for, not simply because of the superiority of imperial arms.

Section 53.11

The Hokuriku Front and the Battle for Nagaoka

Nagaoka, a small domain of the Makino house in Echigo, had in Kawai Tsugunosuke a senior retainer who reformed its army along Western lines and bought modern weapons, reportedly including Gatling guns, from arms merchants of Niigata. Kawai sought armed neutrality. On June 21, 1868 he met the imperial commander Iwamura Takatoshi at Ojiya to ask for it. The government demanded troops and money, the talks failed, and Nagaoka joined the northern alliance.

The government’s forces took Nagaoka castle on July 8, 1868 after crossing the Shinano river. For two months the domain’s forces fought on from the hills. On September 11 Kawai’s men recaptured the castle in a surprise assault from a flank, but they lost it again on September 15. The prefectural history records about 2,511 residences destroyed, and refugees fled to the hills as the town burned. Kawai was wounded and died in Aizu territory in early October. Nagaoka shows the limits of a small domain’s modernization: modern arms made the defense effective for weeks, but could not overcome numbers, supply, and political isolation.

The campaign mattered beyond Echigo. The Hokuriku front was an important western theater that occupied imperial forces for much of the summer and, with Nagaoka’s fall, weakened northern resistance, though it did not by itself decide the Aizu campaign. Nagaoka’s experience also illustrates a common pattern: a domain that tried to avoid choosing sides was forced to choose once the government demanded troops and money. Neutrality required a power the small domain did not have, and the imperial commanders, who were themselves under pressure to show results, had little room to accept it.

Military geography shaped the Echigo campaign. Nagaoka lay on the Shinano, Japan’s longest river, and controlled crossings on the main routes between the Sea of Japan coast and the mountains leading to Aizu. The imperial staff, including Yamagata Aritomo and Kuroda Kiyotaka, had to cross the river and take Nagaoka before advancing inland. The domain’s forces fought from prepared positions along the river and in the surrounding hills. Their recapture of the castle on September 11 was a gamble that depended on surprise and stretched their supply lines; the loss on September 15 left Nagaoka’s people homeless and the domain’s army scattered, and many of its fighters withdrew into Aizu territory.

Section 53.12

Aizu and the Struggle for Northeastern Japan

Matsudaira Katamori had served as Kyoto Protector from 1862, commanding Aizu forces that policed the capital, and had been trusted by Emperor Kōmei. The new government nonetheless treated him as the principal enemy. Imperial armies entered Aizu territory in October after defeating its forces at the Bonari pass on October 6, a force including troops commanded by Itagaki Taisuke of Tosa reached Wakamatsu on October 8, and the siege of the castle town of Wakamatsu began on October 8 and lasted about a month. Aizu’s forces were organized by age into units, the Genbukutai, Seiryūtai, Suzakutai, and Byakkotai. Katamori surrendered on November 6, 1868.

Women took part in the defense. Nakano Takeko led a women’s fighting band, assembled on its own initiative rather than as an official domain unit, and was killed in action in the autumn. Niijima Yae (Yamamoto Yae), who later became a Red Cross volunteer nurse and an educator who helped establish Doshisha’s women’s school, took part in the castle’s defense and is traditionally said to have fired a rifle. Other women nursed the wounded, made cartridges, and put out shell fires. Many families, including that of the senior retainer Saigō Tanomo, died by suicide as the town fell.

The best-known episode involves the Byakkotai, sixteen- and seventeen-year-old boys. On October 8, 1868, a group of about twenty retreated to Iimoriyama; nineteen died by the conventional count (scholars differ on whether all died in the same place and circumstances), many by suicide, and one, Iinuma Sadakichi, survived. The traditional account says they saw smoke over the town, believed the castle had fallen, and chose death out of loyalty. Historians treat this as a traditional narrative needing scrutiny. The survivor’s recollections were recorded years later, and later retellings were shaped by Meiji and twentieth-century ideals of loyalty and sacrifice. The boys’ exhaustion, wounds, and isolation may have mattered. After the surrender Aizu was dissolved; Katamori was confined, and in 1870 Aizu families were resettled in the harsh Shimokita region as the small domain of Tonami, where many endured poverty. A familiar story holds that the government forbade the burial of Aizu’s dead; a general prohibition across defeated regions has not been established, and burial practices were complicated and local.

Why Aizu fought is a question its defenders and its enemies answered differently. Katamori and his retainers pointed to years of service to the court and shogunate and to their repeated petitions for pardon through Yonezawa and Sendai. The government, for its part, held Aizu responsible for violence in Kyoto in the 1860s and refused terms short of surrender. Aizu’s leaders chose to fight when no negotiated settlement remained. The evidence for this reasoning comes mainly from domain petitions and later memoirs, so the motives attributed to individual leaders should be treated as reconstructions.

The fall of Aizu’s defenses was sudden. Much of the domain’s army had been committed to distant fronts, including Shirakawa and the Echigo border, when imperial forces moved through the Bonari pass and reached Wakamatsu within days. The defenders, many of them old men, boys, and recalled troops, withdrew to Tsuruga Castle as the town burned. During the month-long siege the castle was bombarded by artillery while women organized food, cartridges, nursing, and firefighting. The leaders’ decision to hold out, and then to surrender on November 6, reflected a judgment that further resistance would only increase the suffering. After the surrender, the Aizu-Wakamatsu digital archive records that retainers were first confined at places such as Inawashiro and Shiokawa and later transferred to Tokyo and Takada; the domain’s lands were taken.

Section 53.13

Civilians, Soldiers, Refugees, and the Costs of Restoration

The war’s burdens reached ordinary people through requisition, displacement, and destruction, though the surviving evidence is uneven. Village headmen’s records, relief petitions, and temple registers record damage and demands, and samurai memoirs dominate the narrative, so the voices of women, the poor, and the dead are hard to recover. Domain and imperial armies drew on villages for porters (jinpu), grain, horses, and boats, and quartered troops on households. In Nagaoka the municipal history records about 2,511 residences destroyed as the front crossed the Shinano twice in two months, and refugees fled to the hills and surrounding villages.

In Aizu the castle town of Wakamatsu was burned and its castle besieged for a month, and the municipal history documents the destruction of buildings and the displacement of residents; the details of where townspeople sheltered are less well recorded and should not be generalized. After Aizu’s fall, peasant unrest spread, beginning on November 16, 1868, in disturbances (yonaoshi) over rents, taxes, debts, and the conduct of village officials, which show that farmers acted on their own grievances once the old authorities collapsed.

Medical care shows an unusual documentary case. The British legation physician William Willis treated wounded men of both sides in Echigo and Aizu and worked with Japanese doctors. The illustrated record of Akagawa Genreki, preserved at Nagoya University, documents the treatment of gunshot wounds under Willis’s guidance and shows Western surgical methods entering Japanese practice. The surviving record cannot show how many wounded received comparable care.

The memory of the dead was shaped by victory and defeat. The government built the Tokyo Shōkonsha in 1869 to honor the imperial dead, while the dead of Aizu and other defeated domains were memorialized locally. For defeated households, the costs continued after the fighting as loss of rank, stipend, and home.

Multiple Perspectives

Experiencing the War

Court nobles and coalition leaders: Saw the war as securing a legitimate government, though their authority was precarious.
Aizu and northern samurai: Many saw themselves as loyal subjects punished by an illegitimate faction.
Farmers and townspeople: Suffered requisition, flight, and destruction with little political voice.
Ainu communities: Watched a Wajin conflict unfold in their homeland, with colonization to follow.
Everyday Life and Environment

Wartime Communities

For households in Aizu, Nagaoka, and Edo, the war meant lost labor, billeted soldiers, shortages of food, destroyed housing, and refugees. Women nursed the wounded and, in Aizu, fought and died. Surviving defeated families faced poverty, relocation, and stigma.

Section 53.14

Foreign Powers, Diplomacy, and Military Technology

On February 18, 1868 the treaty powers declared neutrality. Their treaties had been made with the shogunate, and two authorities now claimed to speak for Japan. Neutrality was a diplomatic stance toward those claims, not an end to commerce: the treaty ports stayed open, and it lasted until early 1869, when the Restoration government’s control was clear.

The new government used early incidents to establish credibility. In early February the court announced that it would honor the treaties. On February 4, 1868 troops of Bizen fired on foreigners at Kobe, and the foreign representatives arranged armed protection of the foreign quarter on February 5. On February 8 they agreed to withdraw their forces after assurances from the new government (American forces had withdrawn by February 9), and the officer responsible, Taki Zenzaburō, committed seppuku on March 2. On March 8 Tosa troops killed eleven French sailors at Sakai, and after French demands eleven Tosa men committed seppuku on March 16. Harry Parkes was attacked on his way to an audience on March 23, and completed his imperial audience on March 26. Together these events persuaded the powers that the government could discipline its forces and keep its commitments.

The missions pursued different interests. Britain, with commercial priorities and links to Satsuma and Chōshū through Parkes and his staff, including Ernest Satow, leaned toward the imperial coalition without committing to it. France’s Léon Roches had backed the shogunate’s reforms and, according to historical scholarship, encouraged continued resistance in early 1868, but Yoshinobu declined French military intervention; some French officers, notably Jules Brunet, later joined Enomoto as individuals.

Merchants and arms suppliers followed their own interests. Thomas Glover supplied several western domains; Saga’s Armstrong guns were used at Ueno; the Schnell brothers sold weapons to northern domains, and Henry Schnell took service with Aizu and in 1869 took Aizu families to California, where they founded the Wakamatsu Tea and Silk Colony. The ironclad Stonewall, bought by the shogunate, was held under neutrality rules until early 1869, when it was delivered to the government as the Kōtetsu. Foreign actors supplied arms, advice, and diplomatic recognition, but decisions on war, surrender, and government remained in Japanese hands.

Section 53.15

Enomoto Takeaki, the Tokugawa Fleet, and Ezo

Enomoto Takeaki, a naval officer who had studied in the Netherlands, did not hand over the Tokugawa fleet that Katsu had promised. He sailed from Shinagawa on October 4, 1868 (Keiō 4/8/19) with eight ships, including the Kaiyō maru, carrying retainers, soldiers, and French advisers such as Brunet. Storms damaged the fleet. Enomoto’s purpose was to find land where displaced Tokugawa retainers could settle and support themselves; he petitioned the court to let them develop Ezo under Tokugawa patronage. The court refused.

After a long passage the forces landed at Washinoki on December 3, 1868, occupied Hakodate and the Western-style fortress of Goryōkaku on December 9, and took Matsumae castle on December 18. On December 28, 1868 (Meiji 1/11/15) the Kaiyō maru ran aground in a storm off Esashi and sank soon afterward. Its loss removed the regime’s most powerful warship and weakened its ability to contest the imperial navy in 1869. On January 27, 1869 (traditional 12/15), the officers voted to choose leaders, and Enomoto became sōsai, with Ōtori Keisuke and Hijikata Toshizō among the commanders. The regime is often called the ‘Republic of Ezo,’ but it was a provisional military government of former Tokugawa retainers. British and French warships arrived at Hakodate in mid-December, and their captains, meeting Enomoto together with consular representatives, communicated with his regime as the authority controlling the port. Their ministers refused to recognize it as an independent belligerent power, so this practical acknowledgment was not diplomatic recognition of a state.

The Ezo expedition was a political project as well as a military one. Enomoto’s forces included French advisers and former shogunal soldiers. In Ezo they met the weak local administration of the imperial government, whose governor at Hakodate fled, and the Matsumae domain, which had sided with the government and withdrew to Tsugaru when Enomoto attacked. The new regime issued orders and levied taxes and requisitions.

For Ainu communities, the war was an intrusion into a society already subject to Wajin merchants and the fisheries economy. The surviving documents were written by Wajin officials and foreign observers, and Ainu voices are largely absent from them. What is clear is that Ainu labor and land underpinned the commerce of Ezo, that Ainu communities were not consulted about the regime or the war, and that the government that defeated it soon established a colonization office with far-reaching consequences.

Section 53.16

The Battle of Hakodate and the End of the Boshin War

The final campaign unfolded in the spring of 1869. On May 6 a Tokugawa raid on the ironclad Kōtetsu (the former Stonewall) at Miyako Bay failed, and the imperial navy gained command of the sea. On May 20 the government’s expeditionary force, gathered at Aomori, landed at Otobe on Ezo’s southwest coast and advanced on Hakodate by land while ships bombarded the port. The major assault came on June 20, and Hijikata Toshizō, formerly of the Shinsengumi and a senior commander, was killed that day. The Benten battery surrendered on June 24, and Enomoto’s position became untenable.

Enomoto surrendered Goryōkaku on June 27, 1869 (Meiji 2/5/18). Before surrendering he sent a book on maritime law to the imperial commander, Kuroda Kiyotaka, to preserve it; Kuroda, impressed, later argued for his life. Enomoto was imprisoned, pardoned in 1872, and served in several Meiji ministries. The surrender ended the Boshin War.

The Kaitakushi (Colonization Office) was established in the summer of 1869, and the name Hokkaidō was promulgated later that autumn. These acts followed the war and should be distinguished from its conclusion. They turned a battleground of Wajin factions into an administrative and settlement project whose consequences for the Ainu later chapters treat in detail.

The aftermath was less severe than in the north. Enomoto, Ōtori Keisuke, and other leaders were taken to Tokyo and imprisoned; Ōtori, like Enomoto, was pardoned in 1872 and served the government. Treatment of the surrendered varied by rank and category: senior leaders were imprisoned in Tokyo, while many lower-ranking men were later released, though the extent and timing require documentary support. The government’s lenience toward some former enemies reflected its need for trained naval and technical personnel, and contrasts with its treatment of Aizu.

The campaign depended on sea power and the roads of the Oshima peninsula. At Miyako Bay the Kaiten reportedly approached the Kōtetsu under a foreign flag before the boarding attempt was beaten off by Gatling-gun fire. By late June the garrison was cut off and besieged, and Enomoto surrendered on June 27; the surviving documents do not allow a reliable reconstruction of the leaders’ private deliberations.

Section 53.17

Constructing a Government in the Emperor’s Name

The government was built during the war, by improvisation. On January 3, 1868 it announced three provisional offices, which the National Diet Library dates as formally established on January 11: the sōsai, a presidency held by Prince Arisugawa Taruhito; the gijō, councillors from princes, court nobles, and domain lords; and the san’yo, advisers from samurai officials such as Saigō, Ōkubo Toshimichi, Kido Takayoshi, and Gotō Shōjirō. Iwakura Tomomi and Sanjō Sanetomi were the leading court figures. On February 10 this was expanded into the sanshoku shichika, three offices with seven functional sections (religion, domestic affairs, foreign affairs, military, finance, justice, and institutions), and on February 25 into eight bureaus (sanshoku hakkyoku) with a bureau for general administration. Leadership was reshuffled repeatedly as rivalries and wartime needs changed.

On June 11, 1868 the Seitaisho, drafted by Fukuoka Takachika and Soejima Taneomi with reference to translated Western constitutional writings, set out a revived Dajōkan (Council of State) with legislative, executive, and judicial branches, and provided for officials to be selected by ballot and rotated. It mixed Chinese and Japanese precedents with Western models. Its practice was modest: its branches were not independent in a modern constitutional sense, and the ballot held on June 22, 1869 was confined to designated officials, not the public. On August 15, 1869 the government revived ancient court offices, placing a Jingikan (Department of Divinities) above the Dajōkan; this was a reorganization along older court lines, not a modern separation of powers. Ideals of public deliberation and the imperial claim of rule coexisted uneasily.

The gap between claim and capacity was wide. The finance office under Yuri Kimimasa raised forced loans (goyōkin) from merchant houses such as Mitsui, Ono, and Shimada, and from mid-1868 issued inconvertible paper notes (Dajōkan-satsu) that depreciated quickly. Tokugawa lands, and those of enemy domains such as Aizu, were confiscated and many became prefectures (fu and ken) under imperial governors, while the Tokugawa house was reduced to a domain at Shizuoka. Even so, most of the country’s land and revenue remained under domain control. The government relied on the troops of Satsuma, Chōshū, Tosa, Saga, and other domains, so its authority depended on the cooperation of the domains it ultimately aimed to bring under central control. Chapter 54 examines that process.

In practice the government extended its authority by sending officials and troops to take over Tokugawa territories and by securing declarations of allegiance and administrative cooperation from domain lords. Domains that submitted were generally left intact, since the government had neither the personnel nor the funds to replace their administrations. Its own staff came from the same few domains and from court nobles, which gave it a factional character and produced rivalry over posts, income, and policy; the pattern was later criticized under the label hanbatsu (domain clique), a retrospective political characterization rather than a term of 1868. Authority was thus exercised through bargains with domains more than through commands to them.

Section 53.18

The Charter Oath and the Promises and Limits of Restoration

On April 6, 1868, the emperor’s Charter Oath was proclaimed at the Kyoto palace in a ritual before the kami, with court nobles and domain lords in attendance. Its authorship was collaborative. Yuri Kimimasa drafted an initial version emphasizing a council of lords, Fukuoka Takachika revised it, and Kido Takayoshi made further changes, broadening the wording about assemblies and public discussion. The five articles called for wide assemblies and decisions by public opinion, unity of high and low in managing the state, freedom for officials and commoners to pursue their aims, abandonment of ‘evil customs’ in favor of universal principles, and the seeking of knowledge throughout the world.

The oath addressed the lords, nobles, and officials of the new government, and the war was far from over. Its commitments were broad and undefined: it did not say who would deliberate, how assemblies would be chosen, or what limits public opinion would place on the government. The next day, April 7, the government posted public notices addressed to the general population. They included moral exhortations, bans on unauthorized collective protest and on abandoning one’s domain, and the continuing prohibition of Christianity. In the following years thousands of Christians from Urakami near Nagasaki were arrested and exiled. The Christianity-prohibition notice boards were removed by order of February 24, 1873, which did not by itself create comprehensive religious freedom.

The contrast between oath and notices is central. The oath looked to a more open and outward-looking government, while the notices reasserted the Tokugawa repertoire of order. Historians do not agree on whether the oath was a program, a propaganda statement, or an expression of unity among competing factions. Its meaning was reinterpreted, notably by participants in the Freedom and People’s Rights Movement (Chapter 57), who cited it to demand an elected assembly.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Charter Oath and Tokugawa Restrictions

What changed? The shogunate was abolished, an imperial government claimed national authority, and a new capital emerged. What continued? Domain armies, restrictions on Christianity and protest, and many officials. Who experienced change most directly? Tokugawa retainers and defeated domains. Who experienced continuity? Most villagers, whose burdens persisted. Why it matters: It explains why further reforms were needed.

Section 53.19

From Edo to Tokyo: Legitimacy, Victory, and an Unfinished Revolution

On September 3, 1868 an imperial edict renamed Edo Tokyo, ‘eastern capital,’ and on October 23 the era name was changed to Meiji, with one era name per reign. These were separate acts. The emperor traveled from Kyoto to Tokyo in the autumn, arriving on November 26, and the castle became his residence. He returned to Kyoto in early 1869 and made a second journey east in the spring. On April 5, 1869 (Meiji 2/2/24) the government decided to move the Dajōkan to Tokyo, an administrative shift distinct from the emperor’s symbolic movements. Kyoto was never formally abolished as a capital, and the shift of the court and Dajōkan to Tokyo took place gradually, without a single decree.

The move mattered symbolically. A teenage sovereign who had been seen by few outside the court became visible in processions through a city of former shogunal retainers. It placed the government in the Tokugawa political center, with its residences, warehouses, and talent, and away from Kyoto’s court nobility. The pairing of the emperor with new offices and an era name declared that the Tokugawa age had ended.

By summer 1869 the war was over but the revolution was unfinished. Domains kept their armies, taxes, and officials. The government was heavily indebted and depended on Satsuma, Chōshū, Tosa, and Saga. Many samurai, including Tokugawa and other defeated retainers, faced loss of income and status. In 1869 several lords returned their registers of land and people to the throne, a step that began the abolition of the domains examined in Chapter 54. Victory had given the government legitimacy, force, and an administrative core; turning these into a centralized state was the next task.

Chapter Summary

The Restoration emerged from contested decisions. The failure of the 1866 Chōshū expedition weakened the shogunate, and competing plans from Yoshinobu, Tosa, and the Satsuma–Chōshū leaders offered different meanings for restoration. Yoshinobu’s taisei hōkan was a bid to keep influence by changing its form; the January 1868 declaration created a government without Tokugawa leadership. War followed because each side saw the other’s moves as a challenge.

At Toba–Fushimi, imperial banners, defections, and Yoshinobu’s flight gave the coalition a legitimacy that attracted domains. Edo’s surrender was negotiated, though the Shōgitai fought at Ueno. The northern alliance, Nagaoka, and Aizu resisted what they regarded as a faction’s seizure of power, at great cost to civilians.

In Ezo, Enomoto’s Tokugawa fleet formed a provisional government and was defeated in June 1869, a conflict between Wajin factions on Ainu land that preceded colonization. Meanwhile the new government assembled offices, issued the Charter Oath, financed itself by loans and paper notes, and relied on domain troops. The oath’s promises of deliberation stood beside notices restricting protest and Christianity.

Legitimacy was a resource, not a given. The imperial banner, the label chōteki, and court appointments changed the choices of waverers, but they worked together with troops, money, and negotiation. Domains that submitted did so out of conviction, fear, and calculation, and the new government tolerated their autonomy because it could not replace it.

The defeated were historical actors. Aizu and the northern domains argued that the new leaders were a faction acting in the emperor’s name, and their defeat brought dissolution, resettlement in Shimokita, and hardship. Memories such as the Byakkotai story took shape later and must be separated from contemporary evidence. Defeat on the Tokugawa side also did not end careers: Enomoto later served in Meiji ministries.

Civilians bore much of the cost. Conscripted laborers, requisitioned grain, burned towns such as Nagaoka, flight to the hills, and yonaoshi risings show that the war was experienced very differently by samurai, farmers, women, and townspeople. Foreign powers declared neutrality but their merchants armed both sides, and foreign advisers served both; they shaped the supply of arms and the risks of fighting without directing the outcome.

Historians continue to debate whether the Restoration was a revolution, an elite reorganization, or a civil war. This chapter’s evidence suggests it was all three in different respects, and that it was contingent. It created a government with legitimacy and force but with weak institutions, which is why the next stage required the abolition of the domains.

Chapter Reflection

The Boshin War and Restoration show how legitimacy, force, and negotiation combined to create a new government, and how fragile that government was. It depended on domains, financed itself by loans and paper notes, and spoke to subjects in a language of both reform and restriction.

The war connected Kyoto, Edo, the northeast, and Ezo, and began the incorporation of the Ainu homeland into a new state. It also left memories of defeat in Aizu and elsewhere that shaped later regional identities. Chapter 54 turns to how the victors dismantled the domain and samurai order.

The chapter also models historical method. Dates had to be converted between calendars, retrospective prints separated from contemporary reports, and traditional stories such as the Byakkotai’s scrutinized. Students should carry these habits into later chapters, where memory, nationalism, and official narratives will again shape what is recorded.
