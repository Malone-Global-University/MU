Part VII — Tokugawa Japan
45

Roads, Castle Towns, Merchants, and the Urban Economy

Japanese History: From Jōmon Japan to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

Chapter 44 followed rice, cotton, oilseed, silk, paper, and labor out of the villages. This chapter follows them onto the roads, into the boats, through the warehouses, and into the cities, where they became money, credit, wages, food, clothing, and the daily purchases of a population that no longer lived mainly by what it grew itself.

Consider a single bundle of tax rice leaving a village in a domain on the Japan Sea coast. It is assessed in the village, collected by domain officials, carried to a river port or coastal harbor, loaded onto a coasting vessel, and unloaded at a domain-owned warehouse in Osaka. There it is sold through brokers, its value converted into silver or into a paper claim, and the proceeds are remitted to cover expenses that the domain's lord and his retainers incur hundreds of kilometers away, in Edo. Meanwhile, the lord himself, along with a procession of retainers, porters, and baggage, travels the same distance in the opposite direction on a highway lined with inns whose owners, porters, and horse-keepers depend on his passage. In the course of this single cycle, goods, people, money, and information have moved through a political system, a transport system, and a commercial system that were designed for different purposes but had become interdependent.

This chapter is about that interdependence. It examines the economic circulation and urbanization of Tokugawa Japan: the system of alternate attendance (sankin-kōtai) that sent daimyō and their entourages along the highways; the post stations, barriers, and coastal routes that carried people and cargo; the castle towns where warriors and the commoners who served them gathered; the three great cities of Edo, Osaka, and Kyoto; and the markets, money changers, wholesalers, merchant houses, and associations that made exchange possible at scale. It also examines the emergence of a consumer society, in which a widening range of households bought goods beyond bare subsistence. The chapter's central problem is how a warrior-led political order, whose moral discourse often disparaged profit-making and which gave merchants none of the privileges of warriors, came to depend on commercial institutions for its everyday functioning.

The chapter's argument can be stated briefly. Tokugawa political control helped create economic integration, but it did not do so by design or alone. Castle towns concentrated samurai consumers; sankin-kōtai generated recurring movements of people and resources; highways, post stations, coastal shipping, and courier networks connected regions; and merchants developed increasingly sophisticated systems of wholesale trade, credit, money exchange, and market information. Edo became a vast center of political consumption, Osaka a commercial and financial clearinghouse, and Kyoto a center of specialized manufacture. The result enriched merchant houses and widened the gap between formal status and economic power. It did not, however, erase regional differences, and it did not turn Tokugawa Japan into a modern capitalist economy.

A second thread concerns the way historians have changed their picture of the period. An older image presented Tokugawa Japan as a closed, static "feudal" rice economy in which merchants were socially marginal and economically parasitic. Research over several decades has replaced that image with one of extensive commercialization, high urbanization by premodern standards, and sophisticated financial practice, while also warning against overcorrecting into a story in which commerce mechanically leads toward industrial capitalism. This chapter tries to hold both observations together: the Tokugawa economy was dynamic, and it was also preindustrial, regionally uneven, and politically fragmented.

Students should remember what this chapter is not. It is not a history of "the Japanese economy" as a single national unit; the Tokugawa realm consisted of the shogunal domain and roughly 250 to 300 semi-autonomous domains, each with its own fiscal arrangements, and economic integration happened across, and sometimes in spite of, those jurisdictions. It also does not treat Honshu's central corridor as the whole archipelago. Ezo (Hokkaido) and the Ainu communities, the Ryukyu Kingdom, and the maritime periphery had their own commercial worlds, which enter the narrative where they intersected with the domestic market and are treated more fully in Chapter 48.

The evidence for this subject is unusually rich and unusually uneven. Account books, merchant house codes, domain fiscal records, post-station registers, travel diaries, price series, municipal regulations (machibure), maps, guidebooks, and commercially published prints survive in large numbers, but they overwhelmingly reflect the interests of officials, merchant proprietors, and urban elites. Porters, servants, apprentices, day laborers, and village women who supplied labor to the road system appear mainly as entries in other people's records. Historians reconstruct their experience indirectly, and this chapter notes where that is the case.

The chapter fits within Part VII as the point at which Tokugawa Japan moves from production to circulation. It draws on the political structure of Chapter 42, the status order of Chapter 43, and the village economy of Chapter 44, and it prepares directly for Chapter 46, whose subject, the commercial entertainment culture of the floating world, depended on the cities, wealth, and disposable income examined here. By the chapter's end, students should understand how Tokugawa Japan became commercially connected without becoming politically or socially uniform.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how sankin-kōtai functioned as a political institution and why it had major but partly unintended economic consequences.
  2. Describe the Gokaidō highway system, the post-station economy, and the barrier system, and analyze how the Tokugawa state regulated mobility while presiding over an increasingly mobile society.
  3. Assess the role of coastal shipping and inland waterways relative to roads in moving bulk goods.
  4. Explain how castle towns turned samurai government into urban consumer markets.
  5. Distinguish the different urban functions of Edo, Osaka, and Kyoto while recognizing that each city performed several roles.
  6. Analyze why a warrior-led order whose moral discourse often disparaged commerce depended on merchants, money changers, wholesalers, and credit.
  7. Trace the development of Tokugawa money, exchange, and credit instruments and explain the institutions of the Dōjima rice market without projecting modern finance onto it.
  8. Compare the organization of major merchant houses such as Mitsui, Kōnoike, and Sumitomo, and explain why they should not be called zaibatsu.
  9. Evaluate the role of merchant associations (kabunakama) and their changing relationship with authorities.
  10. Interpret evidence for the growth of consumer society, including retail practice, advertising, and everyday purchasing, while recognizing its uneven reach.
  11. Evaluate historical interpretations of sankin-kōtai as economic burden and as integrating force.
  12. Connect the commercial economy of this chapter to the status order (Chapter 43), village economy (Chapter 44), and the floating-world culture of Chapter 46.

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How did a political institution designed to control daimyō generate economic effects its designers may not have intended?
  2. Why did a shift from production to circulation require roads, ships, warehouses, money, credit, and information to work together?
  3. In what sense did Edo consume, Osaka clear and distribute, and Kyoto specialize, and where does that formula break down?
  4. Why did samurai, whose status was defined by service and rice stipends, come to rely on merchants?
  5. How should historians interpret the Dōjima rice market: as a forerunner of modern exchanges, as a distinctive Tokugawa institution, or as both?
  6. How did merchant organizations and authorities negotiate the balance between regulation and commercial autonomy?
  7. What does the growth of consumer society reveal about who participated in markets, and who remained largely outside them?
  8. How did the gap between formal status and economic power reshape Tokugawa society without producing a new ruling class?
  9. In what ways was the Tokugawa economy integrated, and in what ways did it remain fragmented, regional, and preindustrial?

Historical Context

Three earlier chapters supply the foundations of this one. Chapter 42 explained the bakuhan political system: the shogun ruled the large shogunal domain directly and exercised overlord authority over the daimyō, whose domains retained substantial autonomy in administration and taxation. It also introduced sankin-kōtai as an institution of political control, which is recalled here only for its economic effects. Chapter 43 showed that the four-category language of warriors, farmers, artisans, and merchants was an ideology with many exceptions, and that real status distinctions were more varied. Chapter 44 described villages that grew rice, cotton, oilseed, silk, paper, and other commodities, paid taxes largely in kind, and were drawn into regional commercial networks. It stopped before wholesalers, merchant credit, and urban finance, which are the subject of this chapter.

The commercial world of the seventeenth century also had deeper roots. Medieval Japan (Chapter 31) already possessed markets, ports, guilds (za), and money economies, though trade was fragmented by local privileges and political divisions. Sengoku-era daimyō (Chapter 35) built territorial states with fortified headquarters and encouraged merchants and artisans to settle around them. Oda Nobunaga and Toyotomi Hideyoshi (Chapters 39 and 40) weakened many older monopolies, promoted open markets, and separated warriors from the land. The separation of the warrior class from villages, completed in many regions around 1600, is a crucial precondition for what follows: samurai living in towns on stipends needed to buy what they previously might have taken from their own lands.

The seventeenth century added conditions that made large-scale circulation possible. Political unification reduced warfare and allowed the building and maintenance of roads and post stations. A growing population and expanding cultivated land increased agricultural surplus. Domains that needed to convert tax grain into cash turned to merchant intermediaries. Regional specialization developed as villages found markets for crops and manufactures beyond subsistence. Population estimates for the Tokugawa period are uncertain, but the figure of roughly 30 million inhabitants in the early eighteenth century is widely used, and the urban share was high by the standards of contemporary societies. Edo, Osaka, and Kyoto were joined by numerous castle towns and port towns. Students should treat all population figures in this chapter as estimates, since samurai populations were not systematically counted and urban registers varied.

Finally, the geography of the archipelago shaped everything. Mountainous terrain limited the usefulness of roads for bulk freight, while a long coastline, the Inland Sea, and navigable rivers favored water transport. Rice-growing plains such as the Kantō and Kinai plains supported dense populations, while northern and upland regions had different resource bases. The chapter's emphasis on circulation therefore combines political, financial, and environmental factors, and it should be read with the geography of Chapter 1 in mind.

Section 45.1

From Production to Circulation: Connecting the Tokugawa Economy

Chapter 44 described how Tokugawa villages produced and how rulers extracted surplus. This chapter begins at the moment production ends. Rice, cotton, rapeseed, silk, paper, indigo, sake, timber, salt, dried fish, and fertilizer were of little use to the political order or to consumers until they were moved, stored, priced, sold, and paid for. The organizing problem of the chapter is therefore circulation: the movement of goods, money, people, and information across distances that, in 1600, most communities had crossed only partially and irregularly.

A useful way to understand the shift is to distinguish three scales of exchange. The first was the village and its market town, where households sold small surpluses and bought tools and salt. The second was the regional scale of the domain, where officials collected tax in kind and sold part of it, and where castle towns offered concentrated demand. The third was the interregional scale, where commodities moved between domains and to the large cities. All three existed before the Tokugawa period, but the third expanded dramatically in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, and it is this expansion that makes the period economically distinctive. It involved not only more trade but new institutions: warehouse-residences, wholesale houses, standardized exchange practices, and recognized markets.

It is important not to read this as a smooth progress toward a national market. Domains issued their own regulations, and some tried to restrict the export of valued products or protect local producers. Transport remained costly and seasonal. Information moved at the speed of couriers and ships. The Tokugawa economy was integrated in the sense that prices in distant places influenced each other and that major flows of goods and money linked regions, but it was not unified under a single legal and fiscal regime. The chapter's evidence shows both integration and persistent fragmentation.

Section 45.2

Sankin-kōtai: Political Control with Economic Consequences

The constitutional history of sankin-kōtai belongs to Chapter 42, but its outlines must be recalled. The shogunate required daimyō to divide their time between their domains and Edo, typically on a roughly yearly alternation, with variations for domains close to Edo. Their principal wives and close family members were normally required to reside in Edo; arrangements for heirs could vary by house and circumstance. The practice, institutionalized by the revised Buke shohatto of 1635 and further revised in 1642 (including different schedules for fudai and some Kantō lords), kept the domain lords under shogunal observation, limited their ability to build independent bases, and made the shogun's authority visible in recurring ceremonial attendance. Historians have long emphasized this political purpose, and the institution is not rightly described as an economic policy.

Yet its economic effects were large. A daimyō needed residences in Edo, usually several, which required land, buildings, servants, provisions, and maintenance. He needed retainers stationed in Edo, some of whom lived there for extended periods. He needed to move himself and a retinue between Edo and the domain, which meant paying for lodging, porters, horses, and provisions along the route. All this cost money, and it was money that, in a society whose tax revenue came mostly in rice, had to be obtained by selling rice and other products. Expenses associated with alternate attendance could consume an enormous share of a domain's available cash income; estimates for some domains reach roughly half or more, and in some cases still higher, though figures varied widely among domains and over time.

The consequences for the commercial economy follow from this. Because daimyō needed cash in Edo, domains created channels for selling goods and converting rice into money. Because they needed supplies, Edo's merchants and artisans gained a large and relatively predictable clientele. Because they needed transport, the highways, post stations, and shipping networks were used continually. The economic effects were not designed by the shogunate and are better understood as consequences than as policy goals. The causal question is complex: sankin-kōtai accelerated commercial integration, but it did so in combination with the other factors identified in the Cause and Effect feature later in this chapter. Reducing the story to a single line from alternate attendance to economic growth would mistake an important accelerator for a sole cause.

Section 45.3

Daimyō Processions and the Economy of Movement

A daimyō procession was a carefully ordered display of rank and a logistical operation. Weeks or months ahead, domain officials arranged the route, the schedule, and the lodging at each stage. Advance parties reserved inns, and domains sent money in advance or settled accounts afterward. The procession typically included samurai of various ranks, foot soldiers, attendants carrying the lord's palanquin and spears, baggage porters, horse handlers, and cooks. Sizes varied enormously, from a few hundred people for smaller domains to a couple of thousand or more for the largest, and the shogunate periodically issued guidelines to limit retinue sizes, which domains often exceeded in practice because retinue size expressed rank.

Economically, the procession was a moving concentration of purchasing power. It required lodging, meals, fodder for horses, replacement porters, and, at the end, supplies for the Edo residences. Domains that were far from Edo faced journeys that took many days or even weeks, and the cumulative expenses were substantial. The expenses were also unevenly distributed: the route's inns, porters, and horse-keepers received income, but communities that supplied transport labor under official obligations often received compensation below market rates, a point developed in Section 45.5.

Students should distinguish representation from logistics. Prints, paintings, and later popular memory present the procession as a stately, hierarchical pageant, and certain rituals, such as requiring commoners to kneel as a procession passed, are often emphasized. But sources such as travel accounts and domain records show that actual movement involved constant negotiation over lodging, schedules, costs, and sometimes disputes with innkeepers or local officials. The procession's visibility as an emblem of rank should not conceal that it also functioned as an economic event.

Section 45.4

The Five Highways and a Network Centered on Edo

The Tokugawa shogunate developed and administered five principal highways, collectively called the Gokaidō, whose distances were conventionally measured from Nihonbashi in Edo. The Tōkaidō ran along the Pacific coast to Kyoto and, by extension, to Osaka; the Nakasendō took an inland mountain route to Kyoto; the Kōshū Kaidō ran west into the mountains of Kai and joined the Nakasendō; the Nikkō Kaidō led north to the Tokugawa shrine complex at Nikkō; and the Ōshū Kaidō led into the northeast. The Tōkaidō had fifty-three post stations between Edo and Kyoto, covered a distance of roughly 490 kilometers (about 300 miles), and became the best-known road, partly through guidebooks and prints. Typical travelers on foot needed roughly two weeks to cover the distance between Edo and Kyoto.

The road network was administered by the shogunate through its commissioners, who regulated post-station duties, tolls, and standards. Beyond the Gokaidō lay numerous secondary highways (wakikan) and local roads under the domains' control, and a dense web of pilgrimage routes. The Gokaidō should therefore be understood as a trunk system. It linked the shogun's capital to the western core, to the Tokugawa mausoleum, and to the northeast, and it served the shogunate's administrative needs, but it was not the whole of the transport system, and it did not replace local and domain-managed routes.

An Edo-centered road system was a reflection of Tokugawa politics. Its radiating plan placed Edo at the center of a network oriented toward shogunal control and daimyō travel. But the economy it served was not centered on Edo alone. Osaka, as the next section shows, drew on a waterborne commercial network that was at least as important as the road system, and it is a mistake to equate the highway map with the commercial map. Students should compare the two in the Geography Study below.

Section 45.5

Shukuba: Post Stations, Inns, Porters, and Horses

Along the highways stood the shukuba, post stations at intervals of a few hours' walk. Each had official duties: providing porters and horses to relay official messages and baggage, and accommodating travelers on official business. Many stations designated a number of porters and horses to be available for official use, and these were supplemented by additional labor levied on surrounding villages when traffic peaked, a system sometimes called sukegō. The obligations could be heavy for the communities involved, particularly when large processions coincided with agricultural seasons, and complaints, petitions, and negotiations about these burdens appear in the records.

The shukuba also supported an ecology of private commerce. Elite travelers, particularly daimyō and shogunal officials, stayed at the honjin and the secondary waki-honjin, which were usually run by prominent local families and maintained elaborate facilities. Ordinary travelers used inns of various grades, from comfortable establishments with meals to cheaper lodging where guests cooked their own food. Alongside them operated teahouses, shops selling sandals, food stalls, and craftspeople. The station community thus received income from both official and private traffic, and many station towns grew prosperous. Others struggled when official duties outweighed private income or when traffic shifted to other routes.

Historical Vocabulary
Shukuba (post station)

A shukuba was a designated post town on a highway, responsible for providing transport relays, official accommodation, and services to travelers. The term is often translated as "post station" or "post town," and the system is sometimes compared with relay stations elsewhere. In the Tokugawa period, shukuba were both an administrative institution of the shogunate (or domains) and a commercial community whose inhabitants earned their livelihood from lodging, transport, and trade.

The concept matters here because it shows how a political transportation system generated local commercial economies, while also imposing obligations on the communities that supported it. Students should avoid imagining shukuba as quaint tourist stops: they were working towns whose fortunes depended on traffic, official policy, and the willingness of nearby villages to supply labor.

Section 45.6

Barriers, Travel Regulation, and an Increasingly Mobile Society

The roads were instruments of surveillance as well as infrastructure. The shogunate and some domains maintained barriers (sekisho) at strategic points, such as those at Hakone and Arai on the Tōkaidō and at Usui and Kiso Fukushima on the Nakasendō. Officials inspected travelers, and certain categories of travelers needed documentation. The barriers are often summarized in textbooks as controlling "guns entering Edo and women leaving it." That formula reflects regulatory concerns, particularly in the seventeenth century, but it oversimplifies a system whose enforcement varied by place, period, and traveler. Women's passage, for example, was subject to documentation requirements that differed by barrier and over time, and the practical stringency of inspection was not uniform.

Yet the same period saw a striking growth in ordinary travel. Samurai on official duty, merchants, couriers, servants, priests, entertainers, and pilgrims moved along the roads. Pilgrimage was especially important: travel to Ise, to Zenkōji, to Konpira, and to the circuits of temples was socially accepted as religious practice, and mass pilgrimages to Ise, called okage-mairi, occurred in waves, with the 1830 wave involving several million people by common estimates. Guidebooks, maps, and humorous travel narratives circulated, and the road became part of the era's popular imagination. Historians such as Constantine Vaporis have argued that travel by commoners was considerably more common than a rigid image of the "closed" Tokugawa order suggests.

The broader tension is the point: the Tokugawa state regulated mobility while presiding over an increasingly mobile society. Regulations were real, and some travelers encountered delays and inspections. But the combination of dense road infrastructure, post stations, and commercial services made movement practicable for many people, and the state's regulatory capacity was in practice limited. Students should keep in mind that mobility was unequal: elite travelers moved with servants and comfort, while porters, pack-horse drivers, and poor pilgrims endured the road differently.

Section 45.7

Roads Were Not Enough: Coastal Shipping and Inland Waterways

Roads carried people, messages, and light, valuable goods, but bulk commodities such as rice, timber, salt, sake, fertilizer, and cotton moved far more efficiently by water. A pack horse could carry only a small load, and the mountainous terrain made overland freight expensive. A single coastal vessel could carry hundreds of times that quantity. As a result, the great commodity flows of the Tokugawa economy followed sea lanes and rivers, and ports and river towns were as important as post stations.

The seventeenth century saw the systematization of coastal circuits. Merchants and shippers developed routes bringing rice and other goods from the Japan Sea coast and the northeast to Osaka and Edo, in what became known as the western and eastern circuits. The vessels later called kitamae-bune carried goods along the Japan Sea coast, through the Shimonoseki strait, and into the Inland Sea, returning with goods from the Kinai region. Between Osaka and Edo, specialized cargo lines carried bulk goods and, famously, sake barrels from the brewing districts around Nada and Nishinomiya. These shipping lines were commercial enterprises subject to risk from storms and wrecks, and marine insurance-like practices and shipping associations developed to spread the risk.

Rivers and canals supported local and regional distribution. Osaka's network of waterways allowed small boats to carry goods directly to warehouses and wholesalers, while rivers such as the Yodo connected the city to Kyoto and the Kinai interior. In the Kantō plain, river transport supplied Edo. Domestic maritime commerce therefore deserves emphasis: it shows that commercial integration depended on shipping as much as on highways. Overseas trade, including the regulated Dutch and Chinese trade at Nagasaki and the Ryukyu and Korean connections, is a separate subject that Chapter 48 treats. Here the emphasis is on the coastal and inland circuits that tied domestic regions together, including those that carried products of the northern frontier, such as marine goods from Ezo, into the national market.

Section 45.8

Castle Towns: Samurai Government Creates Urban Markets

The castle town (jōkamachi) was the basic urban unit of Tokugawa political geography. Many domains were centered on a principal castle and castle town, while smaller or lower-status daimyō (mujō daimyō) could govern from a jin'ya rather than a castle; around a castle there developed a town containing the daimyō's administration, the residences of senior and junior retainers, and the commoner districts of merchants, artisans, laborers, and service providers. The 1615 one-castle order (ikkoku ichijō rei) required daimyō to keep their principal castle while eliminating other castles in their territories, sharply reducing secondary fortifications; the roughly 250 to 300 domains nonetheless produced a large number of castle towns, ranging from small administrative centers to substantial cities such as Kanazawa, Nagoya, Sendai, Kagoshima, and Hiroshima. Their large share of Tokugawa Japan's urban population is part of what made the period unusually urbanized by premodern standards.

Their economic function followed from the separation of warriors from the land. Samurai living in castle towns received stipends, mostly in rice, and needed to buy food, clothing, housing materials, paper, tools, weapons maintenance, and services. Merchants and artisans settled in designated districts organized by trade, and their products and services supplied the warrior population. The castle town was therefore at once an administrative center and a consumption center, where political concentration created commercial demand. It was also a hierarchical space: districts were allocated by status, and the placement of samurai, townspeople, temples, and low-status groups was deliberate.

Castle towns differed substantially. Large towns with many retainers supported varied commerce, while smaller ones were more modest. Domains pursued different policies: some promoted local industries such as ceramics, lacquer, paper, or textiles; others restricted trade or relied on a few licensed merchants. Castle towns in the north and in poorer domains had fewer resources than those in prosperous regions, and the fortunes of towns changed as domain finances worsened or improved. Students should resist treating them as uniform, and should note that the experience of a samurai in a small castle town differed considerably from that of a shogunal retainer in Edo.

Section 45.9

Edo: Political Capital and Consumption Metropolis

Edo's growth was among the most striking urban developments of the early modern world. In 1590, when Tokugawa Ieyasu took over the Kantō region, Edo was a modest castle settlement. Within a century it had become the seat of the shogunate and a city whose population, by common estimates, approached or reached one million in the eighteenth century, making it among the largest in the world. Such figures are estimates: samurai populations were not systematically enumerated, and commoner registers excluded many residents. The 1657 Meireki fire destroyed much of the city and led to extensive rebuilding and replanning, which further stimulated demand for timber, labor, and building skills.

Edo's character was shaped by politics. The shogun's retainers (including bannermen and housemen), the daimyō residences required by sankin-kōtai, and the servants and officials attached to them made up a very large warrior and dependent population, and samurai residences occupied a large proportion of the city's land area. Commoners (chōnin) were concentrated in densely settled districts, many living in rented tenement rows (nagaya). Because many residents were samurai on duty without families and many commoners were male migrants, Edo's population was often noted as skewed toward men, though the pattern changed over time. Edo drew labor from the surrounding countryside, and many residents were temporary or seasonal migrants.

The result was a city that consumed far more than it produced. Edo needed rice, vegetables, fish, firewood, charcoal, oil, textiles, sake, soy sauce, paper, and manufactured goods brought from the Kantō hinterland and from the Kinai region. Initially, high-quality goods came from the west, and Edo relied on Osaka and Kyoto for refined products, a pattern that began to change in the eighteenth century as the Kantō region developed its own production, such as soy sauce. Edo's merchants included wholesalers organized by commodity, retailers, peddlers, and a large service sector. The city was also a major employer of construction workers, carpenters, and other artisans. It was a consumption metropolis: its prosperity rested on concentrated spending by the political order and the people it brought together.

Historical Debate

Did Sankin-kōtai Burden the Economy or Integrate It?

The question: Sankin-kōtai required domains to spend heavily on Edo residences and travel. Did this institution mainly drain domain finances and impose a costly political obligation, or did it mainly stimulate transport, urban demand, and interregional exchange?

Interpretation A — A system of control and fiscal burden: Scholars influenced by Toshio Tsukahira's classic study of the system emphasize its character as a mechanism of feudal control, designed to weaken daimyō and keep them under shogunal supervision. On this reading, the heavy expenditures were a deliberate or at least welcome means of limiting daimyō resources, and they contributed to domain indebtedness, borrowing from merchants, and fiscal stress in the later Tokugawa period.
Interpretation B — An integrating force: Others, including historians of travel and urban development, stress that the system generated sustained traffic, demand for lodging and supplies, and links between regions. Constantine Vaporis, for example, has argued that the system promoted mobility, communication, and shared cultural experience, and the broader scholarship on commercial and urban growth (such as work by Nakai Nobuhiko and James McClain on early modern commercial change) places alternate attendance among the forces that concentrated consumers in Edo and expanded exchange.

Evidence: Both positions draw on domain budget records, which show large Edo-related expenditures; on post-station and traffic records; on merchant account books; and on estimates of Edo's population and consumption. They differ in emphasis: one focuses on what the system cost the domains, the other on what it generated for those who sold goods and services.

Why it matters: The two readings are not mutually exclusive, and most historians accept that both effects operated at once. What changes is the weight given to each, and the question affects how one understands domain indebtedness, merchant influence, and the sources of urban growth. It also guards against explaining commercial growth as the planned goal of Tokugawa policy.

Student task: Identify one body of evidence that would best support the "burden" interpretation and one that would best support the "integration" interpretation, and explain what additional evidence (for example, comparisons of domains near and far from Edo) might help assess their relative weight.

Section 45.10

Osaka: Warehouse City and Commercial Clearinghouse

Osaka's character was different from Edo's. It was not the seat of the shogunate, though it held a shogunal castle and garrison, and its prominence rested on its location at the head of the Inland Sea, at the mouth of the Yodo River, and at the junction of coastal routes. Its canals and rivers allowed goods to be carried by boat directly to warehouses and wholesalers. Osaka's population is commonly estimated at several hundred thousand in the eighteenth century, and its commoner character was strong: merchants and artisans formed a much larger proportion of its population than in Edo.

Its principal role was as the commercial center for the movement of goods to and from the western and central regions. Domains maintained storehouses and residences in the city, the kurayashiki, where tax rice and other products were received, stored, and sold. Wholesalers, brokers, and money changers handled the sale and redistribution of these goods, including shipments forward to Edo and Kyoto. Osaka was a clearinghouse in the sense that prices, credit, and goods converged there, and its markets set reference prices for important commodities. The popular label "the nation's kitchen" (tenka no daidokoro), often applied to Osaka, reflects this role in handling rice and foodstuffs, but the label is less important than the underlying explanation: Osaka concentrated the institutions that allowed regional goods to be converted into money and redistributed.

The Edo–Osaka contrast is useful but should not be overstated. Osaka also manufactured, Edo also had great merchants, and both cities depended on each other: Osaka's commodity and credit services supplied Edo's demand, while Edo's spending sustained Osaka's trade. The city's merchants also lent to domains, and many domains' financial relationships with Osaka houses became a structural feature of the economy, as later sections describe.

Historical Vocabulary
Kurayashiki (domain warehouse-residence)

A kurayashiki was a combined storehouse and administrative residence maintained by a domain (or sometimes the shogunate) in a commercial city, especially Osaka. Domain officials, often assisted by merchant agents, received tax rice and other products there, arranged their sale, and handled remittances to the domain and to Edo.

The term matters because it shows how the political economy of rice depended on commercial intermediaries. Domains had to convert in-kind revenue into money, and the kurayashiki was the institutional point at which that conversion took place. It also illustrates how domains, though politically autonomous, participated in a shared commercial system. Students should not confuse the kurayashiki with the Edo residences required by sankin-kōtai, which served different purposes.

Section 45.11

Kyoto: Imperial Capital, Craft Production, and Specialized Goods

Kyoto's importance was of a third kind. It remained the seat of the imperial court and of aristocratic households, and it housed the headquarters of numerous temples and shrines. These institutions generated sustained demand for ceremonial goods, textiles, religious objects, and refined products. The city's population, estimated at several hundred thousand in the early eighteenth century, was large though it grew less rapidly than that of Edo and, by some estimates, declined from its earlier peaks.

Kyoto's economic strength lay in specialized, high-skill manufacture. The Nishijin district was renowned for woven silks, and Kyoto produced dyed goods, lacquerware, ceramics, metalwork, paper products, and a wide range of luxury and artisanal articles. Its craftspeople maintained high standards and technical knowledge, and its merchants distributed products across the country. Kyoto was also a center of publishing and learning in the early part of the period. Many techniques that later spread to other regions were developed or refined in the city's workshops, and Edo's elite long relied on Kyoto's goods for their prestige.

This chapter treats Kyoto's economic role; it leaves the city's religious institutions for Chapter 47 and its role as a site of entertainment and cultural consumption for Chapter 46. The simplified formula that "Edo consumed, Osaka cleared and distributed, and Kyoto manufactured and specialized" is a useful memory aid, but each city did all three to some degree. Edo had large manufacturing trades, Osaka had major craft production, and Kyoto was a significant consumer. The formula describes tendencies, not exclusive functions.

Section 45.12

From Rice Stipends to Money: Why Merchants Became Indispensable

The conceptual heart of the chapter lies in a mismatch. The Tokugawa political and fiscal order was denominated in rice. Domain size was expressed in koku of assessed rice production, samurai stipends were set in rice, and taxes were collected largely in kind. But the economy in which samurai and daimyō lived, especially in cities, was monetary. Housing, clothing, servants, travel, ceremonies, and building all required money. A samurai could not pay a tailor in rice stipend certificates, and a domain could not pay for its Edo residence with a warehouse of grain.

A chain of intermediaries filled the gap. Domains sold part of their tax rice through brokers and wholesalers, usually in Osaka and Edo. Samurai who received rice stipends in the shogunal domain relied on stipend brokers (fudasashi), who handled the conversion of rice into money in exchange for fees, and who often lent money against future stipends. Money changers, lenders, and wholesalers extended credit. These services were provided by merchants, who remained commoners without warrior privileges and were sometimes disparaged in moral discourse, but whose operations were essential to the functioning of the warrior order.

This explains why a warrior-led order whose moral discourse often disparaged profit-making could simultaneously depend on merchants. It also explains the recurrent anxiety of officials about rice prices. Because the real income of samurai and domains depended on the exchange rate between rice and money, fluctuations in rice prices, or in prices of other goods relative to rice, affected the fiscal health of the warrior class. The eighteenth century saw repeated official attempts to manage rice prices, and the broader problem of indebted domains and samurai recurs in the status discussion of Section 45.19.

Section 45.13

Money, Exchange, Credit, and Bills

Tokugawa Japan did not have a single national currency in the modern sense. The shogunate issued gold coins (denominated in ryō, bu, and shu), silver currency that was traditionally weighed (in units such as monme and kan), and copper coin, notably the Kan'ei tsūhō of the 1630s, used for small transactions. In practice, a gold-based monetary sphere centered on Edo and the east coexisted with a silver-based sphere centered on Osaka and the west, a division often summarized as "gold in the east, silver in the west." Exchange rates among the three metals fluctuated and were negotiated in the market, and the shogunate periodically recoined money, altering metal content and thereby affecting values.

Domains also issued their own paper notes (hansatsu), redeemable within the domain's territory, a practice that began in the seventeenth century and spread widely. These notes were convenient but their reliability depended on the issuing domain's credibility, and overissue could cause problems. Merchants and money changers managed this complexity. Money changers (ryōgaeshō), including a group of leading Osaka exchange houses, converted among metals, assayed coins, took deposits, made loans, and issued and settled remittance instruments.

Bills of exchange (kawase) made long-distance trade possible without moving coin physically over every journey. A merchant in Edo could pay a supplier in Osaka through an exchange arrangement, with accounts settled between merchant houses and money changers, and with bills and credit instruments circulating within trusted networks. Such practices depended on bookkeeping, reputation, and shared commercial norms. The point for students is not the technical detail of each coin and rate but that Tokugawa merchants had developed a flexible system of payment, credit, and settlement. Its sophistication is evidence that commercial practice was highly developed, even though the system remained fragmented compared with a unified modern monetary regime.

Section 45.14

Osaka's Rice Markets and the Dōjima Exchange

The Dōjima rice market is the chapter's major case study in market institutions. Domains brought tax rice to their Osaka kurayashiki, where it was sold through brokers. Instead of always delivering physical rice, domains and warehouses issued rice bills (kome-gitte), certificates that represented claims on stored rice. Trading in these claims developed into active markets: spot trading in rice bills and, importantly, book-based transactions for future delivery (chōai-mai), in which parties agreed in advance on prices and settled differences at later dates without necessarily exchanging physical rice. Around 1697 the main rice trading moved to the Dōjima district, and in 1730 the shogunate formally authorized the Dōjima market, including both spot trading in rice bills and the book-based forward transactions, in the context of Yoshimune's efforts to address rice prices.

The institution had features that later observers have compared with organized futures exchanges: standardized contracts, regular trading, settlement mechanisms, and the use of claims on a commodity rather than the commodity itself. For this reason, Dōjima is frequently described as a forerunner of modern futures markets, and the Japan Exchange Group's own historical presentation takes this view. Historians should treat such claims carefully. The institutional similarities are real, but Dōjima operated within a particular Tokugawa framework: it was authorized and supervised by the shogunate, tied to the rice-denominated fiscal order, dominated by a limited set of licensed merchants and brokers, and embedded in networks of trust and regulation different from those of modern exchanges. To say that it was identical to a twenty-first-century exchange would be teleological; to say that it was primitive would ignore its demonstrated sophistication.

The market also served political ends. Because rice price levels determined the real income of samurai and domains, authorities were deeply interested in its operation. Shogunal policy under Yoshimune combined efforts to raise rice prices and to regulate merchants with the formal recognition of the market. The relationship between merchants who ran the market and officials who authorized and sometimes restricted it was thus one of mutual dependence and recurring tension.

Section 45.15

Market Information: Couriers, Prices, and Commercial Knowledge

Markets require information as well as roads. Merchants needed to know prices, supply conditions, harvest reports, and the reliability of counterparties, and the Tokugawa economy developed channels to supply that knowledge. Private couriers (hikyaku) carried letters, remittances, and price information between major cities, in addition to the official relay couriers who served the shogunate and domains. Express couriers could cover the Edo–Osaka route in considerably fewer days than ordinary travelers needed, and a regular service for commercial correspondence developed.

Dōjima illustrates how far this could go. Prices determined in the market were communicated to distant places by couriers and by signaling systems, including flag signals, according to descriptions of the market's operations. Merchants in other cities could thus learn Osaka rice prices with limited delay, and local rice and commodity prices were influenced by Osaka's. Merchant correspondence, account books, and reputational networks, in which a merchant's standing within the trading community determined access to credit, completed the system. Literacy and numeracy among merchants, apprentices, and shop managers were practical necessities, and the keeping of accounts was a routine skill. (The wider growth of publishing and education belongs to Chapter 50.)

This section shows that market integration was informational as well as physical. It also shows the limits of integration: information traveled at the pace of couriers and ships, and distant regions received it later and less reliably than the great cities. Price differentials between places persisted, partly because of transport costs and partly because of the information lag, and domains sometimes exploited or restricted information flows to their own advantage.

Section 45.16

Merchant Houses: Mitsui, Kōnoike, Sumitomo, and Commercial Organization

The great merchant houses are best understood as contrasting cases rather than a single heroic type. Mitsui is the clearest example of retail innovation and finance. The family, of provincial origin, established the Echigoya dry-goods shop in Edo in 1673; in its early development, especially by the shop's relocation in 1683, it became famous for cash sales at fixed prices rather than the credit and negotiated pricing common in the trade (sources differ on whether the policy dates from the founding or the relocation), and maintained a Kyoto base for procuring textiles. Over the following decades the house added money exchange and became involved in remitting funds for the shogunate and others. Kōnoike, with roots in sake brewing, developed into a major Osaka house in exchange and commercial credit, lending to domains and also investing in land reclamation. Sumitomo, with origins in Kyoto, became prominent in copper refining and mining, notably through the Besshi mine, developed from 1691, and also operated as a merchant and exchange house.

These houses shared organizational features. Governance rested on the family but typically delegated daily operations to salaried managers (bantō) who had risen through years of apprenticeship. Apprentices, often boys from other families, entered at a young age and advanced through a staged hierarchy. Houses developed branch structures, bookkeeping systems, and written house codes (kakun) that stressed frugality, prudence, and the preservation of the house across generations. Succession could be handled through adoption, so that capable outsiders could assume headship. Reputation, trust, and relationships with domains and officials were central assets, and the loss of official favor or a major borrower's default could endanger a house.

A crucial warning follows. These houses should not be called zaibatsu. The term refers to the family-controlled industrial-financial conglomerates of the Meiji period and after, with modern corporate structures, heavy industry, banking, and close state relationships. Some zaibatsu grew from older houses, and Chapter 56 treats that transformation, but Tokugawa merchant houses operated within a preindustrial, status-regulated commercial order, and their trajectories were not predetermined. Many Tokugawa houses failed, and the survivors adapted to circumstances their founders could not have foreseen.

Section 45.17

Merchant Associations, Kabunakama, Monopoly, and Regulation

Merchants and artisans organized into associations (nakama) to manage competition, maintain quality, allocate access to markets, settle disputes, and share risk. Authorities had reasons to recognize them: associations simplified tax collection, guaranteed suppliers for essential goods, and offered a means of supervising trade and prices. The medieval za had been largely dismantled by Sengoku and early unification rulers (Chapters 39 and 40), but the early Tokugawa period saw the return of wholesale groupings, and by the eighteenth century a more formal system of recognized associations had emerged.

Under Shogun Yoshimune, from the 1720s, authorities began to recognize associations, whose members held shares or licenses known as kabu, hence the name kabunakama. In return for official recognition, and often for payments to the authorities, associations obtained exclusive or privileged access to particular trades. Later governments, especially in the late eighteenth century, expanded the practice and sought revenue from it. The system was therefore neither a continuation of medieval guilds nor a uniform cartel: its character changed with policies, economic conditions, and the relationships between associations and officials.

The associations became controversial. Critics, including some officials, argued that they kept prices high, restricted competition, and strengthened the market power of established merchants. In the Tenpō era the shogunate ordered their dissolution in 1841 as part of its reforms, and they were later restored in 1851. That contest, together with the wider reform program, belongs to Chapter 50. For this chapter, the point is that commercial organization was a matter of negotiation between merchants and authorities, in which regulation and privilege were intertwined.

Historical Vocabulary
Kabunakama (licensed merchant associations)

Kabunakama were associations of merchants or artisans whose members held kabu, transferable shares or licenses, recognized by authorities as conferring the right to participate in a particular trade. The term is often rendered "guilds," but the comparison can mislead: they were not medieval za under another name, nor were they simple monopolies or free markets.

They matter because they illustrate how Tokugawa authorities managed commerce through recognition, privilege, and payment rather than through direct state operation. Their changing legal standing, from tolerated to recognized to dissolved and restored, tracks shifting official attitudes toward merchants and markets.

Section 45.18

Shops, Brands, Advertising, and the Consumer Society

The growth of consumer society is the chapter's most socially visible consequence. In the cities, shops lined major streets, and retailers developed practices to attract customers. Fixed prices and cash sales, as at Echigoya, reduced bargaining and credit risk. Shop signs, curtains (noren) bearing the shop's mark, wrapping papers, and branded reputations helped customers identify trusted sellers. Ready-made and cut cloth, secondhand clothing markets, household goods, medicines, food stalls and restaurants, and a wide range of ordinary goods were available, and urban households could buy items that rural households often made themselves. Peddlers carried goods into neighborhoods, and rental and repair services allowed households with modest means to participate in the market.

Food illustrates the change well. In Edo, takeaway food sellers and stalls offering noodles and other prepared foods served workers and single men who lacked kitchens, and restaurants developed for more prosperous customers. Foodstuffs such as soy sauce, sake, and refined oil were brand-differentiated products produced in specific regions and shipped to cities. The city also saw a growing trade in fuel, water carriers, and household supplies, while neighborhood public baths became part of urban routine.

The key change should not be exaggerated. Not everyone suddenly became affluent. Many urban residents, especially laborers and day workers, lived near subsistence and experienced price rises as hardship. Rural households participated unevenly, and the poorest participated least. The significant development is that a widening range of households, urban and in some areas rural, took part in markets for goods beyond bare subsistence. That participation shaped taste, fashion, and expectations, and it sustained the commercial culture that Chapter 46 treats: theater, prints, pleasure districts, and fashion depended on the same cities, disposable incomes, and dense populations, but they are left for that chapter.

Everyday Life and Environment

Work and Living in the Commercial City

For many people, the commercial economy meant work organized around houses and shops. A shop in an urban district typically housed the proprietor's family, apprentices, and servants in a single establishment. Apprentices began in childhood, performing chores before learning the trade, and servants, many of them young women from rural families, handled cooking, cleaning, and errands. Wages were often paid partly in kind and at the end of fixed terms, and conditions varied from a secure place in a prosperous house to harsh and precarious service. Women also worked as peddlers, seamstresses, innkeepers' staff, and in family businesses, though many of their contributions appear only indirectly in records.

Urban housing was crowded. Most commoners lived in rented tenement rows (nagaya) with shared wells and privies, where neighbors knew each other's business and landlords' agents managed rules and rents. Fire was a constant danger in wooden cities, and fires repeatedly devastated Edo. Day laborers, porters, carpenters, and water carriers earned wages that rose and fell with harvests and prices. In bad harvest years, rising rice prices could produce hunger and unrest, a link that connects urban commerce to the rural crises of Chapter 44. The commercial city offered opportunity, but it also exposed residents to market risk.

Section 45.19

Wealth without Rank: Merchants, Samurai Debt, and the Status Paradox

Chapter 43 showed that status in Tokugawa Japan was an official ranking of social roles, with warriors above commoners. This chapter shows how that ranking diverged from economic reality. Merchants remained commoners, subject to dress and conduct regulations and without warrior privileges, and were sometimes disparaged in moral discourse; but commoners did not form a uniform nationwide legal ladder with merchants legally at the bottom. Yet some commanded great capital and extended credit to samurai and domains. A wealthy merchant could have more liquid wealth than a poorly paid samurai while lacking his formal privileges. The gap between status, income, wealth, and political authority is one of the central contradictions of mature Tokugawa society.

The authorities were conscious of it. In 1705 the shogunate confiscated the enormous property of the Osaka rice-merchant house of Yodoya and expelled its head. Contemporary and later explanations emphasized excessive luxury, while the precise circumstances of the punishment remain disputed; later traditions also associated the house with extensive lending to daimyō. Whatever its exact cause, the case illustrates official anxiety about conspicuous merchant wealth. Sumptuary regulations repeatedly attempted to restrain display by commoners, with limited success. Domain indebtedness to merchant lenders grew in many regions, and some domains resorted to forced reductions of debt, repayment postponements, or promotion of local products and monopolies. Samurai households, especially lower-ranking ones with fixed stipends, struggled when prices rose, and some turned to side occupations, adoption of wealthy commoners' sons, or other arrangements. Marriage and adoption sometimes crossed status lines in ways that formal ideology did not anticipate.

It would be wrong, however, to describe merchants as a new ruling class. They had no formal political power, remained subject to official regulation, and depended on the favor of authorities, as the case of Yodoya shows. Many merchant houses failed because of default by debtors or official action. What changed was not the formal hierarchy but the relationship between it and economic life: a society organized around status found itself relying on commoners who lacked warrior privileges yet held growing economic power. That tension, rather than the overthrow of the status order, defines the late Tokugawa period.

Multiple Perspectives

One Commercial Network, Many Experiences

A daimyō retainer traveling to Edo: For a samurai in a domain procession, the journey meant obeying fixed schedules, managing a restricted travel allowance, and representing the domain's rank. A retainer posted in Edo might spend years away from home in a residence with limited funds, facing prices higher than in the domain, and depending on domain remittances that themselves depended on merchants in Osaka.

An Osaka rice broker or money changer: The broker's income came from fees and transactions, and success depended on information, trust, and relationships with domains. He was wealthy and indispensable yet remained a commoner, subject to official oversight and sudden disfavor. A domain's financial crisis could become his own.
An Edo shopkeeper and his household: The shopkeeper benefited from concentrated consumer demand, and innovations such as fixed-price sales could widen his clientele. His business depended on wholesalers' supply, price stability, and the avoidance of disaster, notably fire.
A young woman servant in a merchant house or inn: Her work was essential but largely unrecorded: long hours, fixed contract terms, limited autonomy, and dependence on the household's fortunes. Her experience, visible mainly through regulations, contracts, and occasional diaries, reminds us that the commercial economy rested on low-status labor.

What Changed

  • Large cities and a dense network of castle towns replaced a more dispersed pattern of settlement and markets
  • Highways, post stations, and coastal shipping circuits connected regions far more regularly
  • Wholesale houses, money changers, bills of exchange, and rice markets provided institutions for credit and exchange
  • Consumer markets for retail goods, food, and services expanded in cities

What Stayed the Same

  • Rice remained the basis of assessment and samurai finance
  • Transport remained preindustrial, dependent on human, animal, and wind power
  • Political jurisdictions remained fragmented among the shogunate and domains
  • The formal status order persisted despite its strains

Who experienced change most directly? Urban residents, merchant households, post-station communities, and domains tied to the Osaka and Edo markets felt change most strongly. Who experienced greater continuity? Many upland and remote villages, northern frontier communities, and subsistence households experienced commerce only at the margins. Why the pattern matters: It shows that commercialization was uneven, and it cautions against treating the Tokugawa economy as uniformly transformed.

Cause and Effect

The Expansion of Interregional Markets and Urban Consumption

Long-term causes

Sengoku-era territorial states and castle towns; the separation of warriors from the land; agricultural expansion and population growth; regional specialization of production; and the geographic advantages of coastal and river transport.

Short-term causes

Political consolidation after 1600; castle-town building; the regularization of sankin-kōtai; investment in highways and post stations; domain needs to convert rice into cash; and merchant initiatives in shipping, exchange, and retail.

Event or transformation

The emergence, mainly across the seventeenth and early eighteenth centuries, of a commercially connected economy centered on Edo, Osaka, and Kyoto, with castle towns and regional ports as nodes.

Immediate effects

Growth of large cities, shipping circuits, wholesale and credit institutions, the Dōjima market, merchant houses, and urban consumer markets.

Long-term consequences

Widening gaps between status and wealth; domain indebtedness; official efforts at regulation and reform; and a commercial urban culture whose entertainments are the subject of Chapter 46. The commercial and financial experience also shaped the context of nineteenth-century change, without making it inevitable.

Historical complexity

No single cause suffices. Sankin-kōtai accelerated integration, but agricultural surplus, population growth, transport, regional specialization, and merchant innovation were independent causes, and many developments began before the institution was formalized.

Historical complexity: The causal pattern is closer to a combination of political consolidation, urban concentration, alternate attendance, agricultural surplus, transport investment, and merchant institutions than to a single line from political control to economic growth.

Section 45.20

An Integrated Economy, but Not a Modern One

The chapter's evidence supports a balanced judgment. Tokugawa Japan possessed dense transport networks, very large cities, specialized production, sophisticated credit and exchange, merchant organizations, commodity markets, and an expanding consumer economy. It was not economically stagnant, and the older image of a static rice-based feudal economy cannot be sustained. The research summarized in works such as the Cambridge History of Japan volume on the early modern period and in studies of travel, urban life, and finance has shown commercial vitality across the period.

Yet the limits are equally clear. Integration was regionally uneven: the Kinai and Kantō cores were far more commercialized than many northern and upland areas. Transport remained preindustrial, with limited speed and capacity. Harvests still mattered enormously, and poor harvests brought hunger and price spikes. Political jurisdictions fragmented regulation, and domains pursued their own fiscal and commercial policies. Status distinctions persisted. Commercial sophistication, in short, should not be treated as proof that industrial capitalism was inevitably approaching. The institutions of Tokugawa commerce, including those of Dōjima and the merchant houses, operated in a context that differed fundamentally from that of nineteenth- and twentieth-century industrial economies, and later developments (Chapter 56) involved new technologies, new state policies, and external pressures, not simply the continuation of Tokugawa trends.

The chapter also leaves a consequence for Chapter 46. The same cities, merchants, disposable income, shops, and dense populations that sustained commercial consumption also created paying audiences for kabuki, bunraku, ukiyo-e, the pleasure districts, geisha, and the wider floating world. The commercial city was not only a place of exchange but a place where leisure, fashion, and entertainment became markets in their own right, and that culture is the subject of the next chapter.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how Tokugawa Japan moved from production to circulation. Chapter 44 explained how villages produced and bore extraction; Chapter 45 has traced how the resulting goods, together with people, money, credit, and information, moved through roads, ships, castle towns, and the great cities. Its central argument is that Tokugawa political control helped create economic integration, but that integration also depended on agricultural surplus, population growth, transport, regional specialization, and merchant innovation.

Sankin-kōtai was a political institution designed to control daimyō, and it had major but partly unintended economic consequences. It required domains to maintain Edo residences and move large retinues, which generated demand for transport, lodging, supplies, and finance. Historians continue to debate whether it should be seen mainly as a fiscal burden or as an integrating force; the evidence supports the view that it was both. Daimyō processions were displays of rank but also moving concentrations of purchasing power, and the road system they used was a trunk network, the Gokaidō, administered from Edo.

Roads and post stations supported local commercial economies and imposed obligations on the communities that supplied labor and animals. Barriers and travel regulations reflected state control, but the society they governed was increasingly mobile, as pilgrimage and travel by commoners attest. Roads were not enough, however: bulk commodities moved largely by coastal shipping and inland waterways, which tied the Japan Sea coast, Inland Sea, Osaka, and Edo together. The commercial map was therefore not identical to the highway map.

Castle towns turned samurai government into urban markets by concentrating stipend-receiving warriors who needed goods and services. Edo grew into an enormous consumption metropolis sustained by political concentration; Osaka became the warehouse city and clearinghouse, where domain kurayashiki, wholesalers, brokers, and money changers converted rice and commodities into money; and Kyoto remained a center of crafts and specialized goods. The formula that Edo consumed, Osaka cleared and distributed, and Kyoto manufactured is useful only if qualified, since each city performed all three functions to some degree.

The shift from rice stipends to money explains why merchants became indispensable. A fiscal order denominated in rice could not function in a monetary society without intermediaries. Tokugawa money was plural, with gold, silver, copper, and domain paper, and merchants developed money exchange, credit, and bills to manage it. The Dōjima rice market, authorized in 1730, illustrates the sophistication of the system, with trading in rice bills and book-based forward transactions, although historians should avoid claims that it was identical to a modern exchange.

Information completed the system. Couriers and signaling carried price information, and merchant correspondence and reputation underpinned credit. Merchant houses such as Mitsui, Kōnoike, and Sumitomo illustrate different paths of retail, finance, and mining, with family governance, branch structures, apprenticeship, and house codes. They should not be called zaibatsu. Merchant associations (kabunakama) show the negotiated relationship between merchants and authorities, in which regulation and privilege were intertwined.

Consumer society expanded the range of households that participated in markets beyond bare subsistence, through shops, branded goods, fixed-price sales, ready-made goods, food services, and secondhand markets. The change was real but uneven: many urban laborers and rural households remained near subsistence, and the poorest participated least. Work in the commercial city rested on apprentices, servants, porters, and laborers, whose experience appears mainly in indirect sources.

The commercial economy widened the gap between formal status and economic power. Merchants remained commoners, yet commanded capital and extended credit to samurai and domains. Officials responded with sumptuary regulation, selective punishment, and reform, but the status order persisted, and merchants did not become a ruling class. This tension between a status ideology and a commercial reality is one of the central contradictions of mature Tokugawa society.

Finally, the Tokugawa economy was integrated but not modern. It was dynamic and sophisticated, yet regionally uneven, preindustrial in transport, vulnerable to harvests, and politically fragmented. Its commercial achievements should not be read as a straight path to industrial capitalism. They did, however, create the cities, wealth, and consumers on which the commercial culture of the floating world, the subject of Chapter 46, was built.

Key Terms

Sankin-kōtai
The system of alternate attendance requiring daimyō to divide time between their domains and Edo, with their principal wives and close family normally residing in Edo; a political control with major economic effects.
Gokaidō
The five principal highways administered by the shogunate, measured from Nihonbashi: Tōkaidō, Nakasendō, Kōshū Kaidō, Nikkō Kaidō, and Ōshū Kaidō.
Tōkaidō
The coastal highway between Edo and Kyoto with fifty-three post stations, the best-known route of the Tokugawa period.
Shukuba
A post station providing transport relays, lodging, and services, and a commercial community burdened with official duties.
Honjin / waki-honjin
Official inns for daimyō and high-ranking travelers, and secondary inns that served the same purpose when the main one was full.
Sukegō
The obligation of villages near a post station to supply extra porters and horses during periods of heavy traffic.
Sekisho
A barrier on a highway where officials inspected travelers and documents; enforcement varied by place and period.
Okage-mairi
Mass pilgrimage waves to the Ise shrines, of which the 1830 wave involved several million people by common estimates.
Kitamae-bune
Cargo vessels of the coastal circuit from the Japan Sea coast to Osaka, carrying rice and regional products.
Jōkamachi
A castle town: administrative center of a domain and a consumption and distribution center for warriors and commoners.
Chōnin
Townspeople, mainly merchants and artisans, ranked as commoners in the status order.
Nagaya
Rented tenement rows in which most urban commoners lived.
Kurayashiki
A domain warehouse-residence in a commercial city where tax rice and other goods were received, stored, and sold.
Tonya
A wholesale house that handled the consignment, storage, and sale of goods between producers and retailers.
Fudasashi
Stipend brokers who converted samurai rice stipends into money and frequently lent against future stipends.
Koku
A unit of rice volume used to express domain assessments and samurai stipends, the basis of the rice-denominated fiscal order.
Ryōgaeshō
Money changers who converted among metals, assayed coin, took deposits, made loans, and settled remittances.
Kawase
Bills of exchange that allowed payment over distance without moving coin physically.
Hansatsu
Paper notes issued by domains, valid within their territory and dependent on the issuer's credibility.
Dōjima rice market
Osaka's rice exchange, consolidated around 1697 and authorized by the shogunate in 1730, notable for trading in rice bills and book-based forward transactions.
Kome-gitte
Rice bills: certificates representing claims on stored rice, traded in the Dōjima market.
Chōai-mai
Book-based transactions for future delivery of rice, settled without necessarily exchanging physical rice.
Hikyaku
Couriers who carried letters, remittances, and information, including private commercial couriers.
Kabunakama
Merchant or artisan associations whose members held recognized shares or licenses in a trade.
Bantō
Salaried managers who ran daily operations of merchant houses after rising through apprenticeship.
Kakun
House codes setting out the principles of conduct, frugality, and succession for a merchant family.
Zaibatsu
Family-controlled industrial and financial conglomerates of the Meiji period and after; not applicable to Tokugawa merchant houses (see Chapter 56).
Sumptuary regulation
Rules restricting clothing, housing, and display according to status, repeatedly issued and imperfectly enforced.
Commercialization
The expansion of market exchange, specialization, and monetary transactions in an economy, which in Tokugawa Japan did not amount to industrialization.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a political order and a commercial order grew into one another. The Tokugawa settlement was built to keep daimyō in check, to stabilize warrior society, and to maintain a rice-based fiscal system. In doing so it concentrated consumers in castle towns and Edo, kept enormous numbers of people on the road, and created demand that merchants answered with shipping lines, credit instruments, wholesale networks, and markets. The commercial world was therefore neither an alien intrusion into a feudal order nor a planned creation of that order; it was a product of their interaction.

The chapter also reveals a larger process: the divergence between formal status and economic reality. Chapter 43 described a warrior-ruled order in which merchants were commoners without warrior privileges, Chapter 44 showed villages drawn into commodity production, and this chapter has shown merchant houses, brokers, and money changers handling the finances of domains and samurai. The strains that resulted, including domain indebtedness, official anxiety about luxury, and recurrent reform efforts, shaped the late Tokugawa period and will return in Chapter 50. They did not overthrow the status order on their own, but they altered the conditions under which it survived.

The chapter connects regions and neighbors as well. Coastal shipping linked the Japan Sea coast, the Inland Sea, Osaka, and Edo, and carried goods from the northern frontier into the national market, which will matter again when Chapter 48 examines Ezo, the Ainu, Ryukyu, and the controlled external trade. Castle towns across Tōhoku, Chūgoku, Shikoku, and Kyushu showed regional variation that no single capital could represent. Later developments, from the merchant houses that adapted to Meiji conditions (Chapter 56) to the urban culture of the floating world (Chapter 46), drew on the networks, institutions, and populations examined here. The lasting lesson is that Tokugawa Japan became commercially connected without becoming politically or socially uniform, and that this combination, rather than any simple narrative of closure or modernization, is what students should carry forward.
