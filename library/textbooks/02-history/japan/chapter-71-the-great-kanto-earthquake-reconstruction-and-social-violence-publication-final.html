The Great Kantō Earthquake, Reconstruction, and Social Violence

Japanese History: From Jōmon Japan to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

On Saturday, September 1, 1923, at 11:58 in the morning, the ground beneath southern Kantō began to move. Many households in Tokyo and Yokohama were preparing the midday meal. Within hours the shaking had been followed by fires that would destroy much of the old commercial and working-class core of Tokyo and the central districts of Yokohama; within days, a different kind of catastrophe, a wave of killings driven by false rumor, had spread across the same region.

The earthquake was several disasters at once. It was a seismic event of the first order, produced by movement along the Sagami Trough south of the Kantō plain. It was an urban fire catastrophe in which, by most reconstructions, the great majority of deaths were caused not by collapsing buildings but by flames, heat, and smoke. It was a humanitarian emergency that interrupted transport, communication, water supply, and food distribution across a region containing millions of people. It was a crisis of government, striking in the very days when Japan had no fully installed prime minister. It was an occasion of mass racial and political violence in which neighborhood vigilante groups, police officers, and soldiers killed Koreans, Chinese, labor activists, anarchists, and some Japanese residents mistaken for outsiders. And it was a turning point in the history of urban planning, engineering, and housing, because the destroyed capital became the object of one of the most ambitious, and most contested, reconstruction programs of the twentieth century.

This chapter keeps those dimensions distinct. It begins with the cities before the disaster, because the physical fabric of early twentieth-century Tokyo and Yokohama, with their dense wooden neighborhoods, reclaimed land, crowded waterfronts, and limited firebreaks, shaped who survived. It then reconstructs the earthquake and fires district by district, including the catastrophe at the former army clothing depot in Honjo, the destruction of Yokohama, the tsunami along Sagami Bay, and the landslides in the mountains of Kanagawa. It examines emergency government, the imposition of emergency military authority, and the collapse of ordinary channels of information. It then devotes nine sections to the massacres of Koreans and Chinese, the Kameido and Amakasu killings, and the problem of accountability, because those events constitute a major historical subject in their own right and cannot be reduced to a paragraph about post-disaster disorder.

The chapter then returns to ordinary survivors: refugees in parks and temporary shelters, the struggle for food and clean water, the experiences of women and children, and the economic damage of lost workplaces and records. Finally it follows reconstruction, from the September 12 rescript affirming Tokyo's place as capital, through Gotō Shinpei's ambitious plans and their reduction by the Diet and the cabinet, to land readjustment, new bridges and parks, the 1924 building regulations, the Dōjunkai apartments, the rebuilding of Yokohama, and the contested commemoration of 1923. The central argument is that natural hazards do not determine social outcomes. Building design, urban density, emergency institutions, colonial prejudice, government decisions, and unequal access to relief shaped both the scale of the disaster and the shape of recovery.

The evidence is unusually rich and unusually uneven. Historians draw on government records, including cabinet decisions, military and police documents, and the Metropolitan Police Department's 1925 compilation Taishō daishinsaishi; on diaries and memoirs; on newspapers (many of them printed under improvised conditions); on photographs and maps; on petitions and diplomatic correspondence from Chinese and Korean sources; on Korean registers of victims; on court records; and on modern scientific reconstructions by seismologists. These sources do not speak with one voice. Official counts, press accounts, victims' organizations, and later scholarship offer different numbers and different explanations, and this chapter identifies the provenance of each figure it uses rather than blending incompatible counts into a single total.

The baseline figures are these. The Japan Meteorological Agency gives the earthquake's magnitude as approximately 7.9, with its hypocenter in western Kanagawa Prefecture at a depth of about 23 kilometers, and modern official estimates put the number of dead and missing at approximately 105,000. Older sources report different totals because of different counting methods, and Section 71.11 explains why. Estimates of the number of Korean victims of the massacres vary widely; no universally accepted definitive count exists, and Sections 71.20 and 71.25 compare the principal estimates and their origins.

The chapter builds on earlier material and looks ahead. Chapter 62 explained colonial rule in Korea and the background of anti-Korean discrimination; Chapter 67 covered the constitutional framework and the cabinets of the early 1920s; Chapter 69 described the labor, socialist, and anarchist movements whose members were targeted in 1923; and Chapter 70 described the metropolitan consumer culture whose physical setting the earthquake transformed. Chapter 72 returns to cultural experimentation, Chapter 73 to financial and rural crisis, and Chapter 74 to the collapse of parliamentary restraint. Students should treat 1923 neither as an isolated natural accident nor as a simple prelude to militarism: it was a moment in which many futures remained open, and in which choices made by officials, neighbors, and reformers mattered.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain the tectonic setting of the 1923 earthquake and distinguish what modern seismology has reconstructed from what scientists knew in 1923.
  2. Analyze how urban density, building materials, ground conditions, and weather turned an earthquake into a firestorm.
  3. Compare the experiences of Tokyo, Yokohama, the Sagami Bay coast, and mountainous western Kanagawa, and explain why a Tokyo-centered narrative is insufficient.
  4. Evaluate casualty and damage estimates by identifying their provenance, categories, and methods.
  5. Explain the political situation of September 1923, the formation of the second Yamamoto cabinet, and the application of emergency martial-law provisions.
  6. Trace how false rumors, official communications, and vigilante organization combined to produce massacres of Koreans and Chinese, distinguishing rumor from fact.
  7. Distinguish the Kameido and Amakasu incidents in terms of victims, perpetrators, institutions, and consequences.
  8. Assess the limits of investigation and prosecution, and explain why convictions cannot serve as a count of victims.
  9. Describe the humanitarian emergency, including displacement, food, water, public health, and the experiences of women and children.
  10. Compare Gotō Shinpei's proposed reconstruction program with the plans actually funded, and evaluate the reconstruction's engineering and housing outcomes.
  11. Interpret competing historical narratives of 1923, including national resilience, state-building, racial massacre, and contested memory.
  12. Evaluate primary sources for creator, purpose, and limits, and recognize whose perspectives are missing.

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  2. How did the geography of damage differ among Tokyo's eastern lowlands, Yokohama, the Sagami Bay coast, and the mountains of western Kanagawa?
  3. What did the breakdown of communication and the timing of the cabinet transition mean for the government's ability to respond?
  4. How did colonial rule, the memory of the March First Movement, and wartime stereotypes make rumors about Korean arson and poisoning credible to some residents?

Historical Context

By 1923 Tokyo was the political, financial, and cultural center of an industrial and imperial state, and one of the largest cities in the world. Its population had grown rapidly through the Meiji and Taishō decades as rural migrants and returning wartime workers filled its factories, shops, and tenements, and the surrounding region was developing as a metropolitan zone in which Yokohama served as the principal international port and a center of the silk trade. Chapter 70 described the department stores, cafés, cinemas, and railways that gave this metropolis its distinctive modern character. This chapter adds the other side of that modernity: a built environment of crowded wooden houses, narrow lanes, and limited open space in which fire risk was chronic.

The political setting was equally important. The cabinets of the early 1920s were short-lived, as Chapter 67 explained, and the death of Prime Minister Katō Tomosaburō on August 24, 1923, left the government under an acting premier at the moment of disaster. Imperial Japan had also become, since 1910, a colonial power in Korea, and tens of thousands of Koreans lived in Japan as workers and students, often in precarious conditions and subject to discrimination (Chapter 62). The movement for Korean independence, which had erupted in the March First Movement of 1919, remained a source of anxiety for the police, and Japanese authorities had developed surveillance practices directed at Koreans in the metropole. Chinese migrant laborers, including many from Zhejiang, also worked in Tokyo's eastern districts.

The domestic political left, whose history Chapter 69 traced, was under constant police observation. Unions, socialist groups, and anarchists existed under the legal framework of the Public Peace Police Law, and the memory of the 1910–1911 High Treason Incident and of the 1918 Rice Riots shaped official attitudes. Military institutions, including the Imperial Army and the military police (kenpeitai), enjoyed substantial autonomy under the Meiji constitutional order. These facts do not explain the violence of September 1923, but they help explain why violence found institutional and social channels.

Finally, the scientific and technical setting mattered. Japanese seismology was among the most advanced in the world, and Tokyo Imperial University scholars had studied the region's earthquake history. In 1905 Imamura Akitsune had published warnings that a major earthquake and fire could devastate Tokyo, a claim that provoked controversy among scientists and public alarm; Ōmori Fusakichi, the senior figure of the field, argued against alarmism. Building codes existed under the Urban Buildings Law of 1919 but did not yet include a quantitative seismic standard. The disaster would transform these arrangements.

Section 71.1 · Division I — The Earthquake: Seismic Origins, Urban Vulnerability, and the First Hours

Tokyo, Yokohama, and Southern Kantō before the Disaster

Tokyo in 1923 was a city of enormous contrasts. Its central districts included the offices of the national government, the Marunouchi business quarter near Tokyo Station, large banks, department stores, and newspaper offices, many of them built of brick, stone, or the new reinforced concrete. But the great majority of the city's residents lived in neighborhoods of closely packed wooden houses with tiled or wooden roofs, separated by lanes too narrow for fire engines and built along canals and the Sumida River. The eastern lowlands, including Honjo and Fukagawa, were home to small factories, warehouses, timber yards, and the tenements of industrial workers, and much of this land had been built up on soft alluvial deposits and reclaimed ground.

The city's growth had outrun its infrastructure. Population had risen rapidly since the Meiji period, and by the early 1920s the city proper held more than two million people, with additional suburban growth beyond the city boundary. The Tokyo municipal government, under Mayor Gotō Shinpei from 1920 to 1923, had drawn up ambitious proposals for road widening and urban improvement, but most of these remained unfunded. Open space was limited: parks were few, and the large temple and shrine precincts, school grounds, and former military or government sites that served as informal refuges would prove decisive in September 1923. The water system, which depended on mains vulnerable to ground movement, offered limited protection against fires that spread across wooden districts.

Yokohama presented a different urban form. Opened to foreign trade after 1859, it had grown into the leading international port of Japan and the principal exporter of raw silk. Its center was built around the foreign settlement and its waterfront, the Bund and Yamate bluff, the commercial district of Benten and Motomachi, and Chinatown, whose merchants and laborers formed a distinct community. Warehouses, customs houses, trading company offices, hotels, and bank buildings stood on filled land along the harbor. Yokohama's population was a fraction of Tokyo's, but its economy depended on a compact waterfront and a single bay, and its dependence on silk and trade made it highly exposed to physical disruption.

Beyond the two principal cities, the southern Kantō region included the Miura and Bōsō peninsulas, the shores of Sagami Bay, the hot-spring and castle towns of Odawara and Hakone, and the railway lines that connected them. Fishing villages lined the coast; mountainous terrain in western Kanagawa carried railways and roads on steep slopes. Chapter 1 described the volcanic and seismic character of the archipelago; the Kantō plain, with its thick sediments, is one of the most densely settled sedimentary basins in the world.

Historians reading this setting should avoid two simplifications. One treats the earthquake as an inevitable consequence of living in a hazardous country. The other treats Tokyo's wooden neighborhoods as a purely 'traditional' vulnerability that modernization would cure. In fact many of the risks of 1923 were produced by modern urban growth: concentrated population, industrial storage of flammable materials, the use of gas and charcoal for cooking in crowded tenements, and the filling of low ground. Modernization created both the opportunities and the vulnerabilities that the disaster exposed.

Section 71.2 · Division I — The Earthquake: Seismic Origins, Urban Vulnerability, and the First Hours

The Sagami Trough, Plate Boundaries, and the Seismic Setting

The 1923 earthquake belongs to a family of great earthquakes generated along the Sagami Trough, the plate boundary where the Philippine Sea Plate moves northwestward beneath the plate on which the Kantō region sits. Modern seismology, drawing on instrumental records from 1923, geodetic surveys of land uplift and subsidence, and geological and historical evidence of earlier events, treats the rupture as a large thrust event on this boundary. The Japan Meteorological Agency places the hypocenter in western Kanagawa at a depth of about 23 kilometers and gives the magnitude as approximately 7.9. The Cabinet Office's disaster-history reports place the event in the same trench-type setting and document its secondary effects, including fires, landslides, and tsunamis.

Several features of the rupture matter for the human history. First, the fault slipped beneath densely populated land and bay, so that strong shaking affected a wide region from the Izu and Miura peninsulas through Yokohama and Tokyo to the Bōsō Peninsula. Second, the movement permanently raised and lowered the ground: stretches of coastline on the Miura and Bōsō peninsulas rose by well over a meter, leaving former sea bottom exposed, while other places subsided. Third, the shaking lasted long enough, and the accompanying aftershocks were frequent enough, that the first phase of fires, rescue, and evacuation took place under repeated shocks. Fourth, the region's geology amplified shaking on soft ground, so that damage was uneven within short distances.

The region had experienced comparable earthquakes before. The Genroku earthquake of 1703 is generally regarded as a similar Sagami Trough event, and geological studies suggest that the largest events recur at intervals of the order of hundreds of years, while smaller damaging earthquakes occur more frequently. Historians should treat recurrence estimates as approximate scientific inferences rather than predictions. In 1923, the available science was substantial but incomplete. Seismographs at Tokyo Imperial University recorded the shocks, and Japanese scholars had developed methods for observing and classifying earthquake intensity, but the plate-tectonic framework did not yet exist. Explanations then emphasized fault movement, crustal deformation, and earlier earthquake catalogs.

The 1905 dispute between Imamura Akitsune and Ōmori Fusakichi illustrates the scientific climate. Imamura argued, on the basis of historical records and the geology of the region, that a large earthquake could strike Tokyo within decades and produce destructive fires; the claim was widely discussed and criticized as alarmist, and Ōmori, who was recognized as the leading figure in Japanese seismology, rejected it. Ōmori happened to be abroad in Australia when the 1923 earthquake struck, and he returned to Japan before dying later that year. Students should be cautious with the popular version of this story, in which Imamura was simply right and Ōmori simply wrong: Imamura's warning concerned the plausibility of a major earthquake, not the prediction of a specific date, and the disagreement was part of a continuing scientific debate about hazard and public communication.

After 1923 the scientific response was institutional. The Earthquake Research Institute was established at Tokyo Imperial University in the mid-1920s, and surveys of structural damage fed into engineering debates examined in Section 71.35. The disaster thus joined the history of seismology to the history of urban policy, although the new scientific resources could not undo the human decisions of the first week of September.

Historical Vocabulary

Historical Vocabulary: Sagami Trough, Fire Whirl, Jikeidan, Martial Law, Land Readjustment, Reconstruction Agency

Sagami Trough is the submarine plate-boundary trough south of the Kantō plain where the Philippine Sea Plate descends beneath the plate on which central Honshu sits; it is the source zone of the 1703 and 1923 earthquakes. Fire whirl (tatsumaki, sometimes rendered 'firestorm vortex') is a rotating column of fire and superheated air produced when large fires and strong winds interact; it can lift burning material and carry flames across open ground. Jikeidan were neighborhood self-defense groups, in 1923 formed spontaneously or under police encouragement to guard against fire, crime, and rumored attackers.

Martial law (kaigenrei) in 1923 referred to the transfer of administrative and judicial powers to military commanders under the 1882 imperial ordinance, applied in part, by Imperial Ordinance No. 398, to a designated area in September 1923 (JACAR calls this administrative martial law) rather than through a wartime declaration. Land readjustment (kukaku seiri) was a planning method by which landowners contributed a share of their land to create roads and open space while plots were redrawn, an alternative to wholesale government purchase. The Imperial Capital Reconstruction Agency (Teito Fukkōin) was the cabinet-level body created on September 27, 1923, to plan and direct reconstruction; it was reduced to a bureau within months.

These terms matter because modern English words such as 'martial law' or 'vigilante' can mislead. The legal basis of 1923 emergency powers was contested and ambiguous, and the jikeidan were not simply private mobs: they existed in a space between neighborhood association and police auxiliary, which helps explain how they acted.

Section 71.3 · Division I — The Earthquake: Seismic Origins, Urban Vulnerability, and the First Hours

September 1, 1923, at 11:58 a.m.: The Mainshock and Its Immediate Effects

The first shock struck at 11:58 a.m. on September 1, 1923. Contemporary accounts describe a first, strong vertical jolt followed by violent horizontal motion that made standing impossible in many places, with roof tiles, chimneys, and walls falling into the streets. The main shaking lasted for a period that witnesses and instruments describe in terms of minutes rather than seconds, and was followed almost immediately by aftershocks, some of them strong enough to cause additional collapses. Dust rose over the city and sounds of breaking timber and glass were widely reported.

Everyday life stopped in the middle of its Saturday routine. The autumn school term was due to begin, and many schools had held opening ceremonies that morning; offices and shops were open or closing for the half-day; and thousands of households were lighting cooking fires. The timing, near midday, would prove decisive. People ran into the streets, which quickly filled with falling tiles, broken wires, and crowds. Tram service and trains stopped, the water supply failed in many districts, and telegraph and telephone lines went down. Streets cracked and bridges sagged, and in low-lying districts the ground opened or subsided.

Reconstructing the moment requires attention to the difference between contemporary perception and later technical analysis. Eyewitnesses, including officials and ordinary residents who left diaries and memoirs, recorded what they saw and heard, and these accounts convey how people understood the event: as a sudden attack of nature, as the end of the world, or as a familiar earthquake that was far worse than anything before. Later seismological reconstruction supplies magnitude, depth, and fault geometry that no one could know on the day. The two kinds of evidence answer different questions, and neither replaces the other.

The immediate effects were differentiated by place. In Yokohama and in parts of western Kanagawa, shaking itself caused massive building collapse and killed many people before fires began. In Tokyo, much of the older housing stock suffered heavy damage but many wooden houses did not entirely collapse, and the deadliest effects arose from the fires examined in Section 71.5. On the coast, the sea receded and returned as tsunami; in the mountains, slopes gave way. The first hours were therefore not a single experience but a set of regional emergencies unfolding simultaneously and, because communications failed, largely without knowledge of one another.

For students, this section should also model a research habit: always ask who recorded an event, when, and with what means of observation. The best-known sources for the first hours were written by people who could observe only the few blocks around them.

Section 71.4 · Division I — The Earthquake: Seismic Origins, Urban Vulnerability, and the First Hours

Buildings, Ground Conditions, and Unequal Physical Damage

Damage in 1923 was not uniform, and the pattern challenges two common assumptions: that Western-style buildings failed while Japanese wooden buildings survived, and the reverse. Surveys after the disaster found that building performance depended on structure, quality of construction, foundation, and ground conditions rather than on cultural style. Many brick and masonry buildings, particularly those with weak mortar, poor bonding, or tall unreinforced walls, suffered severe damage or collapse, and the Ryōunkaku, the twelve-story tower in Asakusa that had been a symbol of modern Tokyo, broke apart. Several large reinforced-concrete and steel-framed buildings, including some in the Marunouchi district, survived with damage, and the Imperial Hotel designed by Frank Lloyd Wright was widely noted for remaining standing, though its survival was less a vindication of any single design principle than the result of particular choices about foundation and structure.

Traditional timber houses behaved in varied ways. Well-built wooden structures with flexible joints could sway without collapsing, but heavy tile roofs on weak frames increased the danger of collapse, and many of the cheaply built tenements and shops of working-class districts failed. In some districts, such as parts of Yokohama and western Kanagawa, shaking destroyed wooden houses wholesale. Historians of engineering emphasize that these differences, rather than a simple contrast between East and West, shaped the debates over building standards examined in Section 71.35.

Ground conditions were crucial. The eastern lowlands of Tokyo and the reclaimed land on Yokohama's waterfront rested on soft alluvial or artificial fill in which shaking was amplified and ground failure, including settlement and fissuring, was common. Liquefaction, the temporary loss of strength of saturated sandy soil, is recognized in modern studies of such environments, although contemporary observers described the effects (cracks, spouting water, and sunken roads) without that concept. On higher ground, such as the Yamanote plateau of western Tokyo, damage was generally lighter. The geography of ground conditions thus partly paralleled the geography of social class: poorer districts were often on lower, softer land.

Public infrastructure also failed unevenly. Water mains broke in many places, bridges and embankments settled, and railway track was twisted. Yet some infrastructure survived and became important, such as certain bridges and the naval radio station at Funabashi, which Section 71.12 discusses. The physical damage was therefore the product of an interaction among structures, soils, and human decisions about where and how to build. It cannot be reduced to the magnitude of the shock.

Students should also remember that damage assessment in 1923 was a human process. Engineers, architects, and officials surveyed buildings after the fact, and their reports, in publications by university and government bodies, influenced policy. The categories used in those surveys, and the buildings chosen for study, shaped the lessons drawn.

Section 71.5 · Division I — The Earthquake: Seismic Origins, Urban Vulnerability, and the First Hours

From Earthquake to Firestorm: Cooking Fires, Wind, and Urban Congestion

The distinction between earthquake and fire is central to understanding 1923. Shaking killed many thousands of people, particularly in Yokohama and the mountainous areas of Kanagawa. But in Tokyo, and on most reconstructions in the disaster as a whole, fire caused the great majority of deaths; commonly cited estimates put the share at roughly nine in ten of all deaths. The initial shaking and the destruction of housing mattered chiefly because they created the conditions for fires and trapped or displaced people who then faced them.

Fires began almost at once at many places. Because the earthquake struck near midday, large numbers of households were cooking over charcoal braziers and gas or other flames, and collapsing buildings overturned them onto wooden floors. Factories and warehouses stored chemicals, oil, and other combustible goods, and some of these ignited. Fire services were overwhelmed from the first minutes: fire stations were damaged, horses and vehicles were disrupted, and above all the water mains failed, leaving firefighters without water. A strong wind, associated with a typhoon passing far to the north over the Sea of Japan region, drove flames across wooden districts, and the winds changed direction during the afternoon, so that fires that had burned in one direction turned back on areas thought safe.

Congestion did the rest. Narrow lanes filled with people carrying furniture, bedding, and cooking goods on carts, wagons, and their backs, and these goods blocked the routes of escape and then fed the fires. Bridges, the sole escape across canals and rivers in some districts, became bottlenecks, and the heat on or near the bridges killed many who gathered there. Open spaces were few, and some of them, including the site discussed in Section 71.7, became sites of tragedy when fire reached the possessions that people had brought with them. The fires merged into conflagrations that burned for roughly two days, and by the time they subsided a large share of the built-up area of central and eastern Tokyo had been destroyed; the National Archives of Japan summarizes the loss as about 40 percent of central Tokyo.

The role of fire warns against fatalism. Fire spread because of choices about building materials, street layout, water supply, and storage of flammable goods. In modern terms, much of the mortality was the consequence of a failure of urban design and emergency capacity. That insight informed the reconstruction programs, which sought firebreaks, wide avenues, parks, and fire-resistant buildings, and it is the starting point for the Cause and Effect study below.

Section 71.6 · Division II — The Destruction of Tokyo, Yokohama, and the Kantō Region

Tokyo in Flames: Honjo, Fukagawa, Asakusa, Nihonbashi, and the Eastern Lowlands

The fire destroyed Tokyo unevenly. Districts on the east bank of the Sumida River, including Honjo and Fukagawa, which were low-lying, densely built, and packed with wooden tenements and small factories, suffered the highest mortality and the most complete destruction. Fires also swept the older commercial core, including Nihonbashi and Kyōbashi, where merchant houses, warehouses, and shops stood in blocks of timber and some masonry, and the entertainment and temple district of Asakusa, where the Ryōunkaku had fallen. Kanda, with its schools and bookshops, burned, and the destruction of libraries, including large collections at Tokyo Imperial University, was a cultural loss as well as a human one.

Other districts survived with less damage. The Yamanote plateau in the west, where the ground was firmer and many residences stood in larger lots with gardens, suffered less. Parts of the government district and areas around the Imperial Palace, where open spaces functioned as firebreaks, were damaged but largely escaped the conflagrations. Some neighborhoods were saved by local action: residents beat back flames with buckets and wet mats, and canals and rivers acted as barriers in some cases but as traps in others. The geography of survival depended on the location of open space, water, wind direction, and the decisions of individuals about where to flee.

The experience of crossing waterways was often decisive. In the eastern districts, people fleeing fire sought bridges and riverbanks, but bridges themselves could burn or become blocked by carts and crowds, and rivers carried burning debris. Many people died of burns, heat, or drowning near the Sumida and its canals. Others survived by entering the water or by reaching open ground, but the open ground was not always safe, as the next section shows.

Social geography mattered. The eastern districts were home to industrial laborers, small shopkeepers, and recent migrants, and also to many Koreans and Chinese workers who lived in lodging houses or tenements in the same districts, a fact that would matter in the days that followed. The commercial core, by contrast, contained the wealth of the city's merchant houses and banks, whose loss had large financial effects (Section 71.29). Mortality, displacement, and the loss of property were therefore distributed according to class and location, which anticipates the unequal recovery examined in Section 71.36.

The Former Army Clothing Depot at Honjo: The Catastrophe at One Evacuation Site

The former army clothing depot (rikugun hifukushō ato) in Yokoamichō, Honjo, was the site of the single deadliest event of the disaster. It is essential to be precise: by 1923 the depot had been closed and the land cleared, and it was a large open lot that the city had acquired for use as a park, not a factory or functioning military facility. Because it was one of the few large open spaces in Honjo, tens of thousands of people fled there after the earthquake, bringing household goods on carts and wagons and on their backs.

By the afternoon, fires approaching from several sides had surrounded the site. Accounts describe a dense mass of people and baggage packed on the ground, and sparks and embers landing on the heaps of goods. At around four in the afternoon, the fire reached the site and a fire whirl, a rotating column of fire and superheated air, swept across it, igniting the belongings and enveloping the crowd. Many died in the immediate firestorm, others from suffocation, and others when panicked people tried to escape across blocked exits and the nearby river. Approximately 38,000 people are estimated to have died here, a very large share of the deaths in Tokyo, and the area was sometimes described afterward as a place of unimaginable devastation.

Why did so many people gather there, and why did it become so deadly? Open space had been a sensible choice, but the quantity of goods brought with them turned a refuge into fuel. Escape routes were limited by the Sumida River and burning streets, and there was little organized evacuation or fire control. Historians emphasize that the disaster resulted from the combination of an open-space refuge, large amounts of combustible belongings, wind-driven fire from multiple directions, and an urban layout that left few alternatives. Responsibility for the failure of planning rests not on the evacuees but on the wider urban conditions and absence of emergency organization.

The site became central to public memory. After the disaster the land was used to hold remains, and a memorial hall (ireidō, later the Tokyo Memorial Hall) was built there and consecrated in the years leading to 1930, together with a park. As Section 71.39 explains, the same site later became a location of commemoration for victims of the massacres. Official commemoration of the Honjo dead emphasized the natural catastrophe and national mourning, a narrative that sometimes overshadowed the human responsibility of the violence that followed.

Students should treat the figure of about 38,000 as an estimate derived from the remains recovered and registered, the best available count for a catastrophe in which bodies were burned beyond identification. The National Archives of Japan holds records relating to the Honjo disaster, and the Metropolitan Police and municipal authorities compiled accounts, but exact numbers cannot be reconstructed with certainty.

Section 71.8 · Division II — The Destruction of Tokyo, Yokohama, and the Kantō Region

Yokohama: Earthquake Destruction in Japan's International Port

Yokohama's experience differed from Tokyo's in important respects. The earthquake's fault lay closer to Yokohama than to central Tokyo, and shaking there was extremely violent. Brick and masonry buildings in the commercial and foreign settlement districts collapsed, wooden houses fell, and the waterfront, much of it filled land, was badly damaged. Fires followed quickly and, in the absence of water, burned through the commercial center, the foreign settlement, Chinatown, and much of the older city. The destruction was so complete that historians often describe Yokohama's central districts as effectively annihilated.

The port suffered extensive damage. Quays and warehouses cracked and sank, and ships in the harbor were endangered by burning oil and by the fire spreading to the waterfront. Customs facilities, bank offices, and the premises of trading companies were destroyed, and with them business records and stocks of silk awaiting export. Foreign residents faced the disaster alongside Japanese: the foreign settlement included hotels, consulates, and businesses, and many foreigners died or were injured, while others were rescued by ships in the harbor. The Chinese community, a significant part of Yokohama's population and economy, suffered heavy losses, and Chinese residents of Yokohama would also be targets of violence in the following days (Section 71.21).

Yokohama's casualties were severe relative to its size. The city lost a very large share of its housing and its central commercial buildings, and tens of thousands of people were displaced; the exact mortality is part of the aggregate counted for Kanagawa Prefecture (Section 71.11). Because the port was the gateway for trade and for relief shipments, its destruction also affected the national economy and the government's capacity to deliver aid by sea. Some trade shifted to other ports, particularly Kobe, a change that outlasted the emergency.

The Tokyo-centered narrative, in which the capital's fires and the Honjo disaster dominate, therefore needs correction. Yokohama's collapse resulted largely from shaking and filled ground, followed by fire, whereas Tokyo's mortality was dominated by fire; the two cities needed different forms of relief and had different postdisaster prospects, as Section 71.38 explains. The comparison table below summarizes the contrast.

The loss of Yokohama also had an international dimension. Its foreign communities were among the first to alert the world to the scale of the disaster, and accounts by foreign residents and visiting sailors form part of the documentary record, alongside Japanese and Chinese sources. As with all such evidence, these accounts reflect the positions of their authors and must be compared.

Section 71.9 · Division II — The Destruction of Tokyo, Yokohama, and the Kantō Region

Sagami Bay, the Bōsō Peninsula, and Coastal Tsunami Damage

The earthquake generated a tsunami that struck the shores of Sagami Bay, the Izu coast, and parts of the Bōsō Peninsula within minutes. The rupture displaced the sea floor, and waves rose against the coast with heights that varied greatly from place to place, in some places reaching many meters. The Cabinet Office's historical report documents the tsunami and its coastal effects and distinguishes them from the damage due to shaking and fire.

Seaside communities such as Atami, Itō, and settlements along the Kamakura and Enoshima shores suffered both shaking and the inundation. In Kamakura, the waves swept across the beach and into low-lying parts of the town, and in the Atami and Izu area they destroyed fishing boats, houses, and port facilities and killed numbers of residents. The tsunami's arrival so soon after the shaking left little time for warning, and in many places people who had run to the shore to escape collapsing houses were caught. The sea also withdrew from some harbors before returning, an event that local fishermen recognized as a warning in some places and not in others.

The earthquake also raised and lowered the land. Along the southern Miura Peninsula and the southern tip of the Bōsō Peninsula, coastlines were lifted by amounts on the order of a meter or more, leaving docks stranded above the water and exposing reef and sea bottom, while other coasts sank. These changes damaged harbors and changed local livelihoods, since fishing grounds and shellfish beds were altered. Geologists later used the uplifted terraces of this coast as evidence for earlier great earthquakes, and the 1923 event thus became part of the scientific reconstruction of recurrence.

Local survival depended on geography and knowledge. Villages with higher ground close at hand could evacuate, while those on flat coastal strips could not. Knowledge of the sea, which in some communities was embedded in oral tradition, helped some people, though it was not uniformly reliable. Fishing communities and port towns also faced a double blow because the tsunami destroyed boats and gear, the material basis of their livelihoods, and recovery depended on loans and aid that were slow to arrive.

Historians should be careful to distinguish tsunami casualties from those caused by shaking and fire in coastal towns, a difficult task given the combined effects and the lack of separate registration in many places.

Section 71.10 · Division II — The Destruction of Tokyo, Yokohama, and the Kantō Region

Landslides, Railway Disasters, and Devastation Beyond the Metropolis

The mountains of western Kanagawa experienced some of the earthquake's most violent consequences. The Odawara area, close to the rupture, was struck by powerful shaking, and Odawara's castle town was heavily damaged by collapse and fire. In the Hakone mountains and the valleys draining to Sagami Bay, slopes failed in numerous landslides and debris flows that destroyed villages, roads, and rail lines.

The Nebukawa disaster is the best-known example. At Nebukawa, on the coastal railway between Odawara and Manazuru, a massive landslide destroyed part of the settlement and swept a train carrying passengers into Sagami Bay, with heavy loss of life. The Cabinet Office's historical report treats the Nebukawa landslide as one of the major secondary disasters of the earthquake. In other valleys, sediments mobilized by the shaking buried houses, and mountain roads and rail tunnels were blocked or destroyed, isolating communities for days.

These regional disasters show that the catastrophe cannot be reduced to an event of Tokyo and Yokohama. Mountainous communities received little immediate attention because roads and telegraphs were cut and because outside rescuers concentrated on the large cities. Local residents had to rely on themselves, with rescue parties organized by neighbors and, when possible, by police or military units from nearby posts. Reports of the extent of damage in these areas reached the central government late and incompletely, a factor that affected the allocation of relief.

The railway disruptions had national consequences. The Tōkaidō line, the principal artery between Tokyo and western Japan, was cut, and the main lines out of Tokyo were interrupted for days, delaying the arrival of food and relief supplies. Some lines were restored relatively quickly, a point examined in Section 71.12, while others, particularly those running through the mountain valleys, required extensive rebuilding. The destruction of railways, bridges, and tunnels also reminds students that the disaster reached the infrastructure of the modern state.

Rural and mountainous communities appear less frequently in the historical record, which was dominated by metropolitan newspapers and official reports focused on the capital. Local histories, prefectural records, and later memorial publications provide essential evidence for these areas, and a complete history of 1923 depends on them.

Counting the Dead, Missing, Injured, and Homeless

Counting the victims of the earthquake involved difficulties that remain visible in the records. The modern official estimate, used by the Cabinet Office and consistent with Japan Meteorological Agency data, is approximately 105,000 dead and missing (the Cabinet Office figure is 105,385). This figure is an estimate drawn from a body of records and reconstructions, not a single contemporary count, and it should be identified as such. The distribution among prefectures was uneven, with the great majority of deaths in Tokyo and Kanagawa.

Older sources give different totals. Contemporary compilations by prefectural and municipal authorities, the Home Ministry, and the police assembled counts during and after the emergency, but the categories differed: some sources distinguished 'dead' from 'missing' and some did not; some counted by the household registration, others by the bodies recovered; and some included deaths from later disease or injury while others did not. Fire made identification of the dead impossible in many cases, and the destruction of household registers and municipal offices removed documentary bases for counting. The absence of survivors who could report a family member missing compounded the problem, as did the movement of refugees to other prefectures.

Property and displacement counts faced similar uncertainties. Official reports listed numbers of houses destroyed by shaking, burned, and washed away, but the overlap among categories was hard to resolve, since a house damaged by shaking might later burn. Estimates of the number of homeless people, which ran into the millions across the region by some contemporary accounts, depended on definitions of 'displaced' and on whether people staying with relatives were counted. Students should treat all such figures as estimates and ask who produced them and why.

The same caution applies to the counts of victims of massacre, which were produced by different bodies using different methods and under very different political constraints (Sections 71.20, 71.21, and 71.25). The estimates of earthquake deaths discussed here do not necessarily include victims of the massacres, and different compilations may or may not have counted them. A responsible historian therefore states the source and date of a figure and does not add or subtract incompatible totals.

The Data Study below sets out the main categories of evidence and how to read them. The point is both technical and moral: counting the dead is an act of attention, and the difficulty of doing it precisely does not diminish the reality of what was lost.

Section 71.12 · Division III — Emergency Government, Rescue, Martial Law, and the Information Crisis

Broken Railways, Telegraphs, Roads, Waterworks, and Administrative Communications

The earthquake destroyed much of the network by which a modern state communicates and moves. The Tokyo Central Telegraph Office and many telephone exchanges were damaged or burned, and wires fell across the region. Railway tracks were buckled, bridges and embankments failed, and the capital's main stations were disrupted. Roads were blocked by debris and refugees, and waterworks failed as pipes broke and pumping stations lost power. The result was that for the first hours and days the central government, the military, and the press had very limited knowledge of what had happened beyond what could be seen from a few vantage points.

Some parts of the system survived or were quickly improvised, and they became strategically important. The navy's radio station at Funabashi, in Chiba, continued to function and was used to transmit messages when wire communications failed, and ships in Tokyo Bay and at sea carried messages and personnel. Some railway lines, especially those to the north and east and the lines serving the Chūō route, were restored sooner than others, and boats on Tokyo Bay and the rivers carried people and supplies. Messengers, bicycles, and motorcycles carried information in a city without telephones. The relative importance of these improvised systems shows how dependent the state had become on networks that a single shock could break.

The consequences for administration were immediate. Authorities struggled to establish the scale of destruction: early reports from different districts were fragmentary and sometimes contradictory, and some officials believed for a time that Tokyo's damage was less than it was. The central government, which was in the process of forming a new cabinet, had to decide on relief without reliable information. Local governments lost their offices and records, and police and fire services, the organizations best equipped to take charge, were themselves disorganized. Prefectural and municipal officials improvised, sometimes with remarkable energy and sometimes with bewildering confusion.

Water and transport shortages shaped everything that followed. Without water, fire could not be fought and sanitation deteriorated; without rail transport, food stocks in the surrounding countryside could not be moved into the city quickly, although the stocks of rice in some warehouses were also destroyed by fire. The movement of refugees out of the city, by rail where possible and on foot, spread news of the disaster across Japan and produced a countervailing flow of volunteers and relief goods, but the initial days were a period of scarcity in the disaster area.

The history of this breakdown is also the history of how rumor gained influence: where reliable information is lacking, accounts that fit existing fears spread quickly. Section 71.16 examines this relationship directly, and Division IV explains its consequences.

Section 71.13 · Division III — Emergency Government, Rescue, Martial Law, and the Information Crisis

Rescue, Evacuation, and the First Acts of Community Self-Help

In the first hours the work of rescue was done largely by neighbors. People who had survived the collapse of their houses dug with their hands and with tools to free those trapped, formed bucket lines to fight fires, and carried the injured to open spaces and to the few hospitals and clinics that still stood. Teachers and students, shopkeepers, workers, and members of local fire brigades and youth associations took part, often at great personal risk, and some died in the effort. Diaries and memoirs preserve many accounts of such actions, and later official histories drew on them.

Communities improvised refuges. Parks, school grounds, temple and shrine precincts, riverbanks, and the grounds of the Imperial Palace received hundreds of thousands of people. Neighbors shared food, water, and cooking utensils; some shopkeepers distributed goods, and some organizations established first-aid stations and information boards on which people posted the names of missing relatives. Such boards became a central feature of the refugee landscape and a vital substitute for the destroyed means of communication.

Historians should, however, be careful with the later narrative of universal solidarity. The disaster did elicit extraordinary cooperation, and this is well documented, but it also saw profiteering, theft, and fear, and the same communities that shared rice also organized the roadblocks examined in Division IV. Cooperation was often organized along existing lines of neighborhood, workplace, and class, and those regarded as outsiders could be excluded from help or treated as threats. Poor residents, lodgers, and migrants had weaker claims on neighborhood networks than long-established householders. A nuanced account recognizes both the mutual aid and its boundaries.

Official responses supplemented these efforts. Military units from garrisons in and near Tokyo, police, and the Red Cross began relief work, and the navy's ships delivered food and medical supplies. Medical personnel, including university hospital doctors and private physicians, treated the injured in makeshift stations. But the scale of need far exceeded what the authorities could provide in the first days, and the Cabinet Office's historical review emphasizes the importance of voluntary assistance in the initial period when official relief had not yet been organized.

The distinction between documented initiatives and later generalization matters for how the disaster has been remembered. In public commemoration, the image of a united nation helping itself became a standard theme. The sources show a more complicated reality, in which mutual aid and persecution coexisted, sometimes within the same neighborhoods and among the same people.

The Government in Crisis: Uchida Kōsai, Yamamoto Gonbee, and Emergency Administration

The political situation in early September 1923 was unusual. Prime Minister Katō Tomosaburō, an admiral who had headed a nonparty cabinet since 1922, died on August 24. No successor had been installed, and Foreign Minister Uchida Kōsai took the post of acting prime minister while the elder statesmen deliberated on a successor. The Cabinet Office's disaster white paper confirms that Japan had no newly installed prime minister when the earthquake struck. Yamamoto Gonbee (also read Gonnohyōe), an admiral who had been prime minister in 1913–1914, had received the imperial command to form a cabinet on August 29 and was working on appointments at the navy officers' club, the Suikōsha in Tsukiji, on the day of the earthquake.

The second Yamamoto cabinet was constituted on September 2, in a ceremony held under emergency conditions at the Akasaka detached palace, where Crown Prince Hirohito, acting as regent on behalf of his father, Emperor Taishō, who was incapacitated by illness, received the new ministers. Gotō Shinpei, the former Tokyo mayor and a famously energetic administrator, became home minister, a position that put him at the head of the police and local administration and made him the central civilian figure in the emergency and in reconstruction planning. The decision to form the cabinet during the disaster reflected a judgment that the government needed full authority at once. Students should avoid placing Yamamoto's government in office on the morning of September 1: the first day's emergency response was conducted by the acting prime minister and the existing ministers.

The government took a series of emergency steps. Before the new cabinet took office, Acting Prime Minister Uchida issued three imperial ordinances: an emergency requisition ordinance to procure relief supplies (No. 396), the ordinance creating the Temporary Earthquake Relief Bureau (No. 397), and the martial-law ordinance (No. 398). The Yamamoto cabinet took office on the evening of September 2. On September 3 it received an imperial relief grant and a message urging officials and the public to cooperate in emergency measures. On September 4 the cabinet decided policy on securing food and supplies, sheltering victims, caring for the injured, controlling vigilante groups (jikeidan), and preventing economic disorder, and issued Cabinet Admonition No. 1 to calm the confusion. A debt moratorium and public-order penalties followed in emergency ordinances of September 7. These decisions, and the wider structure of emergency government, are documented in the National Archives of Japan.

The first day and a half were marked by a double failure of capacity. According to the Cabinet Office's 2023 white paper, an extraordinary cabinet meeting on the morning of September 2 decided to create a Temporary Earthquake Relief Bureau (headed by the prime minister, with the home minister as vice-president) and to proclaim martial law, and the Yamamoto cabinet's investiture took place that evening. The Relief Bureau's first meeting is recorded at about 3 p.m. on September 2, roughly 27 hours after the earthquake. The white paper attributes the slow start partly to the destruction of both the Home Ministry's and the Metropolitan Police Department's headquarters buildings by fire, and partly to the fact that September 1 was a half-day Saturday on which many officials had already gone home to check on their families.

The emergency administration was exercised through decrees rather than through the Diet, which was not in session. The use of imperial ordinances under the constitution's emergency provisions was legal but had political implications: it bypassed parliamentary debate at a time when the government was acquiring extraordinary powers and when its record on public order would later be questioned. The ordinances would require later ratification by the Diet.

The experience of the first days illustrates the dependence of Taishō constitutional government on informal elite coordination. In the absence of an established prime minister, officials, military leaders, and the court acted with considerable autonomy, and the new cabinet inherited and extended arrangements made in haste. The consequences for the treatment of Koreans and political dissidents, discussed in Division IV, were serious, though historians disagree about the extent to which the cabinet as a whole, rather than particular ministries and local authorities, determined policy.

Martial Law, Military Deployment, and the Expansion of Emergency Authority

On September 2 the government extended to the disaster area the provisions of the martial-law ordinance of 1882, a measure that placed local administration and public order under military commanders. The legal basis was disputed: the ordinance had been designed for wartime or insurrection, and its application to a natural disaster was made by an imperial ordinance (No. 398) applying the necessary provisions to a designated area, rather than by a normal declaration. The decision was taken at the cabinet meeting of the morning of September 2, and the provisions were applied that day to Tokyo City and five adjoining counties; the zone was extended to Kanagawa on September 3 and to Saitama and Chiba on September 4. The Kantō Martial Law Headquarters, under General Fukuda Masatarō, was established on September 3, and the Cabinet Office notes that the army began organized relief activity only after that date. Troop strength in the zone peaked at roughly 49,000 in mid-September, according to a Headquarters table held in the National Institute for Defense Studies archive, and fell as police gradually took over public order; the Headquarters was abolished on November 15. JACAR documents the emergency martial-law measures and the command arrangements, and describes the September 2 step as a partial application of martial law (Imperial Ordinance No. 398), what it terms administrative martial law, under which the commander of the Konoe Division, Moriooka Morinari, first served as martial-law commander before the Headquarters took over on September 3. JACAR also records that the Headquarters issued proclamations denying and suppressing rumors, yet that, with communications cut, many killings involving troops and reservists occurred. General Fukuda was relieved of command on September 20, the day Captain Amakasu was referred to a court-martial.

Military deployment took different forms. Soldiers cleared debris, distributed food, guarded warehouses, controlled railway stations, and assisted refugees. Engineers built temporary bridges and carried out demolition to create firebreaks. These activities were important, and the military's organization and transport capacity made it indispensable to relief. At the same time the military and police received extended authority to maintain order, including to search, arrest, and in some circumstances use force against persons believed to threaten the public peace. The line between rescue and repression was not always clear in practice.

The expansion of emergency authority created conditions in which violence could occur with the apparent sanction of institutions. Soldiers were stationed at many of the places where Koreans and Chinese were killed, and some units took part in killings, as Sections 71.20, 71.21, and 71.23 explain. It is important to state clearly that emergency authority did not make the killing of civilians lawful. Martial-law provisions authorized the military to maintain order, but no legal provision licensed the murder of unarmed Koreans, Chinese residents, or labor activists, and contemporaneous government statements, as well as later prosecutions of some perpetrators, acknowledged this, even as official practice allowed many to go unpunished.

Historians distinguish the legal introduction of emergency powers from their practical implementation. Martial-law orders were issued from the capital, but their execution depended on local commanders, police chiefs, and civilians who took the initiative. Some commanders tried to restrain violence and protect threatened people; others tolerated or participated in it. The territorial scope of the emergency zone also mattered, because it shaped which units were present and which institutions had responsibility.

The emergency imperial ordinance of September 7 on penalties for the maintenance of public order (Chian iji no tame ni suru bassoku ni kan suru ken), issued during the emergency, extended criminal liability for disseminating false rumors and for various forms of unrest; it reflected the government's recognition by that date that rumor was a danger, even as officials had earlier contributed to it. Its later use in political prosecutions is a reminder that emergency measures could outlast their immediate purpose.

Section 71.16 · Division III — Emergency Government, Rescue, Martial Law, and the Information Crisis

News, Rumor, Censorship, and the Struggle to Establish Reliable Information

Information was the central scarce resource of the early days. The damage to the telegraph and telephone networks, the destruction of many newspaper plants, and the dispersal of the population left the public without regular news. Radio broadcasting did not yet exist in Japan, so there was no medium for rapid mass announcements; the first radio broadcasts came only in 1925. Many of Tokyo's major newspapers lost their offices, and some resumed publication in improvised form, printing small editions at other plants or relying on printers in other cities. Papers from Osaka and elsewhere sent reporters and newsprint to the capital, and the news of the disaster reached the rest of Japan and the world through ships' wireless and routes out of Yokohama and the unaffected regions.

Authorities used a range of means to communicate: posted notices, handbills, announcements by police and soldiers, and information boards. The government issued proclamations and sought to control the content of news; the censorship apparatus, already extensive, was applied to reports of the disaster, and the authorities sometimes banned or delayed reports that they regarded as dangerous to public order. Informal communication, including oral reports from refugees arriving by train or on foot, filled the gaps, and every retelling added and altered details.

The result was an information environment in which accuracy could not easily be verified. Reports of fires, deaths, and rescue were mingled with false stories, among them the rumors of subversive plots examined in the next section. Some Japanese newspapers published unverified claims about Korean violence, and some officials relayed them. Others attempted to counter such claims; the history of the response to rumor is a history of competing efforts by different parts of the state and society.

The experience demonstrates how disaster conditions create incentives to believe that someone is responsible. Rumors offered an explanation, a target for fear, and a way to act when action was otherwise impossible, and they spread along existing lines of prejudice. For historians, the question is not whether people believed the rumors, which is amply documented, but why some chose to believe and act on them, why institutions failed to stop them, and why others resisted.

This section establishes the institutional setting for Division IV. The reader should keep in mind that the same weeks produced both the practical improvisations of relief and the false accusations that led to massacres, and that both depended on the breakdown of ordinary communication.

Section 71.17 · Division IV — Rumors, Racial Massacres, Political Killings, and State Responsibility

The Origins of Anti-Korean Rumors after the Earthquake

Within hours of the earthquake, false stories began to circulate that Koreans were taking advantage of the disaster to commit arson, poison wells, loot, and prepare armed attacks. These stories had no factual basis; investigations at the time and later by historians found no evidence of a coordinated Korean plot, and the very small number of cases in which Koreans were accused of crimes were not remotely comparable to the claims made. The rumors must be identified as unsubstantiated: they are not a competing version of events but an element of the history of the violence.

Why did these rumors take hold? Colonial rule provided the foundation. After annexation in 1910, Korea had become a Japanese colony, and Koreans living in Japan were subjects of the empire who were treated as inferior and often as potential threats. Many had come to Japan to work in construction, mining, and factory labor, living in lodging houses and often paid less than Japanese workers; they were also visible in labor disputes, as employers recruited Koreans at lower wages, a source of friction. The March First Movement of 1919, with its mass demonstrations for independence and the brutal suppression that followed, had given Japanese authorities and some of the public a sense of Korea as a source of subversion, and police maintained surveillance of Korean students and activists in Tokyo.

The rumors drew on the vocabulary of earlier fears. Stereotypes of Koreans as dangerous and dishonest, spread in newspapers and popular culture, made accusations credible to some who had little contact with Korean neighbors. The belief that Koreans might use a disaster to strike at Japan reflected a projection of colonial guilt: rulers who knew that their rule was resented imagined that the ruled would seek revenge. Historians also point to the 1920 experience of reports of Korean militancy, including incidents in Manchuria and Siberia where Japanese troops fought Korean guerrillas, as a part of the background in military and police circles.

The specific content of the rumors, poison in wells and arson, fit the circumstances of the moment: fires were burning, water was scarce, and many people could see that something burning was the work of the earthquake and not of any attacker, yet some still wished to believe otherwise. The first reports are traced by scholars to Yokohama and Tokyo on the afternoon and evening of September 1, with reports spreading outward along refugee routes and by word of mouth and, from September 2, in official communications.

Not all Japanese residents believed or acted on the rumors, and the credibility of the rumors varied by place, social position, and exposure to the police and the press. The next sections examine how institutions amplified them and how organized groups translated them into violence.

Section 71.18 · Division IV — Rumors, Racial Massacres, Political Killings, and State Responsibility

Official Communications, Police Conduct, and the Amplification of Rumors

The role of the state in the spread of the rumors is one of the most important and sensitive questions in the historiography. The Japanese Cabinet Office's own disaster-history report acknowledges that in some places police and military personnel passed on or lent credence to rumors, and that vigilantes, soldiers, and police took part in killings of Koreans. Scholars have documented a telegram from the head of the Home Ministry's Police Bureau, Gotō Fumio, to local governors. It was carried by courier from Tokyo on the afternoon of September 2 and transmitted from the navy's Funabashi radio station on the morning of September 3 (receipt is recorded at 8:15 a.m. via the Kure Naval District adjutant). In substance, it stated that Koreans were taking advantage of the disaster to set fires, that some in Tokyo carried bombs and poured oil, and it ordered strict control of Koreans' actions. Researchers also report that on September 2 a Saitama prefectural official circulated to county and town heads a notice about a supposed Korean riot that encouraged the formation of vigilance groups. Whether these communications reflected belief in the rumors or an attempt to prepare for possible unrest is discussed, but their effect was to give the rumors an official imprimatur in many localities.

At the local level, police in some districts told residents that Koreans were setting fires and poisoning wells, encouraged the formation of vigilance groups, and in some cases distributed weapons or authorized roadblocks. Soldiers on patrol made similar claims, and some units participated in arrests and killings. Newspapers, including papers printed in improvised form, published reports of Korean 'riots' (futei senjin, 'rebellious Koreans'), and some local newspapers published unverified accounts. These institutional actions gave the rumors authority that popular gossip alone might not have had.

At the same time, there were officials who tried to stop the violence. Some police officers and local administrators recognized the rumors as false, tried to calm residents, and sheltered Koreans in police stations or military barracks. The case of Ōkawa Tsunekichi, head of the Tsurumi police substation, who is commonly reported to have protected Koreans and defended them against crowds, is among those cited by historians, and there were other documented cases of individual courage. The government also, from around September 5, issued statements and notices intended to counter the rumors and to warn against attacks on Koreans, although these efforts came late and were of limited effect. The point is not that the state spoke with one voice but that different parts of it acted differently, and that institutional confusion and prejudice combined with the weak control of local police to allow violence.

Historians continue to debate how far official policy as opposed to local initiative was responsible. The evidence indicates a mixture: central authorities relayed warnings, local police amplified them, and the military's presence and organization enabled both protection and killing. Later governments tended to minimize official responsibility and to describe the violence as the spontaneous work of frightened civilians, a narrative that the documentary record contradicts. Section 71.25 and the Historical Debate there examine these questions.

For the study of historical method, this section demonstrates that official documents can both create and record a rumor. The telegrams, police notices, and newspaper reports are evidence of what authorities said and believed on a given day; they are not evidence that the claims were true.

The Formation of Vigilante Groups and the Militarization of Neighborhoods

The jikeidan, or neighborhood self-defense groups, were central to the violence. Many such groups existed before 1923, organized for fire watching, crime prevention, and local order, and after the earthquake neighborhoods formed or revived them to guard against fire, theft, and the rumored Korean attackers. Their members were typically male householders and shopkeepers, firefighters, and young men from local youth associations and reservist organizations, and they were armed with sticks, swords, hunting guns, bamboo spears, and tools. In some places police and soldiers encouraged them, issued armbands or weapons, or integrated them into patrols.

Roadblocks and checkpoints became the characteristic form of vigilante action. Groups stopped people at street corners, bridges, and railway stations, questioned them, demanded identification, and searched belongings. The aim was to identify Koreans, whom the groups assumed to be dangerous. Since Koreans and Japanese could not be reliably distinguished by appearance, vigilantes used proxies: accent, pronunciation of certain Japanese words, ability to recite particular phrases, and the answers to questions designed to catch speakers who had learned Japanese as a second language. Commonly reported tests included having suspects pronounce words that are difficult for Korean speakers; the specific tests varied by location and are known from witness accounts and later research.

These procedures produced mistaken identifications and extended the violence beyond its original targets, as the next section explains. They also reflected a militarization of neighborhoods in which ordinary civic institutions, such as the fire brigade, the neighborhood association, and the youth group, were turned toward the persecution of a minority. Historians emphasize that the groups were not marginal criminals but members of respectable local society, which complicates any attempt to attribute the killings to a fringe element.

Participation was shaped by local circumstances. In some neighborhoods vigilantes protected Koreans or refused to take part in attacks; in others they carried out murders. In some places police arrested vigilantes, and in others they stood aside or took part. The escalation from insecurity to organized attacks depended on whether local leaders were willing to restrain the groups, on the presence of soldiers or police who either supported or opposed them, and on the presence of Koreans who could be seized. The pattern was thus neither uniform nor random.

The vigilante phenomenon also reveals social hierarchies. The people who controlled the checkpoints were typically established residents with local standing; those stopped were migrants, lodgers, and colonial subjects with little local protection. The checkpoint was a device by which the former exercised power over the latter, and the language of self-defense concealed that relationship.

The Massacre of Koreans: Geography, Victims, and the Colonial Context

The killing of Koreans in the days after the earthquake is documented across Tokyo, Kanagawa, Saitama, and Chiba. Victims were attacked at checkpoints, on bridges and riverbanks, in neighborhoods, at railway stations, and in police stations and military facilities to which they had fled or been taken for protection. The violence peaked between September 2 and about September 6, though isolated killings continued afterward. Methods included beating, stabbing, shooting, and killing with improvised weapons, and bodies were thrown into rivers or buried hastily. These facts rest on witness accounts, police and court documents, journalists' reports, victims' testimonies, and the findings of later scholars.

The geography of the killings reflects the geography of Korean settlement and the movements of refugees. In Tokyo, many Koreans lived in the eastern districts, including Honjo, Fukagawa, Kameido, and the areas along the Arakawa River, and the riverbanks and bridges there are among the best-documented sites of killing. In Yokohama and Kanagawa, Koreans were attacked in the burning city and in the surrounding area; in Chiba, killings occurred in the area of Funabashi and Narashino, where Koreans were held by military authorities; and in Saitama, in places such as Kumagaya and the Kamisato area, groups of Koreans who had been escorted or transferred by police were attacked by crowds. These examples are only a selection, and local research has identified many more sites.

Authorities sometimes took Koreans into protective custody, but custody did not always protect them. In some places, Koreans held in police stations or camps were handed over to crowds or were killed by soldiers or police. In others, officials resisted pressure, and Koreans survived. The documented efforts of some Japanese residents to protect Koreans, by hiding them, vouching for them, or confronting vigilantes, saved lives and remain part of the record. Such protection was real but, in the context of the scale of violence, exceptional.

The violence in the imperial metropole was inseparable from the colonial system. Koreans in Japan were colonial subjects whose presence in the metropole was a product of imperial economic and political arrangements, and the willingness of neighbors and officials to believe in a Korean plot derived from the colonial relationship. Japanese rule in Korea had produced both resistance and a mentality of suspicion among authorities; the massacre brought that mentality into the heart of Japan. For Korean communities, the killings became a founding memory of the colonial experience, and they shaped the politics of the Korean independence movement and of the Korean diaspora.

Victim counts differ widely and must be attributed to their sources. Three figures anchor the discussion, and they come from different investigations with different standards. The Japanese government's contemporary acknowledged count of Korean dead was 233, a figure limited to the cases that officials chose to acknowledge or could not deny. The scholar Yoshino Sakuzō reported a figure of 2,613 that derived from an investigation by a Korean relief group in Tokyo; it is a reported tally, not an independent census by Yoshino himself. The Korean Independent (Dongnip sinmun), published in Shanghai, printed a count of 6,661 on December 5, 1923, based on Korean investigation, and the National Archives of Korea confirms its provenance. These figures must not be averaged or treated as equally comprehensive. The Cabinet Office report acknowledges the involvement of vigilantes, military personnel, and police and notes that estimates range from several hundred to approximately six thousand. Scholarly and Korean archival research has documented estimates in the thousands, including approximately 6,000, but no universally accepted definitive count exists. The uncertainty concerns numbers, not the occurrence of the massacres, which is firmly established.

Section 71.21 · Division IV — Rumors, Racial Massacres, Political Killings, and State Responsibility

The Massacre of Chinese Residents and Workers

Chinese residents and workers were also targets of killing. Tokyo and Yokohama had substantial Chinese communities in 1923: merchants and craftsmen in Yokohama's Chinatown and other districts, students in Tokyo, and migrant laborers who worked in construction and factory jobs, many of them from Zhejiang Province and especially from the Wenzhou area. In the eastern part of Tokyo, near Ōshima-machi in the present-day Kōtō ward, a concentration of Chinese workers lived in lodging houses and worked in small factories and on construction sites. Many of them had organized mutual-aid groups to protect themselves against exploitation and discrimination.

The killings in Ōshima-machi, beginning on or about September 3, are among the best-documented episodes of violence against Chinese. Chinese workers were rounded up, attacked, and killed by groups that included soldiers and police as well as civilians; accounts describe workers taken from their lodgings or arrested and then killed on the riverbanks and in open ground. Chinese sources and later research have identified hundreds of victims, while Japanese official statements acknowledged a much smaller number. Estimates must be attributed to their sources; the Japanese government's acknowledgment of a small number of deaths reflects the limits of official investigation and the interest of the authorities in minimizing responsibility, while Chinese tallies reflect investigations by Chinese organizations and diplomats using testimony and the accounts of survivors. As with the Korean victims, no single definitive figure is accepted by all researchers.

The murder of Wang Xitian is the best-known individual case. Wang, a young Chinese man who had studied in Japan and had become a leader of efforts to organize and assist Chinese laborers, went to Ōshima-machi after the earthquake to help Chinese workers and was arrested and killed by military personnel, apparently on or about September 12. His disappearance prompted inquiries by Chinese representatives. Research, including that of Jiaying Shen, has analyzed the case and the evidence about the identity of his killers and the attempt to obscure it, and Byung Wook Jung has placed the killings of Chinese in comparative perspective with the violence against Koreans.

Several factors explain the targeting of Chinese. Anti-Chinese prejudice had deep roots, intensified by Japan's wars with China and its expanding interests there, and Chinese laborers were resented for competing for work at low wages. Authorities regarded the organized Chinese workers' groups with suspicion, and in the chaos of September some officials and soldiers apparently saw an opportunity to eliminate a group that they treated as threatening. The killings differed from those of Koreans in some respects: they involved a migrant labor population under foreign nationality, and they therefore produced diplomatic consequences, unlike the killings of Korean colonial subjects, for whom Japan alone claimed responsibility.

Recent scholarship describes more than three hundred Japanese entering Ōshima-machi around noon on September 3. For the numbers, one study counts 97 Chinese killed, 24 injured, and 2 missing in Kanagawa Prefecture, with the author warning of possible duplicate records; Niki Kazuko's tallies were extended in 1990 by survivor interviews in Wenzhou; and Chinese reviews of the research speak of more than 700 killed overall. Two cautions apply to the numbers. Chinese investigations of 1923 and Japanese official returns used different categories (dead, missing, injured, and persons otherwise affected) and were compiled at different dates, so they cannot be compared line by line without checking the original tables. And research that has compiled lists of more than seven hundred named victims or affected persons is a documentary achievement, but such a list is not automatically a verified count of deaths; students should ask what each name on a list is documented to show. The killing of the Ōshima-machi laborers and the separate killing of Wang Xitian should also be kept distinct, since they involved different victims, dates, and evidence. The Chinese government and public protested, sent investigators, and demanded accountability. Negotiations followed between Chinese and Japanese authorities over investigation, compensation, and the treatment of survivors, but the Japanese government sought to limit the political damage and the scope of inquiry. Chinese records and petitions are an important source for these events and a reminder that the massacre was not only a domestic Japanese or colonial issue but an episode in Sino-Japanese relations, with echoes in Chinese public memory of Japanese conduct in the following decade.

Mistaken Identity, Dialect, Disability, and the Expansion of Persecution

The vigilantes' reliance on accent and fluency to identify Koreans produced mistaken killings and attacks on Japanese people. Speakers of regional dialects that sounded unfamiliar to Tokyo residents, particularly from the northeast and from southern Japan, were stopped and sometimes beaten. Okinawan speakers were reportedly also at risk, a reflection of the way Okinawans, themselves subject to discrimination, were regarded as unfamiliar by people at roadblocks. People with speech or hearing disabilities, who could not answer questions or recite the required phrases, were also attacked as suspects. These cases are documented in witness accounts, police reports, and court records.

The best-known case is the Fukuda village incident in Chiba Prefecture on September 6. A party of fifteen itinerant medicine sellers from Kagawa Prefecture, who spoke the dialect of their home region, were stopped by a local vigilante group; the group's members, convinced that the strangers were Koreans, attacked them, and nine of the fifteen were killed, among them young children and a pregnant woman. The party came from a community that itself faced social discrimination in Japan, a fact that scholars treat as part of the interpretation, since it shows how the vigilantes' categories of suspicion reached people already marginal. The incident came to trial, and members of the vigilante group were convicted, though with sentences that were comparatively light by the standard of the crime. Historians cite it as a clear example of how the identity tests could turn on ordinary Japanese citizens and of how the violence was legally recognized as murder, at least in a case where the victims were Japanese.

The case is often contrasted with the handling of killings of Koreans and Chinese, where prosecutions were fewer and penalties generally light. That contrast should be treated as a generalization from case studies, not a rule: prosecutions occurred in both kinds of cases, and sentences in the Fukuda case were themselves light. What the evidence supports is a pattern of unequal outcomes, in which public pressure and the identity of the victims affected how far the legal system acted.

This section requires careful framing. Mistaken-identity killings and attacks on Japanese residents demonstrate the danger of vigilantism and arbitrary identity tests, and they have sometimes been invoked by later writers to argue that the violence was an indiscriminate panic. That interpretation is misleading. The incidents involving Japanese victims were a consequence of the systematic targeting of Koreans and Chinese, not a separate phenomenon, and they cannot be equated with the particular, systematic persecution of colonial and foreign populations. The presence of a few Japanese victims does not diminish the fact that the principal victims were Koreans and Chinese, singled out because of who they were.

The social dimensions of identity are also visible here. Dialect, class, and disability marked people as outsiders in a city that was itself the product of migration, and the incident shows how a metropolitan society composed largely of migrants could nonetheless divide people by speech into insiders and outsiders. It is also a reminder that ethnic and national categories in imperial Japan were enforced through everyday practices of recognition and suspicion.

The Kameido Incident: The Killing of Labor and Political Activists

The Kameido Incident involved the killing of labor and political activists by soldiers in the eastern district of Kameido, in the area then known as the Minami-Katsushika district of Tokyo. In the first days of September, police in the district detained a number of men associated with local labor organizations, notably the Nankatsu Labor Association (Nankatsu Rōdō Kai), a union active among factory workers in the area and part of the broader left-leaning currents of the labor movement discussed in Chapter 69. Hirasawa Keishichi, a leader of the association, was among those detained.

On the night of September 3 and into September 4, soldiers of a cavalry regiment, detailed to maintain order in the area, killed about ten of the detained activists at or near the Kameido police station. Officials announced that the men had been killed in the course of a disturbance in which they had attacked the soldiers, an account that the families, the labor movement, and later historians have rejected as unsupported. The killings were carried out by soldiers, apparently in cooperation with police who had detained the men and handed them over; the exact chain of command and the intentions of senior officers remain subjects of research.

The political context was the police and military's long-standing hostility toward labor and socialist organizations, intensified by the emergency and the fear of 'subversion' that the rumors of Korean plots had fed. Officials in the Home Ministry and the military regarded the labor activists of the eastern districts as dangerous, and the Kameido killings can be understood as an extension of the emergency's logic of suspicion to a Japanese political group. It would be a mistake, however, to treat Kameido as merely another case of mob violence: the perpetrators were soldiers acting under the authority of the martial-law structure, and the victims were detained men in the custody of the police.

The labor movement and press reacted with outrage, but censorship limited coverage, and the story emerged only gradually. A funeral and memorial for the victims were held later, and labor organizations pursued the case, with limited success. No senior official was held criminally responsible for the Kameido killings, and the soldiers involved were not punished in a manner commensurate with the crime. The case demonstrates the absence of accountability for state violence against political opponents during the emergency.

Students should keep the Kameido and Amakasu incidents distinct. They involved different victims (a group of local labor activists versus three individuals, an anarchist, a feminist-anarchist, and a child), different locations, different dates, and different institutional actors (a cavalry regiment and police station versus the military police headquarters). Their similarity lies in the political environment that made both possible and in the failure of accountability, not in their details.

The Amakasu Incident: Ōsugi Sakae, Itō Noe, and Tachibana Munekazu

On September 16, 1923, Ōsugi Sakae, Itō Noe, and Tachibana Munekazu were arrested by military police in Tokyo and killed. Ōsugi (1885–1923) was the most prominent anarchist writer of the Taishō years, as Chapter 69 explained. Itō Noe (1895–1923), a feminist writer who had edited the journal Seitō and become an anarchist, was his partner. Tachibana Munekazu, a boy of about six, was Ōsugi's nephew, born in the United States and staying with the couple after being brought to Japan. The three had gone out to visit relatives and were taken into custody by members of the military police.

They were taken to the headquarters of the military police in Kōjimachi and killed that evening by strangulation. Their bodies were thrown into an abandoned well on the grounds and covered with debris. The killings became public after the press learned of the arrests and the bodies were found, and the Army Minister announced that Captain Amakasu Masahiko of the military police had been involved. The case caused widespread public concern and anger, both because of the prominence of the victims and because of the murder of a child.

The military authorities arranged a court-martial; JACAR records that Amakasu was referred to it on September 20, the day the martial-law commander, General Fukuda, was relieved. Amakasu Masahiko was convicted and sentenced to ten years' imprisonment; another military police officer received a lesser sentence, and others were acquitted. Amakasu served only a fraction of his term and was released in the later 1920s, after which he went to France and returned to a career in Manchuria, where he later headed the Manchukuo Film Association. Some contemporary commentators and later historians have argued that Amakasu may have acted on orders or with the support of superiors, a possibility that the court-martial did not examine in depth; the question of higher responsibility remains debated, and the evidence is incomplete. Some Japanese nationalists praised Amakasu as acting out of patriotic motives, and there were petitions for clemency, which gives a picture of the political climate, though the case also produced public criticism of the army.

The killings must be understood as political murder in a climate of emergency. Ōsugi and Itō had been under police surveillance for years, and in the post-earthquake atmosphere military authorities were hostile to anarchists and socialists. At the same time, the victims were not killed in the chaos of September 1-4 but a fortnight later, in a military facility, by officers acting under institutional authority, which distinguishes the case from spontaneous or vigilante violence. The killing of a child is notable and suggests an attempt to eliminate witnesses.

The case's political significance extended beyond the victims. It removed a leading anarchist voice, intensified the movement's sense of vulnerability, and contributed to a perception on the left that the state was prepared to kill. In subsequent years, some anarchists and others responded with acts of retaliation, including the Toranomon Incident of December 1923, an attempt by a young man on the life of the Prince Regent, which brought down the Yamamoto cabinet. Students should treat this causal connection with care: the assailant, Namba Daisuke, acted as an individual, and the connection between his motives and the 1923 killings is a matter of historical interpretation.

Investigations, Trials, Victim Counts, and the Limits of Accountability

The investigations that followed the massacres were limited and uneven. Prosecutors in some prefectures charged vigilantes for killings of Koreans, and trials were held in Tokyo, Saitama, Chiba, and elsewhere. A number of defendants were convicted, but the sentences were generally light, and many were suspended or reduced; later amnesties and clemency measures further reduced penalties. Defendants frequently argued that they had acted to defend their neighborhoods against attackers whom they believed to exist, and courts sometimes accepted this as a mitigating circumstance. Police and military personnel who participated in killings were rarely prosecuted. Press restrictions limited reporting on the killings for weeks; a ban on publication about the Korean killings was lifted only in late October, after which newspapers could report on the trials.

Criminal convictions are not a measure of deaths. Prosecutions concerned particular incidents in which evidence and willingness to prosecute existed, and they therefore capture only a small fraction of the killings. The conviction of vigilantes in a few cases does not establish the number of victims, and the lack of prosecution in other cases does not indicate that no killings occurred. Official prosecution records should never be treated as an exhaustive count of the murdered, and the argument that the massacres were limited because few were convicted confuses the justice system's failure with the historical reality.

Korean and Chinese communities conducted their own investigations. Korean students and activists in Tokyo gathered testimony; Korean organizations in Shanghai compiled lists of victims, among them the count published on December 5, 1923; Chinese groups investigated the killings of laborers. Some Japanese individuals, including scholars such as Yoshino Sakuzō and, in the postwar period, researchers such as Yamada Shōji, contributed to documenting the events. The results differ in numbers and methods, and they were produced under constraints of censorship, danger, and limited access to the sites. Later scholarship, including Jinhee Lee's research on the Korean massacre and on commemoration, and the Chinese-focused work cited in Section 71.21, has tested these accounts against archival sources and local records.

The reasons for the differences in estimates should be understood. Official figures reflect the number of cases the government chose to acknowledge or could not deny. Investigations by victims' communities rely on testimony and reports from survivors, many of whom lacked access to the sites or fled, and on lists whose compilers had no means of verification. Scholarly estimates synthesize these sources with local records. They range from the hundreds to approximately six thousand for Koreans, and they remain uncertain because bodies were concealed, records destroyed, and many victims had no one in Japan to report them missing. The range reflects evidence, not ignorance of the fact of the massacre.

The limited accountability had lasting consequences. It reinforced the impression that violence against colonial subjects would go unpunished, deepened Korean and Chinese distrust, and allowed an official narrative that blamed disorder and rumor while obscuring the roles of police and military. It also meant that the history of 1923 would be contested for a century: whether to acknowledge state responsibility remains a subject of political debate in Japan and across East Asia.

Historical Debate

Historical Debate: Evidence and State Responsibility for the Massacres

The question: How far were the killings of 1923 the result of official policy or institutional action, as opposed to the spontaneous action of frightened civilians?

Interpretation A: Some historians emphasize the role of authorities: Home Ministry telegrams, police warnings, the distribution of weapons, the participation of soldiers and police, and the shaping of the rumor by official channels indicate that the state bore substantial responsibility for creating and enabling the violence, even if no single order to kill has been found.
Interpretation B: Others emphasize the local and social dimensions: the formation of vigilante groups by civilians, the role of preexisting prejudice, the diversity of official actions (including protection), and the absence of evidence of a centrally planned massacre suggest that responsibility was shared among many actors and that the violence arose from the interaction of rumor, fear, and the breakdown of order.

Evidence: Both interpretations rely on Home Ministry and police documents, press reports, court records, testimony of survivors and perpetrators, and local research. Scholars generally agree that the rumors were false and that police, soldiers, and vigilantes took part in killings; they differ on how to weigh central direction against local initiative. The Cabinet Office's own report acknowledges the involvement of vigilantes, military personnel, and police.

Why it matters: The question affects how Japan, Korea, and China remember 1923, whether the state is held accountable, and whether the violence is regarded as a natural by-product of disaster or as a product of imperial hierarchy and institutional failure.

Be careful to distinguish debate over the degree of responsibility from any question about whether the massacres occurred.

Mass Displacement, Emergency Shelters, and Temporary Settlements

By the end of the first night, hundreds of thousands of people had fled to open spaces, and over the following days the number of refugees in the Tokyo region ran into the millions by contemporary estimates, depending on how displacement was defined. Ueno Park, Hibiya Park, the grounds of the Imperial Palace, Shiba and other parks, shrine and temple precincts, school yards, and riverbanks became crowded refuges where families camped on mats, scraps of corrugated iron, and salvaged boards. Some of the sites, such as Ueno Park, remained central refugee locations for weeks.

Authorities organized emergency shelters in the following days. Military units set up tents and barracks, the Home Ministry's relief office oversaw the construction of temporary shacks, and municipal and prefectural governments opened schools and public buildings. Registration procedures were established to record refugees and to help families find one another, and information boards and newspaper notices helped locate the missing. Railways offered free or reduced travel to those leaving the disaster area, and large numbers of refugees moved to relatives and communities elsewhere, spreading across the country. Historians of displacement point out that this dispersal made counting difficult and also diffused the disaster's social consequences far beyond the capital.

The practical difficulties were extensive. Households had lost property, identity documents, and records of land and tenancy; money was not available because banks were closed or destroyed. People slept outdoors in the first days, exposed to heat, then rain and autumn cold, and the lack of sanitation made crowded camps dangerous. Distribution of food and supplies depended on lines and lists, and those who were not registered or who lacked connections found help harder to obtain. Women, children, older people, and the sick were particularly vulnerable.

Temporary settlements evolved into semipermanent communities. In the following months, thousands of barracks and shacks were built, some by the government and charitable groups and some by residents themselves, on vacant land in the burned districts, in parks, and on the outskirts of the city. These settlements formed the first stage of the housing recovery described in Section 71.36, and some lasted for years. Shacks in the burned zone allowed residents to remain near their former workplaces, but their quality was low and tenure insecure.

The distinction between those with and without options structured experience. Wealthier families could rent houses in unaffected areas or move to relatives; the poor and the many migrants, lodgers, and colonial workers had fewer choices, and some lacked any safety net. Social class thus shaped displacement as much as geography.

Everyday Life and Environment

Everyday Life: A Displaced Household Seeking Food and Shelter

A composite reconstruction of patterns documented in diaries, relief records, and later histories. It describes no specific family.

A household from a burned district of Honjo or Kanda might have spent the first night in a park, sleeping on a mat saved from the fire, with a few possessions in a bundle. In the following days the family would have sought news of missing relatives at information boards, lined up for rice balls or rice distributed by the military or relief organizations, and carried water from distant sources. Registration meant a tag or a slip entitling the family to some food, and without a stable address, a record of registration could be hard to keep.

Within a week or two, the family might have built or been assigned a shack, found a corner in a relative's house in the unburned part of the city, or traveled by free train to a village where relatives lived. Which course they took depended on money, connections, and the household's relationship to neighbors and landlords. Children's schooling was interrupted, and the family's income depended on whether the workplace had survived. The story of such a household shows that disaster relief was not merely a matter of food and tents but of rebuilding the social ties and documents that a household needs to function.

Food, Drinking Water, Sanitation, and Public Health

The food supply was disrupted by the destruction of warehouses, the paralysis of transport, and the closure of markets. Rice stocks in the burned districts were partly destroyed, and prices rose sharply in the first days. The government responded by ordering emergency shipments of rice and other staples from other regions, by requisitioning stocks, and by using the military and the navy to transport and distribute food. Emergency regulations penalized hoarding and price gouging, and some local authorities set fixed prices. Rice balls prepared by volunteers and by military cooks became a symbol of the relief effort. Despite these measures, shortages were severe for several days, and distribution reached people unevenly.

Drinking water was a serious problem. The waterworks were damaged and, in the first days, supplies were limited; people used wells, rivers, and water carried from unaffected areas, and rumors of poisoned wells made the search for safe water anxious and, as Division IV showed, deadly. Water tankers and carts supplied some refugee camps, and the repair of mains began quickly in the less damaged districts. Sanitation was a related difficulty: latrines were lacking, waste accumulated, and corpses had to be disposed of in the heat of early September, which created both a practical and a public-health danger. Mass cremations took place at many sites, and authorities worked to clear remains from the burned districts.

Public health officials feared outbreaks of epidemic disease, particularly dysentery, typhoid, and cholera, which were familiar dangers in overcrowded and unsanitary conditions. The government ordered preventive measures such as vaccination, disinfection, and the inspection of food and water, and set up temporary medical stations and isolation facilities. Doctors, nurses, and medical students from across the country came to the disaster area, and hospitals that survived were overwhelmed with injured people. Large epidemics were avoided, a result that officials and later historians credited to preventive measures and to the autumn weather, though sporadic outbreaks of disease did occur.

The medical response also reveals the cooperation and friction among institutions. Government officials, the Red Cross, university hospitals, military medical units, private physicians, and voluntary organizations worked together, but their work was often uncoordinated and subject to competing authority. The Cabinet Office's historical review notes the importance of voluntary services in the early period and the limits of formal organization.

These aspects of relief were less visible than fire and violence but affected more people for longer. The slow work of providing water, food, and basic care determined whether the survivors of the first week would remain healthy through the autumn and winter.

Families, Children, Women, and Voluntary Relief Organizations

The disaster affected different groups differently. Children who were separated from their families in the chaos of the first day were collected at schools, police stations, and relief centers, and orphan care became an important task. Thousands of children lost one or both parents, and relief organizations established temporary homes and arranged adoptions and placements with relatives. Notices of missing children and of children seeking parents filled the information boards. The experience of loss left a long mark on a generation of Tokyo and Yokohama youth.

Women bore a disproportionate share of the work of keeping households alive in refugee conditions: cooking, caring for the sick and children, and finding food and water. Pregnant women and mothers of infants faced special dangers, and temporary maternity and infant-care services were organized. Women also played leading roles in relief organizations. Women's associations, Christian groups, and student organizations collected and distributed clothing, food, and funds, and women professionals such as nurses and doctors worked in the medical stations. Women's participation in relief reinforced arguments, discussed in Chapter 69, that women should have a political role, though their relief work was often presented in the language of traditional service.

Older people, the injured, and disabled survivors were among those most at risk. Those with injuries received treatment in crowded facilities, and some were transported to hospitals elsewhere in Japan; those with mobility limitations faced particular difficulties in fleeing fire and in obtaining food and shelter. Records on disabled survivors are scant, and historians must infer their experience from scattered references.

The Japanese Red Cross, which had a long tradition of wartime and disaster relief, organized medical teams and supply distribution, and religious organizations, including Buddhist temples, Christian missions, and the new religions, set up relief stations and provided shelter and food. Voluntary groups from across Japan sent money and goods, and neighborhood associations and companies organized assistance for their own members. Students' groups, notably from the universities, ran information bureaus, helped with distribution, and organized settlement houses for the poor. These efforts illustrate the strength of civil society developed in the Taishō period, even as other organizations of civil society were taking part in vigilante violence.

The experience of the young and the vulnerable also shaped the politics of reconstruction. The settlement houses and social-work organizations that grew out of relief work became a basis for the social policy and housing initiatives of the following years, including the Dōjunkai (Section 71.36).

Employment, Markets, Property Loss, and the Economic Aftermath

The economic damage was enormous. Much of Tokyo's commercial, banking, and manufacturing infrastructure was destroyed or disabled, including the offices of banks and trading firms, shops, warehouses, and factories in the burned districts. Yokohama's trading houses and silk warehouses were destroyed. The loss of buildings, stocks, machinery, and records was staggering, and tens of thousands of people lost their jobs and incomes. Small shopkeepers and artisans, whose capital lay in their premises and stock, were particularly hard hit, and wage earners in destroyed factories found themselves unemployed.

Financial institutions faced a crisis. Many bank buildings and records were destroyed or inaccessible, and a rush of withdrawals threatened a collapse. The government responded with an emergency imperial ordinance imposing a moratorium on the payment of debts for a period, and the Bank of Japan took extraordinary measures to support banks and provide liquidity, including by discounting 'earthquake bills' (shinsai tegata), notes issued by firms whose operations had been disrupted. These measures averted immediate panic but left a legacy of unredeemed notes and troubled financial positions, a problem that contributed to the banking difficulties of the later 1920s examined in Chapter 73. This chapter treats only the immediate disruption and the necessary connections.

Insurance posed additional problems. Fire insurance policies commonly excluded losses caused by earthquakes, and disputes arose over whether losses were due to fire or to shaking, with large claims from insured firms and ordinary householders. Insurance companies argued that they were not liable, and the government encouraged compromise settlements. The dispute highlights a general pattern: those with property and access to institutions had at least a chance of recovering some losses, while the poor had no such protection.

Markets and employment recovered unevenly. Some trades, such as construction, grew rapidly as demand for materials and labor rose, and the building boom drew workers, including laborers from the countryside and from Korea, into the city. Other sectors, particularly those dependent on the destroyed commercial districts, struggled. Prices rose, and the government attempted to control them. Many displaced businesses relocated to the unburned western and southern parts of the city or to suburbs, a trend that Section 71.37 examines.

The economic consequences of the earthquake therefore combined immediate destruction, financial improvisation, and the creation of new opportunities for some. The balance between these effects varied by class, sector, and location, and the net cost to the national economy remains a subject of estimation rather than certainty.

International Relief, Foreign Communities, and Humanitarian Diplomacy

The disaster provoked an international response. Governments, relief societies, and private citizens in many countries sent money, supplies, and personnel. The United States was among the most prominent: the American Red Cross raised funds, the U.S. Navy's Asiatic Fleet ships went to Japan to deliver supplies and assist, and American organizations and individuals contributed. Other countries, including Britain, France, China, and the countries of Latin America and Europe, also sent aid or announced contributions. In the first days, ships in Yokohama harbor, including the liner Empress of Australia, played notable roles in rescuing people from the burning waterfront.

The distinction between commitments and aid actually delivered matters. Governments and organizations announced offers of assistance, and some were declined, delayed, or redirected by the Japanese authorities, who at times preferred to emphasize self-reliance or lacked the capacity to handle large shipments. Foreign donations funded relief and, as Section 71.36 explains, the establishment of the Dōjunkai. Historians evaluating international relief must therefore compare reports of offers with records of what was shipped and used.

Foreign communities in the disaster area faced the disaster as residents. Foreigners in Yokohama and Tokyo, including diplomats, missionaries, merchants, and teachers, were injured and killed, and many were evacuated by ships. Their experiences, recorded in diaries and consular reports, document the destruction and also the response of Japanese neighbors, some of whom helped foreigners. The Chinese and Korean communities, as the previous sections show, faced violence rather than aid, a contrast that should not be obscured by emphasis on the foreign-relief narrative.

Humanitarian assistance had political implications. The sympathy expressed abroad, particularly in the United States, was widely noted in Japan and was seen by some as a basis for improved relations, which had been strained by American immigration policy and other issues. The 1924 American immigration law, which excluded Japanese immigrants, shortly afterward complicated that picture and produced a sense of betrayal in Japan, showing that gratitude for relief did not translate into policy changes. At the same time, news of the massacres damaged Japan's international image, especially in China and Korea, and the Japanese government sought to manage coverage and to limit foreign reporting.

The international dimension also shows how the disaster connected Japan to wider networks of exchange: shipping, relief societies, the press, diplomacy, and scientific communities. Foreign seismologists, engineers, and architects studied the disaster and drew lessons, and Japanese experts' work became known abroad.

Reconstruction as a National Political Question: Where Should the Capital Stand?

In the first weeks the future of the capital itself was open to question. Some officials, businessmen, and commentators proposed moving the capital to another region, such as Osaka or an inland site, arguing that Tokyo was too dangerous and that a new city could be planned from scratch. Others argued that the destruction was an opportunity to build a modern, planned capital in Tokyo or to relocate administrative functions while leaving commerce in place. The debate involved regional interests, the finances of the state, and the political symbolism of the capital.

The government settled the question quickly. The imperial rescript of September 12, 1923, issued as an imperial act during the regency (Hirohito acting for the ailing Emperor Taishō) and held by the National Archives of Japan, affirmed Tokyo's continuing role as the capital and called for a special organ to deliberate and investigate reconstruction. A Reconstruction Council followed on September 19 and the Reconstruction Agency on September 27. The rescript reflected a decision, made by the cabinet and the court, that moving the capital would be impractical and politically destabilizing and that reconstruction of Tokyo was a national responsibility. Historians treat the rescript as a political act that framed reconstruction as a task of the nation and the emperor, giving the state a central role.

The question of national priorities was also at stake. The government had to decide how much of the national budget to devote to reconstruction at a time when the economy and the national treasury were already strained, and parties and interest groups argued for different distributions: regions outside Tokyo feared that the reconstruction would draw away resources, while Tokyo interests demanded generous support. The Seiyūkai, which was not part of the cabinet, criticized the scale of Gotō's plans and the cabinet's use of emergency powers. The reconstruction thus became a field of political struggle among parties, bureaucracies, and regions, as J. Charles Schencking has emphasized in his study of reconstruction as a national project.

Public participation and sentiment also mattered. Newspapers, civic organizations, and business associations proposed plans and lobbied the government, and many ordinary people simply wanted to rebuild their homes and businesses as quickly as possible, regardless of any grand design. The tension between planners' ambitions and residents' immediate needs recurs throughout the reconstruction.

Understanding reconstruction as a national political question helps explain why it was so contested and why the outcome was a compromise. It was not merely a technical exercise in rebuilding but a decision about what kind of capital, and what kind of state, Japan should have.

Gotō Shinpei and the Imperial Capital Reconstruction Agency

The Imperial Capital Reconstruction Agency (Teito Fukkōin) was established on September 27, 1923, as confirmed by the National Diet Library. Gotō Shinpei, the home minister, was appointed its president on September 29, as the National Archives of Japan records. The creation of a special body outside the ordinary ministries reflected Gotō's belief that reconstruction required centralized authority and a comprehensive vision. The agency drew together planners, engineers, and officials, many of whom had worked with Gotō in his earlier careers in colonial Taiwan, the South Manchuria Railway, and as mayor of Tokyo.

Gotō's planning vision was expansive. He had advocated, as mayor, an extensive program of urban improvement for Tokyo, popularly called the 'eight-hundred-million-yen plan', and after the disaster he proposed to use the destruction as an opportunity to rebuild the capital on a new foundation. His proposals included a network of wide boulevards and arterial roads, new parks and open spaces, improved water and sewer systems, new bridges, a modern port, and the public purchase of burned land in order to replan it. He argued that reconstruction could create a capital worthy of an imperial power and safe from future disasters, and the scale of the proposals varied by stage. The National Archives of Japan records that Gotō's own initial proposal envisioned about ¥3 billion and that the outline plan adopted by the Agency set the budget at about ¥1.3 billion, while the Tokyo Metropolitan Government cites an initial plan of about ¥1.5 billion. These figures come from different stages and definitions and cannot be reconciled without checking each source.

Gotō's vision and style provoked opposition. His approach was seen as high-handed and as an unreasonable burden on the national treasury, and landowners objected to the government's plan to acquire land. Political opponents, notably in the Seiyūkai, argued that the plan was too grandiose and that the immediate needs of survivors should come first. Bureaucratic rivals objected to the concentration of power in the agency. The agency was criticized for relying on emergency measures and for sidestepping the Diet.

The agency's life was short. The National Archives of Japan records that the plan was cut successively by the cabinet and Ministry of Finance, by the Reconstruction Council, and by the Diet, and that the Agency itself was abolished in February 1924, its work continuing under the Home Ministry's Reconstruction Bureau. The fall of the Yamamoto cabinet after the Toranomon Incident coincided with this reorganization, and Gotō left office. The institution nonetheless carried out the planning and implementation of many of the projects that did go ahead, and the staff and methods it developed shaped urban planning for decades.

Students should distinguish between Gotō's proposals and the programs that were actually adopted. The ideas of wide roads, parks, and comprehensive planning were realized in part, but the sweeping land purchase and the scale of the original budget were not, as the next section explains. The history of the agency is a history of ambitious planning meeting political and financial constraints.

Reconstruction Budgets, Land Readjustment, and Property Rights

Money and property rights determined what could be done. The initial reconstruction plan, according to the Tokyo Metropolitan Government, called for approximately ¥1.5 billion in expenditure. In the following months, as the cabinet and the Diet reviewed the plan, it was reduced substantially; the figure cited for the reduced program is approximately ¥468 million. These numbers refer to specific stages of planning and must not be confused with the eventual total expenditure, which differed, or with other contemporary estimates that included municipal and private spending. The National Archives of Japan describes a different sequence of reductions: an Agency outline of about ¥1.3 billion; a cut to about ¥700 million when the plan was limited to the burned districts after negotiation with the Ministry of Finance; a further cut in the Reconstruction Council, where Privy Councillor Itō Miyoji opposed the plan and the Keihin canal was dropped; and a reduction of about ¥100 million in the Diet.

The reduction reflected opposition from several sources. The Diet, including both opposition parties and members of the government's own side, questioned the magnitude of the program and demanded economies. Financial officials worried about the national debt, foreign borrowing, and inflation, while regional interests resented the concentration of resources in the capital. Gotō's proposal for large-scale public acquisition of land was strongly opposed by property owners, who feared compulsory purchase at unfavorable prices and the loss of their land, and by politicians who defended property rights.

The compromise was land readjustment (kukaku seiri). Under this method, landowners in a designated area would contribute a proportion of their land to the public for roads and open spaces, and in return their remaining plots would be reorganized into regular shapes with improved access, with the value of the property supposed to increase. The method allowed the government to create streets and parks without buying the entire area. Land readjustment was applied over a large part of the burned districts, and it transformed the street pattern of areas such as Honjo and Fukagawa. Some owners benefited, but others, especially small owners and tenants, faced disputes over compensation and the reallocation of plots, and the program generated extensive litigation and conflict. Tenants, who did not own land, had little voice in the process.

Financing was provided through national government funds, bonds, and municipal contributions, and the costs were distributed among the national treasury, the city of Tokyo, and the landowners. The details of expenditure are complicated and were disputed at the time. The program's costs rose beyond initial estimates, and the final total depends on how one counts the national, municipal, and private contributions and the related projects.

The history of budgets and land readjustment shows the limits of centralized planning in a political system that protected property rights and required political compromise. It also shows that reconstruction was not a single plan but a sequence of negotiations in which the original ambition was progressively reduced.

Roads, Bridges, Parks, Firebreaks, and the Reconstruction of Infrastructure

The physical reconstruction centered on infrastructure. The most visible changes were new and widened roads. A grid of arterial roads was laid out, including Shōwa Avenue (Shōwa-dōri) and Taishō Avenue, wide thoroughfares intended to serve as both transport routes and firebreaks. Many of the narrow lanes of the old districts were replaced with straighter, wider streets, and in some areas the blocks were reorganized. These improvements were not uniformly realized: many proposed roads were narrower than planned or built partially, and some were never completed. Students should avoid claiming that every proposed boulevard was built.

Bridges across the Sumida River and the canals were rebuilt as permanent structures of steel and reinforced concrete, designed by engineers who paid attention to appearance as well as strength. Several of the bridges, including Eitai Bridge, Kiyosu Bridge, and Kototoi Bridge, became celebrated works of engineering. The reconstruction of bridges was also a response to the experience of the fires, in which bridges had served as bottlenecks and sites of death. Rivers and canals were reorganized, and some were filled in or altered in the course of road and land development.

Parks and open spaces formed a distinctive element. The planners created three large parks, among them Sumida Park, Hamachō Park, and Kinshi Park, in the eastern districts, and a network of small neighborhood parks attached to reinforced-concrete elementary schools, which were designed both as educational facilities and as fire-resistant refuges. The idea that parks and schools should serve as emergency shelters has continued in Japanese urban planning. Water and sewer systems were reconstructed, and transport networks were rebuilt and expanded, including railways and tramways.

These changes reshaped the city. The widening of roads and the introduction of open space altered the experience of the older districts, and the reconstructed roads facilitated the growth of automobile traffic. The program also displaced people. Residents who lost land or houses to roads and parks were not always compensated adequately, and the new street patterns removed some of the social and economic networks of the old neighborhoods. As Section 71.40 discusses, assessments of the reconstruction's achievements depend on whether one emphasizes the physical improvements or their social costs.

The Tokyo reconstruction, with its combination of land readjustment, wide roads, parks, and reinforced-concrete buildings, became a model discussed by planners internationally and a reference point for later disasters, including postwar rebuilding, though the lessons drawn were selective.

Section 71.35 · Division VI — Reconstruction Politics, Urban Planning, and Engineering Reform

Engineering after Catastrophe: The 1924 Building Regulations and Seismic Science

The disaster stimulated a revolution in earthquake engineering. Investigations of damage showed that buildings of different types performed very differently depending on design and construction, and engineers and architects debated how to build safely in an earthquake zone. Prominent Japanese researchers, among them Sano Toshikata, who had advanced the idea of designing buildings to resist a horizontal force proportional to their weight, and Naitō Tachū, an engineer who designed several reinforced-concrete structures and later became a leading figure in seismic design, contributed to this effort. The Tokyo Imperial University's Earthquake Research Institute, founded in 1925, institutionalized research on earthquakes.

In 1924 the government amended the structural provisions of the implementing regulations of the Urban Buildings Law, a change the Cabinet Office identifies as a landmark in the regulation of earthquake resistance. The revised regulations introduced an explicit requirement that buildings be designed to withstand a horizontal seismic force, using a seismic coefficient of 0.1 in the widely cited formulation, and established standards for materials and structure, in addition to rules on the fireproofing of buildings in urban districts. It was among the first national building codes in the world with an explicit seismic provision.

It is important to distinguish these early rules from modern seismic codes. The 1924 regulations were based on a simple static approach, in which a building was assumed to experience a constant horizontal force, and did not account for the dynamic behavior of structures, the properties of soil, or the full range of ground motions. Later earthquakes, including those of the 1940s and 1960s and the great earthquakes of 1995 and 2011, led to repeated revisions, notably in 1950, 1981, and afterward. The 1924 code was thus a beginning, not a final answer, and its coverage was limited to buildings in designated urban areas.

The engineering response also affected architecture and construction practice. Reinforced concrete gained prestige as a material for public buildings, schools, and apartments, and the experience of surviving structures reinforced its use. Architects and engineers developed new designs, and the profession of structural engineering grew. The debate between those who emphasized rigid construction and those who preferred flexible structures continued for decades.

The changes in building regulation illustrate the way in which a disaster can produce institutional learning: an event that exposed vulnerability led to rules, research institutions, and training. They also illustrate the limits of learning, since enforcement was uneven, many buildings were not covered, and the lessons were gradually forgotten and relearned in later decades.

Housing Reconstruction, Dōjunkai Apartments, and Unequal Recovery

Housing was the most immediate and the most unequal dimension of recovery. In the first stage, the government, municipalities, and charities built temporary barracks and shacks, and residents erected their own makeshift dwellings in the burned districts. These provisional houses were cheap and crowded, often lacking adequate sanitation, and many persisted for years. In the longer term, the question was how to provide permanent housing for the large population that had lost homes, many of them renters in working-class districts.

The Dōjunkai, a housing foundation, was established in 1924 as a foundation with funds drawn from donations, including contributions from abroad, to provide housing for disaster victims. It first built wooden houses and then, beginning in the mid-1920s, reinforced-concrete apartment complexes in Tokyo and Yokohama. Tokyo's official urban-planning history documents the program, including the introduction of reinforced-concrete rental housing with modern amenities such as private kitchens, flush toilets in some cases, and shared facilities. The apartments were designed for fire resistance and earthquake safety and were intended as models of modern, hygienic, communal living. Some complexes, such as those at Aoyama and Nakanogō, became well known, and their designs influenced later public housing.

The program's social effects were limited. The apartments were relatively few in number compared to the scale of need, and their rents were beyond the means of the poorest residents; the occupants were disproportionately middle-class families, white-collar workers, and some skilled workers. Residents with low incomes remained in temporary shacks or moved to cheaper areas on the outskirts. The program also introduced new patterns of domestic life, including the use of chairs and tables, apartment living, and neighborhood associations, that attracted attention and some resistance.

Land readjustment also affected housing. Owners whose plots were reduced and renters whose homes were cleared faced displacement, and those with little capital could not rebuild. Rent controls and rent-dispute mediation attempted to protect tenants, but landlords and speculators found ways to profit. The reconstruction thus produced a more modern urban environment but also changed who could afford to live where, and many working-class residents moved to the outer districts and suburbs.

The unevenness of recovery is a central theme. Middle-class and propertied households recovered more fully; poor households, renters, and migrants often did not recover their previous standard of living. Statistics on housing and income, where they exist, support this picture, though they are incomplete.

Neighbors who had known one another for years were scattered, and the neighborhood association had to be reconstituted among new residents. Some families found a place in a Dōjunkai apartment, with its modern kitchen and its rules for shared spaces; others moved to a suburb along a new railway line. For each, the return was not a restoration of the past but the creation of a new life in a transformed city.

Commercial Recovery and the Reorganization of Tokyo's Districts

Commercial recovery began quickly but unevenly. Merchants rebuilt shops on the same sites or on new ones, often in temporary wooden structures at first and then in permanent buildings. Banks reopened in temporary offices, and the major department stores, which had suffered severe damage, rebuilt in reinforced concrete and often relocated or expanded their operations. Theaters and entertainment districts, notably in Asakusa, were rebuilt, and a number of movie theaters and revue houses opened. The emphasis on fireproof building and on modern design gave the new commercial buildings a distinct appearance.

The geography of the city shifted. The earthquake destroyed the old commercial and industrial districts of the eastern lowlands and pushed investment and population toward the west and south. Many residents and businesses who lost their premises in the east moved to the Yamanote plateau and to suburbs along the railways to the west, accelerating a trend toward suburban development and the growth of commercial and entertainment centers at railway terminals, notably Shinjuku, Shibuya, and Ikebukuro. Private railway companies, which developed suburban lines and terminal department stores, benefited from this shift. The earthquake thus accelerated existing trends rather than creating them, a point that historians stress in avoiding the idea that the disaster by itself transformed the city.

Markets and distribution were reorganized. The destroyed central wholesale markets were rebuilt, and in the following years the government created a new system of central wholesale markets, with the Tsukiji market opening in the 1930s as a major outcome of the reconstruction. Transport changes, including the extension of the subway planning and the improvement of tram and bus services, also reshaped commercial geography.

This section does not repeat Chapter 70's account of consumer culture; it focuses on the physical and economic geography that the earthquake altered. The cafés, department stores, and cinemas of the late 1920s existed in a city rebuilt in new places and forms, and the modernity of 'Shōwa Tokyo' owed much to the reconstruction. At the same time, the older working-class and merchant districts of the east, though rebuilt, never recovered their earlier importance, and the social geography of the city tilted westward.

The Reconstruction of Yokohama and the Regional Comparison

Yokohama's recovery followed a different path from Tokyo's. As the port city lost its commercial center and its waterfront infrastructure, the city and the national government focused on restoring the harbor, rebuilding customs facilities and quays, and clearing the ruins. The reconstruction was financed by the national government, the city, and the prefecture, and the Reconstruction Bureau's planning extended to Yokohama, though on a smaller scale than in Tokyo. New roads, bridges, and public buildings were constructed, and the waterfront was reorganized.

One notable outcome was the creation of Yamashita Park, a waterfront park built on land reclaimed in part with rubble and debris from the destroyed city, completed around 1930. The park, together with reconstructed public buildings, gave Yokohama a new waterfront identity. The destruction also removed much of the old foreign settlement, and the reconstruction altered the urban pattern, with foreign residents' presence declining in the following years.

Economic recovery was complicated by competition from Kobe, where silk exports and trade had shifted during the period of Yokohama's paralysis. Yokohama regained much of its position as a port, and it developed as an industrial center along the coast between Yokohama and Kawasaki, with factories and shipyards. The city's population recovered and then grew as the region industrialized. The experience illustrates that port and industrial recovery depended on markets and infrastructure as much as on planning.

Comparing the two cities shows the importance of function and form. Tokyo's reconstruction was conceived as the rebuilding of an imperial capital and involved national political stakes; Yokohama's was the rebuilding of a commercial port, and its reconstruction was governed by trade and local government. Neighboring communities along Sagami Bay, such as Odawara and Kamakura, rebuilt with much less national attention, relying on prefectural and local resources and on private effort. The regional comparison reminds students that the 1923 disaster was a regional event and that its recovery was equally varied.

The reconstruction of Yokohama and of smaller communities also shows how recovery depended on the survival of local institutions, such as chambers of commerce, municipal governments, and religious and civic associations, and on the capacity of communities to organize and make claims on the state.

Memorials, Commemoration, and the Contested Memory of 1923

Commemoration took several forms. The site of the Honjo disaster at Yokoamichō became the location of a memorial hall and a park, with a memorial for the victims of the earthquake and fire, completed and consecrated around 1930 and associated with the ceremonies marking the completion of reconstruction. Annual memorial services were held on September 1, and in 1960 the government established September 1 as Disaster Prevention Day, a national occasion for drills and education. Museums and exhibition spaces, including the reconstruction memorial exhibits associated with the site, collected photographs, objects, and documents, and official histories, such as the Metropolitan Police's volume and later government reports, presented narratives of the disaster and recovery.

These forms of commemoration emphasized natural catastrophe, national mourning, and recovery. The narrative of resilience, in which the people of Tokyo and Yokohama, supported by the state and the emperor, overcame disaster and rebuilt a better city, was central to the ceremonies of 1930. That narrative has real foundations in the documented acts of mutual aid and in the physical reconstruction, but it also tended to omit or marginalize the massacres and political killings. For decades, the victims of those events were largely absent from official commemoration.

Koreans, Chinese, and some Japanese citizens worked to preserve the memory of the massacre victims. Korean organizations in Japan and Korea held memorial services; Japanese citizens' groups, scholars, and activists, beginning especially in the postwar decades, researched the events, collected testimony, and erected memorials. A monument to Korean victims of the earthquake was placed in Yokoamichō Park in 1973, and annual memorial ceremonies have been held there. Researchers such as Jinhee Lee have analyzed the politics of commemoration, including disputes over wording, location, and official recognition. In recent years, controversies over whether public officials should send messages to the memorial ceremonies, and over the characterization of the events, have shown that the memory of 1923 remains politically contested.

The role of documentation is central. The records of Korean and Chinese communities, the research of Japanese historians, the Cabinet Office's acknowledgment in its disaster-history report, and the testimony of survivors form a body of evidence that has gradually entered the public record. Historians must also be alert to attempts to deny or minimize the massacre by emphasizing the uncertainty of numbers or the existence of some prosecutions. The evidence demonstrates that the killings occurred, and uncertainty about the total does not alter that fact.

Cultural representations also shaped memory. Writers, artists, and filmmakers responded to the disaster in diaries, essays, fiction, painting, and photography, some celebrating resilience, others lamenting loss, and some, in later decades, confronting the violence. The novelist Tanizaki Jun'ichirō, for example, moved from the Tokyo region to the Kansai after the earthquake, a move often discussed in literary history as having shaped his later work, though the full treatment of cultural responses belongs to Chapter 72.

Continuity and Change Over Time: Tokyo and Yokohama Before 1923 and After Reconstruction

What changed?

  • Street patterns in large parts of the eastern districts were rebuilt through land readjustment, with wider roads and new bridges.
  • Reinforced-concrete buildings, apartments, and schools became more common, and the 1924 building regulations introduced a seismic design requirement.
  • Parks and open spaces were created as refuges and public amenities.
  • Population and commerce shifted westward and toward suburban railway terminals.
  • Yokohama's waterfront and foreign settlement were reorganized.

What remained continuous?

  • Large areas of dense wooden housing persisted, particularly in poorer districts, so that fire risk remained.
  • Tokyo's role as the political and economic center was reaffirmed.
  • Social inequality in housing, property, and access to relief continued, and colonial and racial hierarchies were not dismantled.
  • Police and military institutions that had acted in 1923 remained largely unreformed.

Who experienced change most directly? Property owners in readjusted districts, residents displaced by new roads and parks, and the middle-class families who moved into new apartments and suburbs. Who experienced greater continuity? Poor renters in unrebuilt areas, colonial and migrant workers who faced continued discrimination, and communities whose victims received no official recognition. Why the pattern matters: It shows that rebuilding the physical city did not automatically rebuild social equality or public trust.

Natural Disaster, Social Catastrophe, and the Politics of Reconstruction: Historical Debate

Historians have interpreted the earthquake in several overlapping ways, and the strongest accounts combine them. As a seismic event, it demonstrates the hazards of a densely settled tectonic region and the role of scientific knowledge. As an urban catastrophe, it reveals how building materials, density, infrastructure, and emergency capacity determined mortality, and why fire rather than shaking dominated the toll. As a state-building opportunity, it enabled the government to expand its administrative reach, to plan the capital on a large scale, and to centralize authority, a theme developed by J. Charles Schencking, who emphasizes both the ambition of reconstruction and the 'chimera' of a national reconstruction that fell short of its promise.

Schencking's study is the central interpretive account of reconstruction as a contested national project: it follows how bureaucrats, politicians, business interests, and residents advanced competing visions and how those visions narrowed in practice. As a racial massacre, the earthquake exposed the violence latent in the imperial order and the readiness of institutions and neighbors to turn colonial prejudice into killing, a perspective developed in scholarship on the Korean and Chinese victims, including the work of Jinhee Lee, Jiaying Shen, and Byung Wook Jung, and in Japanese research. As a moment of contested imperial authority, it shows the ways in which the state used emergency powers and imperial symbols to restore order, and the ways in which the elite, the military, and the police acted with considerable autonomy. The disaster also became a moment in which the left, the labor movement, and civil society confronted the state's repressive capacity, as Chapter 69 anticipated.

Jinhee Lee's work approaches the disaster from the other direction, analyzing how the killings of Koreans were remembered, politicized, and commemorated, and why that memory remains disputed. Interpretations differ on the relationship of these dimensions. A narrative of national resilience emphasizes mutual aid, the speed of recovery, and the achievements of reconstruction; a narrative emphasizing inequality and violence stresses the persecution of minorities, the failure of accountability, and the unequal distribution of reconstruction's benefits. Neither alone is adequate. The first risks erasing the massacres and the social costs of reconstruction; the second risks overlooking the real achievements of relief, planning, and engineering and the many people who resisted the violence or protected victims.

Historians also warn against two teleologies. The earthquake was not simply a natural disaster that interrupted a trajectory of modernization, nor was it an inevitable step toward militarism and the repression of the 1930s. The decisions of 1923, including the formation of the cabinet, the use of martial law, the response to rumors, and the reconstruction plans, were made by particular people under particular conditions and might have been made differently. Likewise, the violence of September 1923 should not be treated as a mere prelude to later atrocities, or as isolated from the continuing history of colonial rule, but as an event with its own causes and consequences, connected to both earlier and later developments.

The debate over reconstruction asks whether the program should be judged by its achievements or by the gap between its ambitions and its results.

Historical Debate: Reconstruction Achievement Versus Unrealized Ambition

The question: Should the reconstruction of Tokyo be understood as a major success of modern urban planning or as a largely unrealized program that left many problems unsolved?

Interpretation A: Some historians emphasize the achievements: the creation of a modern street network, bridges, parks, reinforced-concrete schools and apartments, new building regulations, and institutions of planning, which made Tokyo a more resilient and functional capital and provided models for later planning.
Interpretation B: Others emphasize the limits: Gotō's comprehensive vision was cut back by political and financial constraints; large areas, especially in the poorer districts, remained crowded and vulnerable; the housing program served the middle class more than the poor; and the costs fell unequally. Schencking's study of the 'chimera' of national reconstruction takes this view.

Evidence: Both interpretations rely on plans, budgets, land-readjustment records, maps, housing statistics, and later surveys of urban conditions. They differ in whether they compare outcomes to the pre-1923 city or to the proposed programs, and in whose experiences they treat as representative.

Why it matters: The answer shapes how the disaster is remembered, what lessons are drawn for future disaster planning, and whether state-led planning is seen as effective or constrained.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the Great Kantō Earthquake of September 1, 1923, as a seismic event, an urban fire catastrophe, a humanitarian emergency, a crisis of government, an episode of racial and political violence, and a turning point in urban reconstruction. Its central argument is that natural hazards do not determine social outcomes. Building design, urban density, emergency institutions, colonial prejudice, government decisions, and unequal access to relief shaped who survived and how the cities were rebuilt.

The physical setting was a rapidly modernized metropolitan region whose growth had produced both opportunity and vulnerability. Tokyo's eastern lowlands and Yokohama's waterfront lay on soft or filled ground, and the older districts were packed with wooden housing, narrow lanes, and storage of flammable goods. The earthquake, with a magnitude of approximately 7.9 and a hypocenter in western Kanagawa at a depth of about 23 kilometers, was generated on the Sagami Trough. Shaking destroyed buildings, but it was the fires that followed, driven by wind, fed by cooking flames and crowded streets, and unchecked by failed water mains, that caused the great majority of deaths. The modern official estimate of approximately 105,000 dead and missing is a reconstruction, and older totals differ because of different counting methods.

The geography of destruction was varied. Tokyo's eastern districts and old commercial core burned; roughly 38,000 people died at the former army clothing depot in Honjo, a closed site used as an evacuation area, when a fire whirl swept across the crowd and its belongings. Yokohama, closer to the source, suffered massive structural collapse followed by fire. The Sagami Bay coast was struck by tsunami and by uplift and subsidence of the land, and the mountains of western Kanagawa experienced landslides, including the Nebukawa disaster. The catastrophe was therefore regional, and a Tokyo-centered narrative is insufficient.

The government was unprepared. Prime Minister Katō Tomosaburō had died on August 24, and the second Yamamoto cabinet, with Gotō Shinpei as home minister, was formed on September 2. Communications and transport collapsed, leaving officials uncertain of the scale of destruction. Emergency martial-law provisions were applied to the disaster area beginning September 2, giving military authorities extraordinary powers. Neighbors and volunteers carried out much of the early rescue and relief, though the later image of universal solidarity obscures its limits and its coexistence with persecution.

In this environment, false rumors that Koreans were committing arson, poisoning wells, and plotting rebellion spread rapidly. They had no factual basis, but they found credence among people shaped by colonial hierarchy, anxiety after the March First Movement, and stereotypes of Koreans. Official telegrams and police communications amplified them in some places, while other officials tried to stop them. Neighborhood vigilante groups (jikeidan) established roadblocks and identity checks, and vigilantes, police, and soldiers killed Koreans in Tokyo, Kanagawa, Saitama, Chiba, and elsewhere. Chinese workers, particularly in Ōshima-machi, were also killed, and Wang Xitian was murdered, producing diplomatic consequences. Some Japanese people, identified by dialect or disability as outsiders, were attacked as well, as in the Fukuda village incident.

Political violence against Japanese dissidents formed distinct episodes. At Kameido, soldiers killed about ten labor activists in early September, and on September 16 military police captain Amakasu Masahiko and others murdered the anarchist Ōsugi Sakae, the writer and anarchist Itō Noe, and the child Tachibana Munekazu. Amakasu was convicted by a military court and served a relatively short sentence. Accountability for the massacres was limited: prosecutions were few and sentences light, and convictions cannot serve as a count of the dead. Estimates of Korean victims range from the hundreds to approximately six thousand, depending on the source, and no universally accepted definitive count exists, but the occurrence of the massacres is firmly established.

The humanitarian emergency affected millions. Refugees crowded parks and shelters, food, water, and sanitation were scarce, and relief depended on the military, the Red Cross, voluntary organizations, and neighbors. Children, women, the elderly, and the disabled faced specific hardships. Financial institutions were disrupted, and emergency ordinances and moratoria prevented immediate collapse but left problems for the later 1920s. International relief arrived from many countries, though offers and deliveries differed, and foreign and minority communities experienced the disaster in different ways.

Reconstruction began with the September 12 rescript affirming Tokyo as the capital and the creation, on September 27, of the Imperial Capital Reconstruction Agency under Gotō Shinpei. Gotō's ambitious plans, with an initial program of about ¥1.5 billion, were reduced to about ¥468 million at a later stage of planning, and property owners' resistance led to the use of land readjustment rather than wholesale public purchase. The program nevertheless produced wide avenues, new bridges, parks, reinforced-concrete schools, and the 1924 amendments to building regulations that introduced an explicit seismic requirement. The Dōjunkai built reinforced-concrete apartments, but housing recovery was unequal, and Yokohama's reconstruction followed a distinct, port-centered path.

Memory of 1923 has been contested. Official commemoration emphasized natural catastrophe and national recovery, while Koreans, Chinese, and Japanese citizens and scholars worked to document and commemorate the massacre victims. Historians interpret the earthquake as a seismic event, an urban catastrophe, a state-building opportunity, a racial massacre, and a moment of contested imperial authority, and they caution against treating it as either a purely natural accident or an inevitable step toward militarism.

The chapter concludes that the earthquake revealed both the capacities and the contradictions of modern Japan: extraordinary public and private relief efforts, but also lethal imperial prejudice, failures of state accountability, and a contested project of national reconstruction. It sets up the examination of cultural experimentation in Chapter 72, financial and rural crisis in Chapter 73, and the collapse of parliamentary restraint in Chapter 74.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a natural event becomes a historical one. The earthquake's physical force was extraordinary, but its human consequences were shaped by decisions about how cities were built, how emergencies were governed, and whose lives were protected. The fires that killed most victims, the failure of communications, and the shortage of water were conditions of urban modernity as much as of nature, and the reconstruction that followed treated them as problems that could be addressed by planning, engineering, and law.

The chapter also shows how crisis can reveal the structure of an imperial society. The massacres of Koreans and Chinese, and the killings of labor activists and anarchists, were not random outbursts. They drew on colonial hierarchy, on a police and military establishment with substantial autonomy, and on neighborhood institutions that could be turned toward persecution. At the same time, the documented acts of protection, the work of those who investigated and recorded the killings, and the later commemoration by Korean, Chinese, and Japanese communities demonstrate that other choices were available and that memory depends on sustained effort.

The developments of 1923 shaped later history in several ways. The reconstructed city, with its wide avenues, parks, and reinforced-concrete buildings, provided the physical setting for the mass culture and politics of the late 1920s and 1930s. The building regulations and seismic research became foundations of Japanese disaster engineering. The emergency powers and the habits of military and police autonomy left a legacy that Chapters 73 and 74 will show in new crises. And the unresolved questions of accountability and memory carried forward into the postwar period and into present debates over how Japan remembers its imperial past.

Finally, the chapter connects regions and peoples that a Tokyo-centered story would separate: Yokohama and Sagami Bay, mountain villages and the capital, Korean and Chinese migrants and Japanese neighbors, and Japan and a world that sent relief and also watched the violence. Students who understand 1923 as both a disaster and a test of social and political choices are prepared to approach the cultural experimentation of Chapter 72 and the crises of Chapters 73 and 74 without assuming that the outcome was fixed.
