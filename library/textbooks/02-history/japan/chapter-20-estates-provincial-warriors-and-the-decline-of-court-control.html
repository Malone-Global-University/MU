Part III — Nara and Heian Japan
20

Estates, Provincial Warriors, and the Decline of Court Control

Japanese History: From Jōmon Japan to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

The poems, diaries, and tales of the Heian capital describe a small world of curtained rooms, court ceremonies, and refined feeling. The rice, silk, oil, paper, and labor that sustained that world were produced far away, by people who almost never appear in it, and were delivered to Kyoto through arrangements that were changing during the very decades the great court authors were writing.

Consider a detail from the life of a woman whom Chapter 19 introduced as an author. Murasaki Shikibu's father, Fujiwara no Tametoki, was not a regent or a minister. He belonged to the middle ranks of the aristocracy, and in the 990s he was appointed governor of Echizen, a province on the Japan Sea coast, far from the capital. According to the traditional biographical account, his daughter accompanied him there for a time. Sei Shōnagon's father, Kiyohara no Motosuke, likewise held provincial governorships. The two most celebrated women authors of the Heian court were, in other words, daughters of men whose careers depended on managing provinces. Their fathers' postings were also, in effect, the means by which provincial wealth was converted into aristocratic income, court service, and the leisure that made literature possible.

This chapter asks how that conversion worked, and why it changed. The classical ideal established by the Ritsuryō state (Chapters 13 and 14) imagined a government that registered every household, allotted fields to cultivators, taxed persons and their production according to uniform rules, and administered provinces through officials sent from the capital. By the tenth century very little of that ideal operated as its designers had intended. Taxes increasingly attached to parcels of land and to the prominent cultivators who managed them. Provincial governors were held to fixed remittances and were left to devise their own methods of collecting them. Estates, called shōen, accumulated rights and privileges under the protection of aristocrats, temples, shrines, and eventually retired emperors. Where such arrangements produced disputes, someone had to enforce claims, guard property, pursue offenders, and, when necessary, fight. Men with military skill became steadily more valuable to governors, estate managers, aristocratic patrons, and religious institutions.

The historical problem is easily misdescribed. It is tempting to say that the imperial court "declined" while the "samurai rose," as though two rival systems were exchanging places. The evidence does not support so simple a story. The court continued to grant titles, ranks, and offices; to confirm or reject claims to land; and to serve as the ultimate source of legitimacy for nearly everyone who held power. Estate owners, provincial governors, local magnates, temple leaders, and military households all operated within a world of court rank and documents, and they generally sought to strengthen their positions by obtaining court recognition rather than by rejecting it. What changed was that the court no longer monopolized land revenues, provincial administration, or the means of coercion. Authority became layered: divided among many holders, each dependent on the others, and all still oriented toward Kyoto.

Many kinds of people appear in this chapter: cultivators whose obligations depended on which authority controlled their fields; local managers who negotiated with governors and estate proprietors; governors who sought profit and faced accusations of extortion; monks and temple dependents; aristocratic women who held estate rights; retired emperors who built new centers of household government; and armed families whose service linked the provinces to the capital. Attention will also turn to regions that Kyoto-centered sources treat as peripheries. Kantō, Kyushu, the northeast (Tōhoku), the northern islands beyond it, and the southwestern island chain all shaped, and were shaped by, the transformations traced here.

The evidence is uneven. Court diaries, legal codes, and collections of documents preserve the perspective of aristocrats and institutions, while cultivators appear mainly in petitions, contracts, and registers drawn up by others. Temple archives, such as those of Tōdaiji, Tōji, and Kōyasan, preserved estate documents unusually well, so historians know more about estates held by religious institutions than about those of aristocrats whose records were lost. Narrative accounts of tenth- and eleventh-century warfare, such as the Shōmonki and the Mutsuwaki, were written after or around the events and must be read as literary works as well as records. Historians therefore reconstruct the period by combining textual and material evidence and by noting carefully who produced each source and why.

The chapter has a defined place in the textbook. Chapters 18 and 19 examined how the Heian court exercised power and produced its culture. Chapter 20 explains the changes in land, revenue, provincial administration, religious institutions, and coercive power beneath that court. It does not narrate the mature warrior class, the practice of mounted archery, or the warrior household in detail; those subjects belong to Chapter 21. It also stops short of the Hōgen and Heiji conflicts and the rise of Taira no Kiyomori, which are the subject of Chapter 22. The aim here is to explain why the rise of warriors became possible without pretending that aristocratic Japan simply ended.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why "decline of court control" does not mean the disappearance of government and describe what the court continued to control.
  2. Trace the movement from person-based Ritsuryō taxation toward land-based assessment and rule through local intermediaries.
  3. Define the shōen and explain why it cannot be described simply as private property or as an independent domain.
  4. Distinguish provincial governors, absentee governors, zuryō, deputies, local officials, and local magnates, and explain their overlapping roles.
  5. Analyze the roles of temples and shrines as landholders, employers, litigants, and political actors, and assess the term sōhei critically.
  6. Explain how tenth- and eleventh-century provincial violence, including the Tengyō disturbances, exposed the limits of court coercion.
  7. Assess how Minamoto and Taira lineages gained standing through service to aristocratic and imperial patrons rather than through opposition to the court.
  8. Evaluate Go-Sanjō's estate reforms and explain how retired-emperor government (insei) created a new layer of authority.
  9. Connect mappō thought, Pure Land devotion, and other religious developments to the social and political conditions of the late Heian world.
  10. Interpret primary and secondary evidence for estates and provincial administration, noting whose perspectives it represents and omits.
  11. Contextualize Kantō, Kyushu, Tōhoku, and the northern and southwestern frontiers within the wider transformation of Heian authority.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How did the Heian court lose direct control over land, taxation, and provincial coercion while remaining the principal source of legitimacy, office, and elite status?
  2. In what ways did the court's own practices, including appointments, exemptions, and confirmations of rights, help create the estate system that reduced its direct control?
  3. Why did rule through intermediaries appeal to both the central government and provincial elites, and what problems did it create?
  4. How did different people, including aristocratic proprietors, governors, local managers, temples, and cultivators, experience the same overlapping claims to land and revenue?
  5. Why is it misleading to picture religious institutions in this period as either purely spiritual bodies or armies of professional "warrior monks"?
  6. What does the career of Taira no Masakado suggest about the relationship between provincial violence and the court?
  7. How did retired-emperor government change the structure of Heian politics without restoring an older centralized monarchy?
  8. What kinds of sources allow historians to study estates and provincial society, and what voices do those sources leave out?

Historical Context

Chapters 13 and 14 described the Ritsuryō state, which borrowed elements of Chinese administrative law to build a government of ranked officials, provincial governors, district (gun) magistrates, household registers, equal-field allotments, and standardized taxes and labor obligations. The state supported the capital, the court, and the great temples, and it mobilized labor and soldiers, through this system. Chapter 16 showed that its reach was always uneven. District magistrates were usually drawn from local elite families whose authority predated the state, and the northeastern frontier, where the Emishi communities lived, required special arrangements for warfare, diplomacy, and settlement.

When the capital moved to Heian-kyō (Chapter 17) and Fujiwara regents came to dominate the court (Chapter 18), the formal offices of the Ritsuryō system survived, but the court's daily politics shifted toward ceremony, appointments, marriage alliances, and the management of aristocratic households and their incomes. Chapter 19 showed the cultural achievements of that world. Here it matters chiefly that the court's members needed reliable revenue and that they obtained it more and more through arrangements other than the old system of uniform taxation. The period examined in this chapter runs broadly from about 900 to the eve of the Hōgen conflict in 1156, with earlier precedents mentioned when they help explain later developments.

Two cautions frame the whole chapter. The first concerns periodization. Historians often describe the tenth through twelfth centuries as a period of transition from the "ancient" Ritsuryō order to the "medieval" order of warrior government, but people living through these centuries did not know they were in a transition. They experienced concrete disputes over harvests, appointments, boundaries, and obedience. The second concerns geography. Kyoto documents dominate the record, but the events discussed here took place in valleys, coastal plains, mountain temples, and frontier districts across the archipelago, each with its own conditions.

Section 20.1

Beyond the Brilliant Court: The Problem of Provincial Control

The literature of Heian-kyō presents a tiny world. The people who wrote and read the great poetic anthologies, diaries, and tales numbered at most a few thousand, and their attention rarely left the capital, its mansions, temples, and ceremonies. Yet every aristocratic household needed food, cloth, fuel, servants, horses, building materials, and the means to support ritual and ceremony. Every temple needed lamps, offerings, and the upkeep of its buildings. The court itself required stipends for officials, funds for festivals, and payments to guards and artisans. All of this had to be supplied from land and labor outside the capital. To understand the Heian court it is therefore necessary to ask how wealth reached it.

Under the Ritsuryō ideal, the answer was clear. The state registered households, allotted rice fields, and collected grain, cloth, local products, and labor from the registered population according to the same rules across the provinces. Governors sent from the capital supervised district magistrates, and the government's officials, guards, and temples drew their support from the resulting revenue. The reality was already more complicated by the eighth century, and by the tenth century the model had been substantially replaced. Some income still reached the capital through provincial governors, who delivered goods to the court. Other income flowed directly from estates to aristocratic families, temples, shrines, and imperial households that held rights to it. Some was assigned to individuals or institutions as a source of support. The mechanisms varied, and they often overlapped.

This chapter's title uses the phrase "decline of court control," and the phrase requires care. It does not mean that government disappeared, that the court became irrelevant, or that a fully formed feudal order replaced Ritsuryō rule. It means that the court gradually lost its monopoly over three kinds of resources. The first was land and revenue: more income was diverted into estates, and rights over the same land were divided among several holders. The second was provincial administration: the court governed the provinces increasingly through intermediaries, including governors who contracted for quotas and local elites who managed production and collection. The third was coercion: police and military functions were taken up by governors' followers, estate officials, aristocratic retainers, and religious institutions, and were no longer the responsibility of a centrally mobilized army.

What the court retained is equally important. It remained the source of rank and office, the authority to which petitions were addressed, the body that issued or confirmed exemptions, and the center around which aristocratic and religious life turned. The chapter therefore uses the phrase layered authority for the resulting arrangement. Emperors, regents, retired emperors, temples, governors, estate managers, and local magnates each held a portion of the resources and powers of government, and most of them depended on the others for legitimacy. This is a more useful description than either a strong centralized state or a total collapse.

The regions that supplied this wealth differed. Provinces close to the capital in the Kinai and the Inland Sea coast were tightly linked to Kyoto by water and road. The Kantō and the northeast, where horses were bred and frontier conflicts occurred, had more distant and sometimes tense relationships with the court. Kyushu was overseen by the regional headquarters at Dazaifu and faced the continent. Beyond Tōhoku lay Hokkaido, where communities such as the Satsumon and Okhotsk cultures maintained their own networks of exchange, and to the southwest lay islands with their own developing societies. The Heian court's authority extended differently into each of these places, and those differences are part of the story.

Section 20.2

From Ritsuryō Taxation to a New Rural Order

The Ritsuryō tax system assumed that the state knew who lived where. Household registers recorded members by age and sex, and the state allotted rice fields (handen) to registered persons and then levied a grain tax (so), local products or cloth (chō and yō), and labor service on the population. The system required frequent, accurate registration and a strong ability to reallocate land and enforce compliance. From an early date it faced serious problems. Households falsified their registers, for example by listing men as women, who owed lighter obligations. Heavy burdens encouraged people to abandon their registered homes. Repeated attempts to allocate land also proved hard to carry out, and by the ninth century the periodic redistribution of fields was increasingly ineffective. The latest surviving household registers date from the early tenth century.

The state also altered the land regime through policies intended to encourage reclamation. An edict of 723 granted rights to newly cultivated fields for a limited period, while the 743 Konden Einen Shizai Hō permitted permanent private possession of newly reclaimed fields, within acreage limits determined by status and under administrative rules. These policies expanded cultivation and created durable private claims that became part of the prehistory of the estate, but they did not by themselves abolish taxation or public regulation. Large tracts were also assigned to temples, shrines, and members of the aristocracy as sources of income, so that much of the land was never simply "public" in an unmixed sense.

By the late ninth and tenth centuries, the court had largely abandoned the goal of registering and taxing individuals and moved toward assessing land units and the people who took responsibility for them. Provincial authorities divided paddy land into units commonly called myō and assigned each to a named person, a fumyō, who was responsible for the associated rice tax (kanmotsu) and for the various supplementary levies and labor demands (zōyaku). The cultivators who took on such responsibility included many who worked land under short, renewable arrangements with the provincial government and are often called tato. In practice a single person might combine several roles, and the details varied by province and period. The court also set fixed quotas for the goods each province should deliver, leaving the governor to decide how to collect them. Official copper coinage, which the court had issued since the eighth century, ceased to be produced after the mid-tenth century, and rice, silk, cloth, and other commodities served as common media of exchange until imported Chinese coins became prominent later.

Historical Vocabulary
Tato and Fumyō

Tato (田堵) refers to cultivators, especially in the tenth and eleventh centuries, who contracted to farm paddy land held by the provincial government or by other proprietors, typically under arrangements that had to be renewed. Fumyō (負名) refers to the person made responsible for the tax obligations attached to a land unit, or myō. The two terms often describe overlapping people, and scholars use compound forms such as tato-fumyō. Some tato were small farmers; others managed large holdings, employed labor, and became wealthy. A mid-eleventh-century satirical writing, the Shin sarugakuki, is often cited for its portrait of a rich, large-scale cultivator, but the text is a literary composition by an aristocratic author and should not be treated as a neutral record.

The terms matter because they describe how the state dealt with rural society after direct administration of households weakened. They should not be read as a proto-samurai class. Most tato were cultivators and managers, and only some of their descendants took up military roles. A later expression such as "developer-lord" appears in the historiography, but its use has been debated because it can imply a unified rural warrior elite where the evidence shows varied local situations.

The consequence was causal and profound. The capital came to govern provinces through intermediaries: governors who delivered a sum, and local cultivators and managers who could ensure that production and payments occurred. This arrangement had advantages. It was less costly than maintaining a thorough system of registration, and it made use of local knowledge. Yet it also gave local people leverage. A governor who needed a tato to organize rice deliveries had to bargain, and a tato who could obtain a powerful patron might gain protection from provincial demands. Those incentives drove the growth of the estates described next.

Section 20.3

What Was a Shōen?

The word shōen (荘園) is usually translated as "estate" or "manor," but neither English word fits well. A shōen was not a single compact farm, a private kingdom, or a landlord's freehold in a modern sense. It was a set of lands, often scattered in several places, together with the rights to income from them and the obligations attached to them, held within a structure of proprietors and local managers. The term covered arrangements that changed enormously between the eighth century and the twelfth.

Early estates, in the Nara period and the early Heian centuries, were often temple or aristocratic holdings established by reclamation or purchase. Many of them were initially subject to ordinary taxation and were worked by rented labor or through contracts with local cultivators. What made later estates distinctive was the accumulation of privileges. Proprietors obtained documents from the court or from provincial offices that exempted their lands from certain taxes (fuyu) and, in some cases, barred provincial officials from entering to survey or collect (fu-nyū). Exemptions could be granted at different levels: by the Council of State and the ministry responsible for taxation, or by provincial governors acting on their own authority. These privileges were the result of negotiation, favor, and litigation, and they varied greatly. One estate might be fully exempt while another paid some dues to the province.

Estates commonly came into being in several ways. Local cultivators or officials who had reclaimed or accumulated land might offer it to a powerful patron, a process often called commendation, in order to secure protection from provincial demands. A patron might purchase or receive land from other holders. Temples and shrines might receive land as donations. In the twelfth century in particular, many estates were formally established (risshō) by decisions of retired emperors and other high-ranking figures, which shows that estate creation was often an act of high politics and not only a rural development from below.

A typical estate involved several layers of people. At the top stood the patron or proprietor, often called the honke or ryōke in later terminology, such as a noble family, a temple, a shrine, or a member of the imperial family. This person or institution received income but rarely visited. Local managers, including officers termed gesu and kumon and stewards sometimes called azukaridokoro, handled day-to-day administration, collected dues, and kept records. Cultivators worked the land and delivered rice, cloth, oil, or other goods, as well as labor service. Each of these holders possessed a shiki, a hereditary right and duty attached to a role in the estate, so that several people could hold rights over the income of the same land. To call any one of them "the owner" simplifies a system of divided claims.

It is equally important not to overstate how much land became estate land. Historians today generally emphasize that provincial "public" lands (kokugaryō), administered by the provincial government, continued to exist throughout this period and coexisted with estates, and that the proportion of estates varied across regions and periods. The overall pattern is often described as a system in which estates and public lands existed side by side. Estates were also seldom independent. Proprietors depended on court recognition, so they cultivated relationships with the central authorities. They could also suffer challenges from provincial governors, rival claimants, or reform edicts. The court itself issued edicts, including one in 1045, attempting to restrict newly established estates, and these efforts show both the seriousness of the problem and the court's limited ability to solve it.

Historical Vocabulary
Shōen (estate)

A shōen was a body of land and associated rights and obligations, usually held by a proprietor who received income while local managers and cultivators worked the land. It often carried privileges such as tax exemption or protection from entry by provincial officials, though these differed from estate to estate and had to be maintained through documents and continuing recognition.

The modern English word "manor" evokes a European lord's demesne and the phrase "private property" implies a single owner with exclusive rights; neither fits. A shōen consisted of layered shiki, or hereditary rights, that were divisible and subject to recognition by the court and other institutions. The term is essential to this chapter because the estate system is the main mechanism through which authority was redistributed without an open break in the legal order.

Everyday Life and Environment

Life on an Estate

Note: this is an explicitly composite reconstruction of a type of situation, based on patterns in surviving estate documents and secondary scholarship. It does not describe any named person or single estate, and details would have varied greatly by region and decade.

Picture a cultivator in a mid-eleventh-century estate, perhaps in the Kinai or the Inland Sea region. The family works several plots of irrigated paddy, some perhaps held under an arrangement with a local manager and others under a different claim. Rice is the principal crop, but households would also grow barley, millet, beans, or vegetables on drier plots, cultivate hemp or mulberry for cloth, and depend on nearby mountains and streams for fuel, wood, and fish. Water management is a collective concern: channels, ponds, and embankments must be repaired, and quarrels over water were a familiar source of conflict.

The household's obligations arrive in several forms. Annual dues (nengu), commonly in rice and sometimes in cloth, silk, oil, or paper, go to the estate's proprietor through the manager. Miscellaneous levies and labor service (kuji and buyaku) support the proprietor's household or temple. If the estate lacks full exemption, the provincial government may also assert claims on the land, and governors' deputies might appear seeking additional goods or labor. A cultivator could therefore face demands from more than one authority, and the outcome of a dispute could depend on documents held in Kyoto that the cultivator would never see.

The manager who deals with the household is often a local person who also has ties to the province's government, and who may keep armed followers to protect the harvest and enforce collection. Cultivators may look to such a person for protection but also fear his demands. Women appear in the record as workers in fields and in weaving, and also, among elites, as holders of estate rights. Bad years brought hardship: crop failure, flood, or epidemic could make dues impossible to pay, and the epidemics recorded in the 990s show how disease could ravage both capital and countryside. Religious life was woven into the estate, with a nearby shrine or temple that might itself be the proprietor, and seasonal festivals that combined ritual, obligation, and community identity.

What such a scene reveals is the insecurity of overlapping jurisdictions. A cultivator was subject to a proprietor, a manager, a provincial official, and perhaps a patron's dependent, all claiming a share. The reality of the period was not a single tyrant or a single liberator but a patchwork of claims in which people sought protection, bargained, petitioned, and sometimes fled.

Section 20.4

Provincial Governors, Zuryō, and Local Magnates

Estates did not replace provincial government, and it is a serious mistake to imagine that they did. Provincial governorships remained among the most valued appointments in aristocratic careers precisely because of the revenue attached to them. From the ninth and tenth centuries, the court increasingly held the governor personally responsible for delivering a fixed quota of goods to the central government and allowed him to retain what remained. A governor in this position is called a zuryō. The arrangement gave governors strong incentives to collect efficiently and, when they were unscrupulous, to extort. It also made appointments intensely competitive, and the court came to sell or reward offices through mechanisms such as jōgō, in which a person who paid for a construction project, such as repairing a palace hall, could receive an appointment or reappointment.

Historical Vocabulary
Zuryō

The term zuryō (受領) designates the senior provincial official who took over responsibility for a province's obligations to the central government and who, in return, exercised broad discretion in collecting them. Not every governor was a zuryō in this sense. Some governors held the title but did not go to the province, a practice called yōnin (absentee governorship), and often sent a deputy called a mokudai. Officials who were actually resident in the province worked alongside local staff at the provincial office (kokuga), many of them members of local families.

The term is central to this chapter because it shows how the court delegated. It also alerts students to a social group: zuryō came largely from the middle ranks of the aristocracy, the same social stratum as the fathers of Murasaki Shikibu and Sei Shōnagon discussed in Chapter 19. Their wealth and their dependence on court patronage made them a major force in Heian politics and culture.

Provincial offices were staffed not only by officials sent from Kyoto but by local men, often called zaichō kanjin, drawn from families long prominent in the district. Many descended from the district magistrates of the Ritsuryō period, and offices tended to become hereditary. These local elites knew the land, the people, and the customs, and a governor's success frequently depended on them. They negotiated with governors, managed lands held by the province or by estates, collected dues, litigated over rights, and often served simultaneously as officers of estates and of the provincial office. This overlap shows that public administration and estate society were interwoven and that neither cleanly replaced the other.

Conflicts between governors and local people were frequent and sometimes violent. In 878, the Gangyō Rebellion in Dewa Province, in the northeast, was triggered in part by harsh demands from provincial officials on communities that included people of Emishi descent, and it required intervention by the court. More typical were formal complaints. The most famous, discussed in the Primary Source Window below, is the 988 petition from Owari Province accusing the governor of extortion. Such petitions show local people using the legal system and the court's own language to challenge abuse, which suggests that they still regarded the court as the authority capable of correcting it.

The same local elites who complained about governors could also be among the most powerful people in a province. Section 20.5

Temples, Shrines, and Institutional Landholding

Religious institutions were among the largest and most durable landholders of the Heian period. Their estates supplied the offerings, lamps, food, and labor that sustained rituals and communities of monks or priests, and they linked the countryside to great religious centers. Enryakuji on Mt. Hiei, the headquarters of the Tendai school, held estates in many provinces and was associated with the Hie Shrine at its foot. Kōfukuji in Nara, the family temple of the Fujiwara, and the neighboring Kasuga Shrine exercised authority over Yamato Province that approached that of a provincial government. Tōdaiji, Tōji, Kōyasan, Iwashimizu Hachiman, Ise, Usa Hachiman in Kyushu, and other institutions likewise held extensive rights. Monasteries and shrines also acted as moneylenders and employers, hired artisans and laborers, supported dependents, and litigated in defense of their claims. They were institutions with interests as much as centers of devotion.

Their position also drew them into politics. Religious institutions were closely tied to aristocratic families: sons and daughters of noble houses entered monasteries and convents, often rising to lead them, and temples relied on aristocratic patronage. At the same time, rivalries within and among institutions could become sharp. In 993 the followers of the Tendai monk Enchin separated from Enryakuji, which was dominated by the lineage of Ennin, and established themselves at Onjōji, or Miidera. The resulting rivalry lasted for generations. Enryakuji's growth under abbots such as Ryōgen (912–985) had strengthened its organization and its estate base. Kōfukuji, meanwhile, was linked with the Fujiwara and with the Kasuga Shrine and defended its own interests vigorously against Enryakuji and others.

Religious institutions also developed a distinctive method of political pressure. Groups of monks and temple dependents would descend on the capital carrying sacred symbols, such as portable shrines of the Hie deity for Enryakuji or the sacred tree of Kasuga for Kōfukuji, to present forceful petitions (gōso) demanding the punishment of an official or the reversal of a decision. Because the objects were sacred, the court hesitated to resist them by force, and their presence in the capital placed the government under intense pressure. A later tale, in the Heike monogatari, attributes to the retired emperor Shirakawa a remark that the mountain monks were among the few things he could not control. The saying appears in a warrior tale composed long after his lifetime and should be treated as a later literary characterization rather than a documented statement, but it captures a later literary memory of the political weight of these institutions.

The armed dimension of this pressure requires careful treatment. Popular history often describes a class of "warrior monks," commonly labeled sōhei, who formed the standing armies of the temples. This picture is misleading. The term sōhei is largely a later and modern usage, and contemporary sources use a variety of terms, including "evil monks" (akusō), assemblies of temple monks (daishu), and other expressions that referred to differing groups with differing roles. The participants in temple mobilizations could include ordinary monks, lower-ranking temple personnel, dependents and estate residents, and armed men associated with the temple who were not ordained. Temple communities could mobilize and arm such groups, especially for demonstrations and disputes, but they did not constitute a uniform, permanent caste of professional fighters. Historians such as Mikael Adolphson have argued that the image of monastic armies has been exaggerated and that violence involving religious institutions must be understood within the broader political and social structure in which temples, courtiers, and warriors interacted.

The consequence for the wider argument is that religious institutions were themselves part of the layered authority of the late Heian world. They controlled land, income, labor, and, at times, violence; they influenced appointments and policy; and they had their own aims. When a retired emperor or a regent needed to respond to a demonstration by temple communities, he often turned to armed men in the service of aristocratic families, which added to the growing demand for such men and drew them into disputes at the center. That connection between the religious and military spheres is one reason a chapter on warriors cannot be separated from a chapter on temples.

A local landholder or estate manager: Positioned between the proprietor and the cultivators, the manager needed patrons to protect his position and cultivators to produce the harvest. A temple or shrine proprietor: Estates supported worship, monastic communities, and dependents, and were understood as gifts to the deity or Buddha. Institutional leaders defended them through petitions, alliances with aristocratic families, and, when necessary, collective pressure, seeing the protection of estates as inseparable from the protection of the institution itself.

Section 20.6

Violence, Policing, and the Growing Importance of Armed Families

If provincial government relied on local intermediaries, and if estates and institutions competed over land and revenue, a practical question followed: who enforced claims, guarded property, pursued offenders, and suppressed rebellion? The Ritsuryō state had planned to answer with a conscript army drawn from the registered population. That system had been largely dismantled by the late eighth century. In 792 the court abolished general conscription in most provinces, apart from key frontier areas, and relied instead on smaller forces drawn from sons of district magistrates and other well-off families. The capital also had its own police and judicial office, the kebiishi, established in the early ninth century, and provincial governors could appoint officers to pursue criminals. None of these arrangements provided a large standing army. Violence was therefore handled increasingly through the personal followers of governors, estate managers, aristocrats, and temples, and through local men who might be commissioned when needed.

The Tengyō disturbances of 939–941 show what could happen when this system failed. In the east, Taira no Masakado, a descendant of Emperor Kanmu through a line that had received the Taira surname, was involved in a series of quarrels with relatives beginning in the 930s. According to the Shōmonki, a narrative of his career, he had earlier served in Kyoto under the regent Fujiwara no Tadahira, which shows that he was connected to the court's patronage network. In 939 he became involved in a dispute between a local figure and the governor of Hitachi and attacked the provincial headquarters there. He then took control of several eastern provinces, dismissed and replaced provincial officials, and, according to the text, proclaimed himself a "new sovereign" (shinnō), an act that directly challenged the emperor's authority. The text also reports religious sanction for the claim. In early 940 Masakado was defeated and killed by forces led by Taira no Sadamori, whose father he had killed, and Fujiwara no Hidesato. The court rewarded the victors with rank and office, which illustrates a continuing pattern: it responded to armed challenge by enlisting and honoring provincial fighters.

In western Japan, Fujiwara no Sumitomo, a former provincial official in Iyo, became associated with the piracy that troubled the Inland Sea. The court had reportedly sent him against pirates, after which he became a leader of such groups. His followers raided coastal areas and in 941 attacked Dazaifu itself, after which he was defeated and killed. These events, spread across the archipelago and occurring at roughly the same time, alarmed the court, and traditional Japanese history sometimes treats them as a single crisis. Modern historians caution that the two movements were not coordinated and that the sources, including the Shōmonki itself, are literary and partisan.

Later disturbances continued the pattern. In 1019 raiders known as the Toi attacked Tsushima, Iki, and the coast of northern Kyushu, carrying off captives, and were driven back by local forces under the deputy governor of Dazaifu, with Korean authorities reportedly returning some captives to Japan. In 1028 Taira no Tadatsune rebelled in the eastern provinces of Kazusa, Shimōsa, and Awa, and the rebellion was eventually suppressed in 1031 by Minamoto no Yorinobu. In the northeast, the Zenkunen War (1051–1062) and the Gosannen War (1083–1087), discussed further in Section 20.7, involved the Abe and Kiyohara families and the governors sent from the court. The northern conflicts were embedded in a frontier world that included communities of Emishi descent and distant contacts extending toward Hokkaido, and they cannot be described as simply Japanese against outsiders.

The principal conclusion is limited but important. In this period, armed service became an increasingly important component of provincial authority. Governors needed fighters to collect revenue and to police their provinces; estate managers needed them to protect harvests and to contest boundaries; aristocrats needed them as retainers and guards; and religious institutions needed them to defend their interests. The court itself repeatedly relied on such men, and it granted them ranks and offices. The development of a distinctive warrior culture, with its methods of combat, its households, and its ideals, is a further story, to be told in Chapter 21. Here the point is that the demand for armed men grew out of the way land, taxation, and administration were organized.

Cause and Effect

From Taxation to Armed Households

Long-term causes

The weakening of household registration and equal-field allotment; the state's own edicts permitting long-term landholding; the abandonment of general conscription; and the aristocracy's growing reliance on income from land and offices rather than uniform taxation.

Short-term causes

Fixed quotas delegated to governors; competition for lucrative governorships; the spread of exemptions and patronage; and disputes among proprietors, governors, and local managers over boundaries, dues, and jurisdiction.

Event or transformation

A rural order in which governors, local managers, estates, and religious institutions each exercised part of the functions of government, and in which the court governed through intermediaries.

Immediate effects

Disputes over land and revenue required enforcement; local managers and governors recruited armed followers; violent episodes such as the Tengyō disturbances challenged provincial and court authority.

Long-term consequences

Growing reliance by the court and by elite institutions on military households, whose members gained ranks, offices, and estate rights, and who later became involved in disputes at the center of the court.

Historical complexity

The chain was not automatic. Many local managers never became warriors; some rebels were connected to court patronage; and the court often reconfirmed the same rights that weakened its own control. Regional differences and individual choices shaped the outcome.

Why the pattern matters: The rise of armed families is more usefully explained by institutional change than by a simple story of a weak court and strong provincial fighters.

Section 20.7

Minamoto, Taira, and Service to the Court

Two names dominate later accounts of medieval warfare: Minamoto and Taira. Both began as surnames granted to descendants of emperors who were removed from the imperial line, a practice that reduced the number of princes and princesses the court had to support. The Minamoto (also called Genji) and the Taira (also called Heishi) therefore originated within the aristocracy. The surnames were also broad. Many separate lineages bore them, descended from different emperors and pursuing different careers, and members of the same surname often competed with one another. In this period they were not two unified national camps awaiting a great war. Masakado, whose Taira kin fought him, and Sadamori, who helped defeat him, show how divided a single name could be.

Their rise took place within the court order. Minamoto no Mitsunaka (913–997) served the Fujiwara regents and was rewarded for his loyalty, notably after he reported an alleged plot in 969 that removed a rival from the court. His descendants held provincial governorships and cultivated connections in the Kantō and elsewhere. His son Minamoto no Yorinobu was a client of the Fujiwara regent Michinaga and, in 1031, ended the Tadatsune rebellion, which increased Minamoto influence in the east. Yorinobu's line produced Minamoto no Yoriyoshi, who led the court's forces in the Zenkunen War, and Yoriyoshi's son Yoshiie, who became a leading figure in the Gosannen War. The Minamoto leaders were serving as court-appointed officials and enjoyed the patronage of the regents, and their power in the provinces rested partly on court rank and appointment.

The northern wars illustrate the mixture of public and private in this service. The Zenkunen War arose from a conflict between the Abe family of Mutsu, who had exercised broad influence in the northeast, and the provincial government, and it ended in 1062 with the Abe's defeat, with support from the Kiyohara family of Dewa. In the Gosannen War the Kiyohara themselves were divided, and Yoshiie, then governor of Mutsu, intervened. According to traditional accounts, the court regarded the conflict as a private matter and declined to reward Yoshiie's forces, and he is said to have compensated followers from his own resources. Historians treat such stories with caution, because they were elaborated later, but they suggest a developing bond between leaders and followers. The war's outcome created the conditions for Fujiwara no Kiyohira, whose family ties linked him to both the Kiyohara and the Abe, to establish the Northern Fujiwara, who ruled the northeast from Hiraizumi, where they later patronized Buddhist building and traded in gold, horses, and other northern goods. The region was thus never simply a passive frontier: its leaders built their own institutions and linked the northeast to the court, to continental goods, and to the north.

The Taira lineages that later became prominent gained their standing differently. The branch known as the Ise Taira rose in the late eleventh and early twelfth centuries through service to retired emperors. Taira no Masamori suppressed the rebellion of Minamoto no Yoshichika in 1108, and his son Tadamori continued in service. Retired-emperor households also developed a “Northern Face” service (hokumen). Under Shirakawa in the late eleventh century it increasingly included armed retainers who guarded and accompanied the retired sovereign, but the institution’s military character developed over time rather than appearing fully formed at a single founding date. Historians see such arrangements as part of a wider pattern in which military families gained access to court rank and office through service. Their political importance depended on aristocratic patrons.

The interpretive point is essential: warrior families initially rose through service within the court order, not by rejecting it. Their leaders sought court ranks, governorships, and marriage ties, and their followers sought rewards through them. A later chapter will show how this dependence changed. For now, it is enough to recognize that the same court that lacked a monopoly of force was also the institution to which military families turned for legitimacy and advancement. To narrate this story as the triumph of a rival warrior order over the aristocracy would misrepresent it. A fuller account of these households, their culture, and their ideals belongs to Chapter 21.

Section 20.8

Go-Sanjō, Estate Reform, and Retired-Emperor Government

The court was aware of the problems that estates created for provincial revenue and legal order, and it tried repeatedly to respond. Edicts in the eleventh century, including one in 1045, sought to restrict estates established recently or without proper documentation. They were generally ineffective, partly because many powerful families and institutions were among the beneficiaries of estates and were also the people who carried out the court's policies. The reform attempt of Emperor Go-Sanjō stands out as more determined.

Go-Sanjō acceded in 1068 after a long period as crown prince. His mother was an imperial princess and not a Fujiwara daughter, which meant that he lacked the close family ties to the regent line that most emperors of the preceding period had had. This gave him somewhat greater freedom of action, though the regent Fujiwara no Norimichi remained in place and the Fujiwara continued to hold major offices. In 1069 the court established a Record Office (Kirokujo) to examine the documents on which estate claims were based. Estates that could not present proper documentation, or that interfered with provincial administration, were subject to being abolished, and a specialized body of officials, including learned scholar-officials, scrutinized the paperwork. The reform reportedly affected estates held even by prominent families and institutions, suggesting a serious effort to enforce the rules.

The reforms should not be described as an abolition of estates or a destruction of Fujiwara power. The estate system was too embedded in the interests of aristocrats, temples, and shrines to be eliminated, and the review operated by verifying documents and legal titles, not by rejecting the principle of estates. Its lasting importance was to establish a mechanism for testing claims and to make the court an even more central arbiter of which rights were legitimate. In this sense it shows again that the court's authority over estates was exercised through the legal machinery of documentation and confirmation. Go-Sanjō abdicated in 1072 and died in 1073, and his reform program did not settle the issue; later sovereigns and retired emperors continued to sanction and create estates.

Historical Vocabulary
Insei (government by retired emperors)

Insei (院政) refers to the political arrangement in which a retired emperor (in or jōkō) directed affairs of state from his own household, using his own administrative offices and, often, close retainers. The system is conventionally dated from Shirakawa's abdication in 1086. Retired emperors could issue directives, control appointments, accumulate estates, and use religious patronage, building temples and undertaking pilgrimages.

It matters here because it shows a further layering of authority. Insei did not abolish the emperor, the regents, or the court's offices. It added a new center of decision-making and resources, and it relied on the same offices, rankings, and legal procedures as before. Retired emperors' households also became major landholders and employers of armed men. Students should avoid picturing insei as either a restoration of direct imperial rule or a complete break with the Fujiwara regency: regents remained influential, and the two systems overlapped.

Shirakawa (r. 1072–1086) abdicated in favor of his young son Horikawa but continued to direct policy. The abdication gave him a way to escape the constraints on a reigning emperor, who was bound by ceremonial duties and by dependence on regents and ministers. As a retired sovereign, he could act from his own residence and household, and the offices of that household (in no chō) issued documents that carried great weight. Shirakawa's household drew on middle-ranking aristocrats, many from governor families, as advisers and administrators, and it employed armed guards. Retired emperors built impressive temples, such as Hosshōji, and made repeated pilgrimages to Kumano, activities that demonstrated wealth and religious authority. After Horikawa's death in 1107, Shirakawa's influence continued through his grandson Toba and later great-grandson Sutoku. Shirakawa died in 1129, after which Toba, who had himself abdicated in 1123, dominated politics until his death in 1156.

Retired emperors also became major proprietors and creators of estates. Households connected to Shirakawa, Toba, and their successors accumulated extensive holdings, often by establishing new estates, receiving donations from governors and others seeking favor, or arranging for the estates to be passed to imperial daughters and family members. Women of the imperial family could hold large collections of estate rights, and their control of wealth was an important resource for retired emperors and for imperial succession politics. This process meant that some of the wealth that had once passed to regents now flowed toward retired emperors, while the estate system as a whole continued to grow. Weakening Fujiwara regency did not restore a simple, centralized monarchy; it produced a court in which retired emperors, reigning emperors, regents, aristocratic households, and temples competed and cooperated.

Historical Debate

Did the Shōen System Cause the Decline of the Heian State?

The question: Historians have long asked whether the spread of estates eroded the court's revenue and administrative reach and thereby produced the conditions for warrior government, or whether estates were part of how the court order itself worked.

Interpretation A — Estates as erosion of the state: Influential earlier interpretations, drawing on both Japanese and Western scholarship, emphasized the conflict between public authority and private landholding. In this view, exemptions and immunities removed land from the provincial tax base, weakened the court's capacity to govern, and empowered local lords and warriors who defended their holdings against the state. Local developers who commended land to patrons were seen as a rising social force that would eventually create warrior rule.
Interpretation B — Estates as a component of the court order: Other historians emphasize that estates were created, confirmed, litigated, and protected through court and aristocratic institutions. The historian Kuroda Toshio, for example, proposed that medieval Japan was governed by a system of powerful "gates" or houses, including courtiers, temples and shrines, and later warriors, that shared authority. Studies such as Adolphson's work on monasteries have similarly stressed cooperation and competition among these groups. From this perspective, estates did not stand outside the state; they were mechanisms through which it distributed resources, and public lands and estates coexisted.

Evidence: Both positions draw on the same estate documents, court diaries, edicts, and records of disputes. They differ in how they interpret the evidence of exemptions and disputes, and in whether they treat the persistence of public lands, the court's continuing role in confirming rights, and the frequent alliances between local managers and central patrons as central or secondary.

Why it matters: If estates simply eroded the state, the rise of warriors looks like the natural result of decline. If estates were part of a layered system, the warriors' rise appears as one development within a changing structure of authority, and the court's continuing importance becomes easier to explain. Many current accounts combine elements of both interpretations rather than choosing one.

Student task: Identify one piece of evidence from this chapter that supports the erosion view and one that supports the layered-authority view, and explain which better accounts for the continued importance of the court in the twelfth century.

Section 20.9

Religious Change in the Later Heian World

Religious life in the late Heian period did not consist only of the institutional power described in Section 20.5. It also changed in content and emphasis. A central idea was mappō, the "latter days of the Dharma," a Buddhist notion that after the death of the Buddha his teachings would gradually decline through successive ages until true practice became difficult or impossible. In the calculation commonly used in Japan, the age of mappō was reckoned to begin in 1052. That date fell in an unsettled decade: the Zenkunen War had begun in the north in 1051, and aristocrats recorded fires, epidemics, and other disasters as signs of the times. It would be a mistake to conclude that everyone believed the world was literally ending. Mappō was a framework for anxiety, urgency, and devotion, taken seriously by some, doubted or ignored by others, and interpreted in different ways by different groups.

Pure Land devotion, focused on Amida Buddha and the hope of rebirth in his paradise, grew in importance. Earlier Buddhist practices, including Tendai meditation on Amida, were joined by more accessible forms of devotion. The wandering holy man Kūya (903–972) is remembered for spreading recitation of Amida's name, the nenbutsu, among ordinary people in the capital, and the Tendai monk Genshin (942–1017) wrote the Ōjōyōshū ("Essentials of Rebirth") in 985, a work that describes the sufferings of the hells and the joys of the Pure Land and recommends contemplative and vocal practices. Collections of biographies of people said to have been reborn in the Pure Land circulated as well. Later, the monk Ryōnin (1073–1132) taught a form of nenbutsu practice in which each person's recitation was said to benefit all others. These developments belong to the Tendai world and to popular devotion within it; they should not be confused with the independent Pure Land movements of the Kamakura period, which are the subject of Chapter 26.

Aristocratic devotion found visible form in architecture, sculpture, and ritual. The Byōdōin at Uji, converted from a villa into a temple by the regent Fujiwara no Yorimichi in 1052, includes the Phoenix Hall (Hōōdō), completed in 1053, which houses a gilded Amida statue by the sculptor Jōchō and is surrounded by images of celestial beings. Michinaga, Yorimichi's father, buried sutras on Mt. Kinpu in 1007, an act that survives in archaeological form and reflects hopes for salvation in a future age. Retired emperors' pilgrimages to Kumano and their building of temples show the union of politics and piety. Elsewhere, the Northern Fujiwara commissioned the Golden Hall of Chūson-ji in the 1120s, and Amida halls were built in provincial locations, such as Fuki-ji in Kyushu, showing the spread of these forms beyond the capital.

Religious ideas also shaped the relationship between Buddhas and native deities. The concept of honji suijaku, in which kami were understood as local manifestations of Buddhas or bodhisattvas, became increasingly influential, and shrines and temples were often closely linked. Mountain religious practice, later associated with Shugendō, continued to develop around sites such as Kumano and Kinpusen. Such combinations illustrate the difficulty of dividing religion into separate "Shinto" and "Buddhist" spheres at this time.

Evidence for religious life is uneven. Aristocratic diaries, temple records, and surviving buildings and images document elite practice well. For people outside the elite, historians rely on tales, temple histories, and occasional material remains, which show that nenbutsu practice and pilgrimage extended beyond the aristocracy but do not allow a detailed reconstruction of belief in villages and estates. Women, both aristocratic and non-elite, appear as patrons, pilgrims, and nuns, though their voices are less often preserved. The late Heian religious environment thus mixed elite anxieties, institutional competition, and broader popular devotion, forming the setting from which several later movements emerged.

Section 20.10

A Court That Still Mattered: From Layered Authority to the Warrior Age

By the early twelfth century, a simple narrative in which the court falls and the samurai rise no longer fits the evidence. The emperor and court still conferred titles, ranks, and legitimacy. Kyoto remained the political and cultural center. Aristocrats and retired emperors controlled enormous resources, and provincial governors remained important both as revenue collectors and as figures with their own retinues. Temples and shrines were powerful institutional actors with estates and dependents. Estate rights divided authority in a patchwork that no single office could unify. Local magnates and armed households had become increasingly necessary to provincial governance, and military force was being drawn more directly into disputes among elite institutions.

What had changed was the structure of power. The court no longer possessed the tools for direct rule imagined in the Ritsuryō codes, and it governed by delegating: to governors who met quotas, to estate proprietors and their local managers, to temples that policed their own communities, and to armed men who served under aristocratic patrons. That structure could function so long as the parties agreed on the sources of legitimacy. Conflicts between individual families or institutions could be settled, at least in principle, through appeals to the court, and armed force generally served as an instrument of the disputing parties, subordinate to the court's system of rank.

That equilibrium became fragile when disputes moved from the provinces and the margins of court life to the very center. The death of a retired emperor, competing claims to succession, and rivalry among aristocratic factions raised questions that could not be settled by petitions and documents alone. The armed families who had been employed to manage provincial violence and defend competing rights were now attached, through service and patronage, to the households of the persons in conflict. The final question of this chapter is therefore the entry point for the next part of the book: what happens when these armed families, previously employed to manage provincial violence and defend competing rights, are pulled directly into succession and factional disputes at the highest levels of the court? The answer begins with Chapter 21, which examines the warrior class itself, and continues in Chapter 22, which treats the Hōgen and Heiji conflicts and the rise of the Taira.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From the Ritsuryō Ideal to the Layered Order

What Changed

  • Household registration and equal-field allotment weakened, and taxation shifted toward land units and responsible cultivators
  • Estate privileges expanded, and rights over the same land came to be divided among several holders
  • Governors became remitters of quotas, and local managers mediated between provinces, estates, and cultivators
  • Temples and shrines became major institutional landholders and political actors
  • Retired emperors' households formed a new center of resources and decision-making
  • Coercion passed increasingly into the hands of armed households and dependents

What Stayed the Same

  • The emperor and the system of court ranks and offices remained the foundation of legitimacy
  • Provincial offices and governors continued to function
  • Legal documents, petitions, and central confirmation of rights remained essential
  • Taxation and delivery of goods to the capital continued, though through different channels
  • Kyoto remained the political and cultural center

Who experienced change most directly: Cultivators and local managers, whose obligations and protections shifted with the rearrangement of rights, and provincial governors, whose incomes depended on the new arrangements. Who experienced greater continuity: Kyoto aristocrats, whose status still rested on court rank, even as the sources of their income changed.

Why the pattern matters: It shows that transformation occurred through adaptation within the legal order and not through a sudden break, which helps explain why the court survived the rise of warriors.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has explained how the Heian court, at the height of its political and cultural life, came to share authority with estates, provincial elites, religious institutions, retired emperors, and armed families. Its central argument is that Heian central authority did not simply collapse. Authority became layered, and the layers developed through the court order as much as around it.

The starting point was the transformation of rural taxation. The Ritsuryō ideal of registered households, allotted fields, and uniform taxes weakened, and by the tenth century governments assessed land units and the responsible cultivators associated with them. Provinces were assigned quotas, and governors, called zuryō, were responsible for delivering them, with wide discretion over collection. The court thereby governed through intermediaries.

The estate, or shōen, was the main institutional form through which land and revenue were redistributed. Estates were bundles of rights and obligations, held by proprietors and managed by local officers, often protected by exemptions obtained from the court or governors. They were not private kingdoms or simple private property, and they coexisted with provincial public lands. They were created, confirmed, and contested through the court's legal machinery.

Provincial administration continued alongside estates. Governors remained valuable posts, local officials and magnates shared in provincial administration and estate management, and conflicts such as the 988 Owari petition show people using the court's own procedures to challenge abuse. Local magnates should not be equated with samurai, though some maintained armed followers.

Temples and shrines were among the greatest institutional landholders, and they acted as employers, litigants, and political actors. Rivalries such as that between Enryakuji and Onjōji, and forceful petitions carrying sacred symbols, made religious institutions a major force. The image of standing armies of "warrior monks" is misleading, since temple communities mobilized varied groups of people in specific circumstances.

The reliance on intermediaries created a need for enforcement. The Tengyō disturbances of 939–941, later revolts in the Kantō, wars in the northeast, and border raids in Kyushu showed that provincial violence could challenge the court, and that the court responded by rewarding and employing provincial fighters. Armed service became an increasingly important part of provincial authority.

Minamoto and Taira lineages were broad surnames rather than unified camps. Their leaders rose through service to regents, retired emperors, and the court, seeking rank and office. Their power, at this stage, was tied to the court order. Go-Sanjō's Record Office of 1069 tried to verify estate claims but could not abolish estates, and Shirakawa's abdication in 1086 initiated insei, which added another center of authority and another set of estate holders without restoring a centralized monarchy.

Religious change accompanied these developments. Ideas of mappō, Pure Land devotion, and pilgrimage shaped aristocratic and popular life, as seen in Genshin's writings, the Phoenix Hall, and the pilgrimages of retired emperors. This religious environment prepared the ground for later movements treated in Chapter 26.

Regional variation was pervasive. The Kantō, Tōhoku, Kyushu, the northern frontier, and the southwestern islands all interacted with the court in different ways, and the Northern Fujiwara and other regional powers built institutions of their own. These regions were participants in the transformation, not merely settings for events in Kyoto.

By the early twelfth century, the court retained legitimacy, offices, and enormous resources, but no longer monopolized land, administration, or force. The chapter ends at the edge of 1156, when armed families, previously employed to manage provincial disputes, would be drawn into the highest levels of court politics. That is the subject of Chapters 21 and 22.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it explains how the world of Chapters 18 and 19 was sustained and how it changed. The brilliant court depended on land, labor, and enforcement that were being reorganized beneath it. By showing how estates, governors, religious institutions, and armed households fit together, the chapter replaces a story of sudden collapse with one of gradual adaptation.

It also reveals a larger pattern in Japanese history: authority frequently persisted in form while its substance was redistributed. The court kept its role as the source of legitimacy even as it lost its monopoly over resources and force. Later regimes, including the Kamakura bakufu, would likewise operate alongside the court and draw on its titles and procedures. Understanding this layering helps explain why medieval Japan had several centers of authority at once.

The chapter connects regions that Kyoto-centered narratives often separate. The Kantō, Tōhoku, Kyushu, the northern communities of Hokkaido, and the southwestern islands all shaped and were shaped by these changes, and connections to Korea, China, and the wider maritime world appear in the border raids and the trade of Hakata. Japan's history in this period cannot be told from the capital alone.

Finally, the chapter prepares for what follows. Chapter 21 will examine the warrior class itself, and Chapter 22 the conflicts that drew warriors directly into the politics of the court. Students who understand how the provinces, estates, and temples were arranged will be better prepared to see why those conflicts unfolded as they did, and to resist the assumption that the outcome was predetermined.
