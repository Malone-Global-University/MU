The Russo-Japanese War and the Making of a Great Power

Japanese History: From Jōmon Japan to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

On the night of 8 February 1904, Japanese destroyers approached the Russian Pacific Squadron lying at anchor outside Port Arthur on the Liaodong Peninsula and launched torpedo attacks. Diplomatic relations had been severed two days earlier, but no declaration of war had yet been issued. The war that began that night grew out of nearly a decade of rivalry over Korea and Manchuria, yet it was fought almost entirely on territory that belonged neither to Japan nor to Russia: on the Korean Peninsula, on the soil of the Qing empire, and in the seas between.

The scale of the conflict was unprecedented in Japan's modern experience. The First Sino-Japanese War (Chapter 60) had been fought against Qing forces that had also undergone extensive military modernization, but Japan’s comparatively short campaign ended with a decisive diplomatic settlement. The war with Russia lasted about nineteen months, required the mobilization of roughly a million Japanese men over its course, and produced some of the largest battles yet fought anywhere in the world. At Liaoyang and Mukden, armies numbering in the hundreds of thousands maneuvered across a front of many miles. At Port Arthur, a siege of attrition consumed tens of thousands of Japanese lives. At Tsushima, the Japanese fleet destroyed most of a Russian squadron that had steamed some eighteen thousand miles to reach the theater of war. The conflict has often been called a rehearsal for the industrial warfare of the First World War, with its quick-firing artillery, machine guns, barbed wire, telephones, and trenches.

This chapter insists, however, that the war cannot be understood as a duel between two empires. Korea, which had proclaimed itself an empire in 1897 and declared its neutrality in January 1904, was occupied by Japanese troops within days of the first shots. The Qing government declared its own neutrality but could not prevent two foreign armies from fighting over its Manchurian provinces, requisitioning its grain, and employing its subjects as guides, laborers, and irregular fighters. Southern Sakhalin, home to Ainu, Nivkh, and Uilta communities as well as Russian convicts and settlers, became a bargaining chip. The peace negotiated in the United States transferred leases and railways in Manchuria that Russia had itself obtained from China, and it recognized Japanese predominance in Korea without Korean participation. A history of the war that omits these communities reproduces the assumptions of the imperial diplomats themselves.

The central argument of the chapter is that Japan's victory transformed its international standing, but at enormous military and financial cost, and that the victory should be analyzed in three distinct registers. On the battlefield, Japan won decisive engagements yet could not have sustained an expensive war against Russia indefinitely. In diplomacy, the Treaty of Portsmouth secured recognized Japanese interests without the war indemnity that many Japanese expected. In imperial terms, Japan's enhanced standing came with intensified coercion in Korea and with new territorial and economic privileges in southern Manchuria and Sakhalin. A victory over a European empire was not, for Koreans or for many Chinese, a victory for Asian independence.

The chapter also follows the war's social and political consequences. Newspapers, schools, women's associations, the Japanese Red Cross, and local communities mobilized in support of the army, while a small socialist and Christian minority opposed the war and the government suppressed them. Taxes rose, the state borrowed abroad on an unprecedented scale, and the news of the peace terms provoked the Hibiya riots, the first major urban disturbance of Japan's constitutional era. In Russia, defeat intensified the political crisis that produced the Revolution of 1905. These developments belong in the chapter because they explain why both governments sought peace and why the peace satisfied almost no one.

The evidence for this history is abundant but uneven. Official military histories, diplomatic archives (including those digitized by the Japan Center for Asian Historical Records, JACAR), memoirs such as Sakurai Tadayoshi's account of Port Arthur, foreign military attachés' reports, war correspondents' dispatches, contemporary newspapers, photographs, and woodblock war prints survive in quantity. Korean and Chinese civilians left far fewer records, and historians must read the surviving sources critically and sometimes against the grain. Dates in this chapter follow the Gregorian calendar; Russian sources of the period used the Julian calendar, thirteen days behind. The chapter follows Chapter 60's treatment of the 1895 settlement and prepares the way for Chapter 62, which examines Japanese rule in Korea, and for Chapter 63, which turns to the domestic labor and social conflicts of an industrializing, imperial Japan.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how the Triple Intervention of 1895 and Russia's advance into Manchuria created the strategic rivalry that led to war in 1904.
  2. Distinguish the dispute over Russian military occupation of Manchuria from the contest for influence in Korea, and separate Japanese security arguments from the imperial ambitions that accompanied them.
  3. Analyze the diplomatic setting of the war, including the Anglo-Japanese Alliance of 1902 and the positions of France, Germany, and the United States.
  4. Trace the major land campaigns from the Yalu River through Port Arthur, Liaoyang, Sha-ho, Sandepu, and Mukden, explaining why Japanese operational success did not produce a complete victory.
  5. Evaluate the naval war from the opening attacks to the Battle of Tsushima, avoiding explanations that credit a single weapon, tactic, or commander.
  6. Assess how Korean and Chinese communities experienced the war and how the February 1904 protocol and the 1905 agreement compromised Korean sovereignty.
  7. Explain how Japan financed the war and why financial exhaustion shaped its diplomacy at Portsmouth.
  8. Interpret the terms of the Treaty of Portsmouth article by article and explain why many Japanese judged them inadequate.
  9. Analyze the Hibiya riots as an expression of popular nationalism, class tension, and political mobilization.
  10. Compare Japanese, Russian, Korean, Chinese, European, and American responses to Japan's victory and evaluate its significance for debates about empire, race, and the balance of power.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did the 1895 settlement create rivalry with Russia without making war inevitable, and what choices in Tokyo and Saint Petersburg turned rivalry into war?
  2. How did Japanese policymakers weigh security concerns about Korea against ambitions in Manchuria, and where did defensive reasoning shade into expansion?
  3. What did Korea's neutrality declaration and the Protocol of February 1904 reveal about the gap between formal guarantees and military reality?
  4. Why did Japanese forces win major battles yet fail to destroy the Russian army, and what does this reveal about the limits of operational victory?
  5. How did naval power, logistics, finance, and railways interact to determine what each belligerent could and could not do?
  6. How did civilians in Manchuria and Korea, who were not parties to the war, experience the fighting and the military occupation?
  7. Why did Japan accept a peace without an indemnity, and why did that peace provoke riots in Tokyo?
  8. Was the war a decisive geopolitical triumph, an expensive and limited victory, or both, and what evidence supports each reading?

Historical Context

Chapter 60 explained how the First Sino-Japanese War of 1894–1895 ended in the Treaty of Shimonoseki, which ceded Taiwan, the Pescadores, and the Liaodong Peninsula to Japan, and how Russia, France, and Germany pressured Japan within days to return Liaodong. Japan's leaders drew a lesson that shaped the following decade: diplomatic gains in East Asia could be reversed by coalitions of European powers unless Japan possessed stronger armed forces and reliable allies. Between 1895 and 1904 the government used the Chinese indemnity to expand the army and navy, to establish the gold standard in 1897, and to build industrial capacity such as the state-owned Yawata Steel Works. The Meiji Constitution (Chapter 59) gave the Diet power over the budget, but the oligarchs and the military retained great influence over foreign and military policy.

The Qing empire, weakened by defeat in 1895, struggled to defend its sovereignty. In the late 1890s the European powers extracted leases and railway concessions along the Chinese coast and in the northeast. Russia, building the Trans-Siberian Railway across Siberia, obtained the right to run a branch line, the Chinese Eastern Railway, across northern Manchuria to Vladivostok, and in 1898 it leased Port Arthur and Dalian (Dalny) at the southern tip of the Liaodong Peninsula. Manchuria itself, the homeland of the Qing dynasty, was by 1900 populated largely by Han Chinese farmers and migrants, together with Manchu, Mongol, and Korean communities; it was not an empty frontier awaiting a European or Japanese claimant.

Korea occupied a precarious position between these empires. Formerly a tributary of the Qing, it had been declared independent by the Treaty of Shimonoseki, yet its government lay under pressure from Japan, Russia, and the other treaty powers. Japanese agents had been implicated in the 1895 murder of Queen Min, and King Gojong had taken refuge in the Russian legation in 1896–1897 before proclaiming the Korean Empire in October 1897. Korean reformers, officials, and the court itself pursued strategies of balance, reform, and diplomatic appeal, but the country lacked the military means to defend its neutrality. Understanding the war therefore requires attention to four overlapping arenas: Japanese domestic politics and finance, Russian imperial strategy, Korean sovereignty, and the Qing territory on which the principal campaigns were fought.

Section 61.1

The Unfinished Settlement of 1895 and Russia's Advance into Manchuria

The Triple Intervention of April 1895 was delivered as friendly advice from the Russian, German, and French governments, which urged Japan to relinquish the Liaodong Peninsula on the ground that its possession would endanger the Qing capital and the independence of Korea. Japan, with its army and navy exhausted and without allies, accepted in May; the Qing government agreed to pay an additional indemnity of thirty million taels, and the peninsula was evacuated by the end of the year. A popular slogan of the period, gashin shōtan ("endure hardship and bear the bitterness"), expressed public resentment and a call for national preparation. Historians caution, however, that the government's own course was a calculated program of army and navy expansion financed largely by the Chinese indemnity, not simply an emotional vow of revenge.

Russia, for its part, turned the intervention to its advantage. In 1896 it concluded a defensive agreement with the Qing government (the Li–Lobanov treaty) and obtained a concession for the Chinese Eastern Railway across northern Manchuria, built and operated by a company tied to the Russian state. In March 1898 it leased Port Arthur and Dalian for twenty-five years and gained the right to build a southern branch line connecting them with the main line. These acts occurred during a wider scramble for concessions, in which Germany took Jiaozhou Bay and Britain leased Weihaiwei. They placed Russian soldiers, railway guards, engineers, and a naval base on the very peninsula Japan had been forced to abandon. Yet the rivalry was not fixed on a path to war. Japan and Russia concluded agreements about Korea in 1896 and 1898, and in 1901 Itō Hirobumi explored in Saint Petersburg a settlement in which Russia would hold primacy in Manchuria and Japan in Korea. War became likely only when negotiation, strategic anxiety, and the opportunities created by the Anglo-Japanese Alliance combined, as the next sections show.

Section 61.2

The Boxer Crisis, the Russian Occupation of Manchuria, and Imperial Competition

The Boxer uprising of 1900 gave Russia the pretext to send large forces into Manchuria, ostensibly to protect the Chinese Eastern Railway from attack. Russian troops occupied the main cities of the three Manchurian provinces, including Mukden in October 1900. In July 1900, in the Amur River town of Blagoveshchensk, Russian authorities and Cossacks expelled and killed large numbers of Chinese residents, an atrocity that illustrates the violence of the occupation and that Chinese communities long remembered. When the Boxer crisis ended elsewhere, Russia did not withdraw. A convention of April 1902 promised the evacuation of Manchuria in three stages over eighteen months. The first stage was carried out; the second, scheduled for April 1903, was not, and in that month Russia presented China with new conditions for withdrawal.

Japan, Britain, and the United States protested. The Qing government, lacking the means to expel Russia, was caught between pressure from St. Petersburg and the diplomatic encouragement of the other powers; in October 1903 it signed a commercial treaty with the United States that opened Mukden and Antung to foreign trade. Within Russia, policy was divided: Finance Minister Witte favored economic penetration, while a group of courtiers and speculators around Aleksandr Bezobrazov promoted timber and mining enterprises on the Yalu River, on the Korean border, that Japan saw as a military threat. The appointment of Admiral Yevgeny Alekseyev as Viceroy of the Far East in August 1903 and the dismissal of Witte the same month suggested a harder line, although Russian policy remained inconsistent. For Japanese leaders, a Russia entrenched in Manchuria with a railway, a fortified port, and troops near the Yalu appeared to threaten Korea, whatever the intentions of the Russian government.

Historical Vocabulary

Sphere of Influence, Leasehold, Concession

Sphere of influence, leasehold, and railway concession

A sphere of influence was a region in which an imperial power claimed priority in investment, trade, and political influence and expected rivals to refrain from competing, usually without formal annexation. A leasehold was a territory transferred to a foreign power for a fixed term, such as Russia's twenty-five-year lease of Port Arthur and Dalian; the lessor retained nominal sovereignty, while the lessee exercised administrative and military control. A railway concession granted a foreign company the right to build and operate a line, often with land, mining, and policing rights along the track. Extraterritoriality exempted foreign nationals from local law.

These categories matter because Manchuria was never formally partitioned. Russian and Japanese claims there rested on treaties, leases, and military occupation extracted from the Qing government rather than on annexation, and Chinese sovereignty remained a legal fiction that both sides invoked when convenient. Students should avoid reading "sphere of influence" as a modern synonym for "colony"; the terms described different degrees of control, and the distinctions were often negotiated and contested.

Section 61.3

Korea between Japan and Russia: Sovereignty, Reform, and Strategic Rivalry

For Koreans, the late 1890s were a period of reform and anxiety rather than a mere stage for foreign rivalry. After the murder of Queen Min in October 1895 by a group that included Japanese officials and agents, King Gojong lived in fear for his life and in February 1896 left the palace for the Russian legation, where he remained for a year. He returned in 1897 and proclaimed the Korean Empire, adopting the title of emperor and the reign name Kwangmu. The Kwangmu reforms strengthened imperial authority, modernized parts of the army, introduced new schools, a telegraph and electric tramway in Seoul, and attempts at land surveying. The Independence Club (1896–1898), led by Seo Jae-pil (Philip Jaisohn) and later suppressed, advocated representative institutions and resistance to foreign domination.

Foreign concessions multiplied. Russia obtained timber rights on the Yalu and Tumen in 1896, while Japanese interests built the Seoul–Pusan railway under a concession of 1898 and operated banking and telegraph facilities. Neither power could dominate the Korean court without provoking the other, and Gojong's diplomacy sought to exploit that rivalry, soliciting assistance from the United States, Britain, and Germany as well. Officials such as Yi Yong-ik were associated with Russian ties, while others worked with Japan, and Korean society was divided over how to preserve independence. Korea's weakness was military and financial: its army was small, its revenues were limited, and foreign advisers and concessionaires competed to control them. In January 1904, as war approached, Gojong issued a declaration of neutrality and appealed to the powers to respect it. The declaration was a rational diplomatic move, but without military backing it could not bind the great powers.

Section 61.4

The Anglo-Japanese Alliance of 1902 and the International Diplomatic Setting

The Anglo-Japanese Alliance, signed on 30 January 1902, was the most significant diplomatic achievement of the Katsura government and the first formal military alliance between an Asian state and a European great power. Its terms were defensive: each signatory recognized the other's special interests (Britain in China, Japan in China and, in political, commercial, and industrial respects, in Korea), and each promised benevolent neutrality if the other became involved in war with a single power in the Far East, and military assistance if it was attacked by two or more. The treaty was concluded after Japanese leaders debated an alternative, a bargain with Russia of the kind Itō had explored. Katsura, Komura, and Yamagata preferred the British option, and Itō ultimately acquiesced.

Britain sought to restrain Russian expansion in China without overextending its navy, then stretched by the South African War. France was Russia's ally and financier but wished to avoid involvement in a Far Eastern war that could drag it into conflict with Britain; the Franco-Russian declaration of March 1902 and the Anglo-French Entente of April 1904 helped confine the conflict. Germany encouraged Russian commitments in Asia, partly to divert attention from Europe, while the United States, committed to the Open Door in China, sympathized with Japan. For Japan the alliance's decisive value was negative: if France or Germany again joined Russia, Britain would be obliged to fight. This calculation allowed Japan to gamble that a war with Russia could be kept local. Japan also bought two armored cruisers building in Italy for Argentina, which became the Nisshin and Kasuga, in December 1903, strengthening its fleet just before the war.

Historical Vocabulary

Defensive Alliance, Neutrality, Intervention

Defensive alliance, neutrality, and intervention

A defensive alliance commits its members to assist one another only if attacked, not if they initiate hostilities. The 1902 treaty was defensive in form, but a state confident of allied support might still choose to begin a war, as Japan did. Neutrality is a legal status under which a state refrains from aiding belligerents and expects its territory to be respected; Korea and the Qing empire each declared neutrality in 1904 but could not enforce it. Intervention refers to the coercive involvement of a third power in a dispute, as in the 1895 Triple Intervention.

These distinctions matter because the diplomatic structure of 1904 shaped the war's scale. The alliance reduced the danger of intervention against Japan, while neutrality declarations by Korea and China proved ineffective against the belligerents' armies, a reminder that the legal protection of weaker states depended on the interests of stronger ones.

Section 61.5

The Failed Negotiations of 1903–1904 and Japan's Decision for War

In August 1903 the Japanese government, through Minister Kurino Shin'ichirō in Saint Petersburg, proposed a settlement under which both states would respect the independence and territorial integrity of China and Korea, Russia would recognize Japan's preponderant interests in Korea (including the right to advise and assist in reform and to send troops if necessary), and Japan would recognize Russia's interests in Manchurian railway enterprises. Russian counterproposals of October declined to discuss Manchuria, which Russia treated as a matter between itself and China, and proposed a neutral zone in northern Korea above the thirty-ninth parallel while restricting Japan's military use of Korea. Several further exchanges followed, slowed by the distance between Tokyo and Saint Petersburg, by the Russian court's divided counsels, and by the viceroy's separate authority in the Far East. Neither side was able to reconcile Japan's insistence on a free hand in all of Korea with Russia's insistence on excluding Manchuria from negotiation.

Japanese opinion was divided. In June 1903 seven professors of Tokyo Imperial University had submitted a memorial urging a firm policy, and many newspapers demanded action. Socialists and Christians such as Kōtoku Shūsui, Sakai Toshihiko, and Uchimura Kanzō opposed war, and left the newspaper Yorozu chōhō when it turned to support it; they founded the Heimin shimbun in November 1903. Within the leadership, Itō and Inoue Kaoru counseled caution, but Katsura, Komura, Yamagata, and the army's planners believed that Russian reinforcement of the Far East would soon make war more difficult for Japan. An imperial conference on 4 February 1904 decided on war, and Japan broke off relations on 6 February. Historians generally distinguish Japanese security arguments, which centered on Korea as a strategic frontier, from broader ambitions, which included economic privileges and, in some quarters, claims in southern Manchuria. Russian policy-makers, who underestimated Japan's willingness to fight, made the choice easier by offering concessions too slowly.

Section 61.6

The Japanese and Russian Armed Forces: Technology, Command, and Strategic Geography

Japan's army was a conscript force organized in divisions on a German-influenced model, with a general staff, reserve system, and officer corps trained in domestic academies and, in some cases, abroad. It was equipped with domestically produced Arisaka rifles and field artillery of modest quality; Japan's arsenals expanded during the war, but shell supply, particularly of heavy ammunition, remained a constraint. The navy was among the most modern in the world: its battleships and armored cruisers, the "six-six" core of the Combined Fleet, were largely built in Britain, and its crews had trained intensively since the 1890s. Russia possessed far larger resources, a larger army (even though much of it was committed in Europe), and a navy of three separate fleets in the Baltic, the Black Sea, and the Pacific, but its forces in the Far East were divided between Port Arthur and Vladivostok and its command structure was split between the viceroy, the army commander, and ministers in Saint Petersburg.

Strategic geography imposed asymmetrical difficulties. Russia's lifeline was the Trans-Siberian Railway, a single-track line with a break at Lake Baikal, where winter crossings required ferries, an icebreaker, and, for a time, rails laid on the ice; the circum-Baikal section was completed in 1904, and the line's capacity improved over time. Japan faced a sea-borne challenge: its armies had to cross the Korea Strait and the Yellow Sea, and any loss of naval control would sever supply. Both states therefore saw the early naval balance as decisive. Neither could be sure of winning, and Japanese planners, who expected a long war, aimed to defeat Russian forces in the field and then to negotiate before Russia's reserves arrived in full. The outcome was not predetermined; it depended on choices, errors, and chance, as the following sections show.

Section 61.7

The Surprise Attack on Port Arthur and the Battle of Chemulpo

The Combined Fleet, formed in December 1903 under Vice-Admiral Tōgō Heihachirō, sortied from Sasebo in early February. On the night of 8–9 February, Japanese destroyers attacked the Russian Pacific Squadron anchored in the roads outside Port Arthur, damaging the battleships Retvizan and Tsesarevich and the cruiser Pallada. The Russian ships were not destroyed, and Japanese cruiser bombardments the next day produced no decisive result. The attack, which preceded the formal declarations of 10 February, has been compared by historians and writers to later surprise attacks, though contemporary international law did not yet require a prior declaration; it was criticized in some European newspapers but accepted by others as a legitimate stroke of war, and Britain and the United States did not protest.

At the Korean port of Chemulpo (Incheon), a Japanese squadron under Rear-Admiral Uryū Sotokichi covered the landing of troops of the 12th Division on 8–9 February. On 9 February he presented an ultimatum to the Russian cruiser Varyag and the gunboat Korietz, which were in port with neutral warships: leave or be attacked at anchor. The Russian ships sailed out, engaged the Japanese squadron for about an hour, and returned damaged; their crews scuttled Varyag and blew up Korietz and were taken aboard the neutral ships. At Port Arthur, Tōgō's attempts to bottle up the Russian fleet by sinking blockships, in which Commander Hirose Takeo died in March, were only partly effective, and the Russian squadron under Admiral Stepan Makarov became more active after he arrived on 8 March. The early naval war therefore secured Japan's passage to Korea but did not eliminate the fleet at Port Arthur.

Section 61.8

Korean Neutrality, Japanese Military Occupation, and the Protocol of February 1904

Japanese troops entered Seoul on 9 February 1904, only weeks after Gojong's declaration of neutrality. The Russian minister left the capital, and the Korean government, with Japanese soldiers in the streets and Japanese ships in its harbors, had little room for refusal. On 23 February, Hayashi Gonsuke, the Japanese minister, and the acting Korean foreign minister, Yi Chi-yong, signed the Japan–Korea Protocol. The protocol bound Korea to accept Japanese advice on administrative improvements, pledged Japan to guarantee the security of the imperial house and the independence and territorial integrity of Korea, authorized Japan to take necessary measures if these were endangered and to occupy strategic points as required, and prohibited Korea from concluding agreements contrary to the protocol with third powers. In form it was an alliance; in substance it legitimized military occupation.

The consequences soon appeared. Japan took control of telegraph lines and railway construction, expanded its garrison in Korea, and imposed military-police authority in and around Seoul, with martial-law measures along its lines of communication. A first Japan–Korea Agreement of August 1904 compelled Korea to accept a Japanese financial adviser (Megata Tanetarō) and a foreign-affairs adviser (the American Durham Stevens) and to consult Japan before concluding foreign treaties. Koreans did not remain passive: when Japanese interests proposed taking over uncultivated land in 1904, a public protest movement forced its withdrawal, and newspapers such as the Hwangsŏng sinmun and the Daehan maeil sinbo (founded in July 1904 by the British journalist Ernest Bethell with Korean colleagues) criticized Japanese policy, until Japanese censorship tightened. A pro-Japanese organization, the Ilchinhoe, formed in August 1904 and supplied labor to the army, showing that Korean society contained collaborators and opponents as well as people simply trying to survive.

Section 61.9

The Battle of the Yalu River and the Advance onto the Liaodong Peninsula

The First Army under General Kuroki Tamemoto moved north through Korea in the spring of 1904 and approached the Yalu River, where a Russian Eastern Detachment under General Mikhail Zasulich held positions around Jiuliancheng (Chiu-lien-ch'eng). On the night of 30 April and 1 May, Japanese engineers bridged the river and Japanese artillery, supported by an outflanking maneuver, drove the Russians from their lines. Japanese casualties were about a thousand; Russian losses were roughly double. The battle was small by the standards of later campaigns, but it was widely celebrated as a landmark Japanese victory over a European army, and contemporary observers saw it as evidence of Japan’s growing military strength. It opened Manchuria to Japanese advance and encouraged Japan's financial supporters abroad.

The Second Army under General Oku Yasukata landed at Pitsewo (Biziwo) on 5 May and moved to cut the Liaodong Peninsula at its narrowest point, where the Russians held the fortified heights of Nanshan. On 26 May the Japanese attacked repeatedly across open ground against entrenched positions and machine guns and succeeded only after heavy losses, over four thousand killed and wounded, among them the elder son of General Nogi Maresuke. The Russians withdrew; Japan occupied Dalny (Dalian) on 30 May and cut Port Arthur's land connection with Manchuria. The Third Army, under Nogi, formed in May for the siege, while Oku's army advanced north and defeated a Russian relief force at Telissu in mid-June. By summer, the Russians had been driven from southern Liaodong, but the strongest fortress remained.

Section 61.10

The Early Naval War: Blockade, Mines, the Yellow Sea, and Ulsan

The naval war in the spring and summer of 1904 turned on mines, a weapon used extensively by both sides. On 13 April the battleship Petropavlovsk, flagship of Admiral Makarov, struck a Japanese mine outside Port Arthur and sank, killing Makarov and hundreds of his men, including the war artist Vasily Vereshchagin. Russian offensive spirit in the fleet declined. On 15 May the Japanese lost two of their six battleships, Hatsuse and Yashima, to Russian mines in a single day, and the cruiser Yoshino sank in a collision; Japan concealed the loss of Yashima for some time. The Vladivostok cruiser squadron sortied against Japanese shipping and in June sank the transport Hitachi Maru, killing many soldiers of the Imperial Guard, demonstrating that Japan's sea lines of communication remained vulnerable.

On 10 August Rear-Admiral Wilgelm Vitgeft led the Port Arthur squadron to sea, ordered to make for Vladivostok. Tōgō intercepted it in the Battle of the Yellow Sea. Late in the engagement a shell killed Vitgeft on his flagship and threw the Russian line into confusion; most ships returned to Port Arthur, while a few escaped to neutral ports and were interned. Four days later, on 14 August, Vice-Admiral Kamimura Hikonojō's squadron defeated the Vladivostok cruisers off Ulsan, sinking the Rurik. These battles ended the Russian Pacific Squadron's freedom of movement and removed the immediate threat to Japanese transport, but did not remove the fleet in being at Port Arthur. The capture of the high ground overlooking the harbor, described in the next division, would finish the task.

Section 61.11

The Siege of Port Arthur: Nogi Maresuke, Russian Defenses, and Attrition

Port Arthur was a naval base and fortress whose defenders had spent years improving a ring of forts, redoubts, trenches, and batteries on the hills surrounding the harbor. Its garrison, initially of about forty thousand and later more, was commanded nominally by General Anatoly Stessel, while the practical direction of the defense lay with General Roman Kondratenko, an engineer who organized counter-mining, improvised weapons such as mortars and hand grenades, and maintained the troops' morale. The Japanese Third Army under General Nogi Maresuke, with Major-General Ijichi Kōsuke as chief of staff, was ordered to take the fortress quickly, both to eliminate the fleet and to free its soldiers for the northern campaign. The assumption that a rapid assault could succeed proved mistaken.

The first general assault in August 1904 failed with casualties on the order of fifteen thousand. Subsequent attacks in September and October gained little ground. The attackers learned to advance by sapping, tunneling, and mining, and in the autumn began to use heavy 28-centimeter howitzers, originally coastal defense guns, brought from Japan. Both sides endured disease, exposure, and food shortages, scurvy afflicting the garrison in particular. A memoir by Lieutenant Sakurai Tadayoshi, Nikudan ("Human Bullets," 1906), written by a survivor of the assaults, became one of the most widely read war books in Japan and was translated into English; as a source it offers vivid testimony from a junior officer while also promoting an ideal of self-sacrifice, and historians read it with attention to both aspects. Nogi's critics later accused him of wasting lives; defenders pointed to the difficulty of the position and to the pressure from headquarters for speed.

Section 61.12

203-Meter Hill, the Destruction of the Russian Squadron, and the Fall of Port Arthur

The fourth general assault began on 26 November. The Japanese navy, anxious about the arrival of the Baltic Fleet, pressed the army to take 203-Meter Hill, the height from which spotters could observe the harbor and direct artillery fire on the Russian ships. Fierce fighting around the summit lasted from late November until 5 December, with attackers and defenders repeatedly recapturing parts of the crest at great cost. General Kodama Gentarō, chief of staff of the Manchurian Army, visited the front, and tradition holds that he redirected the attack to the hill; historians continue to debate how much responsibility for the decision lay with Kodama and how much with Nogi's own staff and the navy. Once the hill was in Japanese hands, howitzer fire sank or crippled the Russian battleships in the harbor within days. The last major vessel, Sevastopol, was torpedoed repeatedly before being scuttled at the surrender.

Kondratenko was killed by a shell on 15 December, a heavy blow to the defense. On 1 January 1905 Stessel offered to capitulate, and terms were agreed on 2 January; Nogi and Stessel met at Shuishiying on 5 January in an encounter that became a symbol of chivalry in Japanese accounts. Japanese casualties in the siege are commonly estimated at over fifty thousand killed and wounded, including many dead of disease; tens of thousands of Russians, many of them sick or wounded, became prisoners. Stessel was court-martialed in Russia in 1908 and sentenced to death, a sentence commuted before his release. The controversy over Nogi's command was reshaped by later popular culture: Shiba Ryōtarō's novel Saka no ue no kumo (serialized 1968–1972) portrayed him as an unimaginative commander, while historians have emphasized shared responsibility, intelligence failures, and the limits of the Army's artillery. Nogi's later suicide at the death of the Meiji Emperor in 1912 further transformed him into a symbol, a subject beyond this chapter.

Cause and Effect

The Siege of Port Arthur and the Consequences of Its Surrender

Long-term causes

Russia's 1898 lease and fortification of the base; Japan's belief that a fleet in Port Arthur could cut its sea routes; the lesson of 1895 that gains in Liaodong needed permanent strength.

Short-term causes

The failure to destroy the fleet by naval action in 1904; the approach of the Baltic Fleet; Army and Navy pressure for a rapid fall; underestimation of the fortress.

Event

Months of assaults, sapping, and artillery; the capture of 203-Meter Hill; the destruction of the fleet; Stessel's surrender on 2 January 1905.

Immediate effects

The Russian Pacific Squadron ceased to exist as a fighting force; the Third Army could move north to join the Manchurian campaign; Russian public confidence declined.

Long-term consequences

Japan acquired a base and a lease on Liaodong; the Baltic Fleet's mission became an attempt to reach Vladivostok; the Army drew lessons about offensive spirit and sacrifice that shaped later doctrine.

Historical complexity

Port Arthur was both a naval and a land problem; its capture depended on artillery, logistics, command decisions, and Russian failures as well as infantry sacrifice. Interpretations of the costs remain contested.
Section 61.13

Liaoyang: Ōyama Iwao, Kuropatkin, and the Battle of 1904

Marshal Ōyama Iwao, appointed commander of the Manchurian Army in the summer of 1904, combined the First, Second, and Fourth Armies to advance on Liaoyang, the railway junction where General Aleksey Kuropatkin concentrated his force. The two armies were roughly comparable in size, with the Russians somewhat larger, though the Japanese believed themselves outnumbered. Kuropatkin's strategy was to trade space for time, avoid decisive defeat, and gather reinforcements along the Trans-Siberian Railway until Russia could take the offensive, a strategy at odds with the aggressive views of Viceroy Alekseyev and of the court.

The battle lasted from 24 August to 4 September. Japanese frontal attacks suffered heavy losses, while a flanking movement by Kuroki's army across the Taizi River on 1 September threatened Russian communications. Kuropatkin, uncertain of the numbers opposing him, withdrew northward toward Mukden in good order on 3–4 September. Each side lost on the order of twenty thousand men, and the Japanese held the field but failed to destroy their opponent. Japan's ammunition stocks were severely depleted. The campaign established a pattern: Japanese armies won the ground but not the annihilation they sought, and Kuropatkin's retreat, defended in Russia as prudent, was attacked as timidity.

Section 61.14

The Sha-ho and Sandepu Battles: Stalemate and Exhaustion

In October 1904, with reinforcements arriving, Kuropatkin ordered an offensive south from Mukden against the Japanese positions along the Sha-ho River. The battle (5–17 October) was fought across hills and villages and ended without a breakthrough; Russian losses were on the order of forty thousand, Japanese about half that. Both sides entrenched. As winter came, front lines hardened into trench systems, with barbed wire, field fortifications, and telephone communications, and conditions in sub-zero temperatures imposed severe suffering. The war on the Manchurian front became a war of positions in which neither side had the strength to decide the matter alone.

In late January 1905 Russia attempted another offensive, led by General Oskar Grippenberg of the newly formed Second Manchurian Army, against the Japanese left near Sandepu (Heikoutai). Poor coordination and Kuropatkin's lack of support turned the attack into a costly failure; Grippenberg, embittered, resigned and returned to Russia, and public quarrels among Russian commanders were exposed in the press. Sandepu cost both sides several thousand casualties. The two battles taught Japanese commanders that they could hold against Russian attacks, but also that further offensives would cost heavily. Russia's inability to use its superiority in numbers, and Japan's inability to destroy Russia's armies, defined the strategic stalemate that made Mukden the climactic land battle.

Section 61.15

The Battle of Mukden and the Collapse of Russia's Manchurian Position

Mukden (20 February–10 March 1905) was the largest battle fought anywhere in the world to that date, with over 250,000 Japanese and more than 300,000 Russians engaged along a front of roughly a hundred miles. Ōyama and Kodama planned a wide encirclement. Kawamura Kageaki's newly formed Yalu Army attacked on the Russian left in the east, drawing Kuropatkin's reserves, while Nogi's Third Army, transferred from Port Arthur, swung around the Russian right in the west and threatened the railway north of Mukden. Kuropatkin's decisions to shift reserves, his uncertainty about Japanese intentions, and the chaos of the final retreat turned a hard-fought battle into a Russian defeat.

On 10 March Japanese forces entered Mukden, and the Russian armies withdrew northward toward Tieling and Siping, harassed but not annihilated. Estimates of losses vary widely: roughly seventy to seventy-five thousand Japanese and ninety thousand or more Russians killed, wounded, and captured. The Japanese Army, which had lost many of its trained officers and was short of ammunition and reserves, could not pursue effectively. Kuropatkin was replaced by General Nikolai Linevich, and the Russian army remained a powerful force. The Japanese army marked 10 March as Army Day (Rikugun kinenbi) until 1945. For the people of Mukden, formerly the Qing dynasty's secondary capital, the battle meant a transfer of occupation from one foreign army to another, and for the Qing court it was a demonstration of its inability to protect its own homeland.

Section 61.16

Railways, Artillery, Communications, and the Logistics of Modern War

The war was decided as much by supply as by maneuver. Russia's armies depended on the Trans-Siberian and Chinese Eastern Railways, whose capacity increased from a few trains per day at the outset to a larger flow by 1905, but the line remained a bottleneck for troops, food, and ammunition across nearly six thousand miles. Japan depended on sea transport to Korea and on railways: the Seoul–Pusan line was completed by the beginning of 1905, while military engineers built the Seoul–Uiju line toward the Yalu and light railways to the front, supplemented by Chinese carts and Korean porters. The Yawata Steel Works, opened in 1901, and the arsenals at Osaka and Tokyo, together with imported equipment and coal from Kyushu, supplied the army and navy; shell expenditure repeatedly exceeded forecasts.

The weapons of the war pointed toward the First World War: quick-firing field guns with recoil mechanisms, machine guns, barbed wire, magazine rifles, smokeless powder, telegraphs, field telephones, and indirect artillery fire directed by observers. Medical services also modernized: the Japanese Army's doctors, including the novelist-physician Mori Ōgai, applied bacteriological methods, yet the army's reliance on polished white rice contributed to hundreds of thousands of cases of beriberi, a nutritional disease whose causes the navy had already addressed by changing diets. Foreign military attachés and correspondents observed the war closely and drew varied conclusions, some emphasizing the power of defensive firepower and others the morale of the offensive. The war's lessons were contested, and later armies chose from them selectively.

Section 61.17

The War in Chinese and Korean Communities: Civilians, Labor, and Displacement

In February 1904 the Qing government declared neutrality and, under pressure from the belligerents, designated an area west of the Liao River as a neutral zone; the territory east of it, including the principal battlefields and the cities of Liaoyang, Mukden, and Dalian, was in effect abandoned to the armies. Chinese villagers saw their grain, fodder, draft animals, and carts requisitioned by both sides, their fields trampled, and their villages burned or destroyed in fighting. Both armies employed Chinese guides, interpreters, and laborers, and both recruited or armed irregular bands, often local militia or honghuzi ("bandit") groups, whose loyalties were fluid. Suspected spies were executed by each side, and civilians accused of assisting the enemy were liable to summary justice. Missionaries and doctors, such as the Scottish physician Dugald Christie in Mukden, recorded relief work and suffering among refugees.

In Korea, the Japanese army requisitioned land for railways and military installations, and recruited laborers, sometimes by contract and sometimes by compulsion, to build roads and carry supplies. The Ilchinhoe organized workers for the army in northern provinces. Russian Cossack patrols crossed the Yalu in the spring, prompting flight from northern towns. Records of these experiences are limited. Japanese and Russian official histories describe operations in detail but only incidentally treat civilians; Chinese and Korean sources include scattered petitions, local records, newspaper reports, and missionary accounts. Historians therefore use the sources critically, asking whose voices are absent, and avoid assuming that the silence of the records means that civilians experienced the war passively.

Everyday Life and Environment

Chinese and Korean Civilians in a War Zone

Villages in southern Manchuria and northwestern Korea lived through requisition, labor demands, and the movement of armies across their fields in the planting and harvest seasons. Households sold or hid grain and animals, families moved to cities or distant relatives, and workers contracted with armies for wages that were uncertain in value. The dense network of small farms, carts, and market towns that had grown up in Manchuria with the Han migration of previous decades became part of the supply system of both armies. Winter conditions imposed hardship on soldiers and civilians alike, with fuel scarcity, disease, and damage to housing. For many families, survival depended on kinship networks and on whichever authority could provide protection, whether a local magistrate, a missionary hospital, or an army commander offering payment.

At the same time, some inhabitants found opportunity: carters, interpreters, merchants who supplied the armies, and laborers who earned wages. The effects of the war on land ownership, on migration patterns, and on political attitudes toward foreign powers lasted beyond 1905. Students should consider how the experience of foreign armies on their land shaped Chinese and Korean views of Japan and Russia, and why the surviving evidence tends to emphasize the soldiers' perspective.

Section 61.18

The Russian Baltic Fleet's Voyage to East Asia

In the spring of 1904 the Russian government decided to send the Baltic Fleet to the Pacific, designating it the Second Pacific Squadron under Vice-Admiral Zinovy Rozhestvensky. Its original purpose was to relieve Port Arthur and gain command of the sea; after the destruction of the Port Arthur squadron and the fall of the fortress, its purpose shifted to reaching Vladivostok. The squadron sailed from Libau in mid-October 1904 with ships of uneven quality, many untested crews, and few opportunities for gunnery training. Its voyage of roughly eighteen thousand miles became one of the longest in naval history up to that time.

The voyage began with a crisis. On the night of 21–22 October, in the North Sea near the Dogger Bank, nervous Russian gunners fired on British fishing trawlers from Hull, killing two fishermen, and also fired on their own cruisers. Britain mobilized its fleet and demanded satisfaction; an international commission and Russian compensation resolved the incident, but it showed how the war could spill into Europe. The ships divided, some passing through the Suez Canal and others around the Cape of Good Hope, and reunited at Madagascar. Coaling at sea depended on German colliers under contract, and French authorities, observing neutrality, limited the squadron's stays in their colonial ports, including at Cam Ranh Bay, where the Third Pacific Squadron under Rear-Admiral Nikolai Nebogatov joined in May 1905. Throughout, Russian sailors endured heat, disease, rumors of Japanese torpedo boats, and the news from Port Arthur and Mukden. Rozhestvensky had to choose among the Korea Strait, with Tsushima, and longer routes through the Tsugaru or Sōya Straits. He chose the shortest.

Historical Vocabulary

Combined Fleet, Blockade, Squadron, Coaling, Sea Lines of Communication

Combined Fleet and sea lines of communication

The Combined Fleet (Rengō kantai) was the Japanese navy's wartime formation assembling its main battle fleets under a single commander, Tōgō Heihachirō. A squadron is a tactical grouping of warships, as in the Russian Pacific or Baltic squadrons. A blockade prevents an enemy's ships from leaving port or neutral shipping from reaching it. Coaling was the laborious process of loading coal for steam-powered ships, which made fleets dependent on friendly or neutral ports and colliers. Sea lines of communication are the maritime routes by which armies receive supplies and reinforcements.

These terms matter because the war at sea was fought for control of lines of communication more than for prestige. Japan's army in Korea and Manchuria depended on the Korea Strait and the Yellow Sea, and a Russian fleet that could threaten those routes could have changed the course of the land war. Coaling shaped the Baltic Fleet's route and speed, tying a military operation to the politics of neutral ports and commercial contracts.

Section 61.19

The Battle of Tsushima, May 27–28, 1905

Tōgō had waited at Chinhae Bay on the Korean coast for the Russian fleet, with the main battle force of four battleships and Kamimura's armored cruisers, supported by many smaller cruisers and torpedo craft that scouted the straits. In the early hours of 27 May, the auxiliary cruiser Shinano Maru sighted the Russian ships in the fog, and wireless reports allowed Tōgō to put to sea. Rozhestvensky's fleet, with eight battleships, steamed in two columns toward the strait with a train of transports. In the early afternoon the Japanese main force met it, signaling to its ships that the nation's fate depended on the outcome. Tōgō executed a turn across the Russian line of advance, exposing his ships briefly to Russian fire but bringing a concentration of Japanese guns onto the leading Russian vessels. The flagship Knyaz Suvorov was crippled, Rozhestvensky was wounded, and the battleship Oslyabya was sunk early; others, including Borodino and Aleksandr III, were lost over the afternoon, with command passing among several hands as the fleet lost cohesion.

During the night, Japanese torpedo boats and destroyers attacked repeatedly and sank or crippled additional vessels. On the morning of 28 May, Admiral Nebogatov, with the remnants of the fleet, surrendered after finding himself surrounded; Rozhestvensky, wounded, was captured aboard a destroyer. A few Russian cruisers, including Aurora, Oleg, and Zhemchug, escaped to Manila and were interned; the cruiser Almaz and two destroyers reached Vladivostok. Russian losses included most of the fleet's battleships and about forty-five hundred dead, with some six thousand prisoners; Japan lost three torpedo boats and roughly a hundred men. The scale of the victory astonished observers and has few parallels in naval history.

Section 61.20

Naval Technology, Training, Intelligence, and the Meaning of Tsushima

Tsushima's outcome resulted from the interaction of many factors. Japanese ships were faster than the Russian fleet, which was slowed by transports and by overloaded coal bunkers, and Tōgō's squadrons could therefore choose their position. Japanese crews had trained intensively and had gained battle experience since February 1904; many Russian crews had not practiced gunnery or fleet maneuver. Japanese shells, filled with a powerful high explosive (shimose), caused extensive fires and damage on exposed superstructures, while Russian shells, which used a different explosive, often caused less damage. Rangefinders, better fire control, and effective use of wireless telegraphy, including an indigenous Japanese set, supported coordination. Command also mattered: Rozhestvensky's wound and the confusion of succession left Russian ships without direction, while Tōgō and his staff maintained control.

The battle influenced naval planners across the world. Observers emphasized long-range gunnery with heavy guns, which influenced the design of the all-big-gun battleship Dreadnought (1906), though the ship's design was also driven by developments already underway in Britain and the United States. Torpedoes and mines contributed to the outcome but did not decide it alone. Historians warn against attributing the victory to one weapon or one maneuver: it arose from training, speed, communication, intelligence, and the strategic fact that the Russians were an exhausted fleet far from home.

Section 61.21

The Sakhalin Campaign and the Last Military Operations of 1905

With the peace discussions approaching, the Japanese government wished to hold territory as a bargaining chip. On 7 July 1905 the 13th Division landed on Sakhalin at Aniva Bay and moved against a poorly prepared Russian defense, which included convicts and local volunteers. By the end of the month the island's Russian commander had surrendered. Japan had historic interests in Sakhalin: in 1875 it had exchanged its claims there for the Kuril Islands, and since then Japanese fishermen had worked its waters. Japan also moved forces in northern Korea toward the Tumen River in summer 1905.

Sakhalin was inhabited by Ainu, Nivkh, and Uilta peoples, by Japanese and Russian fishers and settlers, and by convicts of the Russian penal colony. The campaign was not a clash for an empty territory: it affected communities who had no part in the diplomacy that decided its division. The island's occupation shows how military action served diplomatic objectives. It improved Japan's negotiating position, but it did not remove the financial pressures that would determine its willingness to compromise.

For Indigenous residents, the change of sovereignty crossed longstanding networks of fishing, hunting, migration, and exchange. Some Sakhalin Ainu families had been displaced to Hokkaidō after the 1875 territorial settlement, and some returned following Japan's acquisition of southern Sakhalin in 1905. Nivkh and Uilta communities had their own settlements and trading relationships, which cannot be understood merely as parts of Russian or Japanese national populations. The new border and competing colonial administrations constrained mobility and access to resources over time; neither empire consulted these communities at Portsmouth. Their experiences differed, and the treaty's line at the fiftieth parallel did not mark an Indigenous cultural boundary.

Section 61.22

Financing the War: Takahashi Korekiyo, Foreign Loans, and Japan's Fiscal Limits

Japan's expenditures on the war far exceeded its ordinary revenues. The economic historian Mark Metzler estimates total war spending at about ¥1.9 billion, roughly forty percent of which was financed by borrowing abroad. The government raised domestic taxes through an extraordinary special tax law, introduced a tobacco monopoly and, in 1905, a salt monopoly, and sold domestic bonds to banks and the public through campaigns of patriotic saving. These measures burdened rural households and small businesses and set the stage for later political protests over taxation. Japan's adherence to the gold standard, established in 1897, was meant to assure foreign lenders but constrained its monetary policy.

Takahashi Korekiyo, then vice-governor of the Bank of Japan, traveled to London and New York in 1904 to raise loans. The first issue, in the spring of 1904, was floated in both cities at high interest; later issues in 1904 and 1905 were larger and carried lower rates as Japanese victories improved its credit. In New York, the banker Jacob Schiff of Kuhn, Loeb & Co. played a major role; his motives combined financial judgment with revulsion at Russian anti-Jewish persecution, notably the Kishinev pogrom of 1903. Russian borrowing, by contrast, depended heavily on French and German lenders. Foreign lending, however, cannot be equated with foreign government support: bankers responded to commercial incentives and public sentiment, while governments pursued diplomatic goals, and the lenders' interests did not always coincide with those of the British or American states. By mid-1905 Japan faced debt service obligations, a depleted army, and the need for a fourth loan, all pressures on its negotiators.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Japan's Capacity in 1894–1895 and 1904–1905

What changed?

  • The scale of mobilization, from a few hundred thousand men to roughly a million over the war
  • The cost, from a conflict financed largely by domestic bonds to one requiring heavy foreign borrowing
  • The navy, from largely imported vessels to a fleet with trained crews and domestic ammunition production
  • Industrial capacity, with the Yawata Steel Works and expanded arsenals
  • Diplomatic position, with an alliance with Britain instead of isolation

What remained continuous?

  • Dependence on imported ships, machinery, and capital
  • A government in which oligarchs, the military, and the bureaucracy dominated policy
  • Strategic concern with Korea as a security frontier
  • Heavy reliance on rural taxation and conscript labor

Who experienced change most directly?

Soldiers and their families, who bore the casualties; taxpayers and rural households; financiers; and Koreans and Chinese on whose territory the war was fought. Those closest to the court and central ministries saw greater continuity in political structure.

Why does the pattern matter?

The comparison shows that Japan's success rested on institution-building since 1895, not merely on battlefield spirit, and that the same capacities created pressures for further expansion and for political conflict at home.

Section 61.23

Japan at War: Newspapers, Patriotism, Women, Relief Work, and the Home Front

Newspapers expanded their circulation through war reporting, sending correspondents to the front and publishing extras, while photographs, lantern slides, and woodblock triptychs, including series by Kobayashi Kiyochika, brought images of battles to readers. The government censored military information, restricted correspondents, and used newspaper laws to punish opposition. Schools, local governments, women's associations, and neighborhood groups organized send-off ceremonies, collected donations and comfort bags for soldiers, and held memorials. The Patriotic Women's Association, founded in 1901, and the Japanese Red Cross, with its imperial patronage and a large membership, organized nursing and relief; Red Cross nurses served in hospitals and on hospital ships, extending women's public roles within a nationalist framework.

Opposition existed but was marginal and vulnerable. The Heimin shimbun of Kōtoku Shūsui and Sakai Toshihiko, which published antiwar essays and a translation of the Communist Manifesto in November 1904, was fined and suppressed and ceased publication in January 1905. The Christian Uchimura Kanzō argued for peace. In September 1904 the poet Yosano Akiko published, in the magazine Myōjō, a poem addressed to her brother at Port Arthur urging him not to die, which critics such as Ōmachi Keigetsu attacked as disloyal. Families of soldiers faced loss of labor, mourning, and uncertain relief from local governments. Social pressure to display patriotism was strong, and the shared experience of loss created a mass public that would soon protest the peace.

Section 61.24

Russia's Home Front, the Revolution of 1905, and the Crisis of Imperial Authority

The Russian government entered the war with a vast empire, a growing industrial economy, and serious domestic grievances: peasant poverty, industrial unrest, demands from zemstvo liberals for representation, nationalist movements in the borderlands, and revolutionary parties. War did not create these tensions, but military defeat and the cost of mobilization sharpened them. The assassination of Interior Minister Vyacheslav von Plehve in July 1904 showed the strength of revolutionary terrorism; the fall of Port Arthur dealt a blow to confidence; and on 22 January 1905 (Bloody Sunday), troops fired on a peaceful workers' procession led by Father Georgy Gapon in Saint Petersburg, igniting strikes and protests across the empire. In June the crew of the battleship Potemkin mutinied, and in October a general strike forced Nicholas II to issue the October Manifesto, promising civil liberties and an elected Duma.

Japan's intelligence officer Akashi Motojirō funded and contacted Russian opposition groups in Europe, though historians debate how much effect this had. The war weakened the tsarist government's prestige and finances but did not alone cause the revolution, which had deep domestic roots. For the diplomacy of 1905, the relevance of the unrest was that it limited Russia's ability to reinforce its armies and made Witte's and others' arguments for peace more persuasive, even though the tsar and some generals believed that Russia could still win on land. The empire's political capacity, rather than a collapse of its military power, shaped its willingness to negotiate.

Section 61.25

Casualties, Disease, Prisoners of War, and the Limits of Victory

Casualty figures for the war are uncertain. Estimates of Japanese deaths from combat and disease generally range between about eighty and ninety thousand, with several hundred thousand wounded and sick; Russian deaths are estimated somewhat lower, though Russian wounded and prisoners were numerous. Disease killed many: beriberi, typhoid, and dysentery afflicted the Japanese army, and the Russian army suffered from typhus and cold. Veterans returned maimed and in need of pensions, and the state's relief system was limited. The numbers underline why both governments sought an end to the fighting, while the figures themselves, issued by governments, require caution.

Japan held tens of thousands of Russian prisoners, commonly estimated at between seventy and eighty thousand, in camps across the home islands, notably at Matsuyama, and treated them in accordance with the 1899 Hague Convention in a way that Japanese authorities publicized to demonstrate their civilization; Russian officers captured at Port Arthur were released on parole. Russia held relatively few Japanese prisoners. Humanitarian practice was uneven: both armies executed suspected spies, and civilians suffered abuses that official histories rarely record.

Multiple Perspectives

Experiencing the War in Manchuria and Korea

Japanese soldiers:Conscripts and reservists endured long marches, frontal assaults, and disease; accounts such as Sakurai's celebrate sacrifice while also describing the horror of the assaults. Many families lost laborers and relied on community support.
Russian soldiers:Many were reservists transported thousands of miles by rail, serving under a divided command and in climates for which they were ill-equipped. After the defeats, unrest spread among returning troops, and some mutinied in 1905.
Korean residents:Koreans faced military occupation, land requisitions, forced or contracted labor, censorship, and the loss of foreign policy autonomy, while some collaborated and others resisted through petitions, newspapers, and protest.
Chinese civilians:Chinese villagers saw their lands become a battlefield. They bore requisition, burned villages, and suspicion by both armies, and some worked as carters, guides, or irregulars; Qing neutrality offered no protection.

Prisoners of war:Russian prisoners in Japanese camps experienced captivity under rules the government wished to display to the world, while few Japanese prisoners fell into Russian hands.

The groups did not hold equal power: armies decided where fighting occurred, while civilians had few choices and no voice in the peace.

Section 61.26

Theodore Roosevelt, Komura Jutarō, Sergei Witte, and the Negotiations for Peace

After Tsushima, Japan's leaders sought peace on terms they could defend. Through Komura and the envoy Kaneko Kentarō, a Harvard acquaintance of Theodore Roosevelt, they asked the American president to arrange talks, and Roosevelt, who valued a balance of power in East Asia and wished to avoid a collapse of either belligerent, proposed negotiations in June 1905; both governments accepted. The Japanese cabinet distinguished between "absolute" conditions (a free hand in Korea, Russian withdrawal from Manchuria, and transfer of Russia's Liaodong lease and railway) and "compensatory" conditions (an indemnity, the cession of Sakhalin, the surrender of interned ships, and limits on Russian naval power in the Far East). In July, Secretary of War William Howard Taft and Prime Minister Katsura held a conversation in Tokyo, recorded in a memorandum, in which Taft stated that, in his view, Japanese suzerainty over Korea was a logical result of the war and Katsura disclaimed designs on the Philippines. It was not a treaty. In August, a second Anglo-Japanese Alliance extended the alliance's scope and recognized Japan's paramount interests in Korea.

The conference met in the Portsmouth Naval Shipyard in Kittery, Maine, and at Portsmouth, New Hampshire, from 9 August. Komura, with Takahira Kogorō, presented Japan's demands; Witte, with Baron Roman Rosen, refused an indemnity and territorial cession, appealed to American public opinion, and held to Nicholas II's instructions. Talks neared collapse in late August. In Tokyo, an imperial conference on 28 August, aware of Japan's financial limits and the Army's condition, authorized Komura to abandon the indemnity and Sakhalin if necessary. On 29 August a compromise was reached: Russia would cede the southern half of Sakhalin and pay no indemnity. Roosevelt's mediation, Witte's public diplomacy, and Japan's need for peace all contributed. Japan accepted the compromise because it could not credibly continue a war that its finances and manpower could not sustain, and because the territorial and strategic goals it regarded as essential had been met.

Section 61.27

The Treaty of Portsmouth, September 5, 1905: Gains, Compromises, and Limits

The Treaty of Portsmouth, signed on 5 September 1905 and in force after ratification in October, combined recognition of Japanese gains with commitments about Manchuria. In Article II, Russia recognized Japan's paramount political, military, and economic interests in Korea and agreed not to obstruct Japanese measures of guidance, protection, and control there. Articles III and IV provided for the mutual evacuation of Manchuria, except the leased territory of Liaodong, and the restoration of those areas to Chinese administration, with Russia declaring that it held no exclusive concessions there. Articles V and VI transferred to Japan, with the consent of China, Russia's lease of Port Arthur and Dalian and the railway from Changchun to Port Arthur with its branches and associated mines; Article VII restricted the railways to commercial and industrial uses. Article IX ceded to Japan the southern half of Sakhalin, below the fiftieth parallel, with limits on fortification and guarantees of free navigation through the straits. Article XI gave Japanese subjects fishing rights along Russian coasts, and Article XIII provided for the return of prisoners with Russia reimbursing Japan the difference in maintenance costs. There was no indemnity.

The treaty's significance lay in what it did and did not do. It transferred rights Russia held from China, and China's consent was needed; the Beijing agreement of December 1905 secured it. It recognized Japanese primacy in Korea without any Korean representation. It left northern Manchuria's railway in Russian hands, creating spheres divided between the two powers. And it disappointed many Japanese expectations, which had been inflamed by victory announcements and nationalist press campaigns.

Section 61.28

The Hibiya Riots: Popular Nationalism and the Rejection of Peace

News of the terms reached Japan in late August and provoked anger. A coalition of nationalist politicians, journalists, and activists, the Anti-Peace Association (Kōwa mondai dōshi rengōkai), called a protest meeting at Hibiya Park in Tokyo for 5 September, though police closed the park. Crowds, which scholars such as Andrew Gordon have shown included many workers, artisans, and shopkeepers as well as students and activists, broke into the park and then moved through the city. They attacked the pro-government Kokumin shimbun office, the Home Minister's residence, police stations and police boxes, streetcars, and some churches. The government declared martial law in Tokyo on 6 September, and troops restored order after about three days of rioting. More than a dozen died, hundreds were injured, and over two thousand were arrested, with several hundred prosecuted.

The riots were an early instance of urban crowd politics in the constitutional era. Participants shared nationalist grievances about the absence of an indemnity, but also resentment of taxes, hardship, and elite distance from the sacrifices of ordinary people. The cabinet of Katsura resigned in January 1906, succeeded by Saionji Kinmochi's government, which began a period of alternation between the two men and their supporters. The riots also showed how war reporting, nationalism, and political mobilization could turn against the government that had encouraged them.

Section 61.29

Korea after Portsmouth: The Road to the Protectorate of 1905

With Russia's recognition, the British alliance's renewed terms, and the American conversation with Katsura, Japan faced no great-power obstacle to control of Korea. In November 1905, Itō Hirobumi arrived in Seoul as special envoy. After meetings with Gojong, in which the emperor evaded commitment, Japanese troops surrounded the palace on 17 November, and Itō and Minister Hayashi pressed the Korean cabinet to approve an agreement. The prime minister refused, but five ministers (later denounced in Korea as the "five Eulsa traitors") assented, and the Japanese side affixed the seal. The agreement transferred control of Korea's foreign relations to Japan, provided for a Japanese Resident-General in Seoul, and led to the withdrawal of foreign legations. Gojong never ratified it and later sought to repudiate it.

Korean resistance was immediate. Chang Chi-yŏn's editorial in the Hwangsŏng sinmun mourned the loss of sovereignty; the former minister Min Yŏng-hwan and others killed themselves in protest; and righteous armies (ŭibyŏng) began to form in the countryside. Earlier in the year, Korean emissaries including Syngman Rhee had appealed unsuccessfully to Roosevelt, and the United States closed its legation in Seoul within days of the agreement. The details of the protectorate's administration, the 1907 Hague mission, and the 1910 annexation belong to Chapter 62.

Comparative Perspectives

Protection or Coercion in Korea?

Korean accounts:Korean newspapers, officials, and later historians emphasize that the agreement was made under duress, with troops around the palace, without the emperor's seal or ratification, and that it destroyed sovereignty. Many Korean historians regard it as legally invalid.
Great-power views:Britain, the United States, and Russia had already accepted Japanese primacy and did not contest the agreement; Western diplomats withdrew, while some missionaries and journalists sympathized with Koreans but could not change policy.

The legal status of the treaty remains debated: later Japanese and Korean governments have interpreted the 1965 normalization treaty's reference to earlier agreements being "already null and void" in different ways.

Section 61.30

Southern Manchuria, Port Arthur, and Sakhalin in Japan's Imperial Strategy

Japan converted its new rights into institutions. The Liaodong leasehold became the Kwantung Leased Territory, governed from Port Arthur from 1906; the South Manchuria Railway Company, established in 1906 with Gotō Shinpei as first president, took over the southern railway and its mines, ports, and associated enterprises. The railway zone and the garrison that guarded it were the seeds of the later Kwantung Army. Japan negotiated with the Qing government in December 1905 to obtain consent for the transfers; Chinese officials protested but could not resist. Russia retained northern Manchuria's railway, and in 1907 Japan and Russia concluded agreements dividing their spheres. American hopes of commercial equality, including railway schemes of E. H. Harriman, met rebuffs, creating tensions in U.S.–Japanese relations.

In the north, Japan organized southern Sakhalin as Karafuto, with its own administration from 1907, encouraging Japanese fishing and settlement; Ainu and other indigenous residents were subject to Japanese policies, and some Russian residents left. The army's 1907 Imperial Defense Policy identified Russia and, increasingly, the United States as potential adversaries. Japan's new assets required garrisons and investment and created interests that expanded over time. Chinese protests, boycotts, and the rise of nationalist movements in Chinese cities soon followed.

Section 61.31

Japan as a Great Power: Global Reactions, Imperial Rivalry, and Historical Interpretation

Reactions were varied. In Japan, victory strengthened national confidence and the prestige of the military and the emperor, and the great powers gradually raised their legations in Tokyo to embassies. Across Asia and the Middle East, nationalists and reformers, including Sun Yat-sen, Jawaharlal Nehru, and Egyptian and Turkish activists, took inspiration from the defeat of a European empire. Chinese students traveled to Japan in large numbers, and Sun Yat-sen's Tongmenghui was founded in Tokyo in August 1905. For Koreans, however, the victory meant loss of sovereignty, and for Chinese the use of their territory as a battlefield and the transfer of Russian privileges to Japan complicated admiration. In Europe and America, some commentators spoke of a "yellow peril," while others praised Japan's modernization. In the United States, tensions over Japanese immigration, including the San Francisco school board dispute of 1906, soon followed. In Russia, defeat intensified revolution, reorientation toward the Balkans, and a diplomatic rapprochement with Japan in 1907.

Historical Debate

Decisive Triumph or Expensive, Limited Victory?

The question: Did Japan's victory represent a decisive geopolitical triumph, or an expensive and limited success that left its finances and army strained? The question matters because it shapes how historians interpret Japanese policy after 1905.

Interpretation A — A decisive triumph: Japan defeated a great power, secured Korea, obtained a naval base and railway, and won recognition as a great power, transforming East Asia's balance of power and its own diplomatic status.
Interpretation B — An expensive and limited victory: Japan's army was exhausted, its finances were burdened by heavy foreign debt, the indemnity was denied, Russia retained a large army, and the peace provoked riots. Victory also bound Japan to the costs of garrisons and imperial administration.

Evidence: The first position draws on the treaty's terms and international reactions; the second on financial data, casualty figures, the Hibiya riots, and the cabinet's own acknowledgments at the August 1905 imperial conference. Many historians combine the two readings.

Chapter Review and Historical Thinking Checkpoint

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the war fought between Japan and Russia in 1904–1905 and its immediate consequences. The war grew from the unfinished settlement of 1895. The Triple Intervention taught Japanese leaders that gains in Liaodong could be reversed by great-power coalitions, and Russia's subsequent acquisition of the Chinese Eastern Railway and the lease of Port Arthur and Dalian placed a rival power on the peninsula Japan had been forced to abandon. Yet the rivalry did not make war inevitable: Japan and Russia negotiated agreements in 1896 and 1898, and statesmen such as Itō Hirobumi preferred a bargain over a war.

Two disputes, which should be kept distinct, drove the conflict. The first concerned Russia's military occupation of Manchuria during and after the Boxer crisis, and its failure to complete the withdrawal promised in 1902. The second concerned influence in Korea, where the Korean Empire sought to preserve independence through reform and diplomacy but lacked the means to resist. Japanese leaders framed their claims in terms of security, but their demands also included economic privileges and the right to guide Korean administration. The Anglo-Japanese Alliance of 1902 reduced the risk of another intervention against Japan, and the failure of negotiations in 1903–1904 led the cabinet and the genrō to decide on war in February 1904.

The war opened with Japanese attacks at Port Arthur and Chemulpo and with the occupation of Korea, formalized in the Protocol of 23 February 1904 and the agreement of August 1904. Japanese armies defeated Russian forces at the Yalu and Nanshan, isolated Port Arthur, and advanced into Manchuria. At sea, mines and battles in the Yellow Sea and off Ulsan ended the freedom of movement of the Russian Pacific Squadron. At Port Arthur, Nogi's Third Army suffered immense casualties before the capture of 203-Meter Hill allowed artillery to destroy the fleet in the harbor and led to the surrender of January 1905.

On the Manchurian front, battles at Liaoyang, Sha-ho, Sandepu, and Mukden showed both the power of defense and the limits of Japanese resources. Japan won ground and forced Russia from southern Manchuria, but could not destroy Kuropatkin's army. The Battle of Tsushima, in which the Baltic Fleet was annihilated after its voyage of eighteen thousand miles, was the naval climax, and the occupation of Sakhalin completed Japan's military position. The logistics of the war, from railways to shell supply, shaped each belligerent's options.

The war was also a human and financial catastrophe. Japan spent about ¥1.9 billion, with a large share borrowed abroad, raised taxes, and mobilized newspapers, schools, women's associations, and the Red Cross, while socialists and Christians who opposed the war were suppressed. Tens of thousands died in combat and from disease, and Russian prisoners were held in Japan in camps that the government publicized as evidence of civilization. Chinese and Korean civilians suffered requisition, displacement, and violence in a war whose stakes were their own territory, and whose records are far less complete than those of the armies.

In Russia, defeats intensified existing grievances and contributed to the Revolution of 1905, although the war was not its sole cause. The tsarist government's weakened political capacity, together with Japan's financial and military limits, made peace possible. President Roosevelt's mediation, Komura's and Witte's negotiating, and the Japanese cabinet's decision to renounce an indemnity produced the Treaty of Portsmouth, which recognized Japanese primacy in Korea, transferred the Liaodong lease and the southern railway, and ceded southern Sakhalin, but required no Russian payment.

For many Japanese, the settlement was a betrayal of their sacrifices. The Hibiya riots of September 1905 expressed popular nationalism, class tension, and distrust of the government, and led to martial law and eventually to the fall of the Katsura cabinet. The riots were an early example of crowd politics in the constitutional period.

Japan's victory brought it great-power status and prompted admiration across Asia and the colonial world, but it also intensified coercion in Korea. Japanese troops surrounded the palace in November 1905 as the protectorate agreement was imposed, Gojong never ratified it, and Korean protest began at once. In Manchuria and Sakhalin, Japan established institutions, from the Kwantung Leased Territory to Karafuto, that expanded over time.

The principal historical interpretations discussed in the chapter treat the war as a triumph, a limited and costly victory, or both. The evidence supports a combined reading: Japan achieved a decisive strategic outcome, while the costs, the absence of an indemnity, and the consequences for others complicate any simple celebration. Teleological explanations should be avoided: Japanese victory was not predetermined, and the post-1905 expansion was not the only possible outcome.

The chapter's significance lies in the way it links earlier and later themes. It builds on the imperial expansion described in Chapter 60 and prepares for the colonial rule in Korea (Chapter 62), the labor and social conflict of Chapter 63, and the broader militarism and imperial expansion of the following decades.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how Japan's emergence as a great power was shaped by diplomacy, finance, and force, and by decisions made with limited resources. The war also reveals the ways in which an imperial victory could create both prestige and burdens. Japan's alliance with Britain, its railways and arsenals, and its borrowing in London and New York were as crucial as the courage and sacrifice of its soldiers, and the peace was shaped by the exhaustion that victory concealed.

The chapter connects regions that are often studied separately. The war was fought on Chinese and Korean territory, ended with the division of Sakhalin, and influenced Russia's revolution, British naval planning, American diplomacy, and anticolonial movements across Asia. At the same time, it shows that the interests of Koreans, Chinese, and Indigenous Sakhalin residents were treated as secondary by the powers negotiating their fate, a pattern that would continue in Korea and Manchuria.

Several continuities survived beyond 1905. The strategic reasoning that treated Korea as vital to Japanese security became a justification for deeper control; the institutions created in Manchuria and Karafuto expanded; the Army's experience of costly offensives shaped later doctrine; and the popular nationalism displayed at Hibiya remained a force in politics. These developments should not be read as inevitable steps toward later wars, but as conditions that constrained and encouraged particular choices.

Finally, the chapter invites students to hold two propositions together: that Japan's victory was a remarkable military and diplomatic achievement, and that it was bought at great human cost and through the coercion of others. Chapter 62 will follow the consequences of the second proposition in Korea, and Chapter 63 will examine the domestic social conflicts that accompanied the empire's growth.
