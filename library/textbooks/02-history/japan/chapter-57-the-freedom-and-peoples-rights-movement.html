Part VIII — Bakumatsu and the Meiji Revolution

The Freedom and People’s Rights Movement

Japanese History: From Jōmon Japan to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

In January 1874, eight men who had until recently helped govern Japan signed a petition arguing that the government they had served was ruled by a narrow circle of officials, and that a popularly chosen assembly was the remedy. The petition was not only handed to the authorities; it was printed in a newspaper. That second step mattered as much as the first, because it turned an argument within the governing class into a question addressed to the public.

The signers included Itagaki Taisuke, Gotō Shōjirō, Etō Shinpei, and Soejima Taneomi, men who had helped to overthrow the Tokugawa order and had held senior posts in the new regime. Only weeks earlier they had resigned from the government after a bitter dispute over policy toward Korea. Their petition asked that representatives be chosen by the people to take part in making laws and deciding taxes. It was submitted to a government advisory body and, within days, appeared in the press, where it provoked replies from journalists, scholars, and officials who agreed, disagreed, or argued that the country was not yet ready. This chapter begins with that act of publication because the movement it launched depended on newspapers, associations, public speeches, and petitions as much as on any individual leader.

The Freedom and People’s Rights Movement (Jiyū Minken Undō) is the name historians give to the loosely connected campaigns for representative government, constitutional rule, and civil liberties that spread across Japan between the mid-1870s and the late 1880s. It was never a single organization. Its participants included former samurai who had lost their stipends and status, prosperous farmers who paid heavy land taxes, urban journalists and teachers, merchants, students, a small number of women speakers, and, in the early 1880s, impoverished rural debtors whose grievances were economic as much as political. Some wanted a parliament that would check the oligarchs. Some wanted party government on the British model. A few drafted constitutions that recognized a right to resist oppressive rulers. Others sought relief from taxes and debt and had little interest in constitutional theory.

The chapter treats the movement as a problem of political mobilization, ideological argument, and struggle for power. Mobilization is the question of how people organized: how associations formed, how newspapers and public meetings created a political public, how petitions were gathered, and how regional networks coordinated or failed to coordinate. Ideological argument concerns what activists meant by liberty, rights, sovereignty, and representation, and how their constitutional visions differed from one another and from those of the government. The struggle for power concerns the government’s response, which combined concessions, co-optation, surveillance, press control, and force, and which shaped the movement’s fragmentation as constitutional government approached.

Two cautions run through the chapter. First, “people’s rights” was a slogan with a limited and contested meaning. Even activists who spoke of the people usually envisioned a franchise restricted by property, sex, or both, and the voices of women, tenant farmers, outcaste-origin communities, the Ainu of Hokkaidō, and the people of newly annexed Okinawa were rarely central to the movement’s program. Historians must avoid attributing to the movement democratic principles that its organizations did not clearly advocate. Second, the movement was neither uniformly peaceful nor uniformly violent. Most of its activity consisted of writing, speaking, organizing, and petitioning. A few episodes, notably those in Fukushima, Ibaraki, and Chichibu, involved armed confrontation, and the relationship of those episodes to the movement’s national leadership was complicated and in some cases distant.

The evidence is unusually rich. Historians can read the 1874 petition and the newspapers that printed and attacked it; the petitions of 1880; the minutes and ordinances of the government; the regulations through which police supervised meetings; the private constitutional drafts written by activists, one of which was found in a family storehouse in 1968; court records of the rebels tried in the 1880s; memoirs; satirical illustrated journals; and the writings of thinkers such as Fukuzawa Yukichi, Nakae Chōmin, and Ueki Emori. Each source has limits. Government records reflect the viewpoint of those who policed and prosecuted. Newspapers were partisan and subject to censorship. Memoirs written years afterward often shaped the movement’s memory as a heroic struggle.

This chapter links backward and forward in the textbook. Chapters 54 through 56 explained the abolition of the domains and of samurai privilege, the new schooling and legal order, and state-sponsored industrialization and finance; those developments created the grievances, social groups, and fiscal pressures on which the movement drew. Chapter 58 turns to Westernization and the debates over cultural identity. Chapter 59 will examine the Meiji Constitution, the electoral system, and the Imperial Diet in detail. Here the endpoint is a political transition, not a finished constitutional order: by 1890 Japan was about to have a parliament, but the question of who would hold real power under the new institutions remained unsettled, and the movement’s most democratic proposals had not been adopted.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did opposition to the Meiji government first emerge from among former members of that government, and how did a dispute over Korea become a debate over representation?
  2. How did newspapers, public speeches, and associations create a political public that did not exist in the Tokugawa period?
  3. What did activists mean by “liberty” and “people’s rights,” and how did their constitutional proposals differ from one another?
  4. Why did the movement draw such different social groups, and how did their goals converge and diverge?
  5. What was the relationship between the appointed institutions the government created and the elected assembly activists demanded?
  6. Did popular agitation compel the government to promise a national assembly in 1881, or did the ruling elite direct constitutional development on its own timetable?
  7. How did government policy combine concession and repression, and how did each shape the movement?
  8. How did economic crisis in the countryside change the character of political mobilization, and how should historians relate local uprisings to the national movement?
  9. Who was left out of the movement’s idea of “the people,” and what does that reveal about its limits?

Historical Context

The Meiji government that took shape after 1868 claimed legitimacy in the name of the young emperor, but day-to-day authority lay with a small group of men drawn mainly from the former domains of Satsuma, Chōshū, Tosa, and Hizen, together with a few court nobles. Critics would later describe this group with the term hanbatsu, “domain clique,” a label that conveys real patterns of recruitment and patronage but also simplifies a leadership whose members often disagreed with one another. In the early 1870s this leadership carried out sweeping changes: abolition of the domains in 1871, creation of prefectures, the new land-tax system of 1873, conscription, and the dismantling of samurai privilege, all discussed in Chapters 54 through 56. These reforms centralized the state and reduced the hereditary standing of the warrior class. They also left unanswered the question of who, beyond the officials in Tokyo, would have a voice in governing.

The Charter Oath of 1868 had promised that deliberative assemblies would be widely established and that important matters would be decided by public discussion, but its wording was general enough to support very different readings. For the officials it could mean consultation among the elite; for later activists it was a promise of representative government that the oligarchs had failed to keep. Short-lived deliberative bodies of domain delegates in 1869 and 1870 faded away, and the government created in 1871 concentrated power in the Council of State (Dajōkan) and its ministries. The social groups most affected by the reforms were not passive. Former samurai (now classified as shizoku) experienced the loss of stipends and monopoly on military service as a collapse of status. Wealthy farmers (gōnō), who often served as village or district leaders, bore the cash land tax and watched officials from outside the locality set local policy. Merchants, teachers, and local intellectuals saw new schools, newspapers, and translated books open possibilities for participation that the old order had denied them.

Intellectual change also mattered. Translations and commentaries introduced the writings of John Stuart Mill, Herbert Spencer, Rousseau, Montesquieu, and others; Nakamura Masanao’s 1872 rendering of Mill’s On Liberty was among the most widely read early examples. The Meirokusha, a society of scholars and officials founded in 1873, debated enlightenment, rights, and government in a journal. Fukuzawa Yukichi’s works popularized the idea that national strength depended on the independence of the individual. Intellectuals were not unanimous: some argued that popular participation should wait until the public was educated, while others held that rights were natural and inalienable. This debate shaped the arguments with which the movement began.

International events formed another part of the setting. Japan was conducting a hard bargain over the unequal treaties, observing European parliaments on the Iwakura Mission of 1871–73, and watching tensions with Korea and China. The unfulfilled aim of treaty revision gave constitutional government a diplomatic meaning, since Western governments tended to treat a constitutional system as a sign of a “civilized” state. At the same time, Hokkaidō was being developed by a special Colonization Commission, and the Ryukyu Kingdom would be absorbed as Okinawa Prefecture in 1879. These regions, and their Ainu and Ryukyuan inhabitants, stood outside the debates over representation that preoccupied activists in Tokyo, Osaka, and the provinces, a fact that this chapter returns to at several points.

Division I · Section 57.1

The Meiji Restoration and the Problem of Political Representation

The government proclaimed in the Charter Oath of April 1868 that deliberative assemblies would be widely established and that matters of state would be decided by public discussion. The oath was a statement of the new regime’s aims, not a constitution, and its meaning depended on who read it. The phrase translated here as “public discussion” (kōgi yoron) had roots in the late Tokugawa debates over whether the shogunate should consult the daimyō and the imperial court. In 1868 it signaled that decision-making would extend beyond the shogun’s officials, but it did not say how far or through what institutions. Early experiments in consultation, including the Kōgisho of 1869, which gathered domain representatives, produced little lasting power, and the abolition of the domains in 1871 removed the constituency on which such an assembly had been based.

Meanwhile real authority gathered in the Council of State and the ministries, headed by officials who spoke in the emperor’s name. This arrangement carried a tension that runs through the chapter. The reforms of the early 1870s required a strong center, and those who carried them out argued that effective modernization could not wait for popular consent. The same reforms, however, imposed obligations such as conscription, a cash land tax, and compulsory schooling on people who had no formal say in the policies. When critics later wrote that the country was governed by a small clique of officials, they were pointing to a gap between the Charter Oath’s language and the institutional reality.

Historians should be careful with the term “representation” in this period. The Meiji leaders did not oppose consultation in principle; many of them, including Itō Hirobumi and Kido Takayoshi, believed that Japan would eventually need a constitution and an assembly. The disagreement was about timing, composition, and power. Officials generally preferred gradual development in which the government controlled the pace and in which representatives would advise rather than govern. Critics asked for a faster timetable and for an assembly that would reflect the choices of taxpayers. This was an argument among people who often shared a commitment to a strong state, and it became sharper only when the Restoration leadership itself divided.

Division I · Section 57.2

The Political Crisis of 1873 and the Emergence of Organized Opposition

In 1871 a large group of leaders including Iwakura Tomomi, Ōkubo Toshimichi, Kido Takayoshi, and Itō Hirobumi left Japan on a diplomatic and study mission to the United States and Europe. The caretaker government left behind, which included Saigō Takamori, Itagaki Taisuke, Ōkuma Shigenobu, Etō Shinpei, Gotō Shōjirō, and Soejima Taneomi, pushed ahead with major reforms, and in the summer of 1873 it moved toward sending an envoy to Korea, where the Joseon court had refused to accept Japan’s new style of diplomatic communication. Saigō volunteered to go; a Korean refusal or violence might provide a reason for war. The details of this diplomacy and its relation to Japanese expansion belong to Chapter 60. What matters here is that when Iwakura, Ōkubo, and Kido returned, they argued that domestic reform, finance, and the unfinished treaty question required priority over a Korean expedition.

The dispute was decided in October 1873 when the cabinet, with the emperor’s endorsement of the returned leaders’ view, rejected the envoy plan. Saigō, Itagaki, Etō, Gotō, and Soejima resigned. This rupture is often called the Seikanron (“Korea conquest debate”) crisis, though historians have long argued over how far the argument was about conquest, about the treatment of former samurai who might have found employment in a campaign, or about the balance of power among the leaders. The departure left Ōkubo as the dominant figure in the government and sent the dissenters in different directions. Saigō returned to Kagoshima, where he would become the center of the resistance that erupted in the Satsuma Rebellion of 1877 (Chapter 54). Etō, who shared a concern for former samurai, returned to Saga and was soon drawn into armed revolt.

Itagaki and several others chose a different instrument. They did not turn on the government with force but began to argue that the structure of government, not just a decision about Korea, was the problem. Their motives were mixed, and it would be wrong to assume that all were moved by a principled commitment to popular sovereignty. They were former leaders who had lost a contest for power and who recognized that a public appeal could provide leverage. At the same time, they used the language of rights and representation, and in doing so they opened a new kind of politics. The transition from the elite disagreement of October 1873 to the public petition of January 1874 marks the beginning of organized political opposition in the Meiji state.

Division I · Section 57.3

The 1874 Petition for a Popularly Elected Assembly

In January 1874 a group that included Itagaki Taisuke, Gotō Shōjirō, Etō Shinpei, Soejima Taneomi, and four others formed a small society called Aikoku Kōtō (the Public Party of Patriots), and eight of them signed a petition dated January 17. They presented it to the Left Chamber (Sain), one of the government’s advisory organs. The petition was soon printed in the Nisshin Shinjishi, a newspaper published in Tokyo by the British journalist John Reddie Black. Foreign-owned papers had some protection from the government’s control of the Japanese-language press, which made them useful outlets, and the text circulated widely.

Its argument was straightforward. The petitioners claimed that political power had fallen into the hands of officials, neither the emperor above nor the people below, and that this condition would lead to the decline of the state. Their remedy was to establish a council of representatives chosen by the people, and they grounded the claim in a principle of taxation: those who pay taxes have a right to share in government and to approve or reject its policies. The petition did not demand universal suffrage or the end of the emperor’s authority; it appealed to the emperor’s legitimacy and to the unity of the “sovereign and the people.” It also argued that an assembly would strengthen the country, a point of appeal to officials who might be sympathetic to national strength more than to rights.

The publication provoked a debate often called the minsen giin controversy. The government did not adopt the proposal. Prominent intellectuals associated with the Meirokusha, including Katō Hiroyuki, argued that an assembly was premature because the people had not yet been educated enough to exercise political judgment, while others, including some journalists, defended the principle. The argument that “the time is not ripe” would remain a standard reply to calls for representation for years. The petition’s immediate effect was not institutional change but the creation of a public argument and a political vocabulary, and Etō’s subsequent revolt in Saga in February 1874, which ended in his execution, quickly showed that not all former officials chose to work through petitions.

Historical Vocabulary
Minsen giin (民撰議院)

Minsen giin means roughly “popularly chosen assembly” or “assembly selected by the people.” It was the term used in the 1874 petition and the controversy that followed. The word “people” (min) did not necessarily mean all adults; participants in the debate imagined different electorates, and the petition itself did not specify one. The term differs from kokkai (“national assembly”), which became the usual word for a parliament, and from giin (member of an assembly).

The phrase matters because it fixed the issue in terms of who selects representatives, not merely whether the government should consult opinion. Modern readers should not assume that it implied a modern parliamentary system; the petition did not detail the assembly’s powers, its relationship to the emperor, or the franchise.

Primary Source Window

The Petition for a Popularly Elected Assembly, January 1874

Creator: Eight former senior officials, including Itagaki Taisuke, Gotō Shōjirō, Etō Shinpei, and Soejima Taneomi · Date: dated January 17, 1874; printed in the press shortly afterward · Type: Political petition to a government advisory body, republished as a newspaper text

Context: The petition was written within weeks of the signers’ resignations from government in the Korea dispute. It was addressed to a body inside the government but also released to the public, and the newspaper publication gave it a much larger audience than the officials who received it. Digitized copies and related materials are held in Japanese national archival collections, including the National Diet Library and the National Archives of Japan; students should consult a reliable edition and translation instead of relying on summaries.

Paraphrase (not a verbatim quotation): The petitioners argue that power has come to rest neither with the emperor nor with the people but with a body of officials, that such rule will lead the state toward collapse, and that the remedy is an assembly chosen by the people, because those who pay taxes have the right to take part in government and to approve or reject its decisions.

Analysis: The petition shows how its authors combined several kinds of argument. It used the language of rights and taxation, drawn partly from Western political thought, while remaining careful to present an assembly as consistent with imperial rule. It criticized the oligarchs without questioning the legitimacy of the monarchy. It also reveals the position of its authors: men who had held office and who knew the officials they criticized. As a source it cannot show what ordinary taxpayers wanted or whether the “people” it invoked would have supported its program. It also does not describe the electorate or the assembly’s powers in detail, and it was written at a moment when the signers’ own claim to authority had just been defeated inside the government.

Division I · Section 57.4

Risshisha, Aikoku Kōtō, and the Beginnings of Political Association

Aikoku Kōtō itself lasted only a short time. It had few members, many of whom were in Tokyo, and it was weakened when Etō’s revolt and execution in 1874 made association with the former officials politically hazardous. A more durable organizational base grew in Kōchi, the capital of the former Tosa domain, where Itagaki returned in 1874 and helped found the Risshisha (“Society for Establishing Resolve”). The Risshisha combined several functions. It provided mutual aid and employment for former samurai who had lost their income, operated a school, and served as a forum for political study and speech. It also became a model for other localities, a reminder that early “political” associations often grew from social and economic needs of former samurai.

In February 1875 Itagaki, with colleagues from the Risshisha and others, helped establish the Aikokusha (“Society of Patriots”) in Osaka. The Aikokusha aimed to link regional societies in a national organization, and the choice of Osaka, a commercial city well connected by land and sea, made practical sense. Its first meetings drew delegates from various regions, but the group was fragile. Many of the regional associations were small and dominated by former samurai, and when the government made concessions at the Osaka Conference of 1875 (Section 57.10), the Aikokusha’s leadership and purpose were disrupted as Itagaki briefly rejoined the government. The organization faded and was revived in 1878, after the shock of the Satsuma Rebellion had convinced many critics that armed opposition could not succeed.

The Risshisha’s own history shows how fine the line between political association and conspiracy could be. In 1877, while Saigō’s forces were fighting in Kyushu, the Risshisha submitted a memorial criticizing the government and calling for representative institutions; it was rejected, and some members who planned armed action were arrested. Itagaki was not charged, and the incident strengthened the argument within the movement for peaceful means, in which speeches, newspapers, and petitions would substitute for rebellion. Historians distinguish the short-lived or fragmentary organizations of 1874–77 from those that endured, such as the Risshisha and the revived Aikokusha, because the latter provided continuity of personnel, finance, and organizational practice that made the later campaigns possible.

Division II · Section 57.5

Newspapers, Political Journalism, and the Expansion of Public Debate

Modern newspapers in Japanese appeared in the late Tokugawa and early Meiji years, and by the mid-1870s Tokyo, Yokohama, Osaka, and many provincial cities had daily or periodic papers. Some were “large papers” (ōshinbun) that discussed politics and policy in a formal written style aimed at educated readers; others were “small papers” (koshinbun) that carried local news, gossip, and serialized fiction in simpler language for a wider audience. Among the publications that carried political argument were the Tokyo Nichi Nichi Shimbun, edited by Fukuchi Gen’ichirō, who defended a government-leaning gradualism; the Chōya Shimbun; the Yūbin Hōchi Shimbun, which later became associated with Ōkuma’s circle; and, from 1882, the Jiyū Shimbun, the organ of the Liberal Party. Their editors and writers were often former samurai or scholars who had trained in Chinese learning, Dutch learning, or Western languages, and they treated journalism as a form of public service as well as a livelihood.

The press did more than report. It printed texts such as the 1874 petition, hosted debates among writers, and gave local activists a way to learn what others were doing. Editorials argued over constitutional timing, the franchise, and the proper role of parties. Illustrated satirical journals such as the Marumaru Chinbun, founded in 1877, used cartoons and comic writing to mock officials and policies, reaching readers who might not follow long editorials. Newspapers were not read only by individuals; reading rooms, shared subscriptions, and reading aloud in groups meant that a single copy could reach many people. Estimates of circulation and literacy are imprecise, but historians generally agree that the Tokugawa period’s widespread literacy and commercial publishing provided a foundation on which the Meiji press could expand rapidly.

The government quickly recognized the press as a political force. The Newspaper Ordinance and the Libel Law (Zanbōritsu) of June 1875 required registration, set penalties for defaming officials, and gave the authorities broad grounds for punishing writers. Editors such as Narushima Ryūhoku of the Chōya Shimbun and Suehiro Tetchō were prosecuted and jailed, and the practice of putting a nominal “jail editor” in charge to absorb penalties became a bitter joke. The relationship between journalism and political organization grew closer in the 1880s as parties founded their own papers, a development that strengthened party messaging but also reduced the independence of the press. Section 57.17 examines the legal machinery in detail. The key point here is that the press made the movement visible to itself: it allowed activists in Kōchi, Fukushima, or Kyushu to see that they belonged to a national argument.

Division II · Section 57.6

Public Meetings, Political Speeches, and Popular Mobilization

Public speaking was a relatively new practice. The word enzetsu, used for public oratory, was popularized in the early 1870s partly through Fukuzawa Yukichi and his circle, who held speech-practice meetings at Keiō (the Mita Speech Society, founded in 1874) and encouraged students to learn how to argue a case before an audience. Tokugawa society had its storytellers, preachers, and lecturers, but the political speech delivered to an audience of strangers, aimed at persuasion on matters of state, was an innovation. Political societies adopted it quickly. Speakers addressed crowds in theaters, temples, rented halls, schools, and sometimes in the open air. Programs often included multiple speakers, and a lecture might last for hours.

Public meetings were also instruments of organization. They allowed associations to recruit members, announce petitions, collect donations, and coordinate delegates. In 1878–1880 the Aikokusha’s revived congresses and the petition campaigns of the National Assembly League depended on assemblies in many localities to gather signatures and to choose representatives to carry petitions to Tokyo. The practices were not identical everywhere. In some towns political meetings were large public events with admission fees and visiting orators; in others they were smaller gatherings of local notables, teachers, and farmers who read newspapers together and discussed what to do.

The authorities responded by regulating meetings. Police officers attended political gatherings and could interrupt speakers or order them to stop, and the Public Assembly Ordinance of 1880 (Section 57.17) required advance permission and imposed limits on who could attend or organize. Speakers learned to use metaphor, irony, and allusion to evade restrictions, and audiences learned to read between the lines. The public meeting thus became both a means of mobilization and a site of contest over how much political speech the state would tolerate. Historians treat these events as evidence that a political culture based on public argument, not only on elite consultation or local petitioning, was taking shape.

Everyday Life and Environment

Attending Speeches, Reading Newspapers, and Joining Local Associations

For a literate villager, shopkeeper, or schoolteacher in the late 1870s, political life could take several forms. A newspaper might arrive by post or through a local agent, be passed from household to household, or be read aloud at a gathering where those who could not read well listened and discussed. Reading rooms and study societies pooled subscriptions. A speech meeting might be held in a temple hall or rented theater, with speakers announced in advance, a police officer seated at the side to take notes, and an audience that included students, merchants, wealthy farmers, and former samurai. Attendance carried social meaning; in some places it signaled respectability and modernity, in others it risked the displeasure of local officials.

Participation in associations gave people experience with rules of procedure, elections of officers, membership dues, and written minutes, practices that were largely unfamiliar outside of village administration and guild structures. Women were rarely present as speakers, though some attended. Day laborers, tenant farmers, and the poor were less likely to have the time, literacy, or money for membership. The social composition of audiences and organizations therefore shaped the movement’s priorities: it spoke most directly to those who had some property, education, and leisure, even as its rhetoric invoked “the people.”

Division II · Section 57.7

Liberty, People’s Rights, and Competing Political Philosophies

The movement’s intellectual world was diverse. Fukuzawa Yukichi, whose An Encouragement of Learning and later writings made him the era’s best-known popularizer of Western thought, argued for the independence of the individual as the foundation of national independence. In the late 1870s he wrote works favoring parliamentary government on a British model, though he was cautious about immediate radical change and associated more with gradual reform than with mass mobilization. His disciples and followers, many of whom would join the Constitutional Reform Party, brought this orientation into politics. Other writers drew on French thought. Nakae Chōmin, who had studied in France, translated and interpreted portions of Rousseau’s Social Contract in the early 1880s, rendering it in a classical Chinese-based style that gave it authority for readers trained in Confucian learning. His later A Discourse by Three Drunkards on Government (1887) staged a debate among a Western-style democrat, a nationalist expansionist, and a pragmatic observer, showing the range of positions in the movement’s later years.

Ueki Emori, a Kōchi activist, wrote more radical works. His On Heaven-Endowed Human Rights (1879) argued that rights were natural and not granted by governments, a principle with significant implications for the claim that individuals could resist oppressive rule. Natural-rights arguments such as his met intellectual opposition in the 1880s from those who rejected the idea. Katō Hiroyuki, once a defender of enlightenment, repudiated natural rights in the early 1880s and turned toward ideas of social evolution that emphasized the strength of the state and the fitness of societies. This shift illustrates that the debate over rights was not simply a contest between modern thinkers and conservative defenders of tradition; it was an argument about what kind of modernity Japan should pursue.

Activists often combined Western vocabulary with older ideas. Confucian notions of benevolent government, the idea that a ruler should heed the voices of the people, and the Charter Oath’s language of public discussion all provided familiar footholds. For many participants, “liberty” and “rights” meant above all freedom from arbitrary official power and a voice in the affairs of the locality and the nation, not a full program of individual autonomy as understood in later liberal theory. Historians should be cautious in labeling the movement “liberal” or “democratic.” Some leaders accepted a limited franchise, imagined an assembly dominated by property-holders, or supported strong nationalist goals, including more assertive policy on Korea and the treaty revision question, alongside demands for rights.

Historical Vocabulary
Jiyū minken (自由民権)

Jiyū minken combines jiyū (liberty, freedom) and minken (people’s rights or popular rights). The pairing became the slogan of the movement, but the two halves could mean different things. Jiyū often meant freedom from arbitrary official power and, by extension, freedom of speech, assembly, and the press. Minken could mean the right of the people to take part in government through an elected assembly, but also, in its more radical forms, natural rights held by individuals independently of any ruler.

The term should not be equated automatically with modern “democracy.” Many proponents supported a limited franchise based on property, retained the emperor at the center of the polity, and held nationalist aims. Others, such as Ueki Emori, pressed a broader conception of rights. The slogan’s vagueness was part of its strength, because it allowed different groups to participate, and part of its weakness, because it concealed disagreements.

Division II · Section 57.8

From Former Samurai to Regional Political Networks

In the first years the movement’s base was largely former samurai. Their motives combined grievance at the loss of status with a tradition of political involvement and education that equipped them to lead associations. By the late 1870s, as the Satsuma Rebellion’s defeat closed the option of armed samurai resistance and as the local government reforms of 1878 created elected assemblies, the social basis widened. Wealthy farmers, who held local offices and paid high land taxes, became prominent as organizers and donors. Merchants, brewers, village schoolteachers, doctors, priests, and students joined local societies. The historian’s label “wealthy farmers” (gōnō) covers a range of landlords and cultivators who often combined farming with rural commerce, money-lending, or sericulture.

Regional associations multiplied. They differed in size, social composition, and aims. In Tosa the Risshisha drew on a strong tradition of samurai political activity. In Fukushima, in the Tama region west of Tokyo, in the silk-producing districts of eastern Japan, and in parts of Kyushu, local societies reflected distinct economic conditions and political cultures. In Kyushu some groups, such as the Genyōsha founded in 1881, began with an interest in popular rights but moved toward nationalist and expansionist positions, a reminder that “people’s rights” and strong nationalism were not opposites. National gatherings of the Aikokusha and later the National Assembly League brought delegates together, but the delegates often held differing views about whether to prioritize an assembly, tax reductions, treaty revision, or local concerns.

Coordination was difficult. Travel was slow, funds were limited, and the authorities monitored correspondence and meetings. Disputes over tactics divided moderate leaders, who favored petitions and legal means, from militants impatient with delay. Regional associations also competed for leadership, and personal rivalries within the Tosa group, between Tokyo-based leaders and provincial activists, and among the Liberal Party’s factions would later weaken the national movement. The movement is best understood as a network of networks, linked by newspapers, delegates, and shared slogans, but with considerable local autonomy.

Division II · Section 57.9

Women, Political Speech, and the Boundaries of People’s Rights

A movement that claimed to speak for the people must be judged partly by whom it included. Women were not formally part of the political nation in the Meiji state, and the leaders of the movement, with a few exceptions, did not make women’s political equality a central demand. Nevertheless, some women took part. Kishida Toshiko (1863–1901), who had served in the imperial household before leaving to pursue public speaking, became one of the best-known orators of the early 1880s. She spoke in Kyoto, Osaka, and elsewhere on topics including the position of women, and drew large audiences, partly out of curiosity about a female speaker. In 1883 she was prosecuted in connection with a speech in Ōtsu, and the episode illustrates how the state sought to restrict women’s participation: later assembly and association laws would explicitly bar women from joining political associations and attending political meetings.

Other women joined the cause through personal networks and conviction. Fukuda (then Kageyama) Hideko, of Okayama, heard Kishida speak and became an activist; she was arrested in connection with the Osaka Incident of 1885 (Section 57.20) and later became a prominent socialist and feminist writer. These women challenged the movement from within by asking whether rights belonged to half the population, and the answers they received were often dismissive. Many male activists treated the question as secondary or inappropriate, assuming that political participation was properly a male household head’s responsibility.

A limited and unusual set of local episodes complicates the picture. In Kōchi, a woman named Kusunose Kita, a widow who headed a household and paid taxes, protested that she was excluded from voting in local elections despite her tax obligations. In the early 1880s some women household heads in parts of Kōchi were able to vote in certain local assembly elections. These cases are important evidence that the logic of taxation and rights was applied by some people to women, but they must not be confused with national women’s suffrage. They were local, exceptional, and short-lived, and they disappeared as central regulation of local government tightened. Women’s national suffrage would not be achieved until after the Second World War (Chapter 84). The movement’s treatment of women thus shows both an opening for new ideas and the boundaries of the era’s political imagination.

Division III · Section 57.10

The Osaka Conference of 1875 and the Government’s Limited Concessions

By 1874 the government was under pressure from several directions: the Saga revolt, the Taiwan expedition and the resignation of Kido Takayoshi in protest, and the public debate over the assembly petition. Seeking to stabilize the regime, Inoue Kaoru and others arranged a meeting in Osaka in the winter of 1875 among Ōkubo Toshimichi, Kido, Itagaki, and Itō Hirobumi. The negotiators reached an understanding that Kido and Itagaki would return to government in exchange for a commitment to move toward constitutional government by stages. The agreement did not satisfy all parties, and its results were mixed.

In April 1875 an imperial edict announced the gradual establishment of constitutional government and created three institutions: the Genrōin (Chamber of Elders), a deliberative body of appointed members who would examine proposed laws and consider constitutional questions; the Great Court of Cassation (Daishin’in), a high court; and a conference of prefectural governors. The Genrōin was a significant development, because it gave the government a body to consider legislation and to draft constitutional proposals. It was not, however, an elected assembly. Its members were appointed by the government, and it did not represent a popular constituency. The distinction between appointed institutions and elected representative government is essential for understanding why activists regarded the concessions as inadequate.

For the oligarchs, the Osaka compromise was a way to separate moderate critics from radical ones and to channel the demand for representation into forms the government controlled. For Itagaki it was an attempt to gain influence within government and to move gradually toward elected institutions. He soon found the position untenable and left the government again late in 1875, and the Aikokusha was weakened by the episode. In the same year the government introduced the press and libel laws discussed in Section 57.5, which shows that gradual constitutionalism and tightened control went together. The Genrōin would produce draft constitutions in the later 1870s that were not adopted, but its existence signaled that some form of constitution was on the agenda.

Division III · Section 57.11

Prefectural Assemblies, Local Elections, and Political Apprenticeship

In July 1878 the government issued three local government laws that reorganized the relationship between the center and the localities: one on the organization of districts, towns, and villages; one on prefectural assemblies; and one on local taxes. The Prefectural Assembly Regulations created elected assemblies in each prefecture, the first elections for which were held in 1879. Voting and candidacy were limited by sex, age, and land-tax payments: only adult men who paid a prescribed minimum in land tax could vote, with a higher threshold for candidates. This tied the franchise to the class of landholders who were already prominent in rural society.

The assemblies’ powers were limited. They could deliberate on local-tax budgets and related matters, but the prefectural governor, appointed by the Home Ministry, could veto or override decisions and could dissolve assemblies. The assemblies could not legislate on national policy. Even so, they were significant. They provided thousands of local notables with experience in campaigning, debating, drafting resolutions, and confronting officials. Many activists in the movement’s later phases were assemblymen. Kōno Hironaka, who became chairman of the Fukushima assembly, is one example. The assemblies were also arenas of conflict, since disputes between assemblies and governors over budgets, public works, and taxes provided occasions for political confrontation, as the Fukushima Incident of 1882 would show.

The 1878 reforms reflect the government’s approach to participation: to allow elected bodies at the local level under tight supervision, while delaying national representation. Historians have debated whether the local assemblies were genuine steps toward self-government or devices to co-opt local elites. The evidence suggests both. They created a space for political learning and organization that outlasted the limits placed on them, but the central government’s control of the governors, the police, and finance ensured that local assemblies could not easily challenge national policy. Hokkaidō and Okinawa did not receive comparable elected assemblies at this time; Okinawa, annexed in 1879, was governed under special arrangements that preserved some old institutions and withheld representative ones.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Tokugawa Petitioning Traditions and Meiji Electoral and Associational Politics

What Changed

  • Political claims were increasingly addressed to a national public through newspapers and speeches, not only to local lords or officials through channels.
  • Organizations were voluntary associations with written rules, dues, and elected officers, not primarily villages or status groups.
  • Elections for local assemblies, though limited, introduced voting as a means of selecting representatives.
  • The language of rights, constitutions, and taxpayers’ claims appeared alongside older appeals to benevolent rule.

What Stayed the Same

  • Local notables, such as village headmen and wealthy farmers, remained central organizers and leaders.
  • Petitioning remained a primary method, and petitioners still framed claims as appeals to a legitimate authority, now the emperor and the Council of State.
  • Villages continued to use collective action, including protests against taxes and officials, when petitions failed.
  • Officials continued to treat unauthorized collective action as a threat to order.

Who experienced change most directly: Urban journalists, students, and former samurai leaders experienced the largest shift, moving into new forms of public politics. Who experienced greater continuity: Many villagers, tenants, and small cultivators continued to rely on familiar patterns of collective petition and local leadership. Why the pattern matters: The movement should be understood as a combination of inherited practices and new institutions, not simply as the import of Western ideas.

Division III · Section 57.12

The League for Establishing a National Assembly and the Petition Campaign of 1880

After the Satsuma Rebellion, the revived Aikokusha held a series of national congresses in Osaka beginning in 1878. By 1879 and 1880 its meetings drew delegates from many parts of the country, and the focus turned increasingly to petitioning for a national assembly. At the Aikokusha’s fourth congress in March 1880, delegates formed the League for Establishing a National Assembly (Kokkai Kisei Dōmei). Its strategy was to gather signatures in many localities and to send delegates to present petitions in Tokyo. Kataoka Kenkichi of Kōchi and Kōno Hironaka of Fukushima were among those who carried petitions to the government. Commonly cited figures speak of tens of thousands of signatures, with a figure of roughly 87,000 often given for the main presentation, though counts vary among sources and should be treated as approximate.

The government declined to accept the petitions. It argued, in effect, that petitions on such a subject should be submitted through proper channels and that private associations did not have standing to petition for a national assembly. It did not, however, ignore the campaign. In April 1880 it issued the Public Assembly Ordinance (Section 57.17), which tightened control over political meetings and associations. The petitions continued, and in the later months of 1880 many local groups, including prefectural assemblies and local societies, sent additional requests. The campaign thus helped make the national assembly a subject of nationwide discussion and gave local activists a sense of participation in a common effort.

The League’s second congress, held in Tokyo in the autumn of 1880, took a further step: delegates agreed that each association should draft a constitution and that a party should be formed. This shift from asking for an assembly to proposing what its constitution should contain laid the groundwork for the private constitutional drafts of 1881 and for the founding of the Liberal Party. It also exposed differences. Some delegates preferred to continue petitioning; others wanted party organization; and many local associations had priorities of their own. The campaign’s peak, in 1880, thus also marked the point at which the movement’s unity began to give way to more specific, and more divisive, programs.

Historical Vocabulary
Kokkai (国会)

Kokkai means “national assembly” or “Diet,” combining koku (country, state) and kai (assembly). It became the standard term for the national parliament demanded by the movement and later established as the Imperial Diet (Teikoku Gikai) under the Meiji Constitution. In 1874 the petition spoke of a minsen giin, a popularly chosen assembly; by 1880 activists spoke of a kokkai, shifting attention to a national body with defined powers, and to the constitution that would establish it.

The word did not specify how members would be chosen, who would vote, or what powers the body would possess. The government’s own promise of a kokkai in 1881 therefore did not settle these questions.

Division III · Section 57.13

Popular Constitutional Drafts and Competing Visions of Sovereignty

One of the movement’s most remarkable products was a body of private constitutional drafts (shigi kenpō), written by individuals and groups outside the government, mostly in 1880 and 1881. Scholars have identified dozens. They were written by people who were not constitutional lawyers, in some cases in village study groups, and they show how seriously participants took the question of what a constitution should contain. The drafts differed widely, which is why historians avoid speaking of a single “popular” constitutional program.

Some drafts, associated with Fukuzawa’s circle, favored a constitutional monarchy resembling British parliamentary government, with a strong legislature to which the cabinet would be responsible. Others, such as the draft written by Ueki Emori in 1881, went further in protecting rights. Ueki’s proposal retained an emperor but sharply limited his powers, envisioned a legislature of significant authority, and, as historians have emphasized, included a right of the people to resist unlawful or oppressive government. The Itsukaichi draft, written in 1881 by members of a study society in the Tama region and discovered in 1968 in a storehouse by scholars studying the local history of the area, is a lengthy proposal that has attracted attention for its emphasis on the protection of individual rights, including provisions concerning education and equality, and for what it reveals about the political thinking of village-level intellectuals. Students should consult the scholarship on the draft’s specific provisions instead of relying on summaries.

Differences among drafts concerned the emperor’s role, whether the legislature would have one chamber or two, the franchise, the relationship between national and local government, the protection of civil liberties, and the right of resistance. They show that the movement’s participants did not agree on “sovereignty.” Few challenged the monarchy outright; many tried to reconcile an emperor with legislative power or to define the emperor’s authority in constitutional terms. Because the government did not adopt any of these drafts, and because the final constitution gave the emperor sovereign authority (Chapter 59), the drafts are sometimes read as a road not taken. Historians should avoid romanticizing them as proof of a mature democratic tradition ignored by the oligarchs; they were varied, often untested, and written by a small, literate segment of society. They are valuable because they reveal that constitutional thought extended far beyond the government and the capital.

Division III · Section 57.14

The Political Crisis of 1881 and the Promise of a National Diet

By 1881 the government faced both pressure from the movement and division within the leadership. The Council of State’s members had been asked to submit opinions on a constitution. In March 1881 Ōkuma Shigenobu, then a councillor, presented a confidential memorandum that departed from the cautious consensus: he proposed holding a national assembly in 1883 and organizing government on the British model, with party-based cabinets responsible to the legislature. Itō Hirobumi, Iwakura Tomomi, and their advisers, including the legal scholar Inoue Kowashi, favored a slower timetable and a model closer to Prussian constitutionalism, in which executive authority and the emperor’s prerogatives would be strongly protected. Ōkuma’s position was also entangled in rumors about his ties to Fukuzawa’s circle and to business interests.

The immediate trigger was the Hokkaidō affair. The Hokkaidō Colonization Commission (Kaitakushi), created in 1869 to develop Hokkaidō, was approaching the end of its ten-year program. Its head, Kuroda Kiyotaka, proposed to sell its assets, built up at public expense, to a group of businessmen including Godai Tomoatsu at a price and on terms widely viewed as extraordinarily favorable. When news leaked in the summer of 1881, critics in the press and public meetings denounced the sale as a scandal involving officials from Satsuma, Kuroda’s home domain. Ōkuma, who had opposed the sale, was suspected by some of having leaked it as part of a plot against Satsuma interests. In October, as the emperor returned from a tour of the north, the government canceled the sale, dismissed Ōkuma and his associates, and issued an imperial rescript promising that a national assembly would be convened in 1890.

The crisis, often called the Political Crisis of the 14th year of Meiji, strengthened the group around Itō and the Prussian-oriented approach to constitution-making, and it removed from government the figure most closely aligned with the British model. The rescript did not accept any of the movement’s constitutional drafts or its assumptions. It set a date, left the constitution’s content to the government, and warned against radical agitation. The Hokkaidō scandal also illustrates how the region’s development, in which Ainu communities had no voice and were increasingly categorized in official registers as “former natives” (kyūdojin), served as a stage on which the political struggle among Japanese officials and activists was played out.

Historical Debate

Did Popular Agitation Compel the Government to Promise a National Diet?

The question: The imperial rescript of October 1881 promised a national assembly for 1890. Was this primarily a response to the pressure of the Freedom and People’s Rights Movement, or the outcome of a constitutional program that the ruling elite had already begun and controlled?

Interpretation A — Popular pressure as a driving force: Some historians emphasize that petitions, newspapers, public meetings, and local assemblies made constitutional government a national political issue. Government leaders, they argue, could not ignore the scale of the 1880 campaign, and the promise of 1881 was designed to answer it. On this view the movement shaped the timing of constitutional government and the government’s decision to proceed.
Interpretation B — Elite-directed constitutional development: Other historians emphasize that the oligarchs had accepted the principle of a constitution since the Charter Oath and the 1875 edict, that the Genrōin was already drafting proposals, and that the 1881 timetable was the result of a leadership struggle, in which Itō and Iwakura prevailed over Ōkuma. On this view the movement affected the climate but not the fundamental direction, and the government used the promise partly to defuse opposition on its own terms.

Evidence: Both sides draw on government memoranda, such as Ōkuma’s and Iwakura’s, on the Genrōin’s drafts, on the records of petitions and the government’s response to them, on press reports, and on the sequence of events in 1881. Many historians treat these views as overlapping rather than exclusive: the movement and the leadership’s own plans interacted.

Why it matters: The answer affects how students understand the relation between popular politics and the state in Meiji Japan, and whether the Constitution should be seen as a concession to popular demands, a defensive strategy by the oligarchs, or a product of both.

Student task: Identify one piece of evidence from this chapter that supports each interpretation, and propose a kind of additional evidence that would help decide between them.

Division IV · Section 57.15

Itagaki Taisuke and the Liberal Party

Within weeks of the 1881 rescript, activists moved to build a national party. In October 1881 the Liberal Party (Jiyūtō) was founded in Tokyo, with Itagaki Taisuke as president. Its program stressed liberty, the expansion of people’s rights, and the establishment of constitutional government, in language general enough to unite diverse supporters. The party’s leadership included former officials, journalists, and local notables, among them figures such as Nakajima Nobuyuki, Baba Tatsui, Ōi Kentarō, and Hoshi Tōru, whose backgrounds and priorities varied. Its strength lay in regions with active local societies, such as Tosa, parts of eastern Japan including Fukushima, and districts around Tokyo, and it relied on a network of local branches and affiliated newspapers, notably the Jiyū Shimbun launched in 1882.

The party was not unified. Moderate leaders, many of them former samurai or well-to-do landholders, favored legal means and gradual advance. More militant local activists, particularly in rural districts, were impatient with delay and increasingly attracted to confrontation, especially as economic hardship deepened. The party’s leaders were sometimes unable to restrain local branches, and they were also reluctant to disown them. This tension would shape the events of 1882 to 1884.

In April 1882 Itagaki was stabbed during a speaking tour in Gifu by a man who attacked him in the street; he survived. A famous remark attributed to him on the occasion, to the effect that Itagaki might die but liberty never would, became part of the movement’s lore, though historians treat the exact wording and circumstances of such reported sayings with caution. In November 1882 Itagaki and Gotō Shōjirō left on a trip to Europe. The journey’s financing, reportedly arranged through officials with the help of business money, provoked a scandal when the party’s own newspaper criticized it, and it deepened rifts within the party. The incident shows how the government’s ability to influence party leaders through patronage as well as repression complicated the movement’s independence.

Division IV · Section 57.16

Ōkuma Shigenobu, Rikken Kaishintō, and Competing Constitutional Strategies

In March 1882 Ōkuma Shigenobu, together with allies and former officials dismissed with him in 1881, founded the Constitutional Reform Party (Rikken Kaishintō). Its members included journalists and writers such as Yano Fumio, politicians such as Ozaki Yukio and Inukai Tsuyoshi, and men associated with Fukuzawa Yukichi’s circle. Its program favored a gradual transition to constitutional monarchy on the British model, with party cabinets responsible to a legislature. Its base was more urban and more tied to intellectuals and to commerce and industry than the Liberal Party’s, though it also had rural supporters. Ōkuma’s school, the Tokyo Senmon Gakkō (the forerunner of Waseda University), opened in 1882 and became a training ground for party sympathizers.

The two parties differed in leadership, constituency, and style. The Liberal Party drew on the Tosa tradition, regional branches, and, in many places, the leadership of landowners; the Kaishintō relied more on urban opinion and the press. The Liberal Party tended to emphasize popular rights and was more willing to contemplate confrontation, while the Kaishintō stressed constitutional procedure and was more cautious. The two parties also spent considerable energy attacking one another, and the government encouraged and exploited this rivalry. Critics accused the Kaishintō of dependence on business patrons, a charge that linked it, in the eyes of opponents, to Mitsubishi’s interests, while the Liberal Party’s own financial arrangements were criticized from within.

The government also had its supporters. In March 1882 Fukuchi Gen’ichirō and others organized the Imperial Rule Party (Rikken Teiseitō) to defend the government’s gradualist constitutionalism and the imperial role in the constitution. It had little popular base and dissolved the next year, but its creation shows that the political field was no longer a contest between the government and the opposition alone; several organizations competed to define a “constitutional” program. By the mid-1880s the existence of two opposition parties with different strategies, in a system that offered them no seats in a national legislature, was a source of weakness as much as vitality.

Division IV · Section 57.17

Censorship, Police Regulations, and the Limits of Political Freedom

The Meiji government combined its constitutional promises with an expanding apparatus of regulation. The Newspaper Ordinance and Libel Law of 1875 were followed by further press regulations, including revisions in the early 1880s that made licensing, deposits, and penalties more demanding and that gave authorities broad power to suspend or ban publications. Editors faced fines and imprisonment, and papers could be shut down. The costs of publication, already significant, rose, and some papers closed or changed course.

The Public Assembly Ordinance of April 1880 was the central regulation of political meetings and associations. It required notification and police permission for meetings and for the formation of political societies, restricted the ability of societies to establish branches and communicate with one another, and barred certain groups, including soldiers, police, and teachers and students at public schools, from joining political associations. Revisions in 1882 tightened the controls. Police were authorized to attend meetings, to stop speakers, and to dissolve gatherings. These rules directly targeted the strategies the movement had developed: nationwide networks, petition campaigns, and public speaking. They did not forbid all political activity, but they gave local officials wide discretion.

Activists adapted. They held meetings under the guise of social or educational gatherings, formed new societies under new names, used the press to publicize repression, and challenged specific orders in court when they could. Parties built networks through affiliated papers and correspondence that evaded some restrictions. Their adaptations also had costs, since constant harassment drained resources and encouraged both caution among moderates and desperation among others. The pattern of the period combined concession on the principle of a constitution with regulation of the means by which the public could take part in the discussion. It would continue into the Peace Preservation Ordinance of 1887.

Division IV · Section 57.18

The Matsukata Deflation, Rural Debt, and the Social Transformation of the Movement

The economic background is explained in Chapter 56 and is summarized here only as a cause of mobilization. After 1881 Finance Minister Matsukata Masayoshi pursued a policy of tight money and fiscal retrenchment to restore the value of the currency after the inflation that followed the Satsuma Rebellion and to build a central banking system. The result was deflation. Prices for rice and other farm products, including raw silk, fell sharply, while taxes, including the land tax, were fixed in cash and did not fall. Farmers who had borrowed during the inflationary years found their incomes shrinking against their debts. Many lost land through foreclosure, entered tenancy, or left the countryside. The consequences were uneven: some landlords and moneylenders prospered, while small cultivators and sericulture households were especially vulnerable.

These conditions changed the movement. In the late 1870s wealthy farmers had been prominent organizers, motivated by tax burdens and by claims to local political influence. After 1881 the Liberal Party’s rural branches included members of a different kind: small and middle-level cultivators who joined in the hope of relief. The party’s leaders, who were mostly landowners and urban intellectuals, did not always share their members’ priorities, and sometimes belonged to the creditor class. Local activists were caught between the party’s constitutional program and the pressing demands of indebted neighbors. In some districts, associations pursued debt-relief negotiations with creditors and petitions for tax relief alongside political activity; in a few, they led to violence.

This transformation illustrates a point central to the chapter: political demands and economic grievances were intertwined but not identical. Constitutional government was not, in itself, a remedy for debt, and a parliament elected by taxpayers of a certain level would not necessarily have represented the indebted farmers who were most desperate. Understanding why rural participants joined, and why some turned to confrontation when peaceful methods failed, requires attention to both political and economic history.

Division IV · Section 57.19

Fukushima, Kabasan, and Chichibu: Protest, Repression, and Armed Confrontation

Three incidents of 1882 and 1884 are often grouped together but differ considerably. The Fukushima Incident of 1882 grew from a conflict between the prefectural governor, Mishima Michitsune, and the Fukushima assembly, whose chairman was Kōno Hironaka, a Liberal Party leader. Mishima pressed a road-building program that required heavy contributions of labor or money from residents, especially in the Aizu region, and the assembly resisted. When local residents protested in November 1882, police arrested them, and Kōno and other party members were arrested on charges of plotting against the government. Kōno and others were tried and sentenced to prison; they were released under the amnesty that accompanied the constitution in 1889. The incident was a confrontation between elected local politicians and a governor backed by central authority, and it showed how far the state was willing to go to defend its administrative priorities.

The Kabasan Incident of September 1884 involved a small group of Liberal Party activists, mainly from Ibaraki and nearby prefectures and including survivors of the Fukushima conflict, who planned violent action against officials and took refuge on Mount Kaba. Their numbers were small, their plan was poorly supported, and they were quickly suppressed and harshly punished. The episode reflects the radicalization of some party members after repression and, in the view of many historians, the weakness of the party’s national leadership in controlling or disowning them. It was a plot by a militant minority, not a mass uprising.

The Chichibu Incident of November 1884 was of a different order. In the mountainous Chichibu district of Saitama, where many households depended on silk production, falling prices after 1881 had left many in crushing debt. Local farmers organized a Debtors’ Party (Konmintō) under leaders including Tashiro Eisuke. They petitioned creditors and officials for delayed repayment and tax and fee reductions, and when these requests were rejected, they rose in early November, attacking moneylenders, burning records of debts, and clashing with police. The uprising involved thousands and spread across the district and into neighboring areas. The government sent police and then army units, and the rebels were defeated within about a week or two, with some fighting continuing in adjoining regions. Many participants were punished, and several leaders, including Tashiro, were sentenced to death. Some local Liberal Party members took part, but the Liberal Party as an organization had just been dissolved, and the uprising was driven more by economic desperation than by the party’s constitutional program.

Historians therefore caution against treating these events as a single series of “rebellions.” Organizers, aims, scale, and links to the national movement differed. Fukushima was a political struggle within an institutional framework; Kabasan a small insurrectionary plot; Chichibu a mass debtors’ uprising with local political connections. Together they demonstrate the movement’s breadth and the pressures that drove parts of it beyond peaceful methods, as well as the state’s readiness to use force. They also allowed the government and its supporters to portray the movement as dangerous disorder, and they contributed to the sense of crisis within the party leadership that led to its dissolution.

Cause and Effect

Rural Financial Crisis, Political Organization, Repression, and Confrontation

Long-term causes

The shift to cash taxation in the 1870s, the commercialization of agriculture and silk production, an inflationary boom followed by credit expansion, and a tradition of local leadership and collective petitioning set the stage.

Short-term causes

After 1881 tight-money policy produced deflation, falling prices, and mounting debts, while taxes stayed fixed in cash. Meanwhile repression of public meetings and press limited peaceful channels for grievance.

Event or transformation

Rural associations, some tied to the Liberal Party, organized petitions and negotiation. When these failed, small groups plotted violence (Kabasan) and a district-wide debtors’ uprising occurred (Chichibu).

Immediate effects

Police and army suppressed the uprisings, many participants were punished, and the Liberal Party’s leadership, fearing further repression and unable to control local militants, moved toward dissolution.

Long-term consequences

Rural popular politics was discredited in official eyes and the government strengthened its policing and local control. The experience left a memory of grassroots protest that later historians and activists reclaimed.

Historical complexity

No single factor explains these events. Economic policy, local leadership, political ideas, police practice, and the limits of constitutional channels all interacted, and the incidents differed among themselves.

Historical complexity: Economic hardship did not mechanically produce rebellion. People organized, debated, and chose among options, and many suffering districts did not rise.

Division V · Section 57.20

Party Fragmentation, the Osaka Incident, and the Crisis of the Movement

In October 1884, with the party under police pressure, divided between moderates and militants, and unable to control rural branches, the Liberal Party leadership decided to dissolve the organization. The decision was announced at a meeting in Osaka, and Itagaki and the central leadership justified it as a response to repression and to the party’s internal condition. Not all members accepted it, and the dissolution left many local activists without an organization. The Kaishintō was also weakened; Ōkuma and some of his associates left the party in the same year. The movement’s organizational structure, built over a decade, thus broke apart at the moment the government was preparing its constitution.

In November 1885 the Osaka Incident revealed a different current in the movement. A group of activists, including Ōi Kentarō, Kobayashi Kusuo, and the woman activist Fukuda Hideko, planned to travel to Korea to support reformers who had been defeated in the Gapsin Coup of December 1884 and who had ties to Japanese sympathizers. The plan reflected a strand of the movement that combined radical political aims with enthusiasm for a forward policy on the Korean peninsula, and some conspirators reportedly considered raising funds by illegal means. Police arrested more than a hundred people in Osaka and Nagasaki before the plan could be carried out. The leaders received long prison terms and were released in the 1889 amnesty. The affair, to which Chapter 60 returns in the context of Japan’s Korea policy, shows that activists who demanded political freedom at home could also support intervention abroad, and that frustrations after the collapse of the parties encouraged conspiratorial action.

By 1885 the movement faced a dilemma between constitutional campaigning, which meant accepting the government’s timetable and the likelihood that the constitution would be written by the oligarchs, and more radical action, which had been tried and repressed. Many veterans withdrew, some turned to journalism or scholarship, and others stayed in local politics. The government, meanwhile, moved ahead with institutional change: a new peerage in 1884, a cabinet system in 1885 with Itō Hirobumi as the first prime minister, and, in secret, the drafting of the constitution (Chapter 59). The movement’s contribution was real, but its ability to shape the outcome seemed to be diminishing.

Division V · Section 57.21

Renewed Unity, the Peace Preservation Ordinance, and the Road to Parliament

In 1886 a new effort began to reunite the opposition under the slogan Daidō Danketsu (“great union” or “unity of all”). Hoshi Tōru and others called on former Liberal and Kaishintō members and independents to set aside differences, and Gotō Shōjirō became a prominent figure in the campaign. The movement revived public meetings and in 1887 launched a petition drive centered on three demands: relief of the tax burden, freedom of speech and assembly, and a revision of foreign policy to correct the failure of treaty negotiations. The treaty issue gave the campaign a nationalist dimension and a broad appeal. Foreign Minister Inoue Kaoru’s proposals, which included foreign judges in certain courts and aroused criticism even within the government, had provoked indignation, and Inoue resigned in September 1887.

The government responded with the Peace Preservation Ordinance (Hoan Jōrei) of December 25, 1887. The ordinance empowered authorities to order persons judged to be plotting or inciting disorder to leave the area within roughly three ri (about twelve kilometers) of the imperial palace and not to return for up to three years, and it also restricted secret societies and the possession of weapons. Several hundred people, a figure commonly given as about 570, were expelled from Tokyo, including figures such as Ozaki Yukio, Hoshi Tōru, and Nakae Chōmin. The measure illustrates the government’s approach to dissent as the constitution neared completion: it would move ahead with constitutional government but keep the capital free of agitators during the final stage.

While opposition was suppressed, officials, led by Itō Hirobumi with advisers including Inoue Kowashi, Itō Miyoji, Kaneko Kentarō, and German legal scholars, worked on the constitution in confidence, and it was promulgated in February 1889 as a gift from the emperor, a form that stressed that it did not originate from popular pressure. A general amnesty freed many imprisoned activists. The Diet would meet in 1890. The details belong to Chapter 59. For the movement’s history, the key point is that the transition was an elite-managed one and that the question of who would hold power under the new institutions remained open. Many veterans of the movement would enter the new parliament, continuing the struggle in an institutional setting whose limits they had not set.

Chapter Review and Historical Thinking Checkpoint

This chapter has traced the movement from the split among Restoration leaders in 1873 to the eve of constitutional government in 1890. Its central argument is that a network of associations, newspapers, public meetings, petitions, and parties, supported by former samurai, wealthy farmers, urban intellectuals, rural debtors, and a few women, challenged the concentration of power in a small group of officials and helped to make representative government a national political issue. It also shows that the movement was diverse in ideas and goals, that the government combined concessions with repression, and that the constitutional order eventually adopted did not embody the movement’s more democratic proposals.

Before proceeding to Chapter 58, students should be able to distinguish the movement’s three dimensions (mobilization, ideological argument, and struggle for power); explain why the appointed Genrōin and the elected prefectural assemblies did not meet activists’ demand for national representation; describe the differences among Fukushima, Kabasan, and Chichibu; and identify who was left out of the movement’s idea of “the people.” They should also be able to state, with evidence, why the 1881 promise of a Diet did not mean that the government had accepted the movement’s constitutional drafts. These themes will reappear when Chapter 59 turns to the Meiji Constitution and the Imperial Diet.

Chapter Summary

The Freedom and People’s Rights Movement began as a dispute within the Meiji governing elite. In October 1873 the Korea controversy split the Restoration leadership, and several departing officials, notably Itagaki Taisuke and Gotō Shōjirō, turned to public argument. Their January 1874 petition, published in the press, asked for a popularly chosen assembly and grounded the claim in the principle that taxpayers should share in government. The government did not accept it, but the petition established a vocabulary and a public debate.

The movement’s early organizational history was uneven. Aikoku Kōtō was short-lived; the Risshisha in Kōchi and the Aikokusha in Osaka provided more durable structures, but they were limited by the narrow base of former samurai and by the government’s concessions at the Osaka Conference of 1875. After the Satsuma Rebellion closed the path of armed resistance in 1877, the movement turned decisively toward peaceful means: newspapers, speeches, and petitions.

A new political public emerged. Newspapers, from large political papers to satirical illustrated journals, circulated arguments and organized opinion, despite press and libel laws. Public oratory and meetings introduced a form of politics based on persuasion before audiences of strangers. Associations created habits of procedure and organization. These developments broadened participation beyond former samurai to wealthy farmers, merchants, teachers, and students, but they were less accessible to the poor, to tenants, and to most women.

The movement’s ideas were not uniform. Fukuzawa Yukichi and his circle favored gradual, British-style constitutionalism; Nakae Chōmin interpreted Rousseau; Ueki Emori championed natural rights and a right of resistance. Dozens of private constitutional drafts, including Ueki’s and the Itsukaichi draft, showed that constitutional thought was widely distributed, and they differed over the emperor, the legislature, the franchise, and rights. Participants combined Western concepts with Confucian and Tokugawa traditions, and many held nationalist aims. The movement should not be labeled simply liberal or democratic.

The government’s response was a mixture of limited concession and increasing control. The 1875 edict created the appointed Genrōin; the 1878 reforms established elected but constrained prefectural assemblies; the 1880 Public Assembly Ordinance and press regulations restricted organization and speech. The 1880 petition campaign, led by the National Assembly League, represented the movement’s peak in national mobilization, and the League’s call for constitutional drafts marked a turn from asking for an assembly to defining its content.

The crisis of 1881 brought these strands together. Ōkuma Shigenobu’s proposal for an early parliament and party government, the scandal over the sale of Hokkaidō Colonization Commission assets, and rivalries within the government culminated in Ōkuma’s dismissal and the imperial promise of a national assembly in 1890. Historians debate whether popular agitation compelled this decision or whether it reflected an elite-directed process; the evidence suggests that both factors interacted. What is clear is that the promise fixed a date but left the constitution’s content in the government’s hands, and that the Prussian-oriented gradualists gained the upper hand.

After 1881 the movement entered a phase of party organization and social strain. The Liberal Party and the Constitutional Reform Party differed in leadership, constituency, and strategy. The Matsukata deflation devastated many rural households and changed the movement’s social composition. In this setting the incidents at Fukushima (1882), Kabasan (1884), and Chichibu (1884) differed in character: a political confrontation between a governor and an assembly, a small insurrectionary plot, and a mass debtors’ uprising. Together they revealed the movement’s breadth, the pressures on it, and the state’s readiness to use force.

The movement fragmented in the mid-1880s. The Liberal Party dissolved in October 1884, the Osaka Incident of 1885 showed a conspiratorial and Korea-oriented current, and many veterans withdrew. A revival under the Daidō Danketsu banner in 1886–87, combining demands on taxes, civil liberties, and treaty revision, was met by the Peace Preservation Ordinance of December 1887, which expelled several hundred activists from Tokyo.

The constitution of 1889 was drafted in confidence by officials and presented as an imperial gift; the Diet would meet in 1890. The movement had contributed to the creation of representative institutions and to a lasting political culture of associations, press, and public debate, but it did not secure the democratic content its most radical participants had proposed. Its limits are also visible in its exclusions: women, tenants, outcaste-origin communities, the Ainu, and the people of Okinawa were largely absent from its program. Its story is therefore one of both achievement and incompleteness, and its legacy would be contested by later parties, historians, and activists.

Chapter Reflection

The Freedom and People’s Rights Movement matters because it brought into being a political public in Japan: a set of newspapers, associations, speakers, and local assemblies through which people outside the government could argue about how they were governed. It shows that the question of representation arose from within the Meiji revolution itself, since the reforms that centralized the state and dismantled the old status order also generated the social groups and grievances that demanded a voice.

The movement also reveals the shape of the Meiji settlement. The government accepted the principle of a constitution and a Diet but controlled the timetable, the drafting process, and the pace of participation, using regulation and force when it judged necessary. The result was a constitutional order that was real but limited, and that left unresolved the question of who would actually exercise power. Chapter 59 will examine how that order worked, and later chapters will show how party politics, labor movements, suffrage campaigns, and the Taishō democratic period took up questions the movement had raised.

The movement’s limits are as instructive as its achievements. Women, tenants, the Ainu, the people of Okinawa, and communities of outcaste origin were largely outside its program, and its leaders sometimes combined demands for rights at home with enthusiasm for expansion abroad, a pattern that anticipates later developments in Japan’s relations with Korea and China. Its rural episodes show how political and economic grievances could converge, and how the state treated them differently from the activities of respectable assemblymen.

Finally, the movement became a subject of historical memory. Later generations, including postwar historians, found in its drafts and local activism a grassroots democratic tradition; other scholars emphasized the continuity of elite leadership and the narrowness of its social base. Both views illuminate part of the evidence. Students who understand the movement as neither a failed prelude nor a lost democratic alternative, but as a complex historical contest whose outcome was shaped by many actors, will be prepared to read the constitutional chapters that follow.
