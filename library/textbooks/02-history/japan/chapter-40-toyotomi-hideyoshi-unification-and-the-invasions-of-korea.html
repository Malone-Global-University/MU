Part VI — Sengoku Japan and Reunification
40

Toyotomi Hideyoshi: Unification and the Invasions of Korea

Japanese History: From Jōmon Japan to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

In the spring of 1588, the reigning emperor, Go-Yōzei, made a ceremonial visit to Jurakudai, the new palace-fortress of a man whose family had been of no importance a generation earlier. Great lords who had fought one another for decades put their names to oaths of obedience. Within months the same man ordered the weapons of the countryside collected. Four years later, a fleet of ships and an army of well over a hundred thousand men crossed to the Korean coast.

These three events, the oath-taking at Jurakudai, the collection of weapons from villages, and the landing at Busan, belong to the career of Toyotomi Hideyoshi (1537–1598), and the central task of this chapter is to explain how they belong together. Hideyoshi rose from the lower margins of Owari society into the service of Oda Nobunaga and, after Nobunaga's death in 1582, became the most powerful figure in the archipelago. By 1590 nearly every major regional lord from Kyushu to the northeast had submitted to him. Chapter 39 explained how Nobunaga broke or subordinated many of the autonomous centers that sustained Sengoku fragmentation. This chapter asks a different question: how did Hideyoshi turn that unstable inheritance into a far broader political and social settlement, and why did that settlement expand into disastrous continental war?

The answer developed here is that Hideyoshi's regime was distinguished by an expanding capacity to classify, mobilize, redistribute, and command people and resources. Land surveys made villages and their yields visible to central authority. The collection of weapons and a series of orders on status sought to separate warriors from cultivators. The reassignment of territories, the keeping of hostages, and the use of court titles bound the great lords into a single hierarchy centered on Hideyoshi. These are the same instruments, the chapter will argue, that made it possible to assemble an overseas army. The surveys, the sword hunts, the castles, and the Korean invasions are therefore not separate topics but aspects of one political order, and its dependence on one man helps explain both its reach and its fragility.

It is important to be precise about what "unification" meant. It did not mean a modern centralized state administered by uniform institutions from Osaka. Daimyō remained in command of their own domains and retainers, and Hideyoshi governed largely through them, through personal bonds, and through the prestige of court office. What changed was the scale and hierarchy of the system: lords now held their territories within a framework in which Hideyoshi could survey, reassign, summon, and command. Local variation, resistance, and negotiation remained part of every stage of the process, and villages and regions experienced the Toyotomi order very differently from the lords who negotiated it.

The Korean invasions require a perspective that Japanese-centered narratives have often neglected. Korea was a sovereign society with its own dynastic state, Joseon, with a literate officialdom, armies, a navy, a farming population, and a political life of its own. The invasions of 1592 and 1597 were, for Koreans, an overwhelming catastrophe, and for Ming China, a costly war fought beyond its borders. They are known in Korea as the Imjin Waeran and the Jeongyu Jaeran. Understanding them as part of the history of Japan requires understanding them equally as part of the histories of Korea and China, and requires attention to what soldiers, porters, sailors, captives, and villagers on every side experienced.

The chapter draws on several kinds of evidence. Hideyoshi's own orders, letters, and edicts survive in many forms, some in originals and some in later copies, which raises questions of authenticity and context. Survey registers and village documents allow historians to see how the land surveys worked in particular places. Jesuit missionaries such as Luís Fróis wrote extensively about Hideyoshi and his court, and their testimony is valuable but filtered through their own purposes. On the Korean war, historians rely on Korean court records, memoirs such as Ryu Seong-ryong's Jingbirok, the war diary of Admiral Yi Sun-sin, Chinese official histories and dispatches, and Japanese sources, including campaign records and the diary of a Japanese priest who accompanied the army. None of these is neutral, and the chapter will repeatedly ask whose voice a source carries and whose is missing.

The chapter also keeps the frontiers of the archipelago in view. Hideyoshi's authority reached the Ainu trade world through the Kakizaki family of southern Ezo and pressed on the Ryukyu Kingdom, which was asked to support the Korean campaign. These cases, treated in this chapter only as they bear on Toyotomi policy, will be developed in Chapters 32 and 48. The chapter's boundaries are also chronological. It begins where Chapter 39 ends, with Nobunaga's death at Honnō-ji, and it stops with the unsettled succession around Hideyoshi's death in 1598. The coalition breakdown that followed, the battle of Sekigahara, and the Tokugawa settlement belong to Chapter 41.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how Hideyoshi converted his military initiative after Honnō-ji into political leadership of the Oda inheritance.
  2. Analyze why the contests at Shizugatake and Komaki-Nagakute ended in a mixture of conquest, bargaining, and incorporation rather than simple annihilation of rivals.
  3. Distinguish the military, court-derived, and administrative bases of Hideyoshi's authority, including the offices of kanpaku and Taikō.
  4. Describe the campaigns that brought Shikoku, Kyushu, the Kantō, and the northeast into the Toyotomi order, and explain what "unification" did and did not mean.
  5. Explain why measuring and assessing land was an instrument of political power, and evaluate the extent and limits of the Taikō surveys.
  6. Interpret the 1588 sword hunt and related status orders as parts of a gradual process of social differentiation rather than the sudden creation of a four-class system.
  7. Assess how hostages, castle building, territorial reassignment, and court ritual bound lords to the Toyotomi regime, and identify the regime's institutional weaknesses.
  8. Trace the evolution of Hideyoshi's policy toward Christianity and Nagasaki as his authority expanded.
  9. Evaluate competing explanations for the decision to invade Korea, treating Korea and Ming China as historical agents.
  10. Analyze the course of the two invasions, including logistics, Korean resistance, naval warfare, Ming intervention, civilian suffering, and the use of captives.
  11. Connect domestic consolidation and overseas war as expressions of the same mobilizing capacity, and explain why the settlement depended heavily on Hideyoshi himself.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why was Hideyoshi, rather than Nobunaga's sons, retainers, or allies, able to take over the Oda inheritance, and what role did bargaining play alongside battle?
  2. What did "unification" actually mean in the Toyotomi period, and what did it leave unchanged?
  3. Why is knowing the productive value of land a form of political power?
  4. In what sense were the sword hunts and status orders attempts to reorder society, and why is it misleading to say that Hideyoshi "created" the four-class system?
  5. What replaced the autonomous centers that Nobunaga had destroyed, and how stable was it?
  6. Why did Hideyoshi's policy toward Christianity change as his authority grew?
  7. What explanations have historians offered for the invasion of Korea, and how do they overlap rather than simply compete?
  8. Why did spectacular early victories in 1592 not translate into secure conquest?
  9. How did the invasions look from Korea and Ming China, and how did captives and civilians experience them?
  10. How did the same capacity for mobilization that supported domestic consolidation also help produce overextension?

Historical Context

By 1582, Oda Nobunaga had defeated or subordinated a great many rivals in central Japan. He had destroyed the military power of major religious institutions, such as the Enryaku-ji and the Ishiyama Hongan-ji network, and had placed castles, commanders, and economic policies across a broad region (Chapter 39). His authority, however, remained incomplete in several respects. Great domains in the west, in Kyushu, in the Kantō, and in the northeast were not under his control. His commanders held wide delegated authority, which was effective while he lived but fragile without him. And his holdings had been built around his own person and his own family to a degree that left no clear institutional mechanism for succession. Honnō-ji exposed all of these weaknesses in a single morning in the sixth month of 1582.

Sengoku-period daimyō had already developed many techniques of territorial rule (Chapters 35 and 36). Some had carried out their own surveys of land and population, built centralized castle towns, issued law codes, and mobilized armies on a scale unknown in earlier centuries. Hideyoshi did not invent these practices. What distinguished the Toyotomi regime was the attempt to apply and extend such techniques across the whole archipelago, and to embed them in a hierarchy of lords answerable to a single ruler. The scale of warfare had also changed. Armies now relied heavily on massed infantry and firearms, on castles and logistics, and on levies of men proportional to a lord's resources, all of which would shape both domestic consolidation and the Korean campaigns.

The court remained a source of legitimacy even though it controlled little land or military power. Titles conferred by the emperor, such as those held by the Ashikaga shōguns, retained value because they placed their holders within a recognized hierarchy of authority. Hideyoshi's decision to seek court rank and office, rather than the shogunate, was a distinctive choice, and Chapter 39's careful distinction between military power and court legitimacy continues to apply here. Hideyoshi also took part in the growth of maritime and continental interactions. European merchants and missionaries were present in Kyushu (Chapter 37), the Ming trade system and the East Asian maritime world continued to be shaped by piracy and illicit commerce, and relations among Japan, Korea, Ming China, and the Ryukyu Kingdom had undergone long and complicated changes since the Ashikaga period (Chapters 29 and 32).

Korea's situation is also part of the context. The Joseon dynasty, founded in 1392, had presided over a long period of relative peace with its neighbors, interrupted mainly by raids and small conflicts. It was closely tied to Ming China as a tributary state, and its court, a Neo-Confucian bureaucracy marked by factional rivalry, regarded Japan as a mostly peripheral concern. Relations with Japan had long been managed through Tsushima, whose lords, the Sō, depended on trade with Korea. This position would place the Sō in an uncomfortable and consequential role as intermediaries when Hideyoshi began making demands.

Section 40.1

From Honnō-ji to the Oda Succession Struggle

Chapter 39 narrated Nobunaga's death at Honnō-ji on 21 June 1582 (the second day of the sixth lunar month), when Akechi Mitsuhide attacked him and his eldest son Nobutada in Kyoto. For this chapter, what matters is the position in which the killing left the Oda commanders. Shibata Katsuie was campaigning in Etchū, Niwa Nagahide was preparing for an expedition, Takigawa Kazumasu was in the Kantō, and Tokugawa Ieyasu, an ally rather than a subordinate, was in the Kinai region. Hideyoshi was in Bitchū province, besieging the castle of Takamatsu, held by a vassal of the Mōri, the great lords of the west. The distribution of forces across the realm meant that whoever could react fastest, and whoever could secure the greatest political credit from the response, would gain a decisive advantage.

Hideyoshi's response has become famous for its speed. He concluded a settlement with the Mōri, in which the priest-diplomat Ankokuji Ekei played a role, and turned his army back toward the capital region in what Japanese accounts call a great return march. Sources agree that the return was swift, though the exact details of timing and of when he learned of Nobunaga's death are preserved in accounts written later and with a degree of embellishment. At Yamazaki, south of Kyoto, on 2 July 1582, Hideyoshi's forces, joined by other Oda commanders such as Niwa Nagahide and Nobunaga's son Oda Nobutaka, defeated Mitsuhide, who was killed while fleeing. The Akechi revolt had lasted less than two weeks.

The question for historians is why this military success translated into political advantage. Part of the answer is that Hideyoshi presented himself as the avenger of Nobunaga, a role that legitimized his leadership in the eyes of other retainers. Part is that Yamazaki gave him control over the capital region and the support of commanders who had joined the pursuit. And part is a matter of his previous position: as a senior Oda general with long experience in the west, he commanded a large army that was already in the field. None of these factors guaranteed success, however. Shibata Katsuie, the most senior of Nobunaga's retainers, had a legitimate claim to lead the Oda house, and the Oda family itself included adult sons and a grandson.

In the summer of 1582, senior Oda retainers met at Kiyosu in Owari, the so-called Kiyosu conference, to settle the succession and redistribute territory. The accounts of this meeting derive largely from later narratives and popular retellings, so details of its proceedings should be treated carefully. The outcome is clearer: Nobutada's infant son, Sanpōshi (later known as Oda Hidenobu), was recognized as Nobunaga's heir, with his uncles Nobukatsu and Nobutaka receiving territories of their own, and the major commanders, including Hideyoshi, Katsuie, Niwa Nagahide, and Ikeda Tsuneoki, shared the redistribution of lands. Hideyoshi's holdings expanded in the Kinai region, while Katsuie obtained territory that had previously been Hideyoshi's. The settlement was therefore a compromise among rivals, not a resolution. Hideyoshi's staging of a large funeral for Nobunaga at Daitoku-ji in Kyoto in late 1582 can be understood as another effort to claim the role of Oda's principal mourner and successor, in competition with Nobunaga's own family and with Katsuie.

Hideyoshi also began, almost immediately, the practice of surveying land in the provinces that came under his authority. Surveys carried out in Yamashiro province in 1582 are conventionally regarded as the first stage of what later became the Taikō land surveys, discussed in Section 40.4. The point to notice here is that administration and politics were never separate in his rise. Controlling Kyoto and the Kinai region meant controlling the greatest concentration of economic resources in the realm, including merchants and artisans of Kyoto, Sakai, and soon Osaka, and the ability to assign revenues to followers.

Section 40.2

Shizugatake, Komaki-Nagakute, and the Struggle for the Oda Inheritance

The compromise at Kiyosu did not last. Tensions grew between Hideyoshi and Shibata Katsuie, who was supported by Oda Nobutaka, while Nobunaga's second son Nobukatsu eventually aligned himself against Hideyoshi and with Tokugawa Ieyasu. Katsuie's position was weakened by geography. He was based in Echizen, and winter snow limited his ability to move south, which gave Hideyoshi the opportunity to attack Nobutaka's castle in Gifu and consolidate his position in the winter of 1582–1583. In the spring of 1583, Katsuie's forces advanced into Ōmi, where an advance by his nephew, Sakuma Morimasa, was met by a rapid counter-march by Hideyoshi, who returned quickly from Ōgaki to the lakeside region around Shizugatake. In the ensuing battle, one of Katsuie's allies, Maeda Toshiie, withdrew his troops, and Katsuie's army collapsed.

Katsuie retreated to his castle at Kita-no-shō in Echizen, where he and Oichi, Nobunaga's sister, whom he had married, died as the castle fell. Oichi's three daughters, the future Chacha (Yodo-dono), Hatsu, and Ōeyo (Gō), were spared and sent away, and their later lives will matter to the history of the Toyotomi and Tokugawa houses. Nobutaka was forced to take his own life. Maeda Toshiie, whose withdrawal had helped determine the outcome, was soon rewarded by Hideyoshi, an early example of the way the regime could incorporate a former rival whose cooperation or neutrality had political value.

The more difficult conflict was with Tokugawa Ieyasu. In 1584, Oda Nobukatsu, partly out of fear of Hideyoshi's growing power, sought Ieyasu's support. The resulting campaign, centered on Komaki and Nagakute in Owari and Mikawa, was a stalemate in which neither side achieved a decisive victory. At Nagakute, a Toyotomi force sent to raid Ieyasu's home territory was defeated, and two of Hideyoshi's commanders, Ikeda Tsuneoki and Mori Nagayoshi, were killed. Hideyoshi, however, did not seek to settle the matter by repeating assaults. Instead, he negotiated separately with Nobukatsu, who made peace with him in the winter of 1584. Ieyasu, having lost his stated reason for fighting, withdrew, and he sent a son as an adoptive child or hostage to Hideyoshi's household.

The negotiations continued. In 1586, Hideyoshi arranged for his half-sister Asahi to marry Ieyasu and later sent his mother, the Ōmandokoro, to stay with Ieyasu as a further guarantee, after which Ieyasu came to Osaka and formally submitted. This sequence is instructive. Hideyoshi could have continued the war against Ieyasu, and some of his commanders may have wished to do so, but a settlement preserved Ieyasu's forces and territory while placing him in a subordinate relationship, at a time when Hideyoshi's attention was directed toward Shikoku and Kyushu. The result was an arrangement that was as much diplomatic as military, and it would define the Toyotomi order: powerful lords continued to exist, but they were bound by marriage, hostages, titles, and obligations. It also created a long-term situation in which the single most powerful daimyō stood close to Hideyoshi's own regime, a fact whose consequences belong to Chapter 41.

The historical point to emphasize is that Hideyoshi did not simply inherit Nobunaga's authority. He had to defeat some claimants, isolate others, bargain with powerful lords, and incorporate former rivals. The Oda family was not abolished: Nobukatsu and the infant Hidenobu received positions within the new order, though neither retained real independent power. This process of subordination and absorption, as opposed to elimination, helps explain how the regime was able to expand so quickly.

Section 40.3

From Oda Commander to National Ruler

Between 1583 and 1590, Hideyoshi completed the military submission of the archipelago's major powers. His power base was Osaka, where, beginning in 1583, he constructed a great castle on the site of the destroyed Ishiyama Hongan-ji, a location that gave him command of Osaka Bay, the Yodo River, and the routes between Kyoto and the Inland Sea. From there he moved against remaining centers of local autonomy. In 1585 his forces attacked the armed religious and military communities of Kii province, including the Negoro-ji complex and the Saika league, ending their autonomy, and the powerful monastic center of Kōyasan negotiated its submission. The settlement of Hongan-ji, whose headquarters was later moved to Kyoto with Hideyoshi's support in 1591, reflected the preference for incorporation over elimination.

In 1585, a large army under Hideyoshi's half-brother Hidenaga and his nephew Hidetsugu invaded Shikoku, where Chōsokabe Motochika had built a powerful domain. Motochika submitted and was allowed to retain Tosa. In 1587, after Ōtomo Sōrin of Bungo appealed to Hideyoshi for help against the expanding power of the Shimazu of Satsuma, Hideyoshi himself led a very large army to Kyushu. The Shimazu, who had come close to dominating the island, were defeated and submitted, retaining their core territories of Satsuma and Ōsumi and part of Hyūga. Kyushu was then divided among lords loyal to Hideyoshi, and its great port of Hakata was reorganized. The conquest of Kyushu also brought Hideyoshi into direct contact with the Portuguese trading and missionary world, which is discussed in Section 40.7.

The last major campaign of this phase took place in the east. The Later Hōjō, who ruled the Kantō from Odawara, had refused to acknowledge Hideyoshi's authority. In the spring of 1590, an army that was among the largest ever mobilized in Japan surrounded Odawara castle, the headquarters of a formidable, well-fortified domain. The siege lasted for several months, and Hideyoshi used it as an occasion for display, with his lords and retinue encamped near the castle. The Hōjō surrendered in the summer; the former lord Ujimasa and his brother were ordered to commit suicide, while Ujinao, who was Ieyasu's son-in-law, was spared and sent into exile on Kōyasan. Ieyasu was then transferred from his old domains in the Tōkai region to the former Hōjō territories in the Kantō. This large-scale reassignment, which moved a major daimyō away from his long-established base, is an example of the policy of kunigae, discussed in Section 40.6, and its consequences are developed in Chapter 41.

Lords of the northeast, including Date Masamune, were required to demonstrate their submission by attending on Hideyoshi, and several arrived at or near Odawara. Later accounts embroider these arrivals with dramatic details (Masamune's alleged appearance in white funerary dress is a famous example), and they should be treated as traditions rather than established fact. After the Odawara campaign Hideyoshi reassigned territories in the northeast, confirming some lords, dispossessing others, and ordering surveys. These measures were not welcomed everywhere, and uprisings in Ōshū in 1590 and 1591 required military suppression, showing that the submission of regional lords did not automatically mean the acceptance of Toyotomi rule by regional populations.

The edges of the archipelago also came into view. In 1593, Kakizaki Yoshihiro, a lord based at the southern end of Ezo (Hokkaido), visited Hideyoshi at his base in Hizen and received written confirmation of his authority over trade with the Ainu and the right to collect fees from traders. This was not conquest of Ezo, and Ainu communities remained politically autonomous in most respects, but it gave a Wajin family a powerful endorsement that would underpin the later Matsumae domain (Chapter 48). In the south, Hideyoshi used the Shimazu to press the Ryukyu Kingdom for contributions to the Korean campaign. The Ryukyuan court, which maintained its own tributary relationship with Ming China (Chapters 32 and 33), supplied only part of what was demanded, an episode that foreshadows the harder relationship with Satsuma that culminated in 1609.

What, then, did "unification" (tenka tōitsu) mean? It meant that by 1590 no major regional lord remained outside a hierarchy headed by Hideyoshi and that Hideyoshi could reassign, summon, and command lords across the archipelago. It did not mean the abolition of domains or the creation of uniform administration. Daimyō governed their own retainers, collected their own revenues, and maintained their own armies, but within a political framework in which they owed service to Hideyoshi, held lands that he confirmed or transferred, and sent family members and attendants to live under his eye. This was a system of layered authority, not a modern state, and it is the starting point for understanding the Tokugawa order that followed (Part VII).

Historical Vocabulary
Kanpaku and Taikō

The kanpaku was a court office, traditionally held by the heads of the Fujiwara regental families, that gave its holder the authority to assist an adult emperor in governance. Hideyoshi obtained the post in 1585 after being adopted into the Konoe branch of the Fujiwara, since the office was by custom limited to those families. In 1586 he received a new surname, Toyotomi, and was appointed dajō-daijin (grand chancellor), the highest office in the court bureaucracy. In 1591 he passed the kanpaku office to his nephew Hidetsugu and took the title Taikō, which referred to a kanpaku who had stepped down and which Hideyoshi came to use as his popular designation.

The significance of these offices should not be misread. Hideyoshi did not become shōgun, and he did not govern through the court as an Heian regent would have. His real power came from armies, castles, and the daimyō hierarchy, but court rank gave that power a form of legitimacy that was recognized by lords and by the court, and it permitted him to issue orders in the name of the emperor's government. The distinction between military power and court legitimacy, introduced in Chapter 39, remains essential.

Section 40.4

The Taikō Land Surveys

Of all the instruments of Toyotomi rule, the land surveys, known collectively as the Taikō kenchi, may have had the deepest long-term consequences. A kenchi was a survey of land, and the Toyotomi surveys measured cultivated fields, classified them by quality, assessed their expected yield, and recorded who was responsible for cultivating and paying taxes on them. The practice had precedents. Sengoku daimyō had conducted their own surveys, and the Ashikaga and earlier periods had known many forms of land registration. What marked the Toyotomi surveys was their scale and the attempt to apply broadly similar standards across territories that now fell within a single hierarchy.

Why was surveying land a form of political power? A regime that wished to assign domains to followers needed to know what each territory was worth, and a regime that wished to mobilize armies needed to know how many men and how much grain each lord could be required to supply. Surveys provided the information that allowed Hideyoshi to measure rewards and obligations in common terms. They also changed the relationships among those who claimed rights to a field. In the medieval system of estates (shōen) and layered claims, many people could hold different rights to the same land. The surveys tended, at least in principle, to recognize those who actually cultivated land as responsible for paying taxes and to record them by name, simplifying the web of intermediate claimants and increasing the visibility of villages and individual cultivators to higher authority.

Procedures varied, but the main features are well attested. Surveyors used standardized measuring rods and measures, and the unit of area (tan) was redefined so that it could be applied more uniformly. Fields were graded as superior, middle, inferior, or lowest, and a yield estimate expressed in koku of rice was assigned. These figures were compiled in registers, which identified the plot, its area, grade, assessed yield, and the person responsible for it. Village residences were also measured and assessed. The total of these assessments for a village, or for a lord's domain, became its kokudaka, the rated productive capacity on which obligations, such as taxes and military service, were based. By the late 1590s the total of assessments for the realm is often estimated at around eighteen million koku, though such totals are approximations and rested on surveys of uneven thoroughness.

Historical Vocabulary
Kenchi and Kokudaka

Kenchi (land survey) refers to the measurement and assessment of agricultural land. A koku was a measure of volume of rice, roughly enough, by common reckoning, to feed one adult for a year. Kokudaka (literally "koku amount") was the assessed productive capacity of a field, village, or domain expressed in koku. It was an administrative rating and not a simple measure of actual harvest, and it was used to establish tax obligations, to size a lord's domain, and to determine the number of soldiers, laborers, and ships he could be required to provide.

Kokudaka matters because it created a common language for status and obligation. A daimyō was increasingly defined by the assessed size of his domain, and a village by the kokudaka it owed. This system, begun under Hideyoshi, would shape Tokugawa rule for more than two centuries (Chapter 42).

It would be misleading to portray the surveys as a single, perfectly uniform nationwide census conducted at one moment. They proceeded province by province, often in the wake of conquest or territorial reassignment, and they were carried out by different officials with differing degrees of rigor. In some places, local people negotiated, resisted, or underreported, and villages sometimes concealed land. Survey data could be used by individual daimyō to assess their own territories, and the scope of regional variation was large.

Surveys were also politically dangerous. In Higo in Kyushu, Sassa Narimasa, installed by Hideyoshi after the Kyushu campaign in 1587, began surveys that provoked a revolt among local landholders, who feared the loss of their rights and status. Hideyoshi punished Narimasa, who was ordered to commit suicide in 1588, but the underlying policy continued. The Ōshū uprisings of 1590–1591 likewise involved resistance to the reorganization of land and authority. These episodes show that the surveys were not a neutral technical exercise. They shifted power among lords, local magnates, village leaders, and cultivators, and those who stood to lose resisted.

Historical Interpretation

How Revolutionary Were the Surveys?

Historians have long disagreed about how much the Taikō kenchi transformed rural society. Older interpretations tended to emphasize a sharp break, arguing that the surveys effectively ended the old estate system and created a new relationship between ruler and cultivator. More recent work stresses continuity with Sengoku-period practices, the gradual and uneven implementation of the surveys, the continuing importance of village negotiation, and the persistence of layered claims in many places. Most scholars today would describe the surveys as an important stage in a cumulative process, extended and systematized under the Tokugawa, rather than a single revolutionary event.

Section 40.5

Sword Hunts and the Reordering of Society

In the seventh lunar month of 1588, Hideyoshi issued an order for the collection of swords, short swords, bows, spears, firearms, and other weapons from the cultivators of the provinces. The order, known as the katanagari or sword hunt, was accompanied by an edict against piracy issued at about the same time, in which Hideyoshi asserted control over maritime violence and warned lords to prevent raiding at sea. The two measures are best understood together as part of an effort to establish the regime's monopoly over organized armed force.

The sword hunt has often been described as the moment when Hideyoshi "created" the four-class system of warriors, farmers, artisans, and merchants. This is a serious oversimplification. The fourfold division was a Confucian-derived idealization that was not an accurate description of Tokugawa society and had no single origin in Hideyoshi's rule (Chapter 43). What the Toyotomi regime did attempt was a process of social differentiation, in which the boundary between warriors and cultivators was made firmer than it had been in the Sengoku period. In that era, many men moved fluidly between farming and fighting. Village leaders might lead local fighting forces, and ordinary cultivators could be levied as foot soldiers (ashigaru) and sometimes win recognition as warriors. Hideyoshi's own origins, in a family of low standing in Owari, illustrate how fluid such categories could be.

A series of orders sought to reduce that fluidity. In 1591, a status order prohibited warrior retainers' attendants from becoming townspeople or farmers, and forbade peasants from abandoning agriculture to take up trade or other work, and it fixed residence for those who changed place. Its immediate purpose was practical and military: the order's terms for warriors referred especially to lower-ranking military retainers, and it aimed to secure fighting men and agricultural production, and hence taxes, in preparation for the Korean campaign, rather than to define hereditary status categories as such. In 1592, in preparation for the mobilization for Korea, the Toyotomi regime, under Kanpaku Toyotomi Hidetsugu, ordered a nationwide household survey, often called the hitobarai-rei, that required village and town households to be counted by status and occupation. These measures made people, as well as land, increasingly legible to authority. Across the Toyotomi period, warriors tended to concentrate near their lords and in castle towns (jōkamachi), while cultivators were more firmly tied to villages and farming. The outcome was not a clean legal separation, which would be more thoroughly developed under the Tokugawa, but a clear movement in that direction.

Primary Source Window

The 1588 Sword-Collection Order

Creator: Toyotomi Hideyoshi's government, issued in his name and conveyed to lords and officials · Date: seventh lunar month, 1588 · Type: Official order (edict), preserved in several copies and versions addressed to different provinces

Context: The order was issued after the Kyushu campaign and the imperial visit to Jurakudai, at a moment when Hideyoshi's authority across the archipelago was extending but not yet complete. Copies were sent to lords and officials, who were responsible for implementation.

Paraphrase (not a verbatim quotation): Farmers in all provinces are forbidden to possess swords, short swords, bows, spears, firearms, or other weapons. Possessing such arms makes it harder to collect rents and encourages uprisings. The weapons collected will not be wasted: they will be used as nails and clamps for the great Buddha image being built at Hōkō-ji in Kyoto, which will benefit farmers in this life and the next. If farmers devote themselves to cultivation and have no weapons, they and their descendants will prosper.

Analysis: The edict is a rich source because of its combination of justifications. It cites the need for peace and the collection of rents, presents disarmament as a religious benefit, and appeals to the interest of the cultivators themselves. It shows a regime that sought to explain, as well as impose, its policy, and it reveals a view of farmers as a distinct group whose proper role was cultivation. Its limitations are equally important. It states the intentions of the government, not the actual extent of collection, which depended on local officials and varied from region to region, and it does not show how villagers responded, whether by compliance, concealment, or negotiation. Weapons were not necessarily eliminated from rural society, and many villages retained tools and some arms for hunting and protecting crops. Nor does the edict indicate how warriors of low rank, who were increasingly required to choose between status groups, experienced the measure. Only the voice of authority appears here, and historians must look to village documents, diaries, and later records to reconstruct reactions below.

Everyday Life and Environment

Consolidation in the Village Household

For a village household, the new order was experienced as a growing and more intrusive presence of authority. Surveyors measured fields and recorded cultivators' names, so that a household's obligations became tied to written assessments. Officials collected weapons or demanded that they be surrendered, and the 1592 census counted the household's members by status. Taxes remained heavy, and they were joined by demands for labor on construction projects such as castles and roads, and, from 1592, for soldiers, porters, and sailors for the Korean campaigns. Coastal communities were particularly burdened with providing crews and ships.

But the picture was not uniform. Village leaders often stood between households and officials, negotiating over the grading of fields and the distribution of burdens, and some benefited from the clearer recognition of their rights. Greater security from private warfare, one effect of the broader settlement, could be welcome. Women worked in the fields, in textile production, and in managing households as men were drawn away for labor or military service, a burden whose extent cannot be quantified because sources about the experiences of ordinary women are scarce. The surviving record, written largely by officials and lords, tells us much more about what authority wanted than about what villagers thought.

Section 40.6

The Architecture of Toyotomi Rule

Chapter 39 left open a question: what would replace the autonomous centers that Nobunaga had destroyed? The answer was neither a continuation of that fragmentation nor a modern centralized state. Hideyoshi's regime rested on a combination of personal authority, a hierarchy of great lords, court legitimacy, direct control over the realm's richest resources, and a set of coercive and ritual devices that kept lords tied to him.

The regime's physical centers were its castles. Osaka Castle, begun in 1583, was an enormous complex of walls, moats, and a lavishly decorated keep, designed as a statement of power and as a base for the control of the Kinai. In Kyoto, Hideyoshi built the Jurakudai (also read Jurakutei), a palace-fortress completed in 1587 and used for ceremonies including the emperor's visit in 1588, and a Fushimi castle, begun in the early 1590s, later became his main residence. Architecture and ceremony were tools of governance. Great tea gatherings, including the one held at Kitano in 1587, and the use of a gilded tea room, showed the regime's wealth and taste and incorporated aesthetic culture into politics, a theme discussed in Chapter 38. The oaths sworn to Hideyoshi at the emperor's visit in 1588 are an example of ritual that made political subordination public.

The regime also relied directly on the wealth of the realm. Hideyoshi controlled a large block of land held directly by himself, and he held major cities and ports, including Osaka, Sakai, Kyoto, Hakata, and Nagasaki, as well as gold and silver mines. This income supported his army, his building projects, and his gifts and rewards, and in some respects it gave him resources that no daimyō could match. Gold and silver coinage was issued as well. From about this period, the regime also began issuing vermilion-seal licenses to some merchants for overseas trade, a practice the Tokugawa would later expand.

To control the daimyō, Hideyoshi used several devices. One was kunigae, the reassignment of lords to new territories. By moving a lord away from the lands where his family had long held power, the regime weakened his local ties and made him more dependent on Hideyoshi, as the transfer of Ieyasu to the Kantō shows. Another was the holding of hostages: wives, heirs, and other relatives of lords were required to live in Osaka or Fushimi. A third was marriage alliance and adoption, which created kinship ties between the Toyotomi and other families. A fourth was ritual and gift exchange, as well as the system of court rank through which many lords received titles and a Toyotomi surname or other signs of favor. Together these made lords participants in a political society centered on Hideyoshi.

This system had strengths. It allowed unprecedented mobilization, as the Korean campaigns would demonstrate, and it ended the open warfare among regional lords that had characterized much of the previous century. But it also had a fundamental weakness, which was its personal character. The administrative apparatus was small, and a relatively limited group of officials, led by trusted commissioners (bugyō), carried out much of the day-to-day work. Loyalty to Hideyoshi, rather than to enduring institutions, held the structure together. His age, his lack of an adult heir, and the problem of succession (Section 40.11) therefore posed a danger to the system itself.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From the Sengoku Order to the Toyotomi Order

What Changed

  • Regional lords were incorporated into a single hierarchy headed by one ruler, and open warfare among them largely ended.
  • Land surveys and kokudaka ratings created common measures of status and obligation.
  • Efforts to disarm cultivators and fix status boundaries made the distinction between warriors and villagers firmer.
  • The regime could mobilize an army for overseas war, an unprecedented achievement.

What Stayed the Same

  • Daimyō continued to govern domains with their own retainers and revenues.
  • Rulership still rested on personal ties, marriage, and hostages as much as on institutions.
  • The court retained its role as a source of titles and legitimacy without gaining real power.
  • Villages continued to negotiate with authority, and many older rights and customs survived.

Who experienced change most directly? Great lords who were transferred or surveyed, warriors forced to choose a status, and villagers who faced assessment and levies. Greater continuity was experienced by court families, whose role shifted less, and by communities in the more distant frontiers. Why the pattern matters: It shows that unification was a transformation of the relationships among powers, not the creation of a new kind of state, and it explains why the Tokugawa could build on Toyotomi foundations while changing a great deal.

Section 40.7

Christianity, Nagasaki, and the Limits of Religious Toleration

The wider history of Christianity and the Nanban world belongs to Chapter 37. Here the question is narrower: why did Hideyoshi's attitude change as his authority expanded? In the early 1580s, he was generally tolerant of the Jesuits, as Nobunaga had been, and he received missionaries and their patrons with courtesy. Christian daimyō, such as the Kyushu lords and Takayama Ukon, served in his armies. The Jesuits, in turn, hoped that his rise would benefit their mission.

The turning point came in 1587, during the Kyushu campaign. At Hakata, in the sixth lunar month, Hideyoshi issued an order expelling the Jesuit missionaries (bateren) from Japan. The sources give several reasons. Hideyoshi had learned that Nagasaki, ceded earlier by the Christian lord Ōmura Sumitada to the Jesuits in some form of administrative control, was in effect governed by a foreign religious order. He was also troubled by reports of pressure on temples and shrines by Christian lords and of the enslavement or export of Japanese people through Portuguese trade, and he was wary of the loyalty that converts might give to missionaries over their lord. An authority that demanded a monopoly of allegiance from lords and was in the process of centralizing control over land and ports could not easily accept a religious organization with its own international connections.

The edict did not, however, end Christianity or foreign trade. It targeted missionaries, while allowing Portuguese merchants to continue trading, and it was inconsistently enforced. Many missionaries stayed, working more discreetly, and Hideyoshi continued to deal with them when it suited him. Nagasaki, however, was brought under his direct control, and the port became a source of revenue and a channel for trade with Europe and China. Takayama Ukon lost his domain for refusing to renounce his faith.

The next stage came in 1596 and 1597. In late 1596, a Spanish ship, the San Felipe, was wrecked on the coast of Shikoku, and Hideyoshi's officials seized its cargo. Accounts, including some from missionaries, report that members of the crew made statements implying that Spanish missionaries prepared the way for conquest. The evidence is difficult to evaluate, as the reports are secondhand and reflect competing interests. In early 1597, Hideyoshi ordered the execution of a group of Christians in Nagasaki: twenty-six men, including Franciscan missionaries, Japanese Jesuits, and Japanese lay converts, some of them young boys, who had been made to travel from Kyoto and Osaka to Nagasaki, where they were crucified on 5 February 1597. The event became an important martyrdom in Catholic tradition. In the context of this chapter, it shows a regime growing more suspicious of any organization whose loyalties could not be controlled, at a time when Hideyoshi was engaged in the Korean war and was concerned about foreign threats and internal obedience. Persecution under the Toyotomi was limited and episodic, however, compared with the systematic suppression carried out by the Tokugawa (Chapter 47).

Section 40.8

Why Invade Korea?

The decision to invade Korea has puzzled historians since the time it was made. It is tempting to explain it as the product of Hideyoshi's growing megalomania, but this explanation is too simple. It treats the war as a personal aberration and hides the structural and political circumstances in which it was conceived. A more careful approach asks what Hideyoshi and his contemporaries thought they were doing, what the war meant for the regime, and what Koreans and the Ming government believed was at stake.

Hideyoshi expressed continental ambitions well before 1592. By the mid-1580s he was reported to have spoken of conquering Ming China, and in the following years he made demands on other societies as well, writing in 1591, for instance, to the Spanish authorities in the Philippines to demand submission. Documents attributed to him, including a letter to his nephew Hidetsugu in 1592 that survives in copy, set out grand plans in which the emperor would eventually reside in Beijing, though such documents are difficult to read as realistic operational plans and their wording and authenticity require care. The language of these texts suggests a ruler who viewed his rise as an extraordinary achievement and who understood authority in expansive terms.

A series of practical circumstances may have made the project appear feasible and attractive. Japan had a very large, battle-experienced army equipped with firearms. The regime had demonstrated that it could command lords and assess resources. The relationship with Ming China was a sore point: formal tribute and tally trade, which had been conducted under the Ashikaga shōguns, had ceased in the sixteenth century, and although by the late sixteenth century the Ming had partially liberalized private maritime commerce, Japan remained excluded from sanctioned trade relations after the breakdown of formal ties and the conflicts associated with the raiders known as wakō. And the newly subordinated daimyō, especially those from Kyushu and the west, needed to demonstrate loyalty. At the same time, the end of internal warfare left a large military class without the prospect of new lands to win, and it raised the question of how Hideyoshi would reward his followers and keep them occupied.

Diplomacy proceeded through Tsushima. The Sō family, whose economy depended on trade with Korea, was asked to induce the Korean court to submit and to give passage to Japanese forces on the way to China. The Sō and their ally Konishi Yukinaga, the son of a Sakai merchant-pharmacist who became a Kyushu daimyō and had ties to the Korean trade, tried to manage the gap between Hideyoshi's demands and what the Koreans might accept. In 1590, a Korean embassy traveled to Kyoto, which Hideyoshi appears to have received as a tribute mission from a subordinate state, although the Joseon court intended the visit as ordinary diplomacy. The response Hideyoshi sent in 1591 demanded that Korea give passage to his forces against the Ming. The Korean court was divided in assessing the threat. The envoys returned with contradictory impressions, and factional rivalries at court complicated the response. Korea refused to join an attack on its overlord, the Ming, and made modest preparations for defense.

Korea must be seen as an actor and not merely as the terrain on the path to China. It was a state with a population of millions, a Confucian bureaucracy, a military organization that had grown weak in some respects after prolonged peace, a navy, fortresses, and long traditions of resistance to external aggression. It also had a close relationship with Ming China that was understood in Korean political culture as both protection and moral obligation. For the Joseon court, the Japanese demand was an affront and a threat that could not be accepted, even if its capacity to resist was uncertain.

Historical Debate

Why Did Hideyoshi Invade Korea?

The question: What motives and circumstances led Hideyoshi to launch an attack on Korea with the stated aim of conquering Ming China?

Interpretation A — Personal ambition and an East Asian order centered on Hideyoshi: Some scholars emphasize Hideyoshi's own expansive vision, his sense of unprecedented achievement, and his desire for a new regional order, in which he, rather than the Ming emperor, would stand at the center. They point to his letters and demands, to the grandiose plans attributed to him, and to his attempt to restore trade on his own terms.
Interpretation B — Domestic political and military structures: Other historians stress the circumstances of the regime: the need to employ and reward a vast army and its commanders, the use of external war to bind newly subordinated lords more tightly, the absence of new land to distribute at home, and the momentum of a system organized for mobilization. These explanations treat the war as the extension of consolidation rather than a break from it.

Evidence: The arguments rely on Hideyoshi's surviving letters and orders (some preserved only in later copies), diplomatic records from Tsushima, Korea, and Ming China, the organization and timing of the mobilization, and evidence of how the war was supposed to benefit lords. The interpretations are not mutually exclusive, and many historians combine them, arguing that ambition, prestige, regional politics, and the structure of the regime reinforced one another. The older view of simple irrationality has been widely set aside.

Why it matters: Whether the war is seen primarily as an expression of Hideyoshi's personality or of the regime's structure affects how historians view the Toyotomi order itself, whether as a coherent system whose logic led toward overseas expansion or as a personal rule that outran its limits.

Historical Vocabulary
Imjin Waeran and Bunroku–Keichō no Eki

The names given to the wars show how perspective shapes memory. In Korea, the first invasion is called the Imjin Waeran (the Japanese invasion of the Imjin year, 1592) and the second the Jeongyu Jaeran (the renewed disturbance of the Jeongyu year, 1597). In Japan, the conflict is commonly called the Bunroku–Keichō no eki, after the two Japanese era names of the period, with eki meaning a campaign or war. In China, the war is often described as the campaign to aid Korea.

Each name reflects a viewpoint: Korean terms mark an invasion and its aftermath, while the Japanese term uses the vocabulary of regnal eras. Earlier Japanese works sometimes called the war a "Korean expedition," a phrase that hides the fact that it was an attack on a sovereign state. This chapter uses neutral terms while noting these differences.

Section 40.9

The First Invasion of Korea, 1592–1593

Preparations were extensive. Hideyoshi ordered daimyō to supply troops, ships, and provisions in proportion to their assessed domains, a use of the kokudaka system that tied the surveys directly to overseas war. A great staging base was constructed at Nagoya in Hizen, in northern Kyushu, and Hideyoshi himself came there to oversee the campaign, though he never crossed to Korea. Accounts of the army's size vary, but the first wave consisted of well over a hundred thousand soldiers organized in several divisions, supported by sailors, porters, and laborers, many of whom came from coastal communities and villages that had been affected by the levies described earlier. Commanders included Konishi Yukinaga, Katō Kiyomasa, and Kuroda Nagamasa. The Sō family and Tsushima served as a first stage and guides.

The Japanese landed at Busan in the fourth lunar month of 1592 (late May in the Gregorian calendar) and rapidly took Busan and the nearby city of Dongnae, where the Korean defenders put up serious resistance but were overwhelmed. The Japanese advance was extraordinarily fast. Aided by the road network, surprise, battle-hardened infantry organization, and the mass deployment of arquebuses, which the Koreans had not faced in such numbers, they pushed north. A Korean army under Sin Rip, assembled to block the passes, was destroyed near Chungju in the open ground by the river, and the court at Seoul, unable to hold the capital, left. King Seonjo fled north, and the Japanese entered Seoul in early summer. Konishi's forces took Pyongyang in the summer, while Katō Kiyomasa advanced into the northeast, where two Korean princes were captured, in part through the actions of local people. The court withdrew to the border region near the Yalu River, where it appealed for Ming help. In Seoul, palaces were burned, though the sources are inconsistent as to who was responsible and to what extent looting and destruction by Koreans, as well as Japanese, contributed.

Spectacular early advances, however, did not translate into secure conquest, and the reasons are central to understanding the war. The first was geography and logistics. Hideyoshi's plan depended on sea transport to supply the army moving north, but Korea's western coast was dangerous for Japanese ships. Overland supply across mountainous terrain, along roads that were poor by Japanese standards and exposed to ambush, was slow and insufficient, and the army, living partly off the country, faced food shortages as well as a severe Korean winter, to which it was unaccustomed. The second was the Korean navy. Admiral Yi Sun-sin, commander in the southwest, won a series of victories beginning in the early summer of 1592, at Okpo, Sacheon, and elsewhere, using heavily armed ships, including the covered vessel known as the geobukseon (turtle ship), and superior gunnery. At the battle of Hansan Island in the late summer, his fleet destroyed a large part of a Japanese squadron. These victories disrupted Japanese communications and supply by sea and prevented the westward and northward movement that Hideyoshi's plan required.

The third factor was Korean resistance on land. Local gentry, scholars, and former officials raised ŭibyŏng (righteous armies), such as those led by Gwak Jae-u and others, and Buddhist monks, including figures such as Hyujeong and Yujeong, organized monk-soldier units. These irregular forces harassed Japanese supply lines and garrisons, and helped sustain the sense that Korea had not been conquered. Japanese commanders also faced defections, as some Japanese soldiers deserted and served the Korean side, passing on knowledge of firearms. The Korean regular military, though defeated in many early battles, recovered in places, and at the first battle of Jinju in late 1592 the defenders held a fortress against a large Japanese attack.

The fourth factor was the intervention of Ming China. After some hesitation, the Wanli Emperor sent troops under commanders such as Li Rusong. In early 1593 a Ming and Korean force retook Pyongyang, and although the Ming army suffered a setback at Byeokjegwan near Seoul, the Japanese concluded that holding the capital was unsustainable. The Japanese forces withdrew from Seoul in the spring of 1593 and fell back to the southern coast, where they built fortified bases known as wajō (Japanese-style castles). The Korean army under Kwon Yul won an important defensive victory at Haengju near Seoul. In the summer of 1593, however, the Japanese took revenge by capturing Jinju in a second attack, killing many of the garrison and civilians. The war then settled into a prolonged stalemate, with the Japanese holding a strip of southern coast and the Ming and Koreans holding the rest.

Section 40.10

Negotiation, the Second Invasion, and the Collapse of the Continental Project

From 1593 to 1596, the war was largely replaced by negotiation. Japanese and Ming representatives, with Konishi Yukinaga and Sō Yoshitoshi playing leading roles on the Japanese side and the Ming envoy Shen Weijing for China, discussed terms. The talks were shaped by misunderstandings and, according to many accounts, by deliberate misrepresentation. Hideyoshi's demands, as set out in 1593, included the marriage of a Ming princess to the Japanese emperor, the restoration of tally trade, and a division of Korea in which Japan would receive its southern provinces. The Ming court, for its part, understood the negotiations as the acceptance by Hideyoshi of Ming overlordship. According to widely repeated accounts, intermediaries softened or altered each side's messages so that each believed the other was submitting, though the details of who falsified what, and when, are a subject of scholarly dispute and rest on sources written with hindsight.

The failure became evident in the autumn of 1596, when a Ming mission arrived in Osaka. The Ming letter, according to accounts, granted Hideyoshi the title of "King of Japan," within a framework of Ming investiture, and his reaction was reportedly furious. In early 1597 he ordered a new invasion. The second war, called Jeongyu Jaeran in Korea, had more limited aims. Rather than conquering Korea and China, Hideyoshi sought to take and hold the southern provinces, and his armies, again numbering well over a hundred thousand, advanced into Jeolla and Chungcheong. They faced a more prepared opponent. Korea had reorganized, Ming troops were present, and the Japanese were unable to penetrate far beyond the south. A Ming-Korean force halted their northward movement at Jiksan in the autumn of 1597.

At sea, the Korean court's politics produced a crisis. Yi Sun-sin was removed from command in 1597 following political disputes and accusations, and his successor, Won Gyun, lost most of the fleet at Chilcheollyang. Yi was restored, and in the autumn of 1597, at the strait of Myeongnyang, a small Korean force defeated a much larger Japanese fleet in narrow, treacherous waters, an outcome that helped restore Korean control of the sea. In the winter of 1597–1598, a Ming-Korean army besieged the Japanese fortress at Ulsan, where Katō Kiyomasa's garrison suffered severely from cold and shortage of food before relief arrived. Many Japanese soldiers died from disease and exposure, as well as combat.

The second war was also marked by increasingly brutal practices. Hideyoshi ordered that the noses of killed enemies be cut off and sent to Japan as proof of kills, in place of heads, which were too heavy to transport. Commanders issued receipts for shipments of noses, which included those of civilians, women, and children, as Japanese records show. The noses were buried in a mound near the Hōkō-ji in Kyoto, still known as the Mimizuka (ear mound), erected in 1597. Civilians suffered directly in the fighting and its aftermath: villages were burned, harvests destroyed, and people killed or displaced. Famine and disease spread across southern Korea, and the effects on the agricultural economy were severe, though reliable numbers for deaths, which almost certainly ran to many tens of thousands or more, do not exist.

Captives formed another dimension of the war. Japanese forces took many Koreans to Japan, where some were sold, some became laborers or servants, and some were later returned or remained. Estimates of their number vary widely, from tens of thousands upward, and cannot be fixed with confidence. A Japanese Buddhist priest who accompanied the army kept a diary that describes the trade in captives, a rare contemporaneous testimony. Among the captives were skilled artisans, including potters, whose techniques helped shape ceramic production in Kyushu, in places such as Arita, Karatsu, and Satsuma, and scholars, such as the Neo-Confucian Kang Hang, who interacted with Japanese intellectuals including Fujiwara Seika; the extent of his influence on Japanese Zhu Xi learning remains debated (Chapter 47). These cultural consequences emerged from forced removal and must not be treated as a benign "exchange."

Hideyoshi died at Fushimi on 18 September 1598. His death was initially concealed, and the council of regents ordered the withdrawal of Japanese forces. In the autumn, Ming-Korean offensives against Japanese positions at Suncheon and Sacheon failed, but the Japanese had decided to leave, and during their evacuation in late 1598, a naval engagement at Noryang Strait cost the Korean fleet its commander: Yi Sun-sin was killed, according to tradition, as the battle was ending. The last Japanese troops left Korea in the winter. The war had produced none of its intended political results for Japan. Korea had suffered devastation on a vast scale, Ming China had spent enormous resources in a conflict that coincided with other costly campaigns, and the defeat of the invasion strengthened Korean and Chinese memory of Japan as an aggressor. In Japan, the war ended with no territory gained, many dead, and resentments among commanders and lords that would persist.

The War in Korea: Who Experienced What?

Hideyoshi and the commanding lords: For Hideyoshi, the war was an extension of his rule and a demonstration of the regime's power, and for lords such as Konishi and Katō it was an opportunity for distinction and a requirement of loyalty. Their goals and strategies differed, and their rivalry would outlast the war.
Japanese soldiers, porters, and sailors: Drawn from villages and coasts by levies calculated on kokudaka, many endured long sea journeys, cold, hunger, and disease in a foreign land. They also took part in violence against civilians and in the taking of captives, though individual conduct varied and the sources rarely record ordinary men's own words.
The Korean court and officials: The Joseon court faced military collapse, flight from the capital, factional division, and dependence on Ming aid, while its officials struggled to organize defense, supply armies, and cope with famine. The dismissal and restoration of Yi Sun-sin illustrates how politics complicated the war effort.
Korean commoners, righteous armies, and monks: Villagers faced violence, destruction of crops, forced labor, displacement, and capture, while many also took up arms in irregular forces. Local elites and monks led resistance, and the relationship between these groups and the state was sometimes tense.
Ming officials and soldiers: For the Ming court, the war was a defense of a tributary and a buffer for China's own frontier, but also a heavy financial burden. Ming troops contributed significantly to the Korean recovery, while their presence also placed heavy demands on Korean resources.
The lords of Tsushima: The Sō family, dependent on Korean trade, was caught between Hideyoshi's demands and the interests of its economic partner, and its role as intermediary shows how the war disrupted existing networks of exchange.
Cause and Effect

The Failure of the Invasions

Long-term causes

A century of Sengoku warfare had produced large, experienced, firearm-equipped armies and techniques of logistics and levy. Strained relations with Ming China and the end of tally trade created grievances, while Korean-Japanese relations had long run through Tsushima.

Short-term causes

Hideyoshi's unification gave him the authority to mobilize through the kokudaka system. His ambitions, the diplomatic failures of 1590–1591, the need to occupy and reward lords, and the confidence produced by rapid victories led to the decision to invade.

Event and immediate effects

The Japanese won rapid early victories but were stopped by naval defeat, supply failure, irregular resistance, and Ming intervention. Korea suffered mass death, destruction, and displacement, and the war ended without Japanese gains.

Long-term consequences

The war drained the Toyotomi regime, left a legacy of rivalries among its lords, shaped Korean and Chinese historical memory, and brought captives and technologies into Japan. It also left unresolved diplomatic relations that the Tokugawa would later reconstruct (Chapter 48).

Historical complexity: The failure cannot be attributed to a single cause. Naval power, supply, resistance, Ming strength, Japanese strategic assumptions, and the conditions of occupation all interacted. Nor was the outcome predetermined; the early victories show how close Korea came to collapse.

Section 40.11

Hideyoshi's Final Years and an Unsettled Succession

While the war continued, the question of succession became central. Hideyoshi's first son by Yodo-dono (Chacha), Tsurumatsu, was born in 1589 and died in 1591, and Hideyoshi, who had earlier adopted his nephew Hidetsugu as heir, now passed the kanpaku office to him and took the title Taikō. In 1593, Yodo-dono gave birth to another son, Hideyori. The existence of a new heir created a conflict of interest. In 1595, Hidetsugu was accused of misconduct and disloyalty, sent to Kōyasan, and ordered to commit suicide, and his wives, children, and attendants were executed in Kyoto. The motives and the truth of the charges are disputed, and the accounts are colored by later hostility, but the incident demonstrates how severely the problem of succession could affect the regime. It also left Hideyoshi with no adult heir.

In his last years, Hideyoshi tried to construct arrangements that would protect Hideyori after his death. He required the major lords to swear oaths of loyalty to the child, and he arranged a system of councils: a group of senior daimyō, later called the Council of Five Elders (gotairō), which included Tokugawa Ieyasu and Maeda Toshiie, and a group of commissioners, the Five Commissioners (gobugyō), responsible for administration. The structure was meant to balance power among them. Hideyoshi died on 18 September 1598, leaving a regime that remained dependent on the personal bonds he had created, a child heir, and an army and nobility drawn into a war that had not been won.

The chapter ends here. The tensions among the councils and commanders, the questions of loyalty to Hideyori, and the events that followed belong to Chapter 41. What should be recognized at this point is the nature of the problem: Hideyoshi had created a political order of unprecedented scope, but its institutions were not yet strong enough to survive without him.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that Hideyoshi completed the military-political unification that Nobunaga had left unfinished by subordinating regional lords to an archipelago-wide hierarchy and making land, status, and military obligation increasingly legible to central authority. Yet the settlement remained dependent on Hideyoshi personally, and the same capacity for mass mobilization that supported domestic consolidation was redirected into the unsuccessful invasions of Korea.

Hideyoshi's rise began with his rapid response to Nobunaga's death. By making peace with the Mōri, returning swiftly to the capital region, and defeating Akechi Mitsuhide at Yamazaki, he gained a position of leadership that he reinforced at the Kiyosu conference and by conducting Nobunaga's funeral. But he did not simply inherit authority. He defeated Shibata Katsuie at Shizugatake and fought an inconclusive campaign against Oda Nobukatsu and Tokugawa Ieyasu, then resolved it through negotiation, marriage, and hostages. That pattern of conquest combined with settlement characterized the regime.

Between 1583 and 1590, campaigns in Kii, Shikoku, Kyushu, and the Kantō, and the submission of northern lords, brought the great regional powers within a single hierarchy. Hideyoshi used court office, becoming kanpaku in 1585 and later Taikō, and the Toyotomi surname, to give his authority legitimacy without becoming shōgun. Unification meant that no major lord stood outside the system, not that the domains had been abolished. Daimyō continued to govern, but within a framework in which Hideyoshi could survey, reassign, and command them. Frontier relationships, with the Kakizaki of Ezo and the Ryukyu court, show the reach of that framework to the archipelago's edges, while Ainu and Ryukyuan communities retained their own political and economic worlds.

The land surveys were among the regime's most consequential instruments. By measuring fields, grading their quality, assigning kokudaka, and recording responsible cultivators, they provided the information needed to assign domains, collect taxes, and levy troops. They also altered the balance among lords, local magnates, and villagers, and provoked resistance, as in Higo and in the northeast. They proceeded unevenly, built on Sengoku precedents, and were completed and extended by the Tokugawa.

The sword hunt of 1588 and the status orders of 1591–1592 attempted to separate warriors from cultivators and to fix people in their places. They are best understood as stages in a process of social differentiation, not as the sudden creation of a four-class system. For village households, the result was a more intrusive and legible authority, expressed in surveys, disarmament, levies, and censuses, though negotiation, concealment, and local variation continued.

The Toyotomi order replaced the autonomous centers that Nobunaga had destroyed with a structure of castles, direct holdings, territorial reassignment, hostages, marriage alliances, ritual, and court rank. It was more encompassing than any earlier Sengoku arrangement, but its institutions were thin, and its coherence depended on Hideyoshi's personal authority. Toward Christianity, his policy shifted from tolerance to restriction as he brought Nagasaki under control and grew wary of organizations with independent loyalties, culminating in the executions of 1597.

The invasions of Korea were not simply the irrational act of a single ruler, but the result of Hideyoshi's ambitions, strained relations with Ming China, diplomatic failure through Tsushima, and the structure of a regime organized for mobilization. Korea was a sovereign state with its own institutions, and its court, armies, navy, righteous armies, and civilians made decisions and bore the consequences. The first invasion of 1592 achieved rapid advances, but supply failure, Korean naval victories, irregular resistance, and Ming intervention converted it into stalemate.

The failed negotiations of 1593–1596 and the second invasion of 1597 showed the incompatibility of the claims made on each side. The war became more destructive, involving the mass taking of noses, the devastation of villages and harvests, and the capture and removal of Koreans to Japan. Whatever technological and cultural consequences followed from the movement of potters and scholars, they arose from coercion. Hideyoshi's death in 1598 ended the project, leaving Korea ravaged, Ming China burdened, and Japan with no gains.

The chapter ends with the succession problem. The death of Tsurumatsu, the fall of Hidetsugu, the birth of Hideyori, and the councils created in Hideyoshi's last years reveal a regime that had constructed an unprecedented order but could not yet reproduce itself. What happened next is the subject of Chapter 41.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a political order of unusual reach can be assembled quickly and yet remain fragile. Hideyoshi's regime tied together conquest, bargaining, court legitimacy, surveys, status distinctions, and ritual, and in doing so it ended the open warfare among regional lords that had defined much of the previous century. It also revealed the limits of such an order when it rested on one person and on a capacity for mobilization that had been developed in war.

The Toyotomi settlement shaped what followed. The kokudaka system, the practice of reassigning domains, the hostage system, the separation of warriors from villages, and the experience of mobilization on a vast scale all became materials from which the Tokugawa would build a durable order. At the same time, the Toyotomi experience left problems that the Tokugawa had to address, among them succession, the position of great lords, and the management of external relations. These continuities and departures will be traced in Chapters 41 and 42.

The chapter also connects the archipelago to its neighbors in a way that is difficult to ignore. Having largely ended open warfare among the major lords at home, the Toyotomi regime redirected its extraordinary military capacity into Korea, and the invasions left a profound mark on Korea and on Ming China. Korean memory of Japanese aggression, the coerced movement of artisans and scholars, and the interrupted diplomacy that the Tokugawa and Joseon would later reconstruct all stem from these years. Hokkaido's southern frontier and the Ryukyu Kingdom, too, were drawn into the pressures of the Toyotomi era, in ways that anticipate later developments.

Students who understand Hideyoshi's regime as a system of expanding capacity, rather than as the work of one remarkable figure, will be better prepared for Chapter 41, which turns to what happened when that system lost its center, and to Part VII, which examines how the Tokugawa transformed the inheritance of unification into a stable social and political order.
