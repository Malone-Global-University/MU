Women, Families, Education, and New Religions

Japanese History: From Jōmon Japan to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

In 1871, five girls boarded a ship at Yokohama as part of a government mission to the United States and Europe. The youngest, Tsuda Umeko, was about six years old. Three decades later she opened a school of her own in Tokyo. Between the departure and the school lies much of this chapter: the transformation of households, classrooms, religious life, and women's public roles in a country that was changing its government, its economy, and its place in the world.

The previous chapters of this part followed the Meiji constitution, the wars with China and Russia, and the building of an empire. This chapter turns to the places where most people actually experienced those changes: the household, the school, the neighborhood, the shrine or meeting hall, and the church. A farm family in Tōhoku, a merchant household in Osaka, a former samurai family in Tokyo, a mission-school student in Yokohama, and a pilgrim travelling to a village in Yamato did not experience "modernization" in the same way. They lived inside the same state, but they met it through different laws, different institutions, and different expectations about what a woman, a child, a husband, or a believer ought to be.

The central argument of the chapter is that these changes were contradictory. Between the 1870s and about 1916, schooling expanded dramatically, yet it did so in ways that taught boys and girls to expect different futures. The Civil Code of 1898 gave Japan a modern, systematic body of private law, yet its family provisions placed legal authority in the household head and limited the legal capacity of wives. Women found new work as teachers, nurses, midwives, and writers, and some of them argued in public for greater autonomy, while the law barred them from political associations. New religious movements grew out of the experiences of peasants and villagers, and Christianity attracted a small but influential following, yet both operated under official supervision that expected loyalty to the state. This chapter explains these tensions rather than presenting modernization as uniform liberation or uninterrupted oppression.

The chapter begins with the household, because the household was the basic unit through which law, taxation, schooling, and inheritance reached ordinary people. It then examines women's changing positions, the expansion of schooling, urban growth and everyday life, and finally two linked themes of religion and reform: the new religions, above all Tenrikyō and Konkōkyō, and the Christian and Buddhist movements that shaped education, philanthropy, and campaigns for moral and social improvement. The chronology runs mainly from the 1870s to about 1916. Where the religious movements were founded earlier, in 1838 and 1859, the chapter begins before the Meiji Restoration in order to explain how they grew.

Several cautions apply throughout. The word "Japanese" in this period names a state that was still defining its population. Ainu, Okinawan, Burakumin, and other communities met the new schools, family registers, and religious rules in distinct ways, and the chapter keeps their experiences visible. "The Japanese family" was never one thing: households differed by class, region, occupation, and inheritance custom, and the ideal of the multigenerational patriarchal household was often further from daily practice than official rhetoric suggested. Finally, many of the developments here have long afterlives in the debates of the twentieth and twenty-first centuries, which is why historians argue over whether the Meiji household system preserved tradition, invented it, or did both.

The evidence for this chapter is unusually varied. It includes statutes and ministry orders, school regulations, household registers and statistics, the speeches and memoirs of women such as Kishida Toshiko and Fukuda Hideko, the magazine Seitō, the scriptures and records of religious movements, missionary reports, police and religious-bureau documents, and investigative writing about urban poverty. Each of these sources reflects the perspective of its author or institution. Official sources describe what the state wished to regulate, religious texts describe what believers taught, and memoirs often tell the stories of exceptional individuals. Historians must read them together and ask whose voices are missing, especially those of rural women, children, and the poor.

By the end of the chapter, students should understand modern Japanese society neither as a story of Westernizing progress nor as the endurance of an unchanging tradition, but as the contested product of law, schooling, religion, and ordinary decisions. Chapter 65 turns to the mass media and entertainment that carried many of these questions to a wider public, and Chapters 67 to 72 follow women's organizing, religious experimentation, and urban life into the Taishō period.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain the diversity of household arrangements inherited from Tokugawa Japan and distinguish formal family ideals from actual practice.
  2. Analyze how the Civil Code of 1898 defined the household (ie), the authority of the household head, succession, marriage, and divorce, and evaluate its consequences for women.
  3. Trace the development of the ideology of ryōsai kenbo ("good wife, wise mother") and assess how it related to schooling, class, and national strengthening.
  4. Distinguish women's participation in reform movements and public life from their formal legal and political rights, including the restrictions of 1890 and 1900.
  5. Describe the expansion of elementary education, the extension of compulsory schooling, and the creation of a separate girls' secondary and higher-education structure.
  6. Compare educational opportunities across gender, class, and region, including those of rural families, Ainu communities, Okinawans, and Burakumin.
  7. Explain how urban growth, migration, housing, and public-health conditions reshaped family and neighborhood life.
  8. Interpret the origins, teachings, and institutional histories of Tenrikyō and Konkōkyō, distinguishing founders' pre-1868 experiences from their Meiji institutional development.
  9. Evaluate the relationship between new religions, Sect Shinto classification, and the limits of religious freedom under the Meiji state.
  10. Assess the roles of Christian and Buddhist institutions in education, women's reform, philanthropy, and the beginnings of organized social welfare.
  11. Contextualize the founding of Seitō and the "New Woman" within longer debates about autonomy, marriage, and motherhood.
  12. Analyze primary sources, including laws, speeches, and religious and reform texts, with attention to authorship, purpose, and limitation.

Historical Context

Tokugawa society, described in Chapters 43 and 44, was organized by status groups and by households. Samurai, farmers, artisans, and merchants lived under different obligations, and each domain governed its own territory under shogunal supervision. Within each status group the household was the unit that held rank, land, or business, and it was recorded in registers kept for taxation and, from the seventeenth century, for religious inspection. Practices of inheritance, marriage, and work differed considerably, and so did the legal position of women. A samurai wife managed a household within strict rank obligations, a farm wife worked in the fields and household economy, and a merchant's wife might manage accounts and shop work. Education took place in domain schools, temple schools (terakoya), private academies, and the household itself, with wide variation in literacy by region, status, and sex.

The Meiji government, described in Chapters 53 through 59, dismantled the status system and the domains, created a national household registration system in 1871, established a national system of schooling in 1872 (Chapter 55), and set about drafting codes of law modeled partly on French and German examples. It also reorganized religion by separating Shinto and Buddhist institutions, ending the prohibition of Christianity in 1873, and constructing the framework later called State Shinto. The Freedom and People's Rights Movement (Chapter 57) opened a public debate about representation in which a few women took part. The Meiji Constitution of 1889 (Chapter 59) guaranteed religious freedom "within limits," and it left questions of family and women's status to ordinary law.

Industrialization (Chapters 56 and 63) drew young people, especially women, into factories and cities, while most of the population still lived in farming households. By the 1890s and 1900s, the wars with China and Russia (Chapters 60 and 61) had raised the political importance of the nation's population as soldiers, workers, and mothers. This is the setting in which officials, educators, reformers, and religious leaders debated how families should be organized, how girls and boys should be educated, and what moral foundation a modern society required. Chapter 55 explained the creation of the new school system and religious restructuring; this chapter is concerned with how those institutions shaped everyday social relationships.

Section 64.1

Families and Households during the Meiji Transformation

The households of early Meiji Japan were not uniform. Farming families in the Tōhoku region, the Kantō plain, the Inland Sea coast, and southern Kyushu differed in size, in the way they passed on land, and in the roles of women. In some regions the eldest son inherited and remained with his parents; in others, a younger child, or the eldest daughter, might inherit, and a husband might be adopted into the wife's family. Merchant houses in Osaka and Kyoto commonly relied on adoption to preserve a business, choosing a capable son-in-law or a clerk over an incompetent natural heir. Samurai families had been bound to a stipend and a rank; with the abolition of stipends in the 1870s (Chapter 54), many had to find new livelihoods in teaching, government service, farming, or small business. Artisan households were centered on a workshop where family members and apprentices worked together.

Behind these differences lay a shared concept: the ie, the household as a continuing enterprise with a name, property, and an ancestral line, in which individuals were members rather than autonomous owners. In practice, the ie was a flexible institution. Households adopted children to ensure continuity, accepted adopted sons-in-law (mukoyōshi), allowed divorce and remarriage, and adjusted to the economic needs of the moment. Formal ideals, as expressed in domain regulations or Confucian-influenced moral texts, described obedient wives and filial sons. Actual practice, as visible in village records, diaries, and petitions, shows wives managing money and farm work, widows heading households, and families renegotiating marriages. Historians therefore distinguish carefully between what moralists prescribed and what families did.

The Meiji state's first major intervention in household life was the national household registration of 1871, implemented in 1872. This ended the old system of religious inspection registers and produced a standardized register for every household, organized by residence (koseki). The register recorded the household head and members, their relationships, and their status. Its purpose was to know the population for conscription, taxation, and education, but it also gave the household a new legal and bureaucratic definition. Over the following decades, officials and jurists would build family law on this foundation.

Section 64.2

The Civil Code of 1898 and the Modern Ie System

Japan's first modern Civil Code had a troubled history. The government invited the French jurist Gustave Boissonade to draft a code, which was promulgated in 1890 but never took effect after a bitter controversy, sometimes called the Civil Code dispute, in which critics argued that its provisions would damage Japanese customs of loyalty and filial duty. A revised code, drafted by a committee including Hozumi Nobushige, Ume Kenjirō, and Tomii Masaakira and drawing on German as well as French models, was enacted in stages: the first three books on property in 1896, and the books on family and succession in 1898. It took effect in July 1898.

The family provisions defined each household as headed by a koshu, a household head. Family members were required to obtain the head's consent for marriage or adoption, and the head had authority to determine where members lived. A woman entering marriage normally entered her husband's household and register, and a man adopted as a son-in-law entered his wife's. Succession to headship, in the ordinary case, passed to a single heir, normally the eldest son, who inherited the household property and the responsibility for ancestral rites. The code recognized adoption widely, so that households could secure heirs, and it allowed households to branch off by establishing a new ie.

It is tempting to describe these provisions as a codification of ancient custom. They were not. Tokugawa practice, as we have seen, varied enormously, and the samurai pattern of single-heir inheritance was only one model, not universal among farmers or merchants. The code selected, generalized, and enforced a particular version of household authority, mostly drawn from samurai ideals, and applied it to the whole population through the register and the courts. At the same time, it was genuine modernization in the sense that it created a uniform legal system, secured property rights, and replaced domain-by-domain custom with national law. Both statements are true, and the next sections examine how they worked out in practice.

Historical Vocabulary
Ie — the household

The ie (家) was a household understood as an enduring unit with a name, property, occupation or rank, and an ancestral line, to which individuals belonged as members. Related terms include koshu, the legal head of an ie; koseki, the household register that recorded its members; mukoyōshi, a man adopted to marry into his wife's household; and iesōzoku, succession to headship.

The ie is often translated as "family," but this obscures its legal and economic character. An ie could be continued by adoption, so that biological descent was not required, and a person could belong to an ie with no blood relationship to its head. Students should also avoid the assumption that every Japanese household before 1898 was an ie in the legal sense that the Civil Code later defined. The code gave a legal definition to an institution whose practice had varied.

Section 64.3

Marriage, Divorce, Inheritance, and Women's Legal Position

Most marriages in this period were arranged by families, often through an intermediary (nakōdo), and were seen as alliances between households as much as unions between individuals. Under the Civil Code, a man under thirty and a woman under twenty-five needed the consent of the household head and of parents to marry. The minimum marriage ages were seventeen for men and fifteen for women. Marriage was effected by registration, not by ceremony, and many couples in rural areas lived together before registration, sometimes until a first child was born, which reveals a gap between ritual practice and legal form.

The code's treatment of married women was restrictive. A wife was legally subject to limitations on her capacity: certain legal acts, such as borrowing money, entering contracts, or disposing of property, required her husband's permission. The husband administered the wife's property in marriage, although property she owned before marriage remained hers. Grounds for divorce reflected an unequal standard: a husband could divorce a wife for adultery, while a wife could divorce a husband for adultery only if he had been convicted of it. Women's legal inability to be a household head was not absolute, since a woman could succeed to headship in some circumstances, but the code placed male heirs ahead of female ones.

Divorce itself was common, especially by mutual agreement, and rates in the early Meiji decades were high by later standards before declining as the code and the ideal of the stable household took hold. The written law, however, tells only part of the story. Customary arrangements affected inheritance, dowries, the return of wives to natal homes, and the terms of separation, and local courts and village mediators often settled disputes before they reached a judge. Historians who study divorce petitions and family registers find wives negotiating, resisting, and sometimes initiating separation, even within a legal framework that favored husbands. The code also recognized children born outside marriage under certain conditions, and the question of how to register such children revealed the continuing practice of concubinage, discussed in Section 64.9.

Primary Source Window

The Family Provisions of the Civil Code (1898)

Creator: Government codification commission and the Imperial Diet · Date: Enacted 1896–1898; family and succession books in force July 1898 · Type: Statute (translated and paraphrased)

Context: The Civil Code was the product of more than two decades of drafting and debate. Its family books were written by legal scholars who were concerned that the earlier French-inspired draft would undermine household solidarity. The text below is a paraphrase of selected provisions as described in standard legal histories; students should consult an official or scholarly translation for exact wording and article numbers.

Paraphrase (not a verbatim quotation): A member of a household must obtain the consent of the household head in order to marry or to be adopted. The household head may designate the residence of members. A wife enters the household of her husband at marriage, and she may not perform certain legal acts, such as borrowing or disposing of property, without her husband's permission. A husband may seek divorce on grounds of his wife's adultery, but a wife may seek divorce on grounds of her husband's adultery only where he has been convicted of it. In succession, the household's headship and property pass in principle to a single heir.

Analysis: The text shows a legal system that recognized individuals as holders of rights in some respects, such as property, and as subordinate members of a household in others. Its author, a male legal elite, assumed that family order was a matter of public concern. What the source cannot show is how often these rules were enforced in a village, how families evaded or reinterpreted them, or how women themselves understood them. Household registers, court records, and women's writings provide that evidence.

  3. What can historians learn from it about expectations of wives and husbands?

Section 64.4

Household Economies, Children, and Intergenerational Obligations

For most households the economy was not an office or a factory but a farm, a shop, or a workshop in which everyone contributed. Farming families divided tasks by age and sex: men did heavy plowing and irrigation work, women transplanted rice, weeded, raised silkworms, spun, and wove, and children carried water, tended younger siblings, and gathered fuel. Earnings were pooled in the household, with the head responsible for managing land and the obligation of land taxes, which after the land tax reform of the 1870s were paid in money (Chapter 54). Many households lost land to debt or were forced into tenancy, and dependence on cash earnings pushed some members into wage labor away from home.

Industrial migration therefore altered, but did not abolish, family obligation. Daughters sent to textile mills (Chapter 63) often remitted wages to parents, and sons worked in mines, ports, and workshops. Families made strategic decisions about who should stay and who should go, and these were shaped by the need to care for aging parents. There was no national pension system, and the household was expected to support elders. The Civil Code reinforced this by imposing duties of support among relatives. Elderly parents might live with the heir, but life expectancy was short by modern standards, and many households had only one or two generations at any time. Historians who study registers find that the multigenerational household, though idealized, was a stage in the household cycle rather than a permanent condition.

Children were both dependents and contributors. They worked on farms and in shops, were sent into apprenticeship or domestic service at young ages, and, once schooling became compulsory, had to balance school hours with chores. Family decisions about education reflected costs, the need for children's labor, and expectations: a family might keep a son at school while a daughter stayed home, or vice versa. Child mortality remained high, and the practices of infanticide and abandonment, documented in earlier periods, were increasingly condemned and were criminalized by the state, though poverty continued to threaten children's survival. These constraints must be remembered when reading official statistics on school attendance in later sections.

Section 64.5

Ryōsai Kenbo: Good Wife, Wise Mother, and the Ideology of the Modern Family

The phrase ryōsai kenbo, "good wife, wise mother," expressed an ideal of womanhood that became closely associated with Meiji and later schooling. Earlier Meiji thinkers had already argued that mothers shaped children's character and that the nation's strength depended on educated women; the Western-learning scholar Nakamura Masanao wrote in the 1870s about the importance of "good mothers." The phrase itself spread in the 1890s and was adopted in official discourse around the time of the Girls' High School Order of 1899. The ideal presented women's primary contributions as the management of the household, the care and moral training of children, and support for husbands and in-laws. It was not identical to older Confucian injunctions of obedience, since it assumed that women should be educated and informed, but it located women's public significance in the domestic sphere.

Scholars have observed that ryōsai kenbo was a flexible ideology. For the state it linked women's education to national strengthening, a rationale that became more pressing after the Sino-Japanese and Russo-Japanese wars. For educators, including Christian reformers and founders of private schools, it offered a way to justify girls' education to skeptical parents and officials. For women themselves, it could be a constraint, because it confined them to domestic roles, or a resource, because it gave them access to school and a claim to respect. It also described an urban, middle-class ideal: a household with a servant, a husband earning a salary, and a wife with time to supervise children. Rural wives worked in the fields, and working-class women worked for wages, so the ideal did not describe the lives of most women.

The ideology also shaped debates about marriage and consumption. Magazines for women, which expanded in the 1890s and 1900s, offered advice on housekeeping, thrift, child care, and etiquette. The government, particularly after the Russo-Japanese War, promoted thrift and savings campaigns directed at households and the Local Improvement Movement (Section 64.19). At the same time, critics such as the women writing in Seitō would challenge the assumption that women's lives should be defined by marriage and motherhood. The next sections show that the ideal and its critics both developed in relation to changing social realities.

Historical Debate

Did Modern Family Law Preserve Tradition, Reconstruct It, or Do Both?

The question: Was the Meiji household system a continuation of an older "family system," an invention of the modern state, or a combination of the two? The answer affects how historians interpret the legal and social position of women and the origins of later debates about the "traditional Japanese family."

Interpretation A — Survival of feudal or traditional patterns: Influential postwar legal scholars, notably Kawashima Takeyoshi, argued that the household system reflected the persistence of pre-modern, hierarchical social relationships within a modernizing state, and that its authority structures served state purposes. In this view, the Civil Code preserved and legalized a patriarchal tradition that postwar reform later had to dismantle.
Interpretation B — A modern construction: Other historians and sociologists, including Ueno Chizuko, argue that the legal ie, with its uniform household head, registered membership, and single-heir succession, was a modern institution created by the state by selecting and generalizing a samurai model. They stress that Tokugawa practice was diverse and that many farm and merchant families did not follow the pattern the code imposed.

Evidence: The debate turns on how historians read Tokugawa household records, inheritance practices, and village customs, on drafting debates among the framers of the code, and on the changing practice of registration and divorce after 1898. Most recent scholarship emphasizes that both continuity and invention are visible: the code drew on existing ideals and practices, but it made them uniform and enforceable in ways that were new.

Why it matters: If the household system is a survival, reform appears as the removal of an old structure. If it is an invention, then claims that it represents "Japanese tradition" are themselves historical products, which affects how later conservative defenses of the family system should be understood.

Division II · Women, Gender Roles, and the Expansion of Public Life
Section 64.6

Women's Changing Positions in Meiji Society

The phrase "Meiji women" covers very different lives. An upper-class woman in Tokyo might attend a private or mission school, learn English, and marry a diplomat or official; a farm wife in a Nagano village worked in rice fields and sericulture; a widowed shopkeeper in Osaka managed a business; a daughter from a poor family might spend years in a textile mill or as a domestic servant. Marital status also mattered: a married woman worked within her husband's household, an unmarried woman was expected to obey her father, and a widow could sometimes act with greater independence, particularly if she was head of her household. Historians therefore distinguish among elite, middle-class, rural, urban, married, unmarried, and widowed women rather than treating women as a single group.

Several developments widened the range of possibilities. Literacy among girls grew as schooling expanded, and with it came access to newspapers, magazines, and books. Migration to cities and factories removed some women from direct parental supervision, and new institutions such as schools, hospitals, and offices created paid positions. Christian missions provided education and leadership roles. At the same time, the legal and ideological structures described above constrained women's choices, and many women's opportunities depended on the support or tolerance of fathers, husbands, and employers.

One consequence was an expanding but uneven public presence of women in education, reform societies, and the press, matched by continuing exclusion from the vote, from political parties, and from the imperial universities. By the early twentieth century, a woman might be a teacher or a nurse and also be legally unable to join a political association. This combination of expanded participation and persistent legal limitation is the pattern to watch as the chapter proceeds.

Section 64.7

Women and the Freedom and People's Rights Movement

Chapter 57 described the movement for representative government in the late 1870s and 1880s. A small number of women took part in it, and their participation illustrates both the movement's openness and its limits. Kishida Toshiko, born in Kyoto in 1863, had served at court before leaving to speak at movement meetings. In the early 1880s she delivered public lectures arguing that women deserved education and a role in society, and her speeches drew large audiences and attracted police attention. At Ōtsu on 12 October 1883, she delivered her address usually translated as “Daughters in Boxes” (Hakoiri musume), criticizing the restrictions imposed on girls; she was arrested and prosecuted for giving political content to an ostensibly academic meeting. She later married Nakajima Nobuyuki, a movement leader, and wrote under the name Shōen. Fukuda Hideko, from Okayama, opened a school for girls in her home region and became involved in movement politics. In 1885 she was arrested in connection with the Osaka Incident, a plot by movement members to support reform in Korea, and she spent time in prison. She later wrote a memoir and became involved in socialist and women's causes.

These women were exceptional, and the movement as a whole was dominated by men. Yet they were not alone. Local women signed petitions, attended meetings, and supported movement newspapers, and recent scholarship has emphasized that women's participation in public speaking and political discussion in this period was wider than the few famous names suggest. The movement's arguments about natural rights and equality could be turned toward women's status, and some speakers, including Kishida, did so.

The state responded by narrowing the legal space for women's participation. The Law on Assembly and Political Association of 1890 barred women, along with minors and certain others, from joining political associations and from attending political meetings. The Public Peace Police Law of 1900 retained these exclusions in its Article 5. The prohibition on women's attendance at political meetings was partially lifted only in 1922, a development treated in Chapter 69, and women did not obtain the national vote until 1945 (Chapter 84). Women's public activism after 1890 therefore took place through other channels: schools, religious organizations, charitable societies, and the press. The distinction between participation in reform movements and formal political rights is crucial to understanding the rest of this chapter.

Section 64.8

Women's Work Beyond the Factory: Agriculture, Domestic Service, Commerce, and Professions

Women's work in Meiji Japan was far wider than the textile mills examined in Chapter 63. Agriculture employed the largest number: women worked in rice cultivation, in vegetable and tea farming, in sericulture, and in processing. Farm wives often carried the double burden of field work and household labor, and the growth of commercial sericulture in regions such as Nagano and Gunma increased the importance of women's labor in raising silkworms. In fishing communities, women processed catches and sold fish, and in coastal areas of Kyushu and elsewhere, women divers collected seaweed and shellfish.

Domestic service employed large numbers of young women in urban households and shops, often as live-in workers with long hours and little autonomy. Commerce was also a major field: women ran small shops, sold goods in markets, and managed family businesses, particularly in cities. Meanwhile, new professions opened for educated women. Teaching was the largest, as the next division explains. Nursing developed after the Japan Red Cross Society began training nurses in the 1890s and as hospitals expanded. Midwifery was regulated by national rules in 1899 that required licensing, which professionalized the work and displaced some traditional birth attendants. A small number of women entered medicine: Ogino Ginko became the first woman licensed to practice Western medicine in Japan in 1885, and Yoshioka Yayoi founded the Tokyo Women's Medical School in 1900 to train women physicians. Telephone operators and clerks began to appear in the 1890s and 1900s, though in smaller numbers than later.

These openings were real but limited. Women professionals were paid less than men, were often expected to leave when they married, and faced exclusion from the imperial universities and from many occupations. Their work nevertheless demonstrated that educated women could contribute outside the household, and it supplied evidence for the arguments of reformers that women's education was socially useful. The relationship between work and ideology was complicated: the same state that praised ryōsai kenbo also relied on women's labor in farms, shops, and offices.

Section 64.9

Prostitution, Concubinage, Sexual Regulation, and Campaigns for Reform

Licensed prostitution was a significant institution in Meiji Japan, and it illustrates the connections among poverty, family obligation, and state regulation. The Tokugawa pleasure districts described in Chapter 46 continued in modified form. In 1872, after the Maria Luz incident, in which a Peruvian ship carrying Chinese laborers was detained at Yokohama and the Peruvian side raised the question of Japan's own practices, the government issued an edict declaring that prostitutes and entertainers were to be freed from bondage. In practice, the licensed quarters continued under a system in which women were recorded as renting rooms and operating independently, while remaining tied to brothel keepers by debts. Women entered the trade through family sale, loans, or deception, and poverty in rural households contributed to recruitment. Licensing was accompanied by regulation of health examinations aimed at venereal disease, which burdened the women but not their clients.

Women also left Japan as part of this economy. Poor women from regions such as Amakusa and Shimabara in Kyushu were taken or sent to Southeast Asia, China, and elsewhere, where they worked in brothels; they are commonly called karayuki-san. Their circumstances are documented unevenly, and historians debate how much coercion and how much economic strategy shaped individual cases, while agreeing that exploitation and deception were widespread. Concubinage, meanwhile, was practiced by some wealthy and official men; the 1870 criminal code had recognized concubines as a category of relatives, but later codes removed this recognition, and the Civil Code of 1898 did not provide for it. Children born outside marriage could be acknowledged by fathers and registered, which exposed the practice to public discussion.

Opposition grew in the 1880s and 1890s. Christian women, led by Yajima Kajiko and the Japan Woman's Christian Temperance Union (Section 64.28), campaigned for monogamy and against licensed prostitution, and the Salvation Army, which began work in Japan in 1895, organized campaigns to help women leave brothels; in 1900 it was prominent in the movement that pressed for women's freedom to leave. Some prefectures moved to abolish licensing: Gunma did so in 1893, the first. In 1900 a Home Ministry regulation stated that women could voluntarily leave the trade. The state and the reformers disagreed on methods: officials sought to regulate and contain the trade and to protect public health, while abolitionists argued that regulation legitimized exploitation. Licensed prostitution itself persisted until after the Second World War (Chapter 86), and the conflict between regulation and abolition is part of the background of later debates.

Section 64.10

Women Writers and the New Woman: Yosano Akiko, Hiratsuka Raichō, and Seitō

Literature gave women a way to express views that politics barred. In the 1890s, Higuchi Ichiyō wrote short stories about the lives of poor women and was recognized as a major writer before her death in 1896. In 1901 Yosano Akiko published Midaregami ("Tangled Hair"), a collection of poems that celebrated romantic and bodily experience in language that scandalized some critics and thrilled others. In 1904, during the Russo-Japanese War, she published a poem addressed to her brother that expressed opposition to the war, a controversial act discussed with the war itself in Chapter 61. Yosano, who married the poet Yosano Tekkan and raised many children, remained prominent as a poet, essayist, and commentator on women's lives.

In September 1911, Hiratsuka Haru, known by her pen name Raichō, with a group of young women writers, founded the literary magazine Seitō ("Bluestocking"). Its first issue opened with an essay by Hiratsuka asserting that women had once been like the sun and calling for the recovery of women's inner creative power, and it included a poem by Yosano Akiko. The magazine published poetry, fiction, essays, and translations, and it gradually turned to questions of marriage, motherhood, chastity, abortion, and the autonomy of women. The group's members were mostly educated, urban women, many of them graduates of Japan Women's University, and their circle was small. Newspapers, however, took notice of them, sometimes sensationally, labeling them "New Women" (atarashii onna) and mocking their behavior, especially after an earlier 1908 scandal involving Hiratsuka and a writer, Morita Sōhei.

Seitō faced pressure: some issues were banned by the authorities, and its members were criticized by conservative moralists and by family and neighbors. Leadership passed to Itō Noe in 1915, and the magazine ceased in 1916. Its significance lies less in its circulation, which was modest, than in its creation of a public space in which women discussed their own lives and challenged the ideal of ryōsai kenbo. The debates it started, including the "debate on motherhood protection" of 1918 between Yosano and Hiratsuka (Chapter 67), connect directly to the organizing of the Taishō years.

Division III · The Expansion of Schooling and Educational Opportunity
Section 64.11

Elementary Education, School Attendance, and the Growth of Literacy

The school system announced in 1872 (Chapter 55) was ambitious on paper and difficult in practice. Villages were expected to build schools and, for many years, to pay for them through local taxes and tuition fees. Parents often resisted: schools took children away from farm and household work, tuition was a burden, and the curriculum seemed remote from rural life. In the 1870s there were disturbances in some places in which school buildings were attacked, and officials repeatedly adjusted regulations. The Education Order of 1879 and the School Orders of 1886 established compulsory attendance for a basic period, commonly four years, but compliance was uneven. Official statistics, which should be treated as approximate, show that around 1890 well under half of school-age children were counted as attending, that attendance rose to around four-fifths by 1900, and that by the early 1910s it exceeded ninety-five percent.

Two reforms were decisive. The Elementary School Order revised in 1900 standardized a four-year compulsory course and, in general, abolished tuition in ordinary elementary schools, removing a major financial barrier, although families still bore costs for materials, clothing, and lost labor. Then a law of 1907 extended compulsory elementary schooling from four to six years, taking effect in April 1908. The extension reflected state concern about national strength after the war with Russia, and it was costly for local governments and for families who now had to forgo their children's labor for longer. Attendance among girls lagged behind boys' until the early twentieth century and then converged; the gap was largest in rural and poorer areas.

Literacy rose accordingly, though measures vary. Even before the Meiji period many Tokugawa commoners could read at a basic level, so the new schools expanded and standardized an existing pattern rather than creating literacy from nothing. What changed was uniformity: a national curriculum in reading, writing, arithmetic, ethics, and later history and geography, taught in standard written Japanese, produced a shared body of knowledge and a common language of instruction. It also meant that regional dialects, and for Ainu and Okinawan children, entire languages, were treated as obstacles, a subject taken up in Section 64.16.

Section 64.12

Schools, Morality, National Identity, and the Imperial Rescript on Education

The Imperial Rescript on Education, issued in October 1890, was a short statement attributed to the emperor and drafted by officials including Inoue Kowashi and the Confucian scholar Motoda Nagazane. It presented the loyalty and filial piety of subjects as the foundation of the national polity and linked them to ethical virtues such as harmony within families, fidelity among friends, and service to the state. Copies were distributed to schools, together with portraits of the emperor, and schools developed rituals around them: on national holidays students assembled to bow before the portrait and hear the rescript read. Ministry of Education regulations of 1891 incorporated moral instruction (shūshin) into the curriculum on the basis of the rescript.

The rescript shaped how many Japanese remembered their schooling, but its reception was not uniform. Some officials and educators embraced it enthusiastically. Others, including Christians, objected to ceremonies they regarded as religious. In 1891, the Christian teacher Uchimura Kanzō hesitated to bow before the rescript's signature at the First Higher Middle School and was condemned in the press, an episode known as the Uchimura Incident. In 1893 the philosopher Inoue Tetsujirō published an argument that Christianity conflicted with the national polity, which generated a debate about education and religion. In local communities, practice varied with the commitment of the principal and the attitude of teachers and parents; in poor villages ceremonies might be minimal, while in others they were elaborate.

The state also tightened control over textbooks. A bribery scandal in 1902, in which publishers and officials were found to have been involved in textbook selection, led to the adoption of state-compiled textbooks beginning in 1903–1904. These books gave the government direct control over the content of moral instruction, history, and reading in elementary schools. The result was a more centralized and uniform educational ideology, but the lived experience of schooling, mediated by teachers, local customs, and the circumstances of individual children, could diverge from official intention. The gap between official ideals and local practice is a recurring theme of this chapter.

Section 64.13

Girls' Secondary Education and the Girls' High School Order of 1899

Secondary education was organized separately for boys and girls. For boys, middle schools prepared students for higher schools and universities, and for careers in government, business, and the professions. For girls, no equivalent route to the universities existed. Before 1899, girls' secondary schooling was provided by a mixture of government higher schools for girls, private institutions, and mission schools, and its curriculum and status varied. The Girls' High School Order, promulgated on 8 February 1899 and implemented on 1 April, gave this level a legal framework. It stated that the purpose of the girls' high school was to provide the higher general education required for girls, and it required prefectures to establish at least one such school. The number of girls' high schools grew quickly in the following decade, but they enrolled a small fraction of girls, mainly from households that could afford to forgo their daughters' labor and pay for their schooling.

The curriculum combined academic subjects with those regarded as appropriate for women: ethics, Japanese, a foreign language, history, geography, mathematics, science, music, drawing, and gymnastics were taught alongside housekeeping and sewing. Compared with boys' middle schools, the mathematics and science content was reduced and the program was shorter, commonly four years instead of five. Education officials explained these differences by reference to the ideal of ryōsai kenbo, which defined women's future contributions as domestic and maternal. At the same time, the girls' high schools gave their students an education beyond the elementary level, a social experience among peers, and a path to teaching and, for some, to further study.

Parents and students used these schools in different ways. For some families, a girls' high school diploma was a mark of respectability and improved the daughter's marriage prospects. For others, it was a route into teacher training. Some students resented the narrow curriculum, and some educators, particularly at private schools, argued for more rigorous academic training. Girls' high schools thus embodied the tension between expanded opportunity and gendered purpose that runs through the chapter.

Section 64.14

Normal Schools, Women Teachers, and the Professionalization of Education

Teachers were needed in vast numbers once elementary schooling expanded. The government established the Tokyo Normal School in 1872 and a women's normal school in Tokyo in 1875, later known as the Tokyo Women's Higher Normal School and the predecessor of Ochanomizu University; prefectural normal schools followed. The Normal School Order of 1886 under Education Minister Mori Arinori imposed a strict disciplinary regime, emphasizing obedience, military-style drill, and loyalty, to produce teachers who would transmit state-approved values. Higher normal schools for training secondary-school teachers were established, and a higher normal school for women opened in Tokyo in 1890 and another in Nara in 1908.

Teaching became a major occupation for women, particularly in lower elementary grades. The proportion of women among elementary teachers rose over the period from a small minority to a larger share, though it remained lower than that of men, and women were paid less and held fewer senior posts. Teaching offered income and public standing to women who might otherwise have had few options, and it could be a path out of poverty for families of modest means. The expectation that women would leave on marriage, or on becoming mothers, limited careers and kept turnover high.

Teachers also occupied an ambiguous position. They were agents of the state, charged with conducting ceremonies and teaching official ethics, but they were also members of local communities, often drawn from the families of former samurai or village officials. In the countryside, the teacher was sometimes one of the few people with formal education, and he or she might be asked to write letters, give advice, or lead local associations. Tensions arose over pay, which depended on local finances, and over discipline and supervision by inspectors. The relationship between school and community, which began with suspicion in the 1870s, matured by the 1900s into an established, though still negotiated, partnership.

Section 64.15

Women's Higher Education: Tsuda Umeko, Naruse Jinzō, and New Educational Institutions

The imperial universities did not admit women until 1913, when Tōhoku Imperial University admitted its first women students, and even then it was a limited exception. Women who wanted higher education therefore turned to private and mission institutions. Tsuda Umeko was a pioneer. Sent abroad at about six, she lived in the United States for eleven years, returned to Japan in 1882, taught, and made a second period of study in the United States. In 1900 she established the Women's Institute of English Studies (Joshi Eigaku Juku) in Tokyo, which trained women as teachers of English and was notable for its small classes and emphasis on independent thought. The school's later development into Tsuda University is a matter of institutional history, but its founding in 1900 marks an early women-led effort in higher education.

Japan Women's University (Nihon Joshi Daigakkō), opened in 1901 in Tokyo, was founded under the leadership of Naruse Jinzō, an educator influenced by Christian ideas, who had argued in his book on women's education (1896) that women needed higher education to fulfill their roles as citizens, wives, and mothers. Supporters included business figures and officials, and the university's first students came from across the country. Its curriculum included home economics, Japanese literature, and education, and it aimed to combine intellectual and moral training. Like Tsuda's school, it operated outside the imperial university system and offered qualifications that were not equal to a university degree in legal status.

Other institutions followed, including Tokyo Women's Medical School (1900), which prepared women to take physician examinations, and a growing number of private and mission-founded colleges. The ambitions of these schools were considerable, and so were their limitations: they served a small number of women, mostly from affluent families, and they often justified themselves in terms of domestic and social service, because the argument for women's higher education was easier to make in ryōsai kenbo terms. Yet their graduates became teachers, writers, reformers, and activists, and several of the contributors to Seitō were students or graduates of Japan Women's University.

Section 64.16

Unequal Educational Access: Rural Poverty, Hokkaido, Okinawa, and Marginalized Communities

The national school system was not experienced identically. Household income mattered most: even after tuition was abolished in 1900, families had to provide clothing, supplies, and the labor that children would otherwise have contributed. Tenant-farming households, fishing families, and the urban poor were more likely to keep children, especially daughters, out of school or to withdraw them early. Remote mountain and island villages had fewer schools and fewer qualified teachers, and travel distances and winter conditions affected attendance. Attendance rates therefore varied across prefectures and between town and countryside, and statistics aggregated nationally conceal these differences.

For Ainu communities in Hokkaido, schooling was bound up with colonization and assimilation. After the Meiji government formalized Hokkaido's development (Chapter 54), settlers from Honshu arrived in large numbers, and Ainu lost access to hunting and fishing grounds. The Hokkaido Former Aborigines Protection Act of 1899 gave farming land to Ainu households that undertook agriculture and provided for medical assistance and schools, while its use of the term "former aborigines" reflected the official expectation that Ainu would assimilate. In 1901 the Hokkaido authorities issued rules for the education of Ainu children, which in many places led to separate Ainu schools with a short course and a curriculum emphasizing Japanese language and ethics. Missionaries, including the English Anglican John Batchelor, also ran educational and welfare work among Ainu. The effect of these policies was complex: some Ainu valued schooling and used it to gain skills, but it was imposed within a framework that devalued Ainu language and culture and undercut communal land use.

In Okinawa, incorporated as a prefecture in 1879, schooling was used to teach standard Japanese and Japanese loyalty. Attendance in the early decades was far lower than in other prefectures, reflecting poverty, the continuing influence of the old elite, and resistance or indifference, and rose substantially from the 1890s. Educators promoted standard Japanese and discouraged Ryukyuan languages in schools; practices of punishing children for speaking local languages appeared in later decades and are remembered in Okinawan accounts, though their timing and extent varied. Burakumin children, whose communities had been legally freed from outcaste status in 1871 (Chapter 55) but continued to face discrimination, also encountered lower enrollment, hostility in classrooms, and in some places separate arrangements. The same school system that offered opportunity to many therefore marginalized others; the Chapter 94 discussion of minority movements returns to the long consequences.

Division IV · Urbanization, Community Change, and Everyday Life
Section 64.17

Urban Growth, Migration, and Changing Family Arrangements

Cities grew steadily between the 1870s and 1910s. Tokyo, which had been Edo, lost population when daimyō households left in the early Restoration years, then expanded as government offices, schools, factories, and newspapers drew migrants; by the early twentieth century it had roughly two million residents. Osaka, an older commercial center, industrialized rapidly and became a leading textile and manufacturing city. Regional cities such as Nagoya, Kobe, Yokohama, Sapporo, and prefectural capitals grew as ports, administrative seats, or military and industrial centers. Most Japanese still lived in the countryside, but migration created a population that moved back and forth between village and city.

Migrants arrived as students, clerks, shop assistants, factory workers, servants, soldiers, and laborers. Many young men lodged in boardinghouses (geshuku), where landlords provided rooms and meals, and student lodgers formed a large part of urban social life. Servants lived in their employers' houses. Workers in factories often lived in company dormitories (Chapter 63). Urban households were frequently smaller than rural ones and consisted of a couple and children, sometimes with relatives, lodgers, or a servant. Migration did not necessarily sever rural ties: urban workers sent money home, returned for festivals and harvests, and relied on relatives to arrange marriages and provide shelter in hard times.

The household registers and the Civil Code continued to define legal membership by the household of origin, which meant that a person living in Tokyo might legally belong to a household in a distant village. This produced a gap between legal and residential family, and a complex set of obligations across distance. Marriages could be arranged between village and city households, and urban couples sometimes broke from the extended household system in practice, even if not in law. Historians who study this period caution against assuming a simple shift from "extended" to "nuclear" households; the evidence suggests varied, fluid arrangements shaped by class, occupation, and migration history.

Section 64.18

Urban Housing, Public Health, Sanitation, and the Household Environment

Urban growth placed severe pressure on housing and public health. In working-class districts, families rented single rooms in wooden row houses (nagaya) sharing wells, latrines, and drains. Overcrowding, poor ventilation, and unsanitary water contributed to outbreaks of disease. Cholera epidemics struck repeatedly, including major outbreaks in 1879 and 1886 that caused many deaths nationwide, and again in the 1890s. Dysentery, typhoid, smallpox, and tuberculosis were persistent, and plague appeared in 1899. The government responded with quarantine regulations and, in 1897, a law on infectious disease prevention, but enforcement sometimes provoked resistance, particularly where measures such as isolation or the destruction of property disrupted livelihoods.

Modernizing water and waste systems were slow and uneven. Tokyo's modern waterworks, with filtration at Yodobashi, began operating in 1898, and Osaka and other cities followed with their own systems. A Sewerage Law was enacted in 1900. These improvements concentrated in the central and wealthier districts, while poor neighborhoods continued to rely on wells and older systems. Investigative writers documented the contrast: Matsubara Iwagorō's Saiankoku no Tōkyō ("Darkest Tokyo," 1893) and Yokoyama Gennosuke's Nihon no kasō shakai ("Japan's Lower Classes," 1899) described slum households, casual labor, and the extreme poverty of some neighborhoods. Both authors wrote for a middle-class audience and reflected its assumptions, but their work remains an important source on the social geography of the city.

Domestic hygiene became a subject of moral instruction. Schools, women's magazines, and government campaigns taught households to ventilate rooms, boil water, clean, and manage waste, and they linked these practices to the role of the wife and mother. Hygiene thus joined ryōsai kenbo as a domain in which the state and reformers addressed the household. Inequality shaped outcomes, however: a family in a spacious house with a servant and piped water was far better placed to follow the advice than one in a crowded tenement.

Section 64.19

Childhood, Youth, and the Changing Experiences of Growing Up

Childhood in this period was shaped by class, region, and sex. A farm child might attend school in the morning and work in the afternoon, carrying a younger sibling, feeding animals, or helping with seasonal planting and harvest. In cities, working-class children sold goods, carried parcels, minded shops, or entered apprenticeships and service at young ages; apprentices (detchi) lived with masters and were taught a trade in exchange for labor and obedience. Middle-class children, with growing access to secondary schooling, experienced a prolonged period of study and preparation for examinations. Official ideals presented the child as a future subject and soldier or mother, but children's own experiences also included games, songs, festivals, and friendships.

Discipline in schools and households was strict, and corporal punishment was common, although regulations officially discouraged it. School ceremonies and moral instruction aimed to produce loyal subjects. Outside school, youth associations (seinenkai), long present in villages in the form of age-based groups that organized festivals and labor, were increasingly shaped by officials. After the Russo-Japanese War, the Home Ministry's Local Improvement Movement and the Boshin Rescript of 1908, which urged diligence and thrift, encouraged the reorganization of village youth groups under teachers and local officials, and the establishment of reservist associations linked young men to the military. These organizations provided education and sociability and also served state purposes.

Leisure also changed. Urban children played in streets and shrine precincts, read illustrated magazines, and, from the 1900s, encountered new forms of popular culture that Chapter 65 discusses. Rural children participated in seasonal festivals and village events. In both settings, the school year, the school calendar, and school-based events such as sports days (undōkai) created a new rhythm of childhood. The experience of growing up in 1910 differed considerably from that of 1870, but it still differed from place to place and class to class.

Everyday Life and Environment

A Village Schoolchild in the 1900s

The following describes a typical situation reconstructed from scholarship on rural schooling, not a particular individual. A child in a farming village would walk to the local elementary school, perhaps a wooden building that served several hamlets, with a teacher who might be a former samurai's son or a graduate of the prefectural normal school. The day began with lessons in reading, writing, and arithmetic and moral instruction (shūshin), and on national holidays the school held ceremonies before the imperial portrait. After class, children returned to work on the farm; during planting and harvest, absences were common, and teachers had to negotiate with families to keep attendance up.

After 1908, the six-year course meant that children stayed in school into early adolescence, a period when families expected them to take on heavier work. Girls helped with cooking, sewing, and child care, and many left after the compulsory years. Boys who stayed for further study, or whose families could afford it, might go on to a higher elementary course, an agricultural school, or a middle school in a nearby town. Local conditions determined much, and historians use school registers, village records, memoirs, and photographs to reconstruct these patterns.

Section 64.20

Everyday Relationships: Neighborhoods, Marriage, Mutual Aid, and Social Mobility

Urbanization changed how people found support. In villages, mutual aid was organized through household networks, labor-exchange groups, and credit associations (kō and tanomoshi) in which members contributed regularly and drew funds in turn. In cities, neighborhood associations, landlords, shopkeepers, and religious organizations provided comparable functions. Tenement residents depended on neighbors for loans, child care, and help in illness, and landlords and block representatives mediated disputes and passed on information from authorities. Festival associations connected neighbors and reinforced local identities. These networks were not eliminated by migration, but they were reorganized.

Marriage and work changed alongside. Urban couples might choose each other with greater freedom, especially among educated groups, although arranged marriages remained the norm and families kept a strong say. Wage employment made households more dependent on a salary or daily wage, and for the growing salaried middle class, the household was increasingly a unit of consumption rather than production. Social mobility became a theme of public discourse: the ideal of "rising in the world" (risshin shusse) encouraged boys to study for success, while many families found that educational costs strained their resources. Success stories coexisted with the reality that most children of workers and tenants remained in similar occupations.

The history of everyday relationships thus shows both change and persistence. Urban life altered residence patterns, occupations, and consumption, and it brought new institutions. But obligations to kin, local ties, and reliance on informal networks continued, and the city was in many respects an extension of village practices as well as a departure from them. Chapter 70 will examine the later, more commercial urban culture of the 1920s.

Cause and Effect

Urban Migration, Family Structure, Schooling, and Community Life

Long-term causes

Land tax reform and commercialization of agriculture, industrial growth, the end of the status system, and the creation of a national school and household registration framework encouraged movement and reshaped household economies.

Short-term causes

Rural debt and poor harvests, demand for labor in factories and offices after the wars of 1894–1895 and 1904–1905, and new educational and occupational opportunities in cities pushed and pulled people to urban areas.

Immediate effects

Boardinghouses, tenements, and dormitories multiplied; urban households became smaller and more diverse; public-health crises and slums drew official and reformist attention; school enrollment expanded in cities.

Long-term consequences

New urban neighborhoods and associations, a salaried middle class, and organized social welfare emerged, while ties between city and countryside persisted. These conditions underlie the mass society examined in Chapters 67 to 70.

Historical complexity: Migration was not simply a flight from rural poverty. Families made strategic decisions about who should move, and many migrants returned to villages. Urbanization also reflected state policy, military and industrial demand, and educational ambition. No single cause explains the changes in family and community life.

Division V · New Religions: Tenrikyō, Konkōkyō, and Religious Change
Section 64.21

Popular Religion, Healing, and the Emergence of New Religious Communities

Religious life in nineteenth-century Japan was not limited to the formal institutions of Buddhism and shrine Shinto. Villagers and townspeople took part in pilgrimages to famous temples and shrines and to the mountains; they joined devotional associations (kō) that raised funds to send representatives to Ise or to mountain sites; they sought healing from priests, ascetics, and healers; and they responded to teachers who combined moral instruction with ritual. The great mass pilgrimages to Ise, known as okage mairi, and the widespread cults of mountain practice and healing, show a religious landscape that was vigorous, diverse, and often outside official control. Epidemics, famine, and social disruption in the last decades of the Tokugawa period intensified the demand for healing and for hope.

Out of this setting emerged movements founded by individuals who reported direct religious experiences and who gathered followers: Kurozumi Munetada in Okayama, Nakayama Miki in Yamato, and Akazawa Bunji in Bitchū were among them. They are often grouped, with later movements, as "new religions" (shinkō shūkyō). This label, however, is a scholarly category and not evidence that the movements lacked older roots. They drew on popular Shinto, Buddhist, Confucian, and folk practices, on healing traditions and pilgrimage associations, and on the social organization of villages. What was new was often the founding experience, the formation of a community around a founder, and in the Meiji period the movement's relationship to a state that demanded classification.

For the Meiji government, these movements posed administrative questions. The state wished to create a coherent religious framework in which shrine Shinto was a national rite, Buddhism and other religions were private belief, and unauthorized healing and ritual were suspect. Movements that did not fit were subject to police scrutiny, accusations of fraud or superstition, and bureaucratic classification. At the same time, many found that official recognition, as a Shinto sect or a Buddhist affiliate, was valuable, because it gave legal protection. The history of the new religions in this period is largely the history of this negotiation.

Historical Vocabulary
Sect Shinto, New Religion, and Related Terms

Sect Shinto (kyōha Shintō) was an administrative category that developed in the Meiji period for religious organizations with Shinto-related teachings that were not treated as part of the shrine system. By around 1908, thirteen groups were recognized, including Kurozumikyō, Konkōkyō, and Tenrikyō. It should be distinguished from Shrine Shinto, which the state treated as a national, non-religious rite, and from State Shinto, the modern term for the whole framework of state-sponsored shrine practice, ideology, and education. A new religion is a scholarly term for movements founded in the late Tokugawa and later periods; it does not mean they were unrelated to older traditions.

Other key terms include pilgrimage, travel to sacred sites often organized by devotional associations; healing, a practice central to many movements; and religious association, a group of believers organized for devotion, mutual aid, and fundraising. Students should avoid treating all these categories as interchangeable: legal classification, social practice, and belief did not always coincide.

Section 64.22

Nakayama Miki and the Origins of Tenrikyō

Nakayama Miki was born in 1798 in the Yamato region (present-day Nara Prefecture) and married at about thirteen into the Nakayama family, a well-off farming household in Shoyashiki village. She worked in the household, raised children, and was known for piety and kindness. According to the movement's tradition, in the tenth lunar month of 1838, during a ritual intended to heal a family member, she became possessed by a deity she came to know as Tenri-Ō-no-Mikoto, "God of Heavenly Reason," who asked that she become the "Shrine" of God. Her husband and family were at first unwilling, and she is reported to have persisted in her role, which led to a period in which she gave away family property to the poor, an act that impoverished the household. These events are known mainly from the movement's own sources, which are important but represent the perspective of believers.

Over the following decades, Miki gathered followers, especially women, by offering teaching and healing. She was credited with granting protection to women in childbirth, a reason for much of her early popularity. Her teachings, recorded in poetic and prose texts she is believed to have composed or dictated, include the Ofudesaki (a verse scripture, associated with 1869–1882), the Mikagura-uta, and later records of her instructions. They presented a God who is a loving parent of all human beings, taught that people are brothers and sisters, and called for a way of life aimed at the "Joyous Life" (yōki-gurashi), a life of joy to which people are called by cleansing the "dust" of selfish thoughts. The ritual center of the movement is the Kanrodai, a pillar marking the place where humans are believed to have been created.

Miki's followers came from farming families and from poor households in the surrounding region, and women were prominent. Her teaching emphasized equality of persons before God and critiqued the pursuit of wealth. She also had a relationship with her family that was difficult at times, as the family's resources and status were affected by her actions. The community around her developed its own organization, with groups of followers organized around leaders. In the Meiji period she was detained several times by police and officials who accused the movement of unauthorized healing and religious activity, even as followers continued to gather. She died in 1887, aged about eighty-nine, an event followed by a period of reorganization under her successors that Section 64.23 describes.

Section 64.23

Tenrikyō under the Meiji State: Organization, Persecution, and Recognition

The movement's Meiji history is one of expansion and constraint. After the Restoration, the new religious bureaucracy sought to regulate unauthorized religious groups. Officials accused the movement of conducting healing without authority and of encouraging disorderly gatherings, and Miki and her followers were detained on several occasions. Followers sought protection by affiliating with established institutions, including Buddhist temples and Shinto organizations, in order to secure legal cover. Their affiliations changed over time and were sources of tension within the movement, since some members feared that they diluted the movement's distinct teaching.

In 1888, the year after Miki's death, the movement gained organizational recognition when authorities permitted the establishment of the Tenrikyō Church headquarters under the auspices of the Shinto Honkyoku, a central organization for Shinto-related religious groups. This gave it legal status but placed it under another body's supervision. Its leaders had to adapt the movement's practices and teaching to fit the framework, and in the 1890s the movement expanded, spreading beyond Yamato into the Kansai region and elsewhere. In 1896, the Home Ministry issued an instruction that restricted the movement's activities and rituals, reflecting official suspicion that it was heterodox; the restrictions affected how the movement conducted its services and portrayed its teachings.

The state's recognition came in 1908, when Tenrikyō was permitted to become an independent Shinto sect, one of the thirteen recognized Sect Shinto bodies. The movement's path to recognition involved compromise, including accommodation of the state's expectations about loyalty and the emperor, and changes to its organization and doctrinal presentation. Historians have debated whether these changes represented a loss of independence or a pragmatic strategy that allowed the movement to survive and grow. By the 1910s, it had a substantial following and a large pilgrimage center in Tenri, and it also began to send missionaries beyond Japan. Its history illustrates how religious freedom under the Meiji Constitution was conditional: the state recognized religious organizations that accepted its framework, and it treated others as threats to order.

Section 64.24

Konkō Daijin, Toritsugi, and the Growth of Konkōkyō

Akazawa Bunji was born in 1814 in a farming family in Ōtani village in Bitchū Province (present-day Okayama Prefecture) and was adopted as a youth into the Akazawa family. He was a farmer, devout in local religious practice, who endured illness and the deaths of children. In 1859, according to the movement's tradition, he experienced a religious awakening in which he was called by the deity known as Konjin, later understood as Tenchi Kane no Kami ("Golden Deity of Heaven and Earth"). Konjin had previously been feared in popular belief as a deity of direction taboos who punished those who violated restrictions; Bunji's teaching reinterpreted the deity as a benevolent being who sought to save human beings. He took on the role of mediator between the deity and believers, a practice called toritsugi ("mediation" or "conveying"), in which the mediator listens to the believer's concerns and conveys them to the deity and then conveys the deity's response.

Followers came from farming and merchant households, and they came to Bunji for help with illness, family troubles, and anxiety. The movement spread by networks of believers and by individuals who took up mediation. In the Meiji period, Bunji and his followers faced official pressure, including questions about authorization, and in February 1873 the authorities suspended his religious mediation under the new regulatory system. He died in 1883, and the movement continued under successors. A significant development was its connection, from 1885, with Shinto organizations as a form of protection, followed by the movement's efforts to gain independent recognition. In 1900 Konkōkyō was recognized as an independent religious body, a Sect Shinto organization.

Konkōkyō emphasized the equal worth of people before the deity and the mutual dependence between humans and the deity: the deity needs human beings as much as human beings need the deity. This teaching, along with the practice of toritsugi, allowed the movement to address personal problems directly. The movement later expanded to cities and abroad, and its community organization developed with churches and training for mediators. Like Tenrikyō, its path from local religious experience to recognized organization required adapting to state classification.

Section 64.25

Other New Movements, Religious Authority, and the Boundaries of State Control

Tenrikyō and Konkōkyō were part of a wider pattern. Kurozumikyō, founded on the experiences of Kurozumi Munetada, who in 1814 reported an experience of union with the sun deity Amaterasu, taught devotion to the sun and a practice of healing and moral cultivation. Its adherents, who included samurai and farmers in Okayama, built a network of followers, and the movement was recognized as a Shinto sect in 1876, earlier than Tenrikyō and Konkōkyō. Other groups, including those centered on mountain worship and purification, such as Jikkōkyō and Ontakekyō, also took Sect Shinto status. By 1908, thirteen sects had been recognized, a number that included groups with very different histories and teachings.

Ōmoto began later. Deguchi Nao, born in 1837 to a poor family in Fukuchiyama and widowed, experienced possession in 1892 in Ayabe (in Kyoto Prefecture), in which a deity spoke through her and announced a radical renewal of the world. She wrote prophetic messages, in simple script, that criticized the injustices of the era and predicted upheaval. Her association with Deguchi Onisaburō, who joined the movement around 1898–1899 and organized it, shaped its growth into an organization that took the name Ōmoto in the 1910s. The government's later suppression of the movement in 1921, discussed in Chapter 72, is beyond this chapter's boundaries, but its beginnings show that some new religions articulated social criticism more directly than others.

The government's classification system shaped these movements' fates. The Meiji Constitution of 1889 guaranteed freedom of religious belief within limits compatible with peace and order and with the duties of subjects. In 1900 the Home Ministry's former bureau of shrines and temples was divided into a bureau of shrines (jinja kyoku) and a bureau of religions (shūkyō kyoku), formalizing the distinction between shrine rites, treated as national ceremony, and religion. Sect Shinto, Buddhism, and Christianity were placed under the religions bureau, while shrines were administered separately. In June 1913 oversight of religion was transferred to the Ministry of Education; shrine administration remained with the Home Ministry. Established Buddhist institutions, which had lost much of their property and privileges after the early Meiji separation of Buddhism and Shinto, competed with the new movements for followers. The boundaries of religious freedom were thus set by classification, and movements negotiated them according to their circumstances.

Historical Debate

Were the New Religions Challenges to Authority or Adaptations Within It?

The question: Did the new religions offer ordinary people a way to challenge existing social and political authority, or did they chiefly adapt to the framework imposed by the state and by earlier religious institutions?

Interpretation A — Challenge and popular protest: Some scholars, particularly those influenced by the study of popular thought such as Yasumaru Yoshio, emphasize that founders such as Nakayama Miki and Deguchi Nao articulated moral critiques of wealth, hierarchy, and social injustice and offered the poor and women a source of dignity and a vision of renewal. In this view, state pressure shows that the movements were seen as threats.
Interpretation B — Adaptation and accommodation: Other scholars, including those studying Kurozumikyō such as Helen Hardacre, emphasize that the movements built institutions, sought recognition, and adjusted teachings to accommodate imperial ideology and Sect Shinto classification. In this view, the movements created spaces for belief and community but largely worked within the state's framework.

Evidence: Scriptures and founders' sayings, police and religious-bureau records, later institutional histories, and the organizational choices that leaders made in negotiating recognition are the main sources. Different movements, and different periods within a single movement's history, support different conclusions.

Why it matters: The question shapes how historians interpret popular religion's role in modern Japan, whether as a form of resistance, a form of consolation, or both. Many scholars now suggest that these categories overlap rather than oppose each other.

Division VI · Christianity, Religious Education, and Social Reform
Section 64.26

Christianity after the Lifting of the Ban: Churches, Converts, and New Communities

The Tokugawa prohibition of Christianity (Chapter 47) remained in force in the first years of the Meiji government, which in 1868 and the following years deported hundreds of hidden Christians from the Urakami district of Nagasaki, an action that provoked protest from Western diplomats. In February 1873 the government removed the public notice boards that had proclaimed the prohibition, a step that opened the way to open Christian activity, although it did not proclaim religious freedom and continued to treat Christianity with suspicion. Missionaries from the United States and Europe, who had begun working in the treaty ports in the 1860s, now expanded their efforts. Roman Catholic missions, Protestant societies, and the Russian Orthodox mission of Nikolai Kasatkin, who arrived in 1861 and built the Tokyo cathedral completed in 1891, established congregations.

Christian communities took shape around several groups. Samurai families, who had lost status after 1871, formed an important group of early Protestant converts, attracted by the moral and educational aspects of the religion. The "bands" of students associated with the missionary teachers at Yokohama, Kumamoto, and Sapporo produced leaders including Uemura Masahisa, Ebina Danjō, and Uchimura Kanzō. Niijima Jō, who had studied in the United States, founded Dōshisha in Kyoto in 1875 as a Christian school. Japanese Christians soon established their own churches and sought independence from foreign missions, and by the 1880s indigenous leadership was a major theme. The number of Christians remained small, well under one percent of the population, but their influence on education, journalism, social reform, and the early labor and socialist movements was disproportionate.

Suspicion continued. Critics accused Christians of disloyalty, associating the religion with foreign powers, and the Uchimura Incident and the debate over religion and education of the early 1890s expressed that hostility. The Constitution's guarantee of religious freedom was qualified by duties of subjects, and local officials and communities sometimes obstructed Christian activity. Christians responded in different ways: some stressed their patriotism and service to the nation, others criticized the state, and many tried to combine loyalty and faith. The community's history therefore reflects the same negotiation between independence and accommodation seen among the new religions.

Section 64.27

Christian Education, Mission Schools, and Women's Opportunities

Mission schools were among the first institutions to offer systematic education to girls beyond the elementary level. Ferris Seminary in Yokohama, founded in 1870, and other early schools in Tokyo, Kobe, Nagasaki, and elsewhere taught English, Bible, sciences, music, and domestic skills. Some, such as the Meiji Girls' School (Meiji Jogakkō), established in 1885 in Tokyo with Iwamoto Yoshiharu among its leaders, combined Christian values with a liberal education and attracted writers and teachers. Others emphasized training for evangelism. These schools prepared female teachers, Bible women, and leaders in a period when state girls' schooling was limited, and many graduates later worked in public schools and reform organizations.

Mission schools also provided a space in which women could lead. Japanese Christian women served as principals and teachers and organized societies. Their curricula often had elements, such as English language and literature, that state schools did not offer to girls, and they drew students from affluent and middle-class households. At the same time, mission education was shaped by the missionaries' own assumptions about womanhood, which often emphasized domestic virtue and Christian motherhood, an ideal not dissimilar to ryōsai kenbo.

The state eventually moved to separate religion from schooling. Ministry of Education Instruction 12 of 1899 prohibited religious instruction and ceremonies in schools that were recognized by the government, such as those whose graduates received privileges including entry to higher schools or exemption from military service requirements. Mission schools thus had to choose between maintaining religious instruction and retaining official recognition. Some chose to forgo official status, which reduced their students' advantages and enrollment; others complied or found compromises, such as holding religious activities outside the curriculum. The instruction shows both the pressure of official educational ideology and the persistence of Christian education, which continued to produce graduates who played a role in the movements described in the following sections.

Section 64.28

Yajima Kajiko, the Woman's Christian Temperance Union, and Moral Reform

Yajima Kajiko was born in 1833 in Kumamoto, the daughter of a samurai family. She married, left her husband, and became a teacher and then a Christian, and she later served as head of the Joshi Gakuin, a Christian girls' school in Tokyo. In 1886 she took a leading role in founding the Tokyo Women's Reform Society (Tōkyō Fujin Kyōfūkai), which became in 1893 the Japan branch of the Woman's Christian Temperance Union (WCTU) after a visit from an American organizer. The organization campaigned against alcohol, but its agenda was broader, covering the abolition of licensed prostitution, monogamy and opposition to concubinage, improvement of marriage and family life, and the education of women. The historian Elizabeth Dorn Lublin, in her study of the organization, has shown that its leaders pursued wide social reform and public influence.

The society's methods included petitions to the government and the Diet, lectures, journals, and the establishment of facilities for women seeking to leave brothels or for those in need. In the late 1880s it presented petitions in support of monogamy; in later years it supported the movement to end licensed prostitution and, with the Salvation Army, the campaign to enable women to leave brothels freely. It engaged in pacifist and charitable activities and, in time, reached members across the country. Its membership was drawn primarily from Christian and educated women, and it also faced criticism: some saw its moralism as intolerant, others, including socialists and feminists, objected to its emphasis on private morality over structural change. Yajima herself later traveled to the United States and was active into old age.

The society illustrates how women's public activity was channeled. Barred from political associations, women's organizations operated as moral and charitable bodies, a form that was legally acceptable and socially respected. Their arguments, which often appealed to women's roles as mothers and moral guardians, could reinforce the framework of ryōsai kenbo while using it to demand reform and public voice. Their campaigns, by shifting debate about prostitution and concubinage, helped prepare the ground for the later organizing of the Taishō period.

Section 64.29

Buddhist Reform, Religious Philanthropy, and Social Service

Buddhism suffered severe blows in the early Meiji years. The separation of Shinto and Buddhism and the anti-Buddhist movement known as haibutsu kishaku (roughly 1868–1873) led to the destruction of some temples and images, the loss of lands and privileges, and the end of Buddhism's role in the household registration system. Buddhist leaders responded with reform. Shin (Pure Land) Buddhist figures such as Shimaji Mokurai argued for religious freedom and the separation of religion and state. Intellectuals including Inoue Enryō, who founded the Tetsugakukan (a philosophical academy) in 1887, defended Buddhism as compatible with modern philosophy and science. Kiyozawa Manshi, of the Ōtani branch of Shin Buddhism, emphasized inner spiritual cultivation and reform of institutional life.

Buddhist institutions also entered education and social service. They established schools, including universities that grew out of Buddhist seminaries, and developed lay associations for women, young people, and the general public. Women's Buddhist associations (fujinkai) organized education, fundraising, and charity, and chaplains served in the military in the wars of 1894–1895 and 1904–1905. Buddhist philanthropy ran orphanages, schools for the poor, and relief programs, partly in competition with Christian institutions that had gained prestige through their charitable work. Temples remained central to funerary and ancestral rites in households, so that a great majority of families continued to be affiliated with a temple even as the formal registration system ended.

Buddhism's responses show adaptation as well as defense. Buddhist leaders used the language of national service to argue for the religion's place in modern Japan, and many supported the state's wars and empire. The movement also had reformers who pressed for lay participation and social engagement. The relationships among Buddhism, Christianity, the new religions, and Shinto were therefore both competitive and, at times, cooperative, and an understanding of modern Japanese religion requires attention to all of them.

Section 64.30

Charity, Nursing, Orphanages, Health Campaigns, and the Rise of Organized Social Welfare

Poverty, illness, and disaster created a demand for relief that families, villages, and temples had once supplied informally. The Meiji state provided little. The Relief Regulations of 1874 offered limited aid to the poorest, in principle only to those without kin or ability to work, and the government relied on the principle of mutual assistance among family and community. Private organizations and religious bodies filled gaps. Ishii Jūji, a Christian from Okayama, established an orphanage in 1887 that grew into a major institution. The Salvation Army, active from 1895, ran social services, and Yamamuro Gunpei, a Japanese Salvation Army leader, published Shomin no fukuin ("The Gospel for the Common People," 1899), which addressed the lives of the poor. Hannah Riddell, an English missionary, established the Kaishun Hospital in Kumamoto in 1895 to care for people with leprosy at a time when the government's response relied on isolation.

Nursing, as mentioned above, was professionalized by the Japan Red Cross Society, which expanded from its origins in the 1877 Hakuaisha relief society, and by Christian and secular hospitals. Relief was also organized after disasters, such as the Nōbi earthquake of 1891 and the Sanriku tsunami of 1896, in which religious and private groups contributed. Public health campaigns addressed epidemic disease and tuberculosis, and in 1907 the government passed a law on leprosy that provided for the segregation of certain patients, an approach later criticized as a violation of patients' rights. These examples show that welfare in this period combined compassion and control.

By the early twentieth century, officials began to give greater attention to social problems. After the Russo-Japanese War, the Home Ministry promoted relief and the Local Improvement Movement and, in 1908, organized a conference on charity, signaling the state's growing interest in coordinating private efforts. The distinction between private philanthropy and governmental social policy remained sharp, and Japan did not have a comprehensive welfare system until much later. The experiences of this era nonetheless laid foundations for the social-work and social-policy debates of the Taishō period, treated in Chapters 67 to 69.

Section 64.31

Uneven Modernization: Gender, Class, Region, and Religious Difference

The preceding sections show that the changes of the Meiji decades were distributed unevenly. A middle-class urban woman could attend a girls' high school, read women's magazines, and perhaps join a reform society, but she could not vote or belong to a political association, and her legal capacity in marriage was limited. A rural farm woman might attend school for four or six years, then spend her life in field and household labor, her relation to the state mediated by the household head. A teacher gained income and respect, but faced official moral expectations and often retirement at marriage. A believer in Tenrikyō or Konkōkyō found community and spiritual support, but also faced official suspicion until recognition. A Christian found educational and professional opportunities while facing accusations of disloyalty.

Ainu and Okinawan communities experienced the school system as an instrument of assimilation, with consequences for language, land, and identity that differed from those of mainland farm households. Burakumin communities, legally freed in 1871, continued to face discrimination in schooling, marriage, and employment, and the register system could expose origins. Domestic servants and young migrant workers bore the burdens of a modern household and economy without the protections that middle-class households enjoyed. For these groups, modernization offered some openings and imposed new forms of control.

No single experience can therefore represent modern Japan. Statistical averages, such as national attendance rates, conceal wide variation; famous individuals, such as Tsuda Umeko or Hiratsuka Raichō, were exceptional; and official sources reflect the perspective of those who made the rules. The historian's task is to compare experiences, identify who gained and who lost, and recognize that the same law or institution could have different effects in different circumstances.

Section 64.32

Family, School, Religion, and the Making of Modern Japanese Society

The developments traced in this chapter interlocked. The household register and the Civil Code defined legal membership and authority; schools taught ideals of citizenship, family, and womanhood, and used the register and the household to reach children; religious organizations supplied moral and spiritual frameworks and some of the educational and charitable institutions that the state did not; and urbanization changed the conditions in which families lived. The state sought to harmonize these elements around the imperial ideology and the household as a unit of the national polity, and often used the language of the "family state." But the elements did not fit perfectly. Women's education created readers and writers who questioned the roles prescribed for them; new religions and Christian groups created communities with their own loyalties; migration and urban life loosened some ties.

By 1916 Japan had universal elementary education, a legal code that defined the household as the basic unit of society, a set of girls' secondary and higher schools, a growing number of women in professions, an array of recognized religious bodies under state supervision, and a visible, though small, public debate about women's autonomy. These achievements were real and limited. Women remained without political rights; Ainu, Okinawan, and Burakumin communities faced assimilation and discrimination; the poor lived with inadequate housing and welfare; and the state maintained control over religion and education. The Taishō decades inherited both the new possibilities and the unresolved tensions, which erupted in the labor, suffrage, and cultural movements of the 1910s and 1920s.

Section 64.33

Chapter Review and Historical Thinking Checkpoint

This chapter has argued that the transformation of Meiji and early Taishō Japan occurred in households, classrooms, neighborhoods, and religious communities as well as in government and industry, and that these changes were contradictory. Before proceeding, students should be able to demonstrate understanding of the following core points.

First, the Civil Code of 1898 modernized private law and defined the household (ie) as a legal institution with a head and a register, but it did so by generalizing and strengthening a particular model of household authority, with significant legal limits on wives. Second, the ideology of ryōsai kenbo linked women's education to national strengthening, widened opportunities for some women, and narrowed expectations for many. Third, women took part in reform movements, writing, teaching, nursing, and other work, but were excluded from political associations by laws of 1890 and 1900. Fourth, elementary education expanded to near-universal attendance and six years of compulsory schooling by 1908, while secondary and higher education for girls was separate and limited, and access varied with income, region, and community, including Ainu, Okinawan, and Burakumin children. Fifth, urban growth changed household composition, housing, and health conditions while older networks of obligation persisted. Sixth, Tenrikyō and Konkōkyō grew from the experiences of founders before 1868 into recognized religious organizations in 1908 and 1900, negotiating the limits of religious freedom. Seventh, Christian and Buddhist institutions contributed to education, women's reform, and the beginnings of organized social welfare.

Students should be able to compare historical experiences across gender, class, region, and religion; to distinguish written law from practice; to separate participation in public life from formal political rights; and to evaluate the evidence available for each of these questions, including whose perspectives the evidence omits. The review questions that follow test these skills.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how the Meiji transformation reshaped households, gender relations, schooling, religion, and reform between the 1870s and about 1916. Its central argument is that these changes were contradictory: modern institutions expanded opportunities and constrained them at the same time, and different groups experienced them differently.

The household was the basic unit of law and society. Tokugawa Japan had diverse household practices, which the Meiji state began to standardize with the household registers of 1871–1872. The Civil Code of 1898 defined the ie as a legal institution headed by a koshu, whose consent was required for members' marriages and adoptions and who normally passed headship to a single heir. It was a genuine modernization of private law, and it also generalized a hierarchical model of household authority that was not universal in earlier practice, which is why historians debate whether it preserved or constructed tradition.

The code placed wives under legal limitations, including restrictions on their legal capacity and unequal grounds for divorce. Written law, however, did not fully determine practice: arranged marriages, divorces by agreement, customary property arrangements, and women's economic contributions varied by class and region. The ideal of ryōsai kenbo, which spread in the 1890s and became associated with girls' schooling after 1899, defined women's national contribution in domestic and maternal terms. It expanded women's education while narrowing expectations, and it described a middle-class ideal that did not fit most women's lives.

Women's public participation grew but remained legally restricted. A few women took part in the Freedom and People's Rights Movement, but the Law on Assembly and Political Association of 1890 and the Public Peace Police Law of 1900 excluded women from political associations. Women's activity therefore flowed into teaching, nursing, midwifery, writing, and Christian and charitable reform. Licensed prostitution and concubinage were targets of reformers such as Yajima Kajiko and the WCTU, while state regulators emphasized control and public health. Writers such as Yosano Akiko and the women of Seitō opened a debate on autonomy that anticipated the Taishō movements.

Education was the central institutional change. Elementary schooling expanded from limited attendance to near-universal enrollment, aided by the abolition of tuition in 1900 and six-year compulsory schooling in 1908. The Rescript on Education and state-compiled textbooks tied schooling to national loyalty, though local practice varied. Girls' secondary schooling under the Order of 1899 was separate and shorter, normal schools trained teachers, and private and mission institutions, including Tsuda Umeko's school and Japan Women's University, offered women higher education outside the imperial universities, which admitted women only from 1913. Rural poverty, distance, discrimination, and assimilation policy shaped unequal access, especially for Ainu, Okinawan, and Burakumin children.

Urbanization changed family arrangements, housing, and health. Migrants to Tokyo, Osaka, and other cities lived in boardinghouses, tenements, and dormitories; epidemics and poor sanitation drew official and reformist attention; and children experienced schooling, work, and state-organized youth associations. At the same time, kin and neighborhood networks of mutual aid persisted and adapted, so urbanization transformed but did not eliminate older social relationships.

New religious movements grew from the experiences of founders and the devotional landscape of popular religion. Nakayama Miki's experience of 1838 and Akazawa Bunji's of 1859 preceded the Restoration, but their communities developed their institutions under the Meiji state. Tenrikyō gained organizational recognition in 1888, faced restrictions in 1896, and became independent in 1908; Konkōkyō became independent in 1900. Kurozumikyō, Ōmoto, and other movements show the range of new religious responses. The state's Sect Shinto classification and its separation of shrines from religion defined the limits of religious freedom, and historians debate how far the movements challenged or accommodated authority.

Christianity, despite its small numbers, shaped education, women's reform, and philanthropy after the lifting of the ban on Christianity in 1873. Mission schools offered girls education and leadership, though Instruction 12 of 1899 forced difficult choices. Buddhist institutions reformed and entered education and charity. Private philanthropy, led by religious groups, preceded and exceeded state welfare, which only gradually developed after the Russo-Japanese War.

Taken together, these developments show that modernization was uneven. Its benefits and burdens were distributed by gender, class, region, ethnicity, and religion, and the same law or school could mean different things to a middle-class urban family, a tenant farmer, an Ainu child, or a domestic servant. The institutions and tensions established in these decades shaped the labor, suffrage, religious, and cultural movements of the Taishō period.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows where modern Japan was lived. Constitutions and wars (Chapters 59 to 61) set the framework, but families, schools, congregations, and neighborhoods carried change into everyday life. The contradictions traced here, between expanding opportunity and legal limitation, between universal schooling and unequal access, between religious growth and state supervision, were not accidents but features of a society reorganized from above and negotiated from below.

The chapter also connects regions and communities that are often separated. The same household registers and school rules reached villages in Yamato, tenements in Osaka, Ainu settlements in Hokkaido, and Okinawan towns, with quite different results. It connects Japan to the wider world as well: Christian missions, Western legal models, and international reform networks mattered, but were selectively adopted and reshaped. The new religions, by contrast, grew from local experiences and later spread beyond Japan.

Many developments shaped later history. The household system remained in force until the postwar constitution and civil-code reform (Chapter 84). The state's control of schooling and the Rescript became central to wartime mobilization (Chapter 77). Women's reform and writing led into the suffrage and labor movements (Chapters 67 to 69), and the classification of religions underlies the suppression of Ōmoto and later controversies (Chapter 72). Urban life prefigured the metropolitan culture of Chapter 70.

Students who understand this chapter should be wary of simple stories, whether of progress or of unchanging patriarchy. The history of family, school, and faith in Meiji Japan is a history of negotiation among unequal parties, and it prepares the study of mass society, democracy, and crisis that follows.
