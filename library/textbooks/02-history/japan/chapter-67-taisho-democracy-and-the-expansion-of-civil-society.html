Part X — Mass Society, Democracy, and Crisis

Taishō Democracy and the Expansion of Civil Society

Japanese History: From Jōmon Japan to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

In the second week of February 1913, crowds gathered around the Imperial Diet in Tokyo while a cabinet that had held office for barely seven weeks tried to govern against the opposition of the elected lower house, much of the press, and a large part of the urban public. On 11 February the prime minister, Katsura Tarō, resigned. No election had been held and no constitutional clause had been amended. A government had nevertheless fallen because political actors outside the narrow circle of the emperor's advisers had made its position untenable.

The episode matters not because it created democracy in Japan, which it did not, but because it showed that the Meiji Constitution of 1889 could be argued over, reinterpreted, and contested in public. The constitution gave the emperor sovereign authority, made ministers responsible to the throne rather than to parliament, and surrounded the elected House of Representatives with bodies that no voter chose. Yet within those rules, between the accession of the Taishō Emperor in 1912 and the end of his reign in December 1926, political parties acquired a growing claim to form cabinets, newspapers and magazines carried constitutional arguments to unprecedented audiences, and organizations of women, students, outcast communities, Christians, local reformers, and colonial subjects pressed new demands. Historians conventionally call this combination of developments Taishō Democracy (Taishō demokurashii).

That label requires care from the first page. Taishō Democracy was never an official regime, a constitutional amendment, or a name used by the government. It is an analytical term applied by later historians to a period of expanded representative politics and public debate, and its meaning is contested. This chapter argues that Taishō Democracy was a consequential but incomplete expansion of political participation. Parties gained influence over the formation of cabinets, culminating in a conventional practice between 1924 and 1932 that the leader of a lower-house majority should be asked to govern. Citizens increasingly mobilized through newspapers, public meetings, associations, and petitions. Intellectuals defended constitutional government in ways that stretched but did not break the language of imperial sovereignty. All of this occurred within a system that preserved substantial authority for the emperor's advisers, the military, the House of Peers, the Privy Council, and the state bureaucracy. Its central contradiction was that representative government expanded without a corresponding transformation in the underlying distribution of sovereignty and state power.

The people who made and experienced this history were many. Party leaders such as Hara Takashi, Katō Takaaki, Takahashi Korekiyo, and Inukai Tsuyoshi negotiated with genrō, bureaucrats, and military leaders. Scholars such as Yoshino Sakuzō and Minobe Tatsukichi gave constitutional politics its vocabulary. Women including Hiratsuka Raichō, Ichikawa Fusae, and Oku Mumeo claimed the right to speak in public life without holding the vote. Members of Buraku communities founded the National Suiheisha in 1922 and declared their equality and dignity. Christian reformers, social workers, youth association members, students, and local activists built the civic infrastructure through which many people first experienced organized public life. At the same time, the people of Korea and Taiwan lived under a colonial administration in which the debates of metropolitan Japan offered them no voice, and Koreans and Taiwanese formulated their own demands in the March First Movement of 1919 and the Taiwanese petition campaign beginning in 1921. A chapter about democracy in Taishō Japan must keep both sets of experiences in view.

Historians reconstruct this period from a rich but uneven documentary record: debates and reports of the Imperial Diet, cabinet and ministry records, laws and ordinances, the diaries of politicians such as Hara Takashi, newspapers and magazines including Chūō Kōron, the publications of political parties and associations, police reports, petitions, organizational declarations, and the memoirs and later reflections of participants. These sources are unevenly distributed. Politicians, editors, and scholars produced far more preserved text than farmers, factory workers, or colonial subjects, and police and censorship records filter much of what is known about dissent. Throughout the chapter, students are asked to consider who produced each source, for whom, and whose voices are missing.

This chapter builds directly on Chapter 59, which explained the construction of the Meiji constitutional order; on Chapter 65, which traced the growth of commercial media; and on Chapter 66, which examined Japan's wartime expansion. It stops short of the detailed histories reserved for later chapters: the Rice Riots (Chapter 68), labor organization and the suffrage movement (Chapter 69), the modern city (Chapter 70), the Great Kantō Earthquake (Chapter 71), and the breakdown of parliamentary restraint (Chapter 74). The narrative must also resist hindsight. The authoritarian turn of the 1930s was not the inevitable outcome of Taishō politics, just as party government was not the inevitable outcome of Meiji constitutionalism. People in the 1910s and 1920s acted amid uncertainty, and the period's significance lies partly in the possibilities it opened and the constraints that kept those possibilities incomplete.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why historians use "Taishō Democracy" as an analytical label rather than as the name of an official constitutional regime.
  2. Distinguish the formal powers of the emperor, cabinet, House of Representatives, House of Peers, Privy Council, genrō, and military under the Meiji Constitution from the developing expectation of responsible party government.
  3. Compare the first Movement to Protect Constitutional Government of 1912–1913 with the second movement of 1924, identifying their different triggers, participants, and outcomes.
  4. Distinguish Yoshino Sakuzō's minponshugi from popular sovereignty, and Yoshino's political argument from Minobe Tatsukichi's legal theory of the emperor as an organ of the state.
  5. Trace the formation of Hara Takashi's cabinet in 1918 and assess why it is described as the first broadly recognized fully party-based cabinet rather than the first party participation in government.
  6. Analyze the electoral reforms of 1919 and 1925, specifying who gained and who remained excluded in each case.
  7. Evaluate how women's organizations, the National Suiheisha, religious and philanthropic associations, student and youth groups, and local activists expanded civil society beyond formal electoral politics.
  8. Interpret the 1922 amendment of the Public Peace Police Law precisely, distinguishing attendance at political meetings, membership in political associations, and the vote.
  9. Assess how the colonial order in Korea and Taiwan, and the position of other excluded populations, limited any claim that Japan's empire experienced democratization as a whole.
  10. Explain why universal male suffrage and the Peace Preservation Law were enacted in the same year, and what the pairing reveals about the boundaries of reform.
  11. Evaluate competing historiographical interpretations of Taishō Democracy while avoiding teleological explanations of its later fate.

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  4. Why did Hara Takashi's cabinet of 1918 become a turning point, and what did it leave unchanged?

  6. How should historians weigh the representative expansion of the 1920s against the exclusion of women, colonial subjects, and marginalized groups?

Historical Context

Chapter 59 explained how the Meiji oligarchs designed a constitution in 1889 that created a bicameral Imperial Diet while keeping the emperor's sovereignty, the independence of the military command, and the appointed House of Peers outside the reach of electoral politics. The first elections gave a small, property-qualified electorate control of the House of Representatives, and the Diet's power over the budget quickly turned parties into serious political actors. After years of confrontation, Itō Hirobumi himself founded the Rikken Seiyūkai in 1900, accepting that managing the lower house required organized party support. For the next decade, power alternated by negotiation between Katsura Tarō, protégé of the army elder Yamagata Aritomo, and Saionji Kinmochi, president of the Seiyūkai, in a pattern sometimes called the Katsura–Saionji era. This arrangement gave the Seiyūkai access to office but kept the decisive choices in the hands of a small group of senior statesmen.

The decade before 1912 also altered Japan's society. Victory in the Russo-Japanese War (Chapter 61) left heavy debts and expanded the empire, and public anger at the peace terms had erupted in riots in Tokyo in 1905. Annexation of Korea in 1910 (Chapter 62) enlarged the empire and army demands for garrisons. The state responded to socialist agitation with severe repression, as in the High Treason Incident of 1910–1911, and established a specialized political police. Meanwhile, industrial growth, urbanization, near-universal primary schooling, and the expansion of newspapers produced an increasingly literate and politically attentive urban public, including salaried employees, teachers, journalists, students, and shopkeepers, as well as industrial workers (Chapters 56, 63, and 65). Only a small share of adult men could vote, women could not, and colonial subjects had no representation.

The First World War (Chapter 66) intensified these pressures. It brought economic boom, inflation, and new international prestige, while exposing divisions within the political elite over China policy, military authority, and the role of parties. This chapter begins at the point where these structural conditions become political events: the accession of a new emperor in 1912, a crisis over the army's demand for new divisions, and a public response that the Meiji oligarchs had not anticipated. It follows the period to 1926, when the death of Katō Takaaki and the end of the Taishō era closed a phase in which party government and civic organization had advanced, but within a framework whose limits would be tested in the years that followed.

Division I — Constitutional Crisis and the Rise of Party Politics · Section 67.1

Japan at the Beginning of the Taishō Era, 1912

The Meiji Emperor died on 30 July 1912, and his son Yoshihito became the Taishō Emperor. The transition closed a reign of forty-five years during which Japan had acquired a constitution, a modern army and navy, an industrial economy, and an empire. It was marked by dramatic public gestures of mourning, including the suicide of General Nogi Maresuke at the time of the funeral in September, which many contemporaries interpreted as a statement about the passing of an age. The new emperor, however, lacked his father's authority and, as his health deteriorated, he participated less and less in public life. In 1921 his son Hirohito became regent (sesshō), a development that bears on the political position of the throne discussed in Section 67.27.

Japan in 1912 had a population of roughly fifty million people. Most still lived in villages and worked in agriculture, but cities were growing, and the urban population included an expanding class of salaried employees, teachers, civil servants, journalists, professionals, and small business owners alongside industrial workers. Primary schooling was nearly universal, which created a large reading public. At the same time the electorate, limited by a direct-tax qualification, included only a small minority of adult men, and the suffrage excluded women entirely. Colonial subjects in Taiwan (acquired in 1895) and Korea (annexed in 1910) had no representatives in the Diet. These facts set the baseline against which Taishō expansions of participation must be measured.

Politically, the early Taishō years combined stability and strain. The Rikken Seiyūkai commanded a lower-house majority, but cabinet positions had been allocated through negotiations among the genrō, the leaders of the Seiyūkai, and Katsura Tarō's circle. Public finances were strained by debts left from the Russo-Japanese War, and both services pressed for budget increases. The army wanted additional divisions to garrison Korea, while the navy pursued a larger fleet. The government of Saionji Kinmochi was committed to fiscal restraint. The stage was set for a collision between military claims on the budget and a party cabinet that relied on the Diet, and the collision would reveal how little the formal constitution said about who ultimately decided.

Historians use "Taishō Democracy" with several cautions. The label compresses a complex fifteen-year period; many of its characteristic developments began before 1912 or extended beyond 1926; the word "democracy" translates several different Japanese terms with different meanings (Section 67.6); and the period included repression alongside expansion. The term is useful because it names a set of real changes in party politics, public debate, and organized participation. It becomes misleading if it suggests that a liberal democratic regime had replaced the Meiji order. This chapter uses the label as an analytical shorthand and asks, section by section, what was democratized, for whom, and with what limits.

Section 67.2

The Meiji Constitution and the Struggle over Responsible Government

The Meiji Constitution declared the emperor sacred and inviolable and vested sovereignty in him, to be exercised according to the constitution. It created a bicameral Imperial Diet consisting of an elected House of Representatives and a House of Peers composed of hereditary peers, imperial appointees, and elected high taxpayers. The two houses had nearly equal legislative powers, except that budget bills originated in the lower house, so that the peers could block anything the representatives passed. The constitution also established that ministers of state advised the emperor and bore responsibility to him, a formula that did not require cabinets to command a majority in the Diet. The budget provisions added a safeguard: if the Diet failed to pass a new budget, the government could continue the previous year's, a rule that limited the lower house's most powerful weapon.

Authority outside the Diet was extensive. The Privy Council advised the emperor on important matters, including treaties and constitutional interpretation. The military enjoyed an independent right of supreme command, which in practice meant that the General Staff and the service ministers could act without cabinet direction in matters of operations and command. In 1900 an imperial ordinance required that army and navy ministers be active-duty generals and admirals, giving each service a veto over cabinet formation, since a service could refuse to supply a minister and so prevent a cabinet from forming or force one to fall. The genrō, an informal group of senior Meiji leaders including Yamagata Aritomo, Matsukata Masayoshi, and Saionji Kinmochi, advised the emperor on the choice of prime ministers. None of these institutions was elected, and none was responsible to the Diet.

Yet the constitution did not forbid parties from gaining influence, and the lower house's budget and legislative powers gave them leverage. A short-lived cabinet under Ōkuma Shigenobu and Itagaki Taisuke in 1898, composed largely of party leaders, demonstrated that party participation in government was possible, though it collapsed within months. Itō Hirobumi's creation of the Seiyūkai in 1900 institutionalized party participation from within the oligarchy. The result by 1912 was a system in which cabinets required the cooperation of the lower house in practice, but in which who formed a cabinet and what policies it pursued depended on negotiations among unelected elites. The question of Taishō political history can therefore be phrased constitutionally: would the convention develop that the cabinet was responsible, in practice, to the House of Representatives, and would the unelected institutions accept that convention?

Historical Vocabulary
Genrō, Responsible Government, and Sovereignty

The genrō ("elder statesmen") were a small group of Meiji leaders, mostly from the former domains of Chōshū and Satsuma, who after the 1890s advised the emperor on the choice of prime ministers and on other crucial decisions. The term denotes an informal, extraconstitutional role: the constitution does not mention them. By 1912 only a handful survived, and by Yamagata's death in 1922 and Matsukata's in 1924 only Saionji Kinmochi remained.

Responsible government, in the sense used by Taishō reformers, meant that the cabinet should hold office because it commanded the confidence of the elected lower house, as in the British parliamentary model. The Meiji Constitution did not require this, because ministers were responsible to the emperor. Reformers argued for it as a matter of practice and convention, which is why the Taishō debates concern the interpretation of the constitution rather than its amendment. Sovereignty (shuken) means ultimate legal authority in the state. Under the Meiji Constitution the legal locus of sovereignty was the emperor (tennō shuken), an issue that Yoshino and Minobe approached differently (Sections 67.6 and 67.7). Students should keep these three concepts distinct: a cabinet might become more responsible to the Diet without any change in where sovereignty formally resided.

Section 67.3

The Taishō Political Crisis of 1912–1913

The crisis began with a budget dispute. In 1912 the army demanded two additional divisions to garrison Korea, citing the new colonial commitments and the instability following the Chinese Revolution of 1911. The Saionji cabinet, supported by the Seiyūkai and committed to financial retrenchment after the burden of the Russo-Japanese War debt, declined to fund them. In early December the army minister, Uehara Yūsaku, resigned, appealing directly to the emperor. The army then refused to nominate a successor. Because the 1900 ordinance required an active-duty officer, the service's refusal made it impossible for Saionji to fill the post, and on 5 December 1912 his cabinet resigned. The episode illustrated in concentrated form how the independent powers of the military could bring down a cabinet that had the confidence of the lower house.

The genrō were unable to agree on a successor and Saionji himself, who might have been expected to lead the search, declined to continue. The choice eventually fell on Katsura Tarō, who had served as prime minister twice before, who was an army general and Yamagata's protégé, and who at that moment held the posts of grand chamberlain and lord keeper of the privy seal, positions close to the emperor. Katsura's appointment as prime minister drew on his proximity to the throne, which opponents saw as an improper use of the court. His third cabinet took office in late December 1912. Faced with the navy minister's reluctance to join, Katsura secured an imperial rescript on 22 December 1912 directing the naval minister to remain in office, allowing the new cabinet to function. Critics alleged that he was using the emperor's authority to override political opposition, an accusation that became central to the protests described in the next section.

The crisis therefore combined three distinct elements: the military's capacity to destroy a cabinet through the service-minister system, the oligarchic method of choosing a successor, and the use of the throne to shield a government from criticism. Opponents criticized all three under the slogan kensei yōgo, batsuzoku daha ("protect constitutional government, destroy the clan cliques"). The word batsuzoku, translated here as "clan cliques," referred to the dominance of officials from Chōshū and Satsuma, the hanbatsu, in the highest offices. The slogan joined a constitutional principle to a social grievance about who monopolized power, and its appeal lay in that combination.

Evidence for the crisis comes from Diet proceedings, newspaper coverage, and the memoirs and diaries of participants. Official records preserve the formal sequence of resignations and appointments but reveal little about private negotiations, which must be reconstructed from diaries and later memoirs, each shaped by the author's purposes. Newspaper accounts vary according to the political position of the paper, a variation the chapter returns to in Section 67.8.

Section 67.4

Ozaki Yukio, Inukai Tsuyoshi, Public Demonstrations, and Constitutional Protest

The first Movement to Protect Constitutional Government (Dai-ichiji Kensei Yōgo Undō) united politicians from the Seiyūkai, the Rikken Kokumintō, and other groups with journalists, businessmen, lawyers, and urban associations. Its most famous leaders were Ozaki Yukio, a veteran parliamentarian with a long record of opposition to the oligarchy, and Inukai Tsuyoshi, leader of the Kokumintō. The movement organized public meetings in Tokyo and other major cities, issued resolutions, and relied on newspapers sympathetic to its aims. Its form was novel in scale: meetings drew large audiences, the speeches were reported widely, and the movement framed its demands as defense of the constitution rather than as partisan advantage.

The climax came in early February 1913. Katsura had announced plans to form a government party, which would later become the Rikken Dōshikai, and suspended the Diet briefly, then sought to use imperial commands to persuade the Seiyūkai to restrain itself. In the Diet on 5 February, Ozaki delivered a speech accusing Katsura of hiding behind the throne and using imperial commands as weapons against his political opponents; contemporaries and later historians regarded it as one of the most memorable parliamentary speeches of the era. On 10 February large crowds gathered around the Diet building in Tokyo. Some people attacked pro-government newspaper offices, notably that of the Kokumin Shimbun, whose editor Tokutomi Sohō was close to Katsura, and police boxes. Similar protests occurred in other cities, with the Kansai region especially active. On 11 February Katsura resigned after fifty-three days in office.

The outcome showed what political mobilization could and could not accomplish. It demonstrated that a prime minister selected through elite channels could be forced from office by a combination of parliamentary opposition, press criticism, and street protest. It also changed the political calculations of the Seiyūkai and the oligarchs, who saw that attempting to govern against sustained public opposition carried real costs. But it did not change the constitution or the rules for selecting prime ministers. The genrō selected Katsura's successor, Admiral Yamamoto Gonnohyōe, whose cabinet depended on Seiyūkai support. The movement's participants also differed in their goals: some wanted party cabinets, some wanted to remove Chōshū and Satsuma influence, some opposed military expansion, and some acted from party advantage. Katsura himself died in October 1913, leaving his planned party to be organized as the Rikken Dōshikai under Katō Takaaki that December.

The violence of 10 February also complicates the picture. Contemporaries and later historians disagreed about whether the attacks on newspaper offices and police boxes represented political principle, urban disorder, or opportunistic mob behavior. The police response and the relationship between organized leadership and spontaneous crowds are matters that scholarship continues to debate. The crowd would reappear as a political force, with different social composition and much larger scale, in 1918 (Chapter 68).

Primary Source Window

Ozaki Yukio's Diet Speech against the Katsura Cabinet (5 February 1913)

Creator: Ozaki Yukio, member of the House of Representatives · Date and place: 5 February 1913, Imperial Diet, Tokyo · Type: Parliamentary speech, preserved in Diet records and reprinted in the press

Context: Katsura Tarō had assumed office in December 1912 after the fall of the Saionji cabinet and had relied on imperial commands in forming and defending his government. Ozaki addressed the Diet as a leading figure in the constitutional-protection movement.

Faithful paraphrase of the speech's central argument (not a verbatim quotation): Ozaki charged that Katsura and his supporters treated the throne as a protective screen and imperial commands as instruments to attack political opponents, rather than serving the emperor by governing responsibly. He argued that this practice endangered both constitutional government and the emperor's own position by drawing him into partisan struggle.

Analysis: The speech shows how opponents of the cabinet framed their cause in loyalist terms: they did not challenge the emperor's sovereignty but argued that the government's behavior misused it. This strategy was possible and effective precisely because the constitution made the emperor sacred. The source also shows the Diet functioning as a public stage, since the speech was reported widely outside the chamber. Its limitations are those of political oratory: Ozaki was an advocate seeking to persuade, the speech reflects his version of Katsura's motives, and it cannot show the views of the many who attended meetings or crowded outside the Diet.

Section 67.5

From Yamamoto to Ōkuma and Terauchi: The Unsettled Path toward Party Government

Admiral Yamamoto Gonnohyōe's cabinet, formed in February 1913 with Seiyūkai backing, made several concessions to the movement's critique. Seiyūkai members entered the cabinet, led by Hara Takashi as home minister. The government revised the ordinances governing the civil service so that party politicians could hold senior bureaucratic posts, and it changed the service-minister rule to allow reserve officers, which weakened the army's veto over cabinet formation in principle. These changes were significant because they altered the institutional levers that had destroyed the Saionji cabinet, though in practice the services retained influence and the rule was restored to its stricter form in 1936.

Yamamoto's government fell in March 1914 when revelations of bribery involving naval officers and the German firm Siemens, along with a British firm, provoked public outrage and Diet attacks. The scandal showed the press and the opposition exercising a public check on government, but it also damaged confidence in both naval and party institutions. After a brief failed attempt to form a navy-led cabinet, the genrō turned to Ōkuma Shigenobu, a veteran of the Meiji period with a record as a party leader and popular orator. Ōkuma took office in April 1914 with the backing of the Rikken Dōshikai, led by Katō Takaaki. The election of 1915 gave his supporters a large majority, an unusual instance of the government winning a lower-house election by mobilizing the press and party organization. The cabinet presided over Japan's wartime expansion, including the Twenty-One Demands on China (Chapter 66).

In October 1916 the genrō selected General Terauchi Masatake, who formed a cabinet that rejected party affiliation and was sometimes called a "transcendental" (chōzen) cabinet, meaning one that claimed to stand above party competition. Opposition parties responded by consolidating: the Dōshikai and other groups merged as the Kenseikai under Katō Takaaki in October 1916. Terauchi faced a Seiyūkai-dominated lower house after the 1917 election, and he sought to manage it by offering Hara and other party leaders seats on an advisory council on foreign affairs. Hara's cooperation was conditional and strategic, reflecting a patient assessment that the Seiyūkai could advance more by waiting than by confronting Terauchi.

The years 1913–1918 therefore show party influence increasing without becoming an uninterrupted practice. Cabinets changed through a mixture of party pressure, scandal, elite choice, and wartime circumstances. Parties were stronger participants in cabinet politics in 1918 than in 1912, but no rule yet required that a prime minister come from the lower-house majority, and the genrō retained the decisive role. The question remained open until the shock of 1918 changed the elite's calculations.

Historical Debate

Was the 1913 Crisis a Democratic Breakthrough or a Conflict among Political Elites?

The question: The fall of the Katsura cabinet involved crowds, newspapers, and parliamentary opposition. Does this show the emergence of democratic politics, or was it essentially a struggle among factions within a narrow political class?

Interpretation A — A democratic breakthrough: Some historians stress the unprecedented scale of public mobilization. Large meetings, press campaigns, and street demonstrations forced a cabinet from office, a result that would have been unlikely under the Meiji oligarchs' earlier practice. On this view the crisis marks the entry of urban public opinion as a force in cabinet politics and the beginning of the Taishō democratic movement.
Interpretation B — Elite conflict with public participation: Other historians emphasize that the leaders were professional politicians, journalists, and businessmen with party and factional interests; that the mass participants were largely urban men and limited in number compared with the whole population; that the crisis ended with the oligarchs selecting a new prime minister; and that parties did not gain a durable right to form cabinets. On this view the crisis showed the limits of oligarchic control but remained an episode in elite competition.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on the same sources: Diet records, newspaper reports, memoirs, and police accounts. They differ on how to weigh crowd participation against the continuing role of the genrō and parties.

Why it matters: If 1913 is a democratic breakthrough, then Taishō Democracy begins as a popular movement. If it is an elite conflict, then the later expansion of participation requires another explanation. Many scholars adopt an intermediate position, noting that elite competition and public mobilization were intertwined.

Student task: Identify one piece of evidence from this section supporting each interpretation and explain which seems more persuasive. What additional evidence, such as the social composition of crowds, would help resolve the question?

Division II — Democratic Thought, Constitutional Theory, and the Public Sphere · Section 67.6

Yoshino Sakuzō and Minponshugi: Democracy within an Imperial Constitution

Yoshino Sakuzō (1878–1933), professor of political history at Tokyo Imperial University, became the most prominent public intellectual of the Taishō democratic movement. In January 1916 he published in the journal Chūō Kōron a long essay on constitutional government and the way to perfect its operation. The essay argued that constitutional government required more than a written constitution: it required that politics aim at the welfare of the people and that policy be decided in accordance with the people's will as expressed through their representatives. Yoshino made the argument in terms intended to be acceptable under the Meiji Constitution, and its strategy is the key to its influence.

The central conceptual move was to distinguish two things that the English word "democracy" can combine. Yoshino used the term minponshugi, often translated "people-as-the-basis-ism," for a principle of political practice: government should be conducted for the benefit of the people and decisions should reflect public opinion. He distinguished it from minshushugi, the doctrine that sovereignty legally resides in the people, which he regarded as incompatible with Japan's national polity (kokutai), in which sovereignty belonged to the emperor. By separating the location of sovereignty from the way sovereign power was exercised, Yoshino made a case for universal male suffrage, responsible party cabinets, reform of the House of Peers and the Privy Council, and civilian control over the military, without openly challenging the emperor's legal position.

Scholars have debated how to evaluate this argument. Some see it as a pragmatic strategy that enlarged what could be said publicly under the constraints of censorship and the political climate. Others regard the avoidance of popular sovereignty as a limitation that made the movement vulnerable to critics who could argue that the practice of responsible government implied a transfer of sovereignty. Both agree that the essay supplied a vocabulary adopted by journalists, party politicians, and students. Yoshino himself continued to write prolifically, supported universal suffrage, criticized Japanese policy in China and Korea on some occasions, and helped found organizations such as the Reimeikai in 1918 (Section 67.9).

The text is also a translation problem: minponshugi and minshushugi are both rendered "democracy" in some English writing, which can obscure the distinction Yoshino drew.

Section 67.7

Minobe Tatsukichi and the Constitutional Theory of the Emperor as an Organ of the State

While Yoshino argued as a political scientist about how politics ought to be conducted, Minobe Tatsukichi (1873–1948), professor of constitutional law at Tokyo Imperial University, argued as a jurist about what the constitution meant legally. In works beginning with his 1912 lectures on the constitution, Minobe developed what came to be called the organ theory (tennō kikan setsu). It held that sovereignty belongs to the state as a legal person, and that the emperor, as the highest organ of the state, exercises the state's sovereign powers according to the constitution. The theory drew on German legal scholarship about the state as a juridical entity and gave Japanese constitutional law a systematic framework.

The implications were significant. If the emperor acted as an organ of the state according to constitutional rules, then the ministers' advice, the Diet's consent, and other constitutional procedures were part of how sovereign authority was exercised, rather than limits imposed on a personal sovereign. Minobe therefore supported the view that cabinets should act with the confidence of the Diet in practice, and he rejected interpretations that placed the emperor's personal will beyond constitutional procedure. His position was influential among legal scholars, bureaucrats, and many politicians, and became widely regarded as the orthodox academic interpretation during the 1910s and 1920s.

The organ theory was contested from the beginning. In 1912–1913 Minobe engaged in a public dispute with Uesugi Shinkichi, also a constitutional scholar, who argued that the emperor was the personal bearer of sovereignty and that Minobe's theory diminished the throne. The disagreement demonstrates that constitutional interpretation was itself a field of political conflict. Conservative critics treated the emperor's personal sovereignty as an essential part of the national polity. Minobe and his supporters regarded the organ theory as compatible with imperial dignity and necessary for a modern legal order. The debate would return with much greater consequences in 1935, when the theory became the target of a nationalist campaign (a development that belongs to Chapter 74 and later chapters).

Students should keep Minobe and Yoshino apart. Yoshino's argument concerned the operation of politics and the proper aims of government; Minobe's concerned the legal characterization of sovereignty. Their conclusions overlapped in supporting constitutional government and parliamentary practice, and both challenged overly authoritarian readings of the constitution, but they proceeded from different disciplines, different premises, and different vocabularies. A reader who merges them risks missing both the diversity of Taishō constitutional thought and the specific vulnerabilities of each position.

Historical Vocabulary
Minponshugi, Minshushugi, the Organ Theory, and Constitutionalism

Minponshugi is the term Yoshino Sakuzō used for a principle of political practice that government should aim at the people's welfare and reflect their will. Minshushugi denotes democracy in the stronger sense of popular sovereignty. The distinction mattered because under the Meiji Constitution advocating popular sovereignty risked charges of challenging the national polity, whereas minponshugi could be defended within existing law. In English the two are often both translated "democracy," which flattens a distinction contemporaries considered crucial.

The organ theory (tennō kikan setsu) is Minobe Tatsukichi's legal doctrine that the emperor is the highest organ of a sovereign state rather than the personal owner of sovereignty. Constitutionalism (rikkenshugi) refers to the principle that governmental power should be exercised according to constitutional rules and limits. In Taishō debates, constitutionalism served as a shared language across political positions, though people disagreed about what it required.

Section 67.8

Newspapers, Magazines, Intellectual Debate, and Public Opinion

Chapter 65 traced the growth of the newspaper and magazine industry. This section asks how that infrastructure carried political argument. The major daily newspapers, such as the Asahi and Mainichi chains based in Osaka and Tokyo, reached readerships in the hundreds of thousands, and general-interest monthlies such as Chūō Kōron and, from 1919, Kaizō reached educated readers who wanted sustained analysis. Editors played important roles: they commissioned articles, selected authors, and shaped which arguments received national attention. Yoshino's influence owed as much to his place in Chūō Kōron as to the essay's logic.

Public lectures, speech meetings, and debating events supplemented print. Political parties organized speaking tours, university faculty gave public lectures, and associations hosted meetings. The result was a circuit in which arguments moved among forms: a scholar's essay would be summarized in newspapers, discussed in student societies, quoted in party speeches, and answered by critics. Readers outside Tokyo encountered these arguments through national periodicals, regional newspapers, and local discussion groups.

The state regulated this expanding public sphere. The Newspaper Law of 1909 and the Publication Law allowed authorities to ban issues, suppress sales, and prosecute writers, and censorship was applied unevenly but consequentially. A notable example came in August 1918, when the government prohibited newspaper reporting on the Rice Riots, provoking protests from the press and an incident at the Ōsaka Asahi that is discussed in Chapter 68. Newspapers also varied in their political positions: some supported party government, some the oligarchic establishment, and many sought commercial success by avoiding extremes. A historian using newspapers as evidence must therefore ask which paper, in which year, under what constraints, and for what readership.

Section 67.9

Universities, Students, Debating Societies, and Civic Education

Universities were important sites for the production and spread of political ideas. Imperial universities trained future bureaucrats, judges, and politicians, and their faculty such as Yoshino and Minobe taught students who would enter government. The University Ordinance of 1918, which took effect in 1920, recognized private institutions such as Waseda and Keiō as universities and expanded higher education, enlarging the population of students and graduates. Student societies conducted study groups, lectures, and debates on constitutional, social, and international questions.

Two organizations of December 1918 illustrate the pattern. The Reimeikai ("Dawn Society") was organized by Yoshino and the economist Fukuda Tokuzō to bring scholars and the public together in lectures supporting democratic reform. The Shinjinkai ("New Man Society"), founded by students at Tokyo Imperial University, began as a discussion group influenced by the Russian Revolution, the Rice Riots, and international currents such as postwar democratic movements, and later included students who became socialists and communists. These groups show intellectual energy and networks linking professors, students, and the public, and also the range of political directions that energy could take.

Authorities viewed student politics with ambivalence. The state wanted educated officials and technicians but feared radical ideas. The Ministry of Education and police monitored student groups, universities disciplined or expelled students in some cases, and the political police established after the High Treason Incident watched suspected radicals. Debates about academic freedom recurred, and the Peace Preservation Law of 1925 would add a statutory weapon, applied first to student groups in the Kyoto Gakuren incident that began in late 1925 (Section 67.32). The university thus served simultaneously as a site of expanded debate and of surveillance.

Civic education extended beyond universities. Teachers, journalists, and associations organized lectures on citizenship, local self-government, and political participation, while the state sponsored its own versions of civic training through schools and youth organizations, a subject developed in Section 67.19. The difference between independent civic education and state-guided education was one of the period's fault lines.

Section 67.10

Competing Visions of Democracy: Liberalism, Imperial Nationalism, Socialism, and Internationalism

Taishō political thought was diverse, and the democratic language of the era did not belong to a single tradition. Liberal constitutionalists, including Yoshino and many party politicians, sought to expand representation and responsible government within the existing constitution. They emphasized individual rights, parliamentary procedure, and the legitimacy of party competition, usually while accepting the emperor's sovereignty and the national polity as given, and often while supporting Japan's empire. Some liberal writers, including Yoshino at times, criticized specific aspects of imperial policy in Korea and China, while others justified expansion on economic or strategic grounds.

Imperial nationalists, a second broad current, valued the emperor-centered national polity and viewed party politics with suspicion as divisive and corrupt. Some nationalists nevertheless supported expanded suffrage or social reform as ways of integrating the people more closely into the nation. Others rejected the party system altogether and called for direct imperial rule or national reorganization, positions that gained force in later decades. Conservative bureaucrats and the Yamagata circle defended "transcendental" cabinets as expressions of national unity above party interest.

Socialists and other radicals, a third current, argued that political rights were inadequate without social and economic equality. Their numbers were small and the state repressed them heavily after 1910, but the Russian Revolution of 1917, the Rice Riots, and the growth of organized labor revived socialist activity, including the founding of the Japan Communist Party in 1922 as an illegal organization. Their debates among Marxists, anarchists, and social democrats, and their relationship to labor and suffrage organization, are treated in Chapter 69. For this chapter their significance is that they extended the range of democratic claims beyond party competition to questions of class and property.

Internationalism formed a fourth strand. The postwar atmosphere, the League of Nations, and the principle of national self-determination associated with Woodrow Wilson influenced discussions in Japan and its colonies. Some Japanese liberals saw international cooperation as consistent with domestic reform. Korean and Taiwanese activists cited the same principles against Japanese colonial rule, as Section 67.23 shows. These strands were not sealed from one another; individuals and organizations moved among them, and the same words could carry different meanings. The diversity explains why Taishō Democracy cannot be defined by a single program.

Division III — Hara Takashi and the Development of Party Government · Section 67.11

The Political Turning Point of 1918

By 1918 the Terauchi cabinet faced accumulating difficulties. Wartime prosperity had brought rising prices, and the cost of rice in particular rose sharply during 1918, affecting urban consumers and rural buyers alike. The government's decision to send troops to Siberia in August 1918, in cooperation with the Allied intervention in Russia, raised the demand for military rice and encouraged speculation. Between July and September, protests, petitions, and riots spread across much of the country in what are known as the Rice Riots. Chapter 68 treats their chronology, geography, social composition, and policing in detail. For the present chapter their significance is political: they exposed the government's loss of public confidence and showed a degree of popular mobilization beyond anything seen in 1913.

The government's response compounded its problems. It restricted newspaper reporting on the disturbances, provoking criticism from the press, and deployed troops and police to suppress the riots. Terauchi, who was in poor health, resigned on 21 September 1918. The genrō then faced a choice. A further non-party cabinet might invite a renewed crisis, while party leadership might alienate the army and the Yamagata circle. Saionji, who had long accepted a role for parties, and Yamagata, who was reluctant but recognized the risk of confrontation, accepted the Seiyūkai president Hara Takashi, who had patiently built the party's lower-house position and cultivated relations with the genrō.

They altered elite calculations, but the genrō chose Hara because of his political record, his willingness to cooperate with the establishment, and the absence of alternatives that could manage the lower house and avoid further unrest. A different set of choices was possible, as the cabinets of 1922–1924 would show.

Section 67.12

Hara Takashi and the Formation of a Party Cabinet

Hara Takashi (1856–1921) came from a samurai family of the Morioka domain in northeastern Japan, a former domain that had sided with the shogunate in the Boshin War, and he built a career as a journalist, diplomat, businessman, and politician rather than as a member of the Chōshū–Satsuma establishment. He joined the Seiyūkai at its founding, served as home minister in several cabinets, and became party president in 1914. He declined a peerage and, when he became prime minister, was widely called the "commoner prime minister" (heimin saishō), a title that reflected his lack of noble rank and his origin outside the dominant domain cliques. The label should not obscure that Hara was a seasoned insider with deep ties to the bureaucracy and business.

The cabinet formed on 29 September 1918 contained, with limited exceptions, Seiyūkai members. The army minister (Tanaka Giichi), the navy minister (Katō Tomosaburō), and the foreign minister (Uchida Kōsai) were nonparty figures, a compromise that respected the services' prerogatives and the traditions of foreign policy. Most other portfolios, including home affairs, finance, justice, education, communications, and railways, went to party men. This composition made it the first broadly recognized fully party-based cabinet. The qualification is important: the Ōkuma–Itagaki cabinet of 1898 had also been composed largely of party leaders, and the Seiyūkai had participated in earlier cabinets, so the 1918 cabinet was a significant advance, not the first appearance of parties in government.

Hara's governing style was pragmatic and gradualist. He accepted the constitutional framework, worked within it to expand the party's reach, and avoided open confrontation with the army and the Yamagata circle. He supported the established position on Siberia, resisted immediate universal suffrage (Section 67.13), and sought to strengthen the party through control of appointments and expenditure. Admirers regarded this as realistic management of a constrained system, while critics at the time and later saw it as accommodation of the very forces that limited parliamentary power. Hara kept a detailed diary that survives and offers historians an unusual window onto political calculation.

Section 67.13

Parliamentary Reform, Election Law, and the Expansion of the Electorate

The first election law of 1889 limited the franchise to men aged twenty-five or older who paid at least fifteen yen in direct national taxes, a qualification that excluded most of the population. A revision in 1900 reduced the qualification to ten yen and altered the districts, increasing the electorate to roughly a million, still a small fraction of the population. Hara's government revised the law in 1919, lowering the qualification to three yen and replacing the large multi-member districts with smaller, mostly single-member districts. The reduction roughly doubled the electorate to about three million men, approximately five percent of the population.

The political calculations behind the reform were mixed. The Seiyūkai's strength lay in rural and small-town constituencies where its networks of local notables could mobilize voters, and the small-district system favored candidates with strong local ties. Lowering the qualification enlarged the electorate in areas where the party's brokers were effective, while remaining far below universal suffrage. Hara opposed immediate universal suffrage on the grounds that Japan was not ready and that the social order might be endangered, and in February 1920 he dissolved the Diet when opposition parties, responding to widespread popular demonstrations for the vote, introduced a universal suffrage bill. In the election of May 1920 the Seiyūkai won a large majority. The universal suffrage movement and its organization belong to Chapter 69; the point here is that the 1919 reform expanded participation while preserving a restrictive property qualification.

Exclusions remained substantial. Women had no vote, nor did men below the tax threshold, which excluded most workers, tenant farmers, and many small shopkeepers. Residents of Taiwan and Korea elected no representatives. Okinawa first obtained House of Representatives electoral districts in 1912, covering Naha, Shuri, and selected mainland-Okinawa districts; Hokkaido first returned representatives in 1902 from the urban districts of Sapporo, Otaru, and Hakodate. Their delayed and limited incorporation, compared with older prefectures, illustrates regional inequalities within the electoral system. People of Korean and Taiwanese origin living in the metropole who met the tax qualification could in principle vote, an anomaly that underscores that the franchise followed residence and property, not colonial citizenship in any simple way.

Section 67.14

Governing through Parties: Budgets, Public Works, Patronage, and Local Networks

The Seiyūkai's political method combined national policy with local patronage. Hara's cabinet pursued what is sometimes called a positive policy, with emphasis on expanding railways, improving ports and roads, increasing education, and promoting industry and national defense. Railway construction, particularly of local lines, attracted strong local demand and became the paradigmatic example of the party's approach: a prefectural or district elite would ask the party to bring a line to its region, and the government would respond with construction funds. Higher education received increased support through the 1918 ordinances expanding universities and higher schools.

Party influence extended into administration. Hara and his allies appointed party-connected officials as prefectural governors and other local officers, and the government began reforming the county (gun) as a unit of local administration, a process that eventually led to its abolition as an administrative layer by 1926. These appointments helped the party extend its network into the localities, and critics charged that they politicized the bureaucracy. Supporters countered that bureaucratic appointments had previously served the oligarchs and that party responsibility to the electorate was preferable to appointments by unelected elites.

The result was a system that combined representative accountability with clientelism. On one hand, parties competed for votes by delivering benefits, which made them responsive to local demands in a way that the oligarchic bureaucracy had not been. On the other, patronage linked party strength to access to state funds, encouraged corruption scandals, and tied the party to local notables rather than to the unenfranchised majority. Contemporary critics used the phrase gaden insui, "drawing water to one's own rice paddy," for the practice of directing public projects toward constituencies that supported one's party. Historians continue to assess how much this practice weakened or strengthened the legitimacy of party government.

The social base of the Seiyūkai deserves attention. Its strongest support came from landowners, local notables, and small and medium business interests in provincial districts, while the Kenseikai drew more support from urban professionals, bankers, and business interests with ties to large firms. These patterns were tendencies, not rigid rules, and both parties contained diverse interests, but they help explain why the Seiyūkai could expand the electorate in 1919 with relative confidence and why urban reform movements often looked to the Kenseikai and smaller groups.

Section 67.15

Opposition Parties, the House of Peers, the Military, and the Limits of Hara's Government

Hara's government governed with a strong lower-house majority after 1920, but it remained constrained. The opposition Kenseikai criticized the government for corruption, for refusing universal suffrage, and for what it described as the party's accommodation of the establishment. The smaller Kokumintō under Inukai also attacked the Seiyūkai. Parliamentary opposition was an active feature of politics, which is itself evidence of the development of a competitive party system, but it did not threaten to unseat the government as long as the Seiyūkai held its majority.

The more serious constraints came from outside the lower house. The House of Peers, composed largely of nobles and imperial appointees, could amend or reject legislation, and Hara cultivated its leaders to avoid obstruction. The Privy Council retained its role in treaties and important ordinances. The army and navy retained their independent command rights and influence over budgets, and Hara's army and navy ministers were career officers who represented their services' interests in cabinet. Hara accepted the Siberian expedition's continuation, though it became increasingly unpopular, and he allowed defense expenditures to remain a significant share of the budget. The Yamagata circle's influence over the bureaucracy and the services persisted, though it was weakening as Yamagata aged.

Hara also confronted criticism that party government meant selfish partisanship. This accusation had a long history, and it appeared in nationalist and bureaucratic denunciations of parties as pursuing narrow interests at the nation's expense. Scandals involving party figures, whether true or exaggerated, fed this criticism. The tension between the party's role as a representative organization and its role as a distributor of patronage meant that its legitimacy rested on fragile foundations. Hara's government thus illustrates the structural position of Taishō party politics: powerful enough to govern, constrained enough that its power depended on continuing accommodation of unelected institutions.

In colonial policy, Hara's government responded to the March First Movement in Korea by appointing Admiral Saitō Makoto as governor-general and announcing a policy of so-called cultural rule, which relaxed some restrictions on publication and association while maintaining colonial control. It also began opening the possibility of civilian governors-general in Taiwan, where Den Kenjirō became the first civilian governor-general in 1919. These changes were presented as liberalization but did not alter the colonial hierarchy, a point developed in Section 67.23.

Section 67.16

The Assassination of Hara Takashi in 1921 and the Fragility of Party Rule

On the evening of 4 November 1921, as Hara walked through Tokyo Station on his way to a Seiyūkai gathering in Kyoto, a young railway switchman named Nakaoka Kon'ichi, aged eighteen, stabbed him fatally. The assassin was arrested immediately and later sentenced to life imprisonment. Accounts of his motives, drawn from his statements and the subsequent trial, emphasize his anger at perceived corruption and at the politicians and business interests he associated with the government. The killing belongs to a pattern of political violence that recurred in Japanese politics, including attacks in the early 1920s on prominent figures and the assassinations of the early 1930s discussed in Chapter 74.

Hara's death exposed the fragility of the governing arrangement he had built. The Seiyūkai depended heavily on his leadership, his personal relationships with genrō and officials, and his command of party patronage. The party had no clear successor of comparable authority, and factional rivalries within it quickly became visible. The cabinet was reorganized under Takahashi Korekiyo, Hara's finance minister, whose leadership proved short-lived (Section 67.24). The episode demonstrated that party government in 1921 rested on individuals and networks, not on an established rule requiring that cabinets be formed from the lower-house majority.

Reactions to the assassination were mixed and reveal the polarized political climate. Party leaders and many newspapers condemned the killing and mourned Hara, whose funeral was elaborate. Critics of the party noted that Hara's death elicited sympathy for the assassin among some who viewed the government as corrupt, though the extent of such sympathy is difficult to measure from surviving evidence. The event also reinforced official concern about political violence and about the potential for individuals to act against prominent political figures, a concern that shaped police policy. Historians generally interpret Hara's death as a significant setback for party government but not as its end, since the principle he had advanced remained alive and reasserted itself in 1924.

Division IV — Civic Associations and the Expansion of Political Participation · Section 67.17

Women's Civic Organization and the New Women's Association

Women's participation in public life predated Taishō. The journal Seitō ("Bluestocking"), launched in 1911 by Hiratsuka Raichō and a circle of young women writers, had challenged conventional expectations of marriage, family, and women's roles, while organizations such as the Japan Women's Christian Temperance Union, founded in 1886, had campaigned on moral reform. After the First World War, women increasingly sought a place in explicitly political discussion. They had little formal means: the Public Peace Police Law of 1900 barred women from attending political meetings and joining political associations (Section 67.18), and the franchise was closed to them.

In March 1920 Hiratsuka Raichō, Ichikawa Fusae, and Oku Mumeo founded the New Women's Association (Shin Fujin Kyōkai) in Tokyo. The association had three principal aims: to amend Article 5 of the Public Peace Police Law so that women could attend political meetings and join political associations, to revise provisions in the civil and public health law regarding marriage and venereal disease, and to promote women's social and economic position. Its core political campaign was petitioning the Diet. The organization used lectures, a journal, petition drives, and lobbying of parliamentarians, and it cultivated relationships with sympathetic members of the lower house.

These women worked as civic actors without the vote, and their practice challenges a definition of political participation limited to electoral politics. They organized, spoke publicly where permitted, published, and negotiated with male politicians who held the power to change the law. Their efforts were not unanimous: women differed on tactics and priorities, with some focusing on suffrage, some on protective legislation for working women, and some on issues of motherhood and the family. The association dissolved in late 1922, after achieving part of its goal. Ichikawa Fusae traveled to the United States in 1921 and returned to found the Women's Suffrage League in 1924, and other women organized groups such as the socialist Sekirankai in 1921.

Section 67.18

The 1922 Reform of the Public Peace Police Law and Women's Political Rights

Article 5 of the Public Peace Police Law of 1900 listed categories of persons who could not join political associations, including active military personnel, police, teachers and students at certain schools, minors, and women, and it separately prohibited women and minors from attending or sponsoring political meetings. The law had been enacted to regulate labor organization and political activity after the founding of the Seiyūkai and growth of the labor movement, and it gave the police broad powers to dissolve meetings and prohibit assemblies. For women it meant that attending a public lecture on political topics could be treated as illegal.

The New Women's Association petitioned the Diet in 1921 and 1922 to amend the article. In 1922 the Diet passed a revision removing women from the ban on attending political meetings, with effect from April of that year. The amendment did not remove women from the prohibition on joining political associations. Nor did it grant the vote or the right to stand for office. In practice it allowed women to attend and participate in political meetings and to organize meetings of their own for discussion, which expanded women's presence in public life, while formal party membership and electoral participation remained closed. Students should distinguish carefully three separate things: attending political meetings, joining political associations, and voting. The 1922 reform removed the ban on women attending and sponsoring political discussion meetings but did not confer association membership or the vote.

The amendment's significance is double. It represented a real if limited victory for organized women and a precedent for petitioning as a method of political change. It also illustrated the characteristic structure of Taishō reform: partial concessions that widened participation without changing the underlying distribution of rights. Women's suffrage campaigns continued, and the Women's Suffrage League formed in 1924 demanded the vote. The vote itself came only after 1945 (Chapter 84).

Section 67.19

Students, Youth Associations, and the Development of Organized Public Participation

Section 67.9 examined university students and debating societies. This section considers a broader landscape of youth organization. Urban university movements, concentrated in Tokyo, Kyoto, and a few other cities, attracted a small, elite group of young men and, in a few cases, women. They produced intellectual networks and radical politics, and they were closely watched by authorities. But most young Japanese did not attend university. Rural and small-town young people joined local youth associations (seinendan), community organizations that conducted evening schooling, physical training, festival preparation, and mutual aid.

The state took a strong interest in these local groups. In 1915 the Home and Education ministries issued guidance encouraging youth associations to cultivate loyalty, diligence, and community service; in 1925 the Dai Nippon Rengō Seinendan (Greater Japan Federation of Youth Associations) became a national coordinating body. These developments combined ministry guidance, local initiatives, and associations with Meiji Shrine–related national activities rather than representing a single spontaneous grassroots movement. These associations thus occupied an ambiguous place in civil society. They provided real opportunities for education, sociability, and leadership among young men in villages, and they were also instruments through which the state sought to shape young people's values. A parallel instrument was the Imperial Military Reservists' Association, founded in 1910, which organized former conscripts in communities across the country.

Other youth organizations developed outside state sponsorship, including church groups, labor-affiliated youth leagues, and political study circles. The existence of both state-guided and independent associations demonstrates the competition over the meaning of civic participation: whether it meant loyal service within an established order or a right to organize for reform. Historians therefore distinguish carefully between organizations that expanded citizens' capacity to act independently and those that mobilized citizens for officially defined purposes. Both increased the density of organized public life, but they pointed in different political directions.

Section 67.20

Buraku Liberation, the National Suiheisha, and Equality from Below

The communities now called Buraku, formerly classified under the Tokugawa status system as eta and hinin and associated with occupations such as leatherwork, butchery, and certain forms of labor deemed polluting, were legally emancipated in 1871 (Chapters 43 and 55). Legal equality did not end discrimination. Buraku people continued to face prejudice in marriage, employment, education, and residence, and they lived disproportionately in poor and segregated communities. Official responses in the late Meiji and Taishō periods included "improvement" programs that treated Buraku communities as objects of reform and often reinforced stigma, along with government and semi-official bodies in the early 1920s that promoted harmony (yūwa) between Buraku and other communities.

The National Suiheisha (Zenkoku Suiheisha, "National Levelers' Society") was founded on 3 March 1922 at a gathering in Kyoto. Its founders, who included Saikō Mankichi, Sakamoto Seiichirō, and Komai Kisaku, came from Buraku communities, many from Nara Prefecture, and some had been active in earlier improvement movements. The founding declaration, which adopted a deliberately dignified and defiant tone, rejected pity and charity from outsiders and affirmed that Buraku people would achieve liberation through their own collective efforts. The organization campaigned against discriminatory language and treatment through "denunciation" (kyūdan) meetings, at which individuals accused of discriminatory conduct were confronted publicly, an approach that provoked controversy and sometimes police intervention.

The Suiheisha grew rapidly, establishing local branches across central and western Japan, and within a few years it had thousands of members. It cooperated at times with labor and tenant movements and was debated within the broader socialist current, and it faced internal disagreements about strategy and political alignment in the later 1920s. Its significance for this chapter is twofold. It demonstrated that democratic claims could come from a marginalized community asserting rights and dignity through its own organization, and it widened the meaning of civil society beyond the educated urban middle class. Its history also shows the limits of formal equality: the 1871 edict had abolished the legal categories, but the Suiheisha's founding fifty-one years later testified to the persistence of discrimination in practice.

Section 67.21

Christian Reformers, Religious Associations, Philanthropy, and Civic Service

Voluntary organizations devoted to welfare and moral reform formed a significant part of Taishō civil society. Christian reformers, though a small minority of the population, had an influence beyond their numbers. Figures such as Abe Isoo, a Christian socialist and professor, Yamamuro Gunpei of the Salvation Army, and Kagawa Toyohiko, who worked among the poor in Kobe and later organized in labor and cooperative movements, combined religious motivation with social investigation and service. Christian schools, hospitals, settlement houses, and temperance and anti-prostitution campaigns created institutions and networks that shaped debates about poverty, family, and public morality.

Buddhist and new religious organizations also engaged in social service, building schools, orphanages, and relief programs, and some Buddhist reform movements discussed social questions. These efforts operated alongside government programs, including the district welfare commissioner (hōmen iin) system established in Osaka in 1918, which organized local volunteers to investigate and assist poor households, and the semi-official Harmonization Society (Kyōchōkai), founded in 1919 with government and business support to promote cooperation between labor and capital. These initiatives illustrate that civil society in this period included organizations of varied political character, some independent and reformist, some state-guided and conservative.

Philanthropy and social investigation had political significance. Reformers who documented poverty, urban slums, and child labor contributed evidence to debates about social legislation, which advanced slowly in the period, including factory legislation and measures such as health insurance later in the 1920s. Voluntary service also gave participants, particularly women and members of the middle class, experience in organizing and public speaking. Yet the paternalism of some charity was criticized, as Section 67.20 showed in the case of Buraku improvement programs. Historians therefore weigh the contribution of religious and philanthropic associations to civic development against their frequent assumption that the poor were objects of assistance and not participants in public life.

Section 67.22

Local Politics, Community Organizations, Farmers, and Working People

Much political participation in the Taishō period happened locally. Municipal and village assemblies, elected by restricted franchises, handled questions of taxation, schooling, roads, and public works. Local newspapers, chambers of commerce, agricultural cooperatives, neighborhood associations, and temple and shrine groups provided forums for discussion and collective action. Villagers submitted petitions to prefectural and national authorities about taxes, irrigation, and school funding, continuing practices with roots in the Tokugawa period (Chapter 44).

Farmers and working people participated in civil society through organizations that increasingly adopted modern forms. Agricultural cooperatives, established under legislation of the Meiji period, expanded in the 1910s and 1920s. Tenant farmers began organizing unions in response to rent disputes after the First World War, and industrial workers formed unions and mutual-aid associations, notably the Yūaikai founded in 1912 and its successors. These movements are best understood as parts of civil society, in which working people organized to protect their interests, communicate demands, and gain recognition. Their detailed histories, conflicts, and relationships with political parties are treated in Chapter 69, and this chapter notes only that they expanded the social range of organized participation beyond the educated and propertied classes.

Local politics also illustrates the pattern of mixed accountability. Local notables, such as landowners, village headmen, and merchants, often dominated assemblies and acted as political brokers connecting localities to parties and the state, as Section 67.14 described. At the same time, local grievances sometimes became national issues when local organizations petitioned, when newspapers took up a cause, or when parties sought votes. The relationship between social grievance and demand for public accountability thus ran through multiple channels, formal and informal, some responsive to the lowest social strata and some not.

Everyday Political Life

Attending a Public Meeting or Organizing a Local Petition

Political participation for most Japanese in this period did not mean casting a ballot. For a townsperson in the early 1920s it might mean attending a public lecture or speech meeting in a rented hall or temple, perhaps after reading an announcement in a local newspaper. Such meetings were subject to police regulation: organizers had to notify authorities, uniformed police could be present, and a meeting that police judged improper could be dissolved. A speaker had to calibrate language to avoid interruption, and audiences learned the conventions of applause, heckling, and procedural order.

Organizing a local petition involved a different set of practices. A group of residents concerned about a school budget, a railway station, irrigation rights, or a local tax would draft a statement, gather signatures or seals, and submit it to the village or town assembly, the prefectural governor, or the Diet. Success depended on connections to influential figures, the support of local newspapers, and the timing of political events. Evidence for such activity survives unevenly in local archives, newspaper reports, and police files, and historians must reconstruct experience from documents produced by those who ran meetings or monitored them. Women, who could attend political meetings legally only after 1922, and the poor, who often could not afford the time or the dress expected in public halls, were less visible in such records.

Section 67.23

Democracy across an Unequal Empire: Korea, Taiwan, and Excluded Populations

The expansion of parliamentary politics in the metropole coincided with the maintenance of a colonial order in which Koreans and Taiwanese had no representation in the Diet and lived under governors-general who held legislative and executive authority. Korea, annexed in 1910, was governed through a military-dominated administration that restricted press, assembly, and association in the 1910s. Taiwan, acquired in 1895, had a long record of colonial rule marked by suppression of resistance and measures to promote development and assimilation (Chapters 60 and 62). Politicians who defended constitutional government in Tokyo seldom extended the principle to the colonies, and some regarded imperial expansion as a mark of national success.

Colonial subjects nonetheless articulated their own political demands. In Korea, mass demonstrations began on 1 March 1919, when a group of religious and community leaders issued a declaration of independence in Seoul and crowds took to the streets there and in many other towns. The movement, influenced in part by wartime discussion of national self-determination, spread widely and was suppressed by police and military forces, with extensive arrests and deaths. Estimates of casualties differ sharply between Korean and Japanese sources and remain a subject of historical dispute, though the scale of participation and repression is well established. The movement led to the formation of a Korean provisional government in exile in Shanghai and prompted the Japanese government to revise colonial policy toward a so-called cultural rule that loosened some restrictions on publication and associations while preserving colonial hierarchy.

In Taiwan, a movement began in 1921 to petition the Imperial Diet for the creation of a Taiwanese representative assembly. The first petition, submitted on 30 January 1921, preceded the Taiwan Cultural Association, founded in October that year. The petition movement drew on Taiwanese intellectual and student networks and later gained support through the association. Its leading figures included Lin Hsien-t'ang (Rin Kendō), Lin Cheng-lu (Rin Teiroku), and Chiang Wei-shui (Shō Isui), among others familiar with Japanese debates about constitutional government. The petition campaign continued annually into the 1930s and was ultimately unsuccessful. It is notable because it adopted the language and methods of metropolitan reformers, petitioning legal institutions, to claim a political voice for colonial subjects within the empire. The colonial government's response combined limited tolerance with repression.

Other populations were similarly positioned. Okinawans and Ainu lived in prefectures governed by the metropolitan state and subject to assimilation policies, including language and education measures, yet participated in the Diet's electoral system on restricted and later terms than residents of older regions. Ainu communities in Hokkaido, subject to the Hokkaido Former Aborigines Protection Act of 1899 and to pressures of settlement and land loss, produced writers such as Chiri Yukie, whose collection of Ainu oral narratives was published in 1923, and began organizing in ways that would lead to the founding of the Hokkaido Ainu Association in 1930. Koreans resident in the metropole, whose numbers grew with labor migration, faced discrimination in employment and housing, and their experience would become starkly visible in the aftermath of the Great Kantō Earthquake (Chapter 71). In each case, the expansion of civic life in the metropole coexisted with the exclusion or subordination of other populations.

Division V — Cabinet Instability, Elite Authority, and Political Realignment, 1921–1924 · Section 67.24

Takahashi Korekiyo and the Difficult Succession after Hara

After Hara's death, the Seiyūkai chose Takahashi Korekiyo, the finance minister, as party president and prime minister in November 1921. Takahashi, a former Bank of Japan governor with a long record in finance, was an experienced administrator but lacked Hara's command of the party's networks and its factions. He inherited a cabinet whose members had been chosen by Hara and who competed for influence, and he faced tensions between party figures focused on patronage, those with ties to the bureaucracy, and those who wanted a more assertive party line.

Takahashi's government dealt with the Washington Conference of 1921–1922, which produced naval limitation and security treaties, and with continuing pressure to withdraw from Siberia. Its domestic position was weakened by disagreements within the cabinet over a reshuffle and over fiscal policy. Several ministers resigned, and Takahashi concluded that he could not restore unity. The cabinet resigned in June 1922, only about seven months after taking office. The resignation was not forced by defeat in the Diet or by a lower-house vote, but by internal division within the governing party, a fact that illustrates the fragility of party rule when leadership was uncertain.

The episode matters for the argument of the chapter. If party government had been firmly established as a rule, the Seiyūkai, as the majority party, could have selected another leader and continued. Instead the succession was settled by the genrō and the court, which turned in June 1922 to a nonparty admiral. The Seiyūkai retained its lower-house majority and continued to influence policy, but the choice of prime minister reverted to elite selection. A party cabinet had not automatically established permanent party rule.

Section 67.25

Katō Tomosaburō, Cabinet Politics, and Postwar International Cooperation

Admiral Katō Tomosaburō, who had served as navy minister under Hara and as a principal Japanese delegate at the Washington Conference, formed a cabinet in June 1922. It drew on members of the House of Peers and the bureaucracy with Seiyūkai support. The cabinet is an example of the mixed pattern of the early 1920s: led by a military man, staffed by peers and officials, and dependent on a party majority in the lower house. It gave the Seiyūkai significant influence but not leadership.

The main domestic political question of the cabinet concerned the Washington treaty system. The naval limitation treaty set ratios among the major powers' capital ships, and the related agreements on Pacific possessions and China altered Japan's strategic position. Japan's acceptance of a lower ratio than some naval officers wanted provoked disagreement within the navy between those who accepted limitation, associated with Katō, and critics who regarded it as a surrender of national security. The controversy concerned defense expenditure as well as strategy: limitation allowed the government to reduce naval spending and shift resources, while retrenchment campaigns pressed for lower military budgets. The cabinet also completed the withdrawal of Japanese troops from Siberia in the autumn of 1922, ending an intervention that had been costly and unpopular.

International cooperation thus became a domestic political question. Supporters, including many liberals and business groups, regarded cooperation with Britain and the United States as consistent with prosperity and reduced armaments. Critics in the services and among nationalists regarded the treaties as constraints imposed by foreign powers. Detailed diplomacy belongs to other chapters. Here the point is that foreign policy and defense spending were part of the struggle over who controlled state priorities. Katō Tomosaburō fell ill with cancer and died in August 1923, leaving his cabinet leaderless.

Section 67.26

The Second Yamamoto Cabinet and the Political Crisis of 1923

After Katō's death, the genrō and court selected Admiral Yamamoto Gonnohyōe, who had headed the earlier cabinet of 1913–1914, to form a government. He was still negotiating the composition of his cabinet when the Great Kantō Earthquake struck on 1 September 1923, and the cabinet took office on 2 September amid the emergency. Chapter 71 treats the disaster, the rescue and reconstruction, and the episodes of rumor and violence that followed. For this chapter, the relevant point is that the cabinet assumed extraordinary responsibilities, including martial law in Tokyo and surrounding areas, and created a reconstruction agency under Home Minister Gotō Shinpei, a planner with ambitious proposals for rebuilding Tokyo.

The cabinet contained a mixture of peers, bureaucrats, and a limited number of party figures, and it was not a party cabinet. Its reconstruction plans encountered resistance in the Diet and in the Privy Council over costs, and parties pressed for retrenchment. The government's emergency measures, including financial relief for banks, also became political issues. The cabinet had been in office only a few months when a political shock ended it.

On 27 December 1923, in what is known as the Toranomon Incident, a young man named Namba Daisuke fired at the carriage carrying Prince Regent Hirohito in Tokyo. The prince was unharmed. Namba had been influenced by radical ideas, and his attack shocked the political world, since it targeted the regent himself. The cabinet resigned in collective responsibility on 29 December, even though it had no direct involvement, and the police chief and other officials also took responsibility. The resignation illustrates a political culture in which the throne's security was a matter of the highest sensitivity, and the incident strengthened official concern about radical ideology, a concern that bore on the Peace Preservation Law of 1925 (Section 67.32).

Section 67.27

The Genrō, the Privy Council, the House of Peers, and the Authority to Choose Cabinets

Why could nonparty cabinets continue to be formed in 1922–1924 despite a decade of agitation for representative government? The answer lies in the institutional structure of cabinet selection. Under the constitution, the emperor appointed the prime minister. In practice he did so on the advice of the genrō, and as the original genrō died, the process narrowed. Yamagata Aritomo, the dominant figure of the older generation, died in February 1922, and Matsukata Masayoshi died in 1924, leaving Saionji Kinmochi as the sole genrō. Saionji, who had been Seiyūkai president and had long accepted a role for parties, became the pivotal figure whose recommendations determined the choice of successive prime ministers.

Saionji's practice evolved. In 1922 and 1923 he recommended nonparty or mixed figures, reflecting concern about party instability and the preferences of the court and the peerage. By 1924 he had come to the view that, when a cabinet fell after losing lower-house support, the leader of the majority or leading party should be asked to form the next government, a norm that later writers called the "normal way of constitutional government" (kensei no jōdō). That evolution was not automatic and depended on the political events of 1924. Other institutions also mattered. The Privy Council, led by conservative figures, could resist treaties and ordinances it considered improper, and the House of Peers, with its powerful bloc of conservative members, could block lower-house legislation. The Imperial Household Ministry and court officials also influenced the information reaching the throne.

The emperor's constitutional position changed during these years. Because the Taishō Emperor's illness made him unable to fulfill public duties, the crown prince Hirohito became regent in November 1921. The regency kept the throne's functions in operation but with an inexperienced young prince, and it placed greater weight on advisers such as the court officials and Saionji. The throne thus remained central to the legitimacy of cabinets, yet the individual on the throne played little independent role in choosing them. Historians therefore distinguish the emperor as a source of legitimacy from the actual processes that selected prime ministers, which were carried out by a small group of advisers.

Section 67.28

Kiyoura Keigo's Cabinet of Peers and the Crisis of Parliamentary Legitimacy

Following the Yamamoto cabinet's resignation, the genrō and court turned to Kiyoura Keigo, a career bureaucrat and chairman of the Privy Council, who took office on 7 January 1924. His cabinet was overwhelmingly drawn from the House of Peers, apart from the military ministers, and contemporaries called it a cabinet of peers (kizokuin naikaku). It had no party base in the lower house and had been formed without consulting the parties. Parties and the press interpreted it as a deliberate reassertion of the privileged class and an affront to the principle of representative government after the experiences of the preceding decade.

The reaction was swift. The Kenseikai under Katō Takaaki, the Seiyūkai under Takahashi Korekiyo, and the Kakushin Kurabu (Reform Club) under Inukai Tsuyoshi each denounced the cabinet, and newspapers campaigned against it. The situation was complicated by a split in the Seiyūkai: a faction led by Tokonami Takejirō broke away to support Kiyoura, while the main body under Takahashi joined the opposition. The government dissolved the lower house in January 1924 and called an election for May. The prospect of a general election, in which the three opposition groups could cooperate and the government had no organized party, shaped the next stage of the crisis.

The causal sequence is clear. Hara's assassination weakened party leadership, the cabinets of 1922–1923 demonstrated that elite appointment practices survived, and Kiyoura's appointment, perceived as the most blatant such practice in years, provided the immediate context for renewed constitutional protest. The contrast with 1912–1913 is instructive: the first movement arose from a cabinet's fall due to the military; the second arose from the deliberate formation of a nonparty cabinet in the face of an established party system.

Division VI — The Second Constitutional Movement and the Reform Settlement, 1924–1926 · Section 67.29

The Second Movement to Protect Constitutional Government

The second Movement to Protect Constitutional Government (Dai-niji Kensei Yōgo Undō) united three parties that had been competitors: the Kenseikai under Katō Takaaki, the main body of the Seiyūkai under Takahashi Korekiyo, and the Kakushin Kurabu under Inukai Tsuyoshi. Their leaders held a series of meetings in January 1924, issued joint statements denouncing the Kiyoura cabinet, and pledged to defend constitutional government by contesting the coming election cooperatively. The alliance involved difficult bargaining over candidate lists and seat allocation, since the parties competed in the same districts.

The movement's objectives combined principle and interest. On principle, the parties opposed the formation of a cabinet by peers and bureaucrats without regard to the lower house and demanded that cabinets reflect the majority of the elected house. They also advocated reforms to the House of Peers, retrenchment, and, in the case of the Kenseikai and the Kakushin Kurabu, universal suffrage. In interest terms, each party hoped to benefit from displacing the government, and the Seiyūkai's split meant the main body had strong reasons to cooperate. Historians therefore distinguish party interests from constitutional principles, noting that the two coincided in 1924 but were not identical.

The public campaign differed in character from 1913. There were large meetings and extensive press support, but the movement was more organized, party-led, and electoral in focus, and it was less marked by street disorder. Newspapers, associations, and lecturers supported the campaign, and the movement's leaders presented the election as a referendum on the question of who should form cabinets. Public enthusiasm appears to have been real, but the evidence of its extent is uneven and shaped by the partisan press.

Section 67.30

The May 1924 Election and the Principle of Party Cabinet Government

The general election of 10 May 1924 produced a decisive result for the three constitutional-protection parties. The Kenseikai emerged as the largest party in the House of Representatives, the Seiyūkai main body remained strong, and the Kakushin Kurabu held its position, so that together the three held a clear majority of the 464 seats. The Seiyū Hontō, the breakaway faction supporting Kiyoura, won a substantial bloc but not enough to sustain the government. Kiyoura resigned in early June 1924 without waiting for a vote of no confidence, accepting that he could not govern against the lower house.

Saionji then recommended Katō Takaaki, as leader of the largest party, to form a government. Katō took office on 11 June 1924 at the head of a coalition of the three parties. This sequence, in which a nonparty government yielded to an election result and the leader of the winning bloc was appointed, was the decisive step in establishing a convention. Over the following eight years, until 1932, prime ministers were typically leaders of the majority or leading party in the lower house, and cabinets changed when parties lost or gained confidence. The practice was not legally required, and it depended on the acquiescence of Saionji, the court, and other institutions, but it created a genuine norm.

Historians therefore treat 1924 as a turning point more than 1918. In 1918 a party leader formed a cabinet because the genrō chose him; in 1924 a cabinet resigned and was replaced because an election had produced a majority. The distinction is subtle but important: the second case shows the lower house's electoral verdict affecting the choice of cabinet. It also shows why the movement mattered. Its slogan was a defense of the constitution, but its effect was to extend the practical meaning of the constitution so that cabinets reflected the elected house. The change came through political practice, not through constitutional amendment, which meant that it could be reversed through political practice as well.

Section 67.31

Katō Takaaki's Government: Party Cooperation and Reform

Katō Takaaki (1860–1926) was a former diplomat who had served as foreign minister and ambassador to Britain, a business-connected politician, and the long-time leader of the Kenseikai. His coalition cabinet included Takahashi Korekiyo as minister of agriculture and commerce, Inukai Tsuyoshi as communications minister, and Shidehara Kijūrō as foreign minister, whose policy of international cooperation became known as Shidehara diplomacy. The composition reflected a bargain among three parties, with ministries distributed among them, and the cabinet's task was to manage that bargain while delivering on commitments made during the campaign.

The government pursued administrative retrenchment, including reductions in the army (the so-called Ugaki reduction of 1925, which cut four divisions while modernizing equipment), a limited reform of the House of Peers in 1925 that adjusted membership categories but left the house powerful, and a program to reduce the number of officials. It also reformed the electoral law, as the next section describes. Its management of coalition politics was delicate. The Seiyūkai, which had joined primarily to oust Kiyoura, had different priorities and disliked some Kenseikai policies, and the party's leadership changed in 1925 when Takahashi retired and General Tanaka Giichi became president, shifting the party's tone.

The relationship between reform and parliamentary confidence was central. To pass legislation, Katō had to hold his coalition together and manage the House of Peers, which could obstruct bills. Concessions to the Peers and to conservative interests were a price of enacting reforms, as the Peace Preservation Law shows. The government thus illustrates how party government operated in practice: it could accomplish reform, but each reform was shaped by bargaining with unelected bodies and by internal coalition tensions.

Section 67.32

The Reform Paradox of 1925: Broader Suffrage and the Peace Preservation Law

In March 1925 the Diet passed two statutes that together define the character of the period. The Universal Male Suffrage Law, promulgated in May, abolished the tax qualification and gave the vote to men aged twenty-five and older who met a residence requirement, with candidacy open to men thirty and older. It raised the electorate from about three million to about twelve million, around a fifth of the population. Women remained excluded, as did recipients of poor relief and certain others, and colonial subjects in Korea and Taiwan had no representation. The law culminated a campaign that had been building since before the First World War and whose organization is treated in Chapter 69.

At nearly the same time the Diet passed the Peace Preservation Law (Chian Iji Hō), promulgated in April 1925 and effective in May. It prescribed severe penalties, up to ten years of imprisonment, for those who organized or knowingly joined an association whose aim was to alter the national polity (kokutai) or to reject the system of private property. The government explained it as a measure to deal with communism and anarchism, concerns heightened by the Russian Revolution, the founding of the Japan Communist Party in 1922, the Toranomon Incident, and the establishment of diplomatic relations with the Soviet Union in 1925, which officials feared would increase radical influence. The law's definition of its targets was vague, and the phrase "national polity" lacked a precise legal meaning, which allowed broad application. It was first used against student groups in the Kyoto Gakuren incident, with arrests beginning in late 1925, and its scope and penalties were expanded in 1928 (Chapter 74).

The pairing of the statutes expresses the period's central tension. One law admitted millions of new voters to the political system, and the other created tools to police political ideas and association. Historians interpret the pairing in different ways. Some see a deliberate strategy of the governing elite: concede the vote to avoid unrest while building legal means to exclude radical challenge from the new electorate. Others emphasize that the two laws responded to different pressures and were passed by different coalitions, with the Peace Preservation Law the price paid to conservative institutions for accepting suffrage. Both readings agree that the expansion of participation and the strengthening of control were simultaneous and linked.

Section 67.33

The Final Taishō Cabinets and the Political Position Reached by 1926

The coalition began to fray after 1925. The Seiyūkai, under its new president Tanaka Giichi, left the government in the summer over disagreements about policy and political tactics, and Katō resigned in August 1925, then promptly formed a new cabinet drawn from the Kenseikai alone. This sequence, in which a prime minister resigned and was immediately reappointed to lead a single-party government, shows the convention functioning but also the volatility of coalition arrangements. The Kenseikai government had a plurality but not a stable majority and depended on negotiation with other groups.

Katō Takaaki, who had been ill, died on 28 January 1926. Saionji recommended Wakatsuki Reijirō, the Kenseikai's leader and Katō's home minister, who took office without an election, a further instance of the convention that the governing party's leader succeeded. Wakatsuki's cabinet faced mounting difficulties, including financial problems that belong to Chapter 73. The Taishō Emperor died on 25 December 1926, ending the era; the following period, the Shōwa era, began with only days left in 1926.

By the end of 1926, party government existed as a practice. The two major parties, the Kenseikai (renamed the Rikken Minseitō in 1927) and the Seiyūkai, alternated in office in ways that reflected lower-house strength. Universal male suffrage had been enacted, and the first election under it was imminent. Civic organizations had expanded, women had won the right to attend political meetings, and the Suiheisha and other movements had become part of public life. But the unelected institutions remained powerful, the military's independent command remained unchanged, the Peace Preservation Law was in force, and colonial subjects and women remained outside the electoral system. The future course was not determined: parties could consolidate their position or lose it, depending on political choices, economic conditions, and the conduct of the military, subjects treated in Chapters 73 and 74.

Division VII — The Achievements, Boundaries, and Legacy of Taishō Democracy · Section 67.34

Democracy without Full Popular Sovereignty: Constitutional and Social Limits

The preceding sections allow a synthesis of the boundaries within which Taishō politics operated. Constitutionally, the emperor remained the holder of sovereignty and the source of legitimacy, and the Meiji Constitution was never amended. The military retained its independent right of supreme command, and the General Staff and service ministers could act without cabinet direction; the requirement that service ministers be serving officers, though relaxed in 1913, remained a potential weapon. The Privy Council retained authority over treaties and ordinances, the House of Peers retained powers equal to the lower house in legislation, and the bureaucracy, though increasingly penetrated by party appointments, retained expertise and institutional continuity. The genrō and then Saionji retained the informal power to recommend prime ministers.

Socially, participation was uneven. Women could not vote, hold office, or join political parties. The poor, recipients of relief, and many in precarious circumstances were excluded or marginal. Buraku communities faced discrimination even as they organized. Koreans, Taiwanese, Okinawans, Ainu, and other populations occupied positions of colonial exclusion or incorporation on unequal terms. Police and censorship constrained speech, assembly, and association, and the Peace Preservation Law added a new legal instrument. The extension of rights in the metropole therefore coexisted with a broader structure of hierarchy within the empire.

These limits distinguish an expansion of representative institutions from a full democratization of sovereignty and rights. The Taishō changes were real: more men voted, parties gained influence over cabinets, associations proliferated, and public debate widened. But the distribution of ultimate authority had not been fundamentally transformed. It is this combination, rather than either the changes or the limits alone, that defines the period.

Historical Debate

How Democratic Was Japan under the Meiji Constitution?

The question: Historians disagree about whether the label "democracy" fairly describes Japan in the 1920s, and about how much weight to give institutional change as opposed to the persistence of constraints.

Interpretation A — A meaningful democratic transformation within constraints: Some historians emphasize that cabinets increasingly answered to the lower house, the electorate quadrupled, civic organizations flourished, and public debate widened. On this view, the change was substantial enough to justify the label, and the constraints were the normal limits of a transitional system that might have evolved further.
Interpretation B — A limited and unstable liberalization: Others stress that sovereignty remained with the emperor, that unelected bodies retained decisive powers, that women and colonial subjects were excluded, and that the Peace Preservation Law criminalized dissent. On this view, the label overstates the change, which depended on elite acquiescence and was reversible.

Evidence: Both views rely on the same facts: electoral statistics, cabinet formation, laws and ordinances, organizational records, and police and censorship practices. They differ on which facts define the system's character.

Why it matters: The interpretation shapes how historians explain what followed. If the system was meaningfully democratic, its later breakdown requires explaining a collapse. If it was fundamentally limited, later developments appear as an extension of existing constraints. Many historians combine elements of both.

Section 67.35

What Was Taishō Democracy? Achievements, Contradictions, and Historiographical Debate

Historians have interpreted the period in at least four overlapping ways. One emphasizes democratic transformation: the growth of parties, the principle of lower-house responsibility, and the expansion of civil society mark a decisive shift from oligarchic to popular politics. A second stresses partial liberalization, regarding the changes as significant but bounded, a loosening of authoritarian structures without a transfer of sovereignty. A third emphasizes elite accommodation: the genrō, bureaucrats, and military leaders accepted party government when it served their interests and could retract it when it did not, so that expanded participation was a managed concession. A fourth describes an unstable constitutional experiment whose outcome remained open and was shaped by contingency, including economic crises, political violence, and international developments.

These interpretations are not mutually exclusive, and each draws on real evidence. What changed by 1926 was considerable: cabinets formed on party lines, the electorate expanded dramatically, women and Buraku communities organized publicly, intellectuals debated constitutional government openly, and the press carried political argument to national audiences. What did not change was equally significant: the constitution, the sovereignty of the emperor, the independent military command, the unelected institutions, the colonial order, and the limits on speech and association.

The chapter should end with a caution against hindsight. The authoritarian turn of the 1930s was not inevitable from the beginning of the Taishō period. Parties were stronger in 1926 than in 1912, and contemporaries could imagine different futures. The later breakdown of parliamentary restraint (Chapter 74) reflected economic crisis, military initiative, political violence, party failings, and international pressures, and cannot be attributed to Taishō Democracy as a single cause. The period's significance lies partly in what it made possible and in the structural limits that left its achievements vulnerable.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that Taishō Democracy was a consequential but incomplete expansion of political participation. Between 1912 and 1926, parties gained influence over cabinet formation, citizens mobilized through newspapers, public meetings, and associations, intellectuals defended constitutional government, and organized groups challenged established hierarchies. These developments took place within a constitutional monarchy that preserved substantial authority for the emperor's advisers, the military, the House of Peers, the Privy Council, and the bureaucracy. The central contradiction was that representative government expanded without a corresponding transformation of sovereignty and state power.

The crisis of 1912–1913 opened the period. The army's demand for two divisions and the service-minister system brought down the Saionji cabinet, and Katsura Tarō's third cabinet, formed with the court's help, provoked the first Movement to Protect Constitutional Government under Ozaki Yukio and Inukai Tsuyoshi. Crowds, newspapers, and parliamentary opposition forced Katsura's resignation after fifty-three days. The crisis demonstrated the power of public mobilization but left the rules of cabinet selection intact, and the following cabinets of Yamamoto, Ōkuma, and Terauchi showed party influence rising without becoming a rule.

Intellectuals supplied the movement's vocabulary. Yoshino Sakuzō's 1916 essay argued for politics conducted for the people and decided according to their will, distinguishing minponshugi from popular sovereignty and thereby making democratic reform defensible under an imperial constitution. Minobe Tatsukichi's organ theory offered a legal framework treating the emperor as the highest organ of the state, supporting cabinet responsibility without challenging imperial dignity. Their arguments differed in discipline and method and met opposition from conservative interpretations, while newspapers, magazines, universities, and associations spread them. Liberal, nationalist, socialist, and internationalist currents competed to define what democracy meant.

The wartime inflation and Rice Riots of 1918 produced the political opening in which the genrō accepted Hara Takashi, who formed the first broadly recognized fully party-based cabinet. Hara expanded the electorate in 1919 by lowering the tax qualification, but rejected immediate universal suffrage and built support through railways, public works, and local patronage. His government combined representative accountability with clientelism and remained constrained by the Peers, the military, and the Yamagata circle. His assassination in 1921 revealed the dependence of party rule on personal networks.

Civil society expanded in many directions. The New Women's Association pressed for amendment of Article 5 of the Public Peace Police Law, and in 1922 women won the right to attend political meetings, though not to join political associations or to vote. The National Suiheisha, founded in 1922, asserted Buraku equality and dignity through collective action. Students, youth associations, Christian reformers, welfare organizations, farmers, workers, and local activists built networks of participation, some independent and some guided by the state. At the same time Koreans and Taiwanese, excluded from the Diet, formulated their own demands in the March First Movement and the petition campaign for a Taiwanese assembly, showing that democracy in the metropole coexisted with colonial hierarchy.

The years 1921–1924 showed that party government was not yet permanent. Takahashi's cabinet fell through party disunity, Katō Tomosaburō's and the second Yamamoto cabinets were nonparty or mixed, and the Toranomon Incident ended the latter. The genrō and court, especially Saionji, retained the decisive role in cabinet selection, and Kiyoura Keigo's cabinet of peers in January 1924 appeared to reassert elite authority. The response was the second constitutional-protection movement, uniting the Kenseikai, Seiyūkai, and Kakushin Kurabu, whose victory in May 1924 led to Katō Takaaki's coalition cabinet and the establishment of a convention that cabinets should reflect the lower house.

The reform settlement of 1925 captured the period's paradox. Universal male suffrage raised the electorate to some twelve million, while the Peace Preservation Law created powerful new restrictions on political association directed at certain ideas. Women, recipients of relief, and colonial subjects remained excluded. The coalition frayed, Katō died in January 1926, and Wakatsuki Reijirō succeeded him as the Taishō era ended in December 1926, with party government established in practice but not secured in principle.

Historians interpret these developments as democratic transformation, partial liberalization, elite accommodation, or an unstable constitutional experiment, each emphasizing different evidence. The chapter has stressed that the period's achievements were real and its limits structural, and that its eventual breakdown cannot be attributed to Taishō Democracy as a single cause or treated as inevitable. The next chapters examine protest, labor, suffrage, mass culture, and economic crisis, which shaped how this fragile settlement would fare.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a political order could change without being formally transformed. The Meiji Constitution was never amended, yet within it parties acquired a recognized claim to govern, public opinion became a force that cabinets had to reckon with, and new organizations asserted rights that the law had not granted. Understanding this process helps explain why Japan's political development cannot be told as a simple movement from authoritarianism to democracy or back.

The chapter also reveals how participation and exclusion were intertwined. The same decade that brought universal male suffrage produced the Peace Preservation Law; the same debates that defended constitutional government left colonial subjects outside the Diet. Women, Buraku communities, Koreans, Taiwanese, Ainu, and Okinawans experienced the period differently from urban male voters, and their demands, from the 1922 reform of the police law to the Suiheisha declaration and the petition campaigns, show that democratic claims could come from many directions and meet different obstacles.

Its consequences extended beyond 1926. The convention of party cabinets, the electorate of 1928, and the organizations born in these years shaped the politics of the following decade, while the unelected institutions and legal instruments preserved here set the terms under which parliamentary restraint would be challenged. The next chapters follow these threads through protest, labor, mass culture, disaster, and economic crisis, and the later chapters on militarism and war return to the question of how fragile arrangements gave way. Students who understand Taishō Democracy as a real but bounded achievement are prepared to approach those developments without either nostalgia or fatalism.

Finally, the chapter connects the archipelago to the wider world. Wilsonian ideas of self-determination, the Russian Revolution, and postwar internationalism circulated in Tokyo, Seoul, and Taipei, and shaped both reformers and the officials who feared them. Japanese democratic politics took shape within an imperial system and an international order, and its history cannot be separated from either.
