The Manchurian Incident and the Collapse of Parliamentary Restraint

Japanese History: From Jōmon Japan to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

On September 18, 1931, a small explosion near a railway line outside Mukden became the pretext for Japanese military operations across northeastern China. The attack was staged by officers who expected a military success to force the government’s hand. Within eight months the political crisis had reached Japan’s capital, where Prime Minister Inukai Tsuyoshi was assassinated.

The Manchurian Incident was not a spontaneous defensive response to a Chinese attack on a railway. The sabotage was carried out by Japanese army officers, who then presented the military expansion as protection of treaty rights and Japanese residents. The resulting campaign violated Chinese sovereignty, disrupted civilian life, and exposed serious failures in the Japanese constitutional order. Japan’s government announced a nonexpansion policy, but officers operating outside normal authority increasingly determined the actual course of events.

This chapter follows five closely related processes: the constitutional vulnerabilities of party government; the planning and execution of the Manchurian Incident; attempts by civilian and military authorities to contain expansion; the radical nationalist conspiracies and assassinations that weakened the cabinet system; and the international diplomatic crisis ending in Japan’s withdrawal notice from the League of Nations.

The subject demands a careful distinction between people and institutions. The Kwantung Army was not identical with the entire Japanese army; the army was not identical with the cabinet; and the court was not identical with nationalist extremists. Hirohito’s documented choices require examination without assuming that every officer acted on his prior instructions. Chinese and Korean residents of the northeast were not passive scenery for these Japanese institutional disputes. They had political commitments and immediate interests of their own, and they suffered consequences that differed from the fate of Japanese party politicians.

The evidence includes official Japanese military proclamations, the private papers of Harada Kumao and Saionji Kinmochi, Chinese appeals and international diplomatic correspondence, the League of Nations Lytton Report, newspaper coverage, criminal proceedings, and subsequent scholarship. Official proclamations may establish how events were justified without establishing that the justification was true. Diary descriptions may illuminate elite decision-making while leaving ordinary lives obscure. The three source windows in this chapter disclose whether an archival text or only its institutional description was examined.

The main chronology runs from the London Naval Treaty controversy of 1930 to the League withdrawal notice of March 1933, with the 1935 effective withdrawal date identified for clarity. Chapter 73 examined Japan’s economic crisis; that crisis formed part of the political atmosphere but did not cause military insubordination by itself. Chapter 75 will examine Manchukuo’s governing structures and the further transformation of the Japanese state. Chapter 74 asks a narrower and more difficult question: how could a constitutional system with elections, parties, cabinets, and official military hierarchies lose the practical ability to restrain unauthorized war and political violence?

Historical Context

At the end of the 1920s, Japanese governments depended on both formal constitutional institutions and conventions that had developed through party politics. Universal male suffrage had expanded the electorate, but imperial appointments, the Privy Council, unelected peers, and professional military leadership preserved centers of power outside direct electoral control. Chapters 67 and 69 traced the growth of this political system; this chapter examines the moment when its weaknesses became acute.

Japan had held railway and leasehold rights in southern Manchuria since the outcome of the Russo-Japanese War and subsequent arrangements with China. The South Manchuria Railway connected commercial investments, a large Japanese presence, and a military garrison responsible for specified interests. But rail and lease rights did not confer Japanese sovereignty over China’s northeastern provinces. Chinese political consolidation and competing railway construction raised tensions that Japanese officers interpreted in divergent ways.

The world depression, agricultural hardship, and criticism of elite corruption deepened dissatisfaction with political leadership, as Chapter 73 demonstrated. Nationalist organizations used this climate to demand a return to direct imperial rule or a national reconstruction under military guidance. These ideas did not enjoy unanimous support, and economic suffering by itself did not determine the choice to sabotage a railway or assassinate a prime minister. The explanation requires attention to individual decisions and the institutions that failed to punish them.

The international environment included the League of Nations, the 1928 Kellogg–Briand Pact, the Nine-Power Treaty principles concerning China, and disputes over the Washington naval order. Japan was part of these arrangements while pursuing privileged interests in China. The central dispute of 1931–1933 was therefore also about whether Japan could maintain membership in a treaty-based diplomatic system while defending the territorial results of an unauthorized military campaign.

74.1 — Party Government and the Limits of Taishō Democracy

By 1930 Japan possessed elected representatives, a competitive party system, and a public accustomed to following cabinet politics in the newspapers. Universal male suffrage, established in 1925 and first used in a general election in 1928, greatly enlarged the electorate, although women still could not vote. The Seiyūkai and the Minseitō organized campaigns, selected candidates, and competed over budgets, diplomacy, taxation, and social policy. The practice known as kensei no jōdō—an established path of constitutional government—had made leaders of major parties the usual choices for prime minister since 1924. Yet this development rested on an understanding among politicians, elder statesmen, and court advisers. It was not an article of the Meiji Constitution. The cabinet's legitimacy was formally connected to the emperor rather than to a parliamentary confidence vote, and the House of Peers remained an important unelected chamber. An electoral mandate could enlarge political influence without making the armed forces institutionally subordinate to a party majority.

Japan's constitutional setting was divided among bodies that could frustrate each other. Ministers made administrative policy, but the army and navy general staffs exercised separate authority in operational matters. The Privy Council advised the emperor on specified constitutional and state questions. The House of Peers could obstruct legislation and budgets. Within the parties, senior leaders had to manage factions, patrons, and alliances with local notables. Saionji Kinmochi, the last surviving genrō or elder statesman, played an important informal role in recommending prime ministers. His advice helped connect party politics with imperial appointment, but it did not establish a parliamentary mechanism capable of compelling every institution to obey cabinet decisions. The political system combined real party competition with older elements of imperial governance and with military chains of command whose responsibilities were not readily reconciled. This mixture helps explain why institutional crisis did not require an immediate formal suspension of the constitution.

Party leadership was under pressure even before the Manchurian Incident. The banking crisis of 1927, the world depression after 1929, and bitter debates over gold-standard policy damaged confidence in established policymakers. Rural households suffering from falling agricultural prices could view parliamentary bargaining as distant from their needs. Political scandals and accusations of dependence on financial groups gave nationalist critics material with which to attack the parties, although allegations were not all equivalent to demonstrated misconduct. Newspapers enlarged the audience for such criticisms, while universal suffrage made public opinion an important consideration for both government and opposition. The fact that parliamentary government was imperfect did not mean it lacked supporters: political parties maintained large organizations, newspapers defended constitutional practice, and some military officers also believed that political stability required respect for the established chain of authority.

It is essential to distinguish a crisis of legitimacy from an absence of politics. The electoral system created meaningful contests over national policy, and cabinet leaders could negotiate and sometimes constrain military proposals. The Hamaguchi government would demonstrate considerable political resolve in the naval treaty dispute. Yet the parties also pursued each other aggressively, occasionally lending credence to attacks on the constitutional position of rivals rather than defending the principle of party government as a whole. Nationalist groups argued that the political parties were divisive and selfish and that direct service to the emperor offered a higher form of unity. Such claims were political interventions, not neutral descriptions of public consensus. The chapter therefore treats party weakness as an outcome requiring explanation, not as proof that democratic politics was artificial or that military predominance was inevitable.

The transition from competitive elections to the end of party cabinets was neither a simple switch from democracy to dictatorship nor merely a sequence of assassinations. It involved failures of institutional cooperation, changing expectations about acceptable violence, and disputes about who could speak in the emperor's name. The key question is how elected politicians and appointed ministers responded when officers acting abroad produced crises that exceeded the authority delegated to them. Examining that response requires attention to the constitution, to the decisions of particular officials, and to the pressures created by events on the ground. The Manchurian Incident would reveal both the possibilities for restraint and the severe costs of restraint that was attempted inconsistently and not sustained.

74.2 — The Meiji Constitution, Supreme Command, and Military Autonomy

The Meiji Constitution of 1889 vested sovereignty in the emperor and treated him as the supreme commander of the army and navy. In practice the imperial prerogative of supreme command, tōsuiken, was exercised through the army and navy general staffs, which had channels of access to the throne distinct from the cabinet. The army and navy ministries, by contrast, administered budgets, personnel, and other service affairs and participated in cabinet government. This separation between military administration and operational command was no mere technicality. A civilian prime minister who wanted to halt an operation might have to negotiate with a war minister, a chief of staff, and a commander in the field, each with different loyalties and procedures. Nor did the emperor simply issue all such orders in person. Recommendations reached him through formal and informal channels, and officials often represented their own preferred policies as the fulfillment of imperial duty.

The appointment of service ministers illustrates how legal text and political practice diverged. A regulation introduced in 1900 restricted army and navy ministers to generals and admirals on the active-duty lists. In 1913 that restriction was relaxed to permit qualified officers from the reserves as well. The active-duty rule was not restored until May 1936. Nevertheless, throughout the intervening period professional officers still occupied the two ministries, and a service's refusal to cooperate in the selection of a minister could create a serious cabinet-formation crisis. The precise 1931 problem was not a statutory monopoly restricted to active-duty officers; it was that the armed services retained an entrenched institutional position and independent professional hierarchy. Confusing the later 1936 restoration with the earlier period gives the wrong account of how political leverage worked when the Manchurian Incident occurred.

Military autonomy had limits. The existence of supreme command did not confer permission upon a field officer to fabricate an attack on Japanese property or begin an unauthorized war. Operations still required orders, resources, and in relevant circumstances imperial authorization. The Kwantung Army, stationed in Manchuria to protect specific Japanese interests, belonged to a hierarchy that extended to commanders and general staff officers in Tokyo. Officers could be transferred or disciplined and their deployments could be restricted. Indeed, episodes in late 1931 show that the Tokyo General Staff sometimes tried to stop or reverse advances. The central historical problem is that rules and sanctions worked unevenly: some disobedient units received retrospective support, some attempted advances were checked, and punishment for the initial conspiracy never adequately restored the authority that had been violated.

A second problem lay in the vocabulary of constitutional loyalty. Opponents of cabinet decisions could accuse ministers of infringing the emperor's command prerogative, even when the decision concerned treaty-making or expenditure rather than battlefield orders. The emperor was thus invoked by incompatible sides. Civilian authorities could claim that obedience to imperial law required soldiers to follow lawful orders; radical officers could claim that extraordinary action embodied a higher national or imperial mission. It would be misleading to portray those competing claims as legally equal. The constitution structured the dispute, but individuals chose whether to comply with operational directives, accept military explanations, or use patriotic language to conceal deliberate insubordination. The concept of gekokujō—subordinates overriding superiors—describes the challenge to hierarchy, but should not obscure the enabling responses of superiors.

The emperor's position also requires specificity. Hirohito was not merely a decorative symbol, but neither did he personally design the railway plot at Liutiaohu. Evidence about his knowledge, preferences, orders, and subsequent assent must be distinguished from conjecture. When a force from Korea crossed into Manchuria without prior imperial sanction on September 21, for example, later approval helped regularize an accomplished fact rather than demonstrate advance authorization. When he communicated preferences after Inukai's assassination, he rejected an overtly fascist choice for prime minister. These episodes do not resolve every debate about imperial responsibility. They show why the court, the military leadership, and junior officers cannot be compressed into a single political actor, and why official sanctions after an event are not the same as advance approval of its planning.

Historical Vocabulary

Supreme Command, Ministerial Eligibility, and Gekokujō

Tōsuiken means the emperor’s prerogative of supreme command, exercised through distinct military institutions. The army and navy general staffs addressed operations, while service ministries participated in cabinet administration. The relationship among these organs was not identical to modern parliamentary civilian control.

Military ministerial eligibility: the 1900 active-duty restriction was relaxed in 1913 to permit qualified reserve officers; active-duty-only eligibility returned in 1936. Military officers nonetheless continued to hold service ministries throughout this interval.

Gekokujō describes defiance by subordinates toward superiors. Used carefully, it identifies the challenge to authorized command without excusing the senior officers and civilian officials who subsequently accepted or rewarded disobedience.

74.3 — The London Naval Treaty Controversy and the Radicalization of Nationalist Politics

The London Naval Conference of 1930 brought the naval powers back to the question of armaments that had been addressed at Washington in 1921–1922. A new treaty sought to limit categories of cruisers, destroyers, and submarines and to reduce the danger of a competitive building race. Japan's delegation, operating under Prime Minister Hamaguchi Osachi and its civilian government, accepted a compromise that fell below the preferred strength of sections of the Naval General Staff. For supporters of the treaty, restraint promised savings at a time of economic hardship and a more stable international environment. They argued that diplomacy, budgets, and the broad disposition of foreign relations properly belonged to constitutional government. Naval opponents believed the agreed ratios might endanger the ability to defend Japan and its imperial lines of communication in a possible war against the United States.

The treaty was signed in London on April 22, 1930. The following months were dominated by controversy in Japan, and the imperial ratification followed on October 2, 1930. The distinction matters: it was during the interval between signature and ratification that the political campaign against the agreement developed. Seiyūkai opponents, hard-line naval officers, and right-wing commentators argued that the government had intruded upon supreme command by agreeing to naval strength figures without accepting the full demands of the service's operational specialists. Other officials rejected that reading and maintained that treaty obligations and naval budgets could not be placed beyond responsible governmental decision-making. The argument was not a simple quarrel over a percentage. It became a test of who could determine national defense policy and what procedures counted as legitimate constitutional action.

Hamaguchi's cabinet prevailed in the immediate dispute, but victory carried political costs. Nationalists characterized the cabinet as excessively deferential to Britain and the United States, while critics accused party politicians of treating military security as a bargaining tool. The treaty controversy exposed tensions within the navy itself: not every admiral opposed limitation, and political officials and professional officers disagreed over the strategic value of cooperation. The dispute also revealed how an opposition party could invoke military autonomy to discredit a rival ministry, even when such tactics weakened the authority of future elected cabinets. Students should ask why the supreme-command argument had greater political impact in 1930 than a technical question about naval tonnage would ordinarily have produced.

On November 14, 1930, a nationalist gunman shot Hamaguchi at Tokyo Station. He survived the attack initially and attempted to continue political duties while gravely wounded, but he died in August 1931. The injury and subsequent incapacitation affected the government before the Manchurian Incident, contributing to an atmosphere in which physical violence against top officeholders was no longer inconceivable. The shooting was not the work of every opponent of the London Treaty, and legal political opposition must be distinguished from assassination. Nevertheless, public campaigns that represented constitutional ministers as traitors could help normalize a climate in which private violence claimed to defend a higher national interest. The attack demonstrated that parliamentary victory over a policy dispute was not necessarily enough to protect the people who had won it.

The legacy of the London controversy lay partly in precedent. It showed that constitutional ambiguity could be mobilized in mass politics, that professional military arguments could be made into weapons in party competition, and that nationalist organizations could target leaders personally. But the outcome did not predetermine September 1931: the treaty was ratified, the cabinet continued, and there remained officials committed to international cooperation. The historical problem is why a political system that had survived this confrontation did not subsequently impose effective penalties when an army unit crossed from argument into unauthorized operations. That difference—between sharp but lawful institutional dispute and the creation of facts by force—is central to the remainder of Chapter 74.

74.4 — Manchuria before 1931: Imperial Interests and Competing Claims to Authority

Manchuria was not an empty frontier available for Japanese strategic designs. It was a region of northeastern China with large Chinese populations, migrants from Shandong and elsewhere, Manchu communities, Korean settlers, and other groups, connected by trade, agriculture, and railway development. Japanese interests there had expanded after the Russo-Japanese War. Under arrangements transferred from Russia and subsequent agreements with China, Japan held the Kwantung Leased Territory at the southern tip of Liaodong, rights connected with the South Manchuria Railway, and a military garrison to protect specified leased and railway interests. The railway company's assets included lines, stations, workshops, enterprises, and associated urban facilities. Japanese officials interpreted the network as central to commercial security; Chinese nationalists regarded the extensive privileges as infringements upon sovereignty that should ultimately be recovered.

The railway was more than transportation infrastructure. It linked the port of Dalian through important stations such as Mukden and Changchun, supported Japanese commercial investment, and offered the Kwantung Army a corridor along which it could move troops and supplies. Yet rights along the railway were not the same as sovereignty over all Manchuria. Chinese authorities exercised jurisdiction over large areas outside the leased zones, maintained armies, administered customs and policing, and pursued their own railway projects. Competition over taxation, land acquisition, railway construction, jurisdiction, and policing produced recurring friction. Japanese arguments for the defense of a line and its employees could be broadened into claims about the security of a vast region, and it was precisely that enlargement of the asserted mission that made the corridor politically explosive.

The region's Chinese political history also matters. Zhang Zuolin had built a power base in the northeast before being assassinated in June 1928 in a bombing arranged by Kwantung Army officers. Japanese conspirators hoped to force an outcome favorable to their ambitions, but the killing damaged their position and helped reveal the risks of insubordination. Zhang's son Zhang Xueliang subsequently aligned northeastern China with the Nationalist government in Nanjing, while retaining significant regional autonomy. Chinese authorities attempted to strengthen administration and extend control over transport networks. Tokyo's diplomats often sought negotiated management of Japanese rights, but some Kwantung Army officers saw Chinese nationalism, new rail projects, and calls for recovery of privileges as evidence that Japan had to act before its position deteriorated further.

Both Japanese and Chinese perspectives were internally diverse. Japanese settlers and railway employees might fear attacks or economic insecurity, yet they did not all advocate war. Chinese merchants depended on trade that crossed political boundaries; farmers experienced military requisitions, land pressures, and changing markets; and Korean migrants had their own history of displacement, leasehold agriculture, discrimination, and political organizing. Some Korean settlers appealed to Japanese authorities for protection; others opposed Japanese imperialism and resisted attempts to speak for them. Earlier clashes, including the 1931 Wanbaoshan dispute involving Korean and Chinese farmers, were used to inflame national tensions. Such disputes had real local causes but could also be exploited by states and newspapers pursuing broader agendas.

The political and military stakes intensified with the global depression and its effects on Japanese and Chinese trade, although the depression did not force one unavoidable response. For some officers, control of the northeast promised strategic depth, resources, and freedom from foreign vulnerability. For Chinese nationalists, preserving territory and legal sovereignty was integral to state formation. The Japanese Foreign Ministry, army headquarters in Tokyo, and the Kwantung Army differed over the proper use of force. The collision between incompatible claims therefore had two levels: a cross-border dispute over China's northeast and an internal Japanese struggle over who could make a decision as consequential as initiating a major campaign. Explaining both levels prevents the crisis from appearing as an isolated explosion on an otherwise insignificant railway.

Historical Vocabulary

Manchuria, Mukden, Railway Rights, and a Fait Accompli

Manchuria is a conventional English name for China’s northeast, here referring principally to the three northeastern provinces. Mukden is the historical English rendering for Shenyang; Liutiaohu is the location of the railway explosion nearby. “Kwantung” refers to the Japanese army and leased district centered on southern Liaodong, not to a Japanese sovereign right over all of northeastern China.

A railway concession or treaty-derived administrative right is not equivalent to national sovereignty. A fait accompli is an action that creates new facts on the ground and pressures others to accept them rather than reverse them. “Manchukuo” was the state proclaimed in 1932 under Japanese military domination; its claimed independence must be evaluated against its establishment through occupation.

Section 74.5 · Division II — The Manchurian Incident and the Breakdown of Civilian Control

74.5 — The Kwantung Army, Ishiwara Kanji, Itagaki Seishirō, and the Road to Conspiracy

By 1931 the Kwantung Army had become a small but politically ambitious formation with responsibilities disproportionate to its size. Its assigned task was to guard the Kwantung Leased Territory and interests associated with the South Manchuria Railway. The force had to consider what could be done with limited manpower, an extended railway, and potential reinforcements from Japanese units stationed in Korea. Its officer corps was not uniform. Some understood security as the protection of established treaties; others believed that Japan's political and economic future required effective control over northeastern China. That distinction is essential because the conspiracy that created the Manchurian Incident was not authorized in advance by the Japanese cabinet and cannot be presented as the inevitable policy of every serving officer. Nor was it simply the private act of an isolated individual: conspirators found sympathetic audiences and opportunities within a wider military hierarchy.

Ishiwara Kanji, an operations staff officer, and Itagaki Seishirō, another influential staff officer, became central planners. Ishiwara combined military calculations with a sweeping worldview in which East Asia's geopolitical future, a transformed Japanese society, and an eventual global struggle were connected. The intellectual ambition of this scheme should not make it seem more coherent or inevitable than it was. Itagaki's organizing role, staff contacts, and attention to the immediate security situation helped translate strategic ideas into operational preparations. Their plan relied on the disparity between Japanese readiness around key railway centers and the dispersed organization of northeastern Chinese forces. Seizing Mukden rapidly could disrupt Chinese command and create an appearance of decisive success before political authorities in Tokyo had time to intervene. Such an operation required deliberate deception as well as military skill.

The choice of a railway incident as the pretext reflected the legal and propagandistic value of the railway. Japanese representatives could appeal to treaty rights and the obligation to protect property and Japanese residents; a sudden alleged attack on the line might seem to demand immediate defensive action. The planners therefore sought not merely a tactical advantage but a story that would allow expanded operations to be described as a response to Chinese aggression. Their expectations depended upon several uncertain calculations: whether Chinese troops would resist in force, whether the commander and Tokyo officers would endorse or tolerate the outcome, whether the public would accept the initial narrative, and whether foreign governments would respond decisively. In retrospect, success can obscure the considerable risks of initiating an unauthorized regional war with a comparatively small force.

The conspiracy occurred in an army already affected by disputes over reform and discipline. Some influential officers in Tokyo suspected an incident might be engineered and attempted to communicate caution. Others were reluctant to confront colleagues or wished for a stronger Japanese position in Manchuria. At the highest level, advance knowledge, knowledge of an imminent risk, and advance authorization of a particular sabotage act are different things. Historians therefore examine orders, telegrams, diaries, and later testimony to establish who knew what and when. Accounts made after Japanese conquest, or in later criminal and war-crimes proceedings, require attention to the interests and memory of witnesses. Still, the available evidence clearly identifies Japanese officers as the authors of the railway sabotage; claims that it was an established Chinese attack are part of the historical propaganda that must be analyzed.

Commander Honjō Shigeru and the staff hierarchy present another problem of responsibility. A commander might not have designed every detail of the plot yet could assume responsibility for operations once they began; officers with authority to halt deployments could instead approve new movements in the language of emergency protection. That chain of responses made a tactical deception into a large political crisis. Responsibility did not stop at the men who laid explosives. It extended to those who organized seizures, accepted false explanations, failed to investigate obvious irregularities, and ultimately ratified gains produced by disobedience. The distinction between authorship of the initial act and subsequent institutional responsibility is fundamental to any serious explanation of how parliamentary restraint broke down.

74.6 — September 18, 1931: The Liutiaohu Explosion and the Mukden Incident

Late on September 18, 1931, an explosive charge damaged a short stretch of the South Manchuria Railway near Liutiaohu, outside Mukden, now Shenyang. The damage was modest enough that a train could pass the area soon afterward. Japanese troops nevertheless began attacking Chinese positions and occupied crucial facilities around the city, while the Kwantung Army announced that Chinese soldiers had attacked the line and provoked Japanese defensive action. The gap between the small physical damage and the broad military response is an important clue to the operation's purposes. The explosion served as a manufactured justification for a campaign already being prepared. The historical question is not whether the Japanese allegation deserves equal standing with the established sabotage; it is how the allegation was constructed, circulated, believed, and converted into authority to employ force.

The immediate target included the Chinese barracks at Beidaying, a crucial part of the Northeastern Army's local position. Japanese units moved against communications, transport points, and other installations to prevent an organized counterattack and to consolidate control of the urban center. The streets of Mukden did not become an abstract chessboard: government employees encountered sudden changes of command; merchants faced interrupted trading hours and uncertain policing; soldiers confronted orders that could require withdrawal or resistance; and civilians heard contradictory accounts of gunfire and sovereignty. The Japanese army's swift successes should not be taken as evidence that Chinese society acquiesced to conquest. The choices of Chinese commanders were made in a divided political landscape in which the central Nationalist government, regional leaders, and local units disagreed about the military costs of a general war.

The army's first proclamations framed the operations as protection of Japanese lives, lawful railway interests, and public order. One proclamation preserved by the Japan Center for Asian Historical Records and associated with Kwantung Army commander Honjō Shigeru asserted that troops of China's Northeastern Army had damaged the railway and opened hostilities. This is valuable evidence of the Japanese justification issued at the time, not evidence that Chinese troops committed the sabotage. The record's provenance matters: it is an official military communication made under circumstances in which the author had a direct interest in securing public and institutional approval. Comparing that record with later investigations, testimony, and diplomatic reporting allows students to identify propaganda without dismissing the source's usefulness for understanding military intentions and administrative messaging.

The first occupation extended beyond the immediate point of sabotage. Within days Japanese forces took or advanced toward other important rail junctions and regional centers, including Changchun and Jilin. The precise order of operations mattered because control of railway lines simplified reinforcement, supply, and communication. It also helped commanders frame each additional move as necessary to protect the position taken in the previous one. The operational logic thus generated a chain of escalating commitments. An action originally described as localized defense could be redefined to require protecting a larger perimeter, and then a larger one again. Political leaders in Tokyo received information after some operations had already begun, which narrowed their options and increased the risk that refusal to support troops would be represented as abandoning them.

The incident also created an international legal problem immediately. China was a sovereign state and had not authorized Japanese forces to seize its administration; Japan's railway privileges did not confer a general right to occupy the northeast. Chinese authorities protested and sought international investigation, while Japanese diplomats faced the task of explaining actions that officials in Tokyo had not ordered. The 1928 Kellogg–Briand Pact, the League Covenant, and the Nine-Power Treaty framework were invoked during subsequent diplomatic debate. None alone supplied a simple enforcement mechanism, but together they provided grounds for questioning Japan's resort to force. The 1931 operation therefore joined military disobedience inside Japan to an international challenge to Chinese sovereignty.

As a turning point, September 18 is best understood as the successful conversion of a fabricated emergency into a political and military fact. It did not instantly give the Kwantung Army secure control of every part of Manchuria, and it did not end all central-government resistance in Tokyo. What it did was create a new baseline. From that point, officials were asked not simply whether an invasion should begin but whether troops should be withdrawn from positions already taken, with all the political risks of appearing to surrender a claimed national victory. That asymmetry is the essence of a fait accompli, and it explains why the scale and timing of the first night were as important as the explosion itself.

74.7 — Military Expansion and the Failure of the Nonexpansion Policy

Prime Minister Wakatsuki Reijirō's cabinet responded to the crisis by announcing a policy of nonexpansion. Foreign Minister Shidehara Kijūrō sought diplomatic containment, while officials in Tokyo tried to prevent a localized incident from becoming a broad war. The policy was not an empty phrase: cabinet discussions, military directives, and later attempts to halt particular movements show that some senior officials exercised authority in opposition to escalation. Yet the government faced a dangerous information and enforcement problem. Field officers described each new advance as a defensive necessity, the Army Ministry had its own institutional interests, and political leaders feared a loss of face or the collapse of the cabinet if they tried to force a humiliating withdrawal. The conflict between a stated policy and actions undertaken in its name is central to the episode.

The unauthorized movement of Japanese forces from Korea was especially revealing. On September 21 the Korea Army sent its 39th Mixed Brigade across the Yalu into Manchuria without the required prior imperial sanction. Its commander argued that the Kwantung Army needed urgent reinforcement, but a claim of military necessity did not supply the missing authorization. On September 22, the cabinet decided to cover the expenses of the operation, and sanction was subsequently obtained. This sequence must be kept precise: the troop movement was unauthorized when it began; later approval regularized an action already completed. By underwriting the crossing, central authorities helped weaken their own insistence that operations remain within established limits. At the same time, historians should avoid treating every post-hoc decision as evidence that the entire government had secretly planned the offensive.

The months after the first seizure were marked by a struggle within the Japanese military as well as between the services and the cabinet. Operations toward Qiqihar in the north and Jinzhou in the southwest were at points challenged by directives from Tokyo. General Staff officers attempted to restrain further advances, and some initiatives were checked or delayed. The existence of these efforts undermines the crude picture of a united army smoothly carrying out one master plan. But unsuccessful or temporary checks could also demonstrate to ambitious field officers that direct action and persistence might ultimately prevail. Each new move created another claim that Japanese soldiers and residents would be endangered if the army withdrew. Thus military expansion became self-reinforcing even when particular advances were contested.

Financing, logistics, and political accountability were equally important. A modern army could not conquer and hold a vast region through personal enthusiasm alone. Units required ammunition, food, rail transport, replacement personnel, and official money. A cabinet that insisted upon nonexpansion while providing support for unauthorized deployments created a contradiction that opponents and soldiers could exploit. Yet refusing funds for soldiers already in combat could also appear reckless to a population hearing reports of Japanese troops under threat. This was one way public relations shaped constitutional authority: the army's initial narrative made resistance to escalation look like indifference to national security, and officials lacking confidence in their own coalition hesitated to challenge it publicly.

Shidehara's diplomacy reflected an alternative strategy, not an absence of patriotism. It rested on preserving Japanese interests through negotiation, avoiding a major war with China, and maintaining cooperation with the treaty system that had structured international relations during the 1920s. Japanese diplomats sought local settlements and attempted to explain the situation abroad, while Chinese representatives objected that fundamental sovereignty had been violated. Both diplomacy and military force were political choices. The eventual defeat of the nonexpansion policy should not be presented as natural or necessary: it resulted from military insubordination, conflicting instructions, selective enforcement, party divisions, and the progressive acceptance of territorial gains. This distinction is essential if students are to understand how state authority can be eroded while the legal machinery of the state continues to operate.

Cause and Effect

How a Military Fait Accompli Overrode a Nonexpansion Policy

Preconditions: fragmented constitutional authority; railway claims; officers willing to act beyond instructions; a public susceptible to reports of endangered compatriots.

Trigger and mechanism: staged sabotage was reported as a Chinese attack; troops seized Mukden; the government announced nonexpansion; commanders expanded operations and described each move as necessary protection.

Institutional responses: the Korea Army crossed the Yalu without prior imperial sanction; subsequent expense authorization and regularization helped normalize the deployment. The General Staff also imposed some temporary restrictions, showing that resistance was possible but inconsistently effective.

Result: occupation created political facts that were difficult to reverse, and a government formally opposed to expansion ultimately accommodated much of its outcome. This is an explanation of choices and changing incentives, not a claim that military victory was inevitable.

74.8 — Chinese Sovereignty, Resistance, and the Human Consequences of Invasion

The Manchurian Incident occurred on Chinese territory and first altered the lives of people living under Chinese civil institutions. The Northeastern Army under Zhang Xueliang possessed far greater numbers than the Japanese troops initially available at railway centers, but its units were dispersed, equipment and training were uneven, and command decisions were complicated by the relationship between Zhang and the Nationalist government in Nanjing. Zhang's forces largely withdrew from Mukden rather than initiating a general battle, a decision sharply criticized by many Chinese contemporaries. The choice was made in the context of fears of wider war and uncertainty about the government's capacity to sustain it. It did not mean there was no Chinese resistance or that the entire population accepted Japanese rule.

Chinese resistance took different forms as the campaign spread. Local units and volunteer forces fought in areas outside the first Japanese seizures. The defense associated with Ma Zhanshan around the Nen River bridges in Heilongjiang during November 1931 became a prominent example of armed resistance, even while higher-level diplomacy pursued investigation and international support. Other groups organized boycotts of Japanese goods, protested in cities, raised relief funds, or sought publicity abroad. The Nationalist government appealed to the League of Nations and claimed rights under the international treaties to which Japan had agreed. These efforts varied in effectiveness and could be divided by Chinese political rivalries, but they demonstrate that the invaded country was an active participant in the diplomatic and military conflict.

For civilians, the conflict disrupted taxation, policing, transport, and everyday commerce. Railway stations and administrative buildings changed hands; military movements generated demands for housing, transport, or supplies; and merchants faced closures, boycotts, interrupted shipments, and uncertain property rights. Farmers feared requisitions and the movement of armies through their districts. Refugees moved away from fighting or from rumors of imminent occupation. The impact differed by location, class, and season, and surviving official reports rarely provide a complete account of how families managed these disruptions. Students should not invent a representative diary entry to make the account vivid. The historian can instead distinguish what contemporary diplomatic reports document about occupations and displaced populations from what remains poorly recorded about individual households.

The position of Korean migrants and settlers was especially complex. Korea had been annexed by Japan in 1910, and Korean farmers and laborers had moved into northeast China for many reasons, including economic hardship and colonial pressure. Japanese officials sometimes claimed intervention was needed to protect Koreans, treating them as persons subject to Japanese authority; Chinese residents could regard such claims as extensions of colonial power. At the same time, Korean independence activists operating in Manchuria opposed Japanese imperial rule, while many ordinary Korean families primarily sought secure land and livelihoods. There was no single Korean response. Chinese-Korean local disputes could be intensified by nationalist newspapers, and Chinese and Korean civilians both faced danger from changes of military control. An adequate chapter must avoid presenting Korean communities merely as instruments of Japanese policy.

The international inquiry also had limits as a source for civilian experience. Foreign diplomats and the League commission gathered testimony, traveled through parts of the contested area, and assessed the competing legal claims, but language, security, access, and diplomatic purpose affected what they could see. Japanese proclamations reveal the army's intended public explanation; Chinese appeals reveal the Nationalist government's priorities; neither automatically speaks for farm households, Chinese workers, or Korean settlers. Comparing documents from different authors is therefore more informative than treating any official narrative as a transparent window onto the street. Even casualty claims require attention to time and place rather than unexamined repetition of dramatic totals.

By foregrounding Chinese sovereignty and civilian agency, this section changes the moral and analytical scale of the chapter. The problem was not only that Japanese officers disobeyed Japanese politicians; the operations they launched invaded another country and subjected its residents to coercive military power. Parliamentary weakness inside Japan and violation of rights in China were connected but were not identical experiences. The chapter should hold both in view while reserving the detailed institutional history of Manchukuo's subsequent rule for Chapter 75. That separation allows students to understand why the first months of occupation were themselves historically consequential, before the later puppet-state apparatus was formalized.

74.9 — From Military Initiative to Territorial Control, 1931–1932

The Kwantung Army's initial hold over railway centers was not the same as durable territorial government. To transform scattered victories into control of the northeast, Japanese forces sought additional troop deployments, secured strategic junctions, and weakened Chinese authorities across a much larger geography. Operations around Qiqihar in late 1931 showed both the difficulty of expansion and the determination of field commanders to continue despite restrictions. In the southwest, movement toward Jinzhou, an important remaining Chinese administrative and military center, threatened the final substantial Nationalist position in southern Manchuria. Its fall to Japanese forces in early January 1932 marked another stage in the replacement of Chinese administrative authority with military occupation. This was a sequence of campaigns and contested decisions, not a single successful blitz in September.

The further the army advanced, the harder it became for civilian ministers to insist that Japan had only been protecting preexisting railway rights. The territory under occupation was far larger than the leased and railway zones. Political officers and military planners began discussing how an ostensibly independent state might stabilize the military achievement while avoiding the appearance of outright annexation. Propaganda emphasized regional self-government, public order, and cooperation among ethnic communities; the actual conditions of establishment were shaped by Japanese force. Distinguishing proclaimed independence from genuine self-determination is essential. Chinese sovereignty had been challenged through military operations, and an institutional facade could not erase the origin of the new authority.

The appearance of Puyi, the former Qing emperor, became central to this political project. He had lived under constrained circumstances since the fall of the Qing dynasty and was available to Japanese intermediaries as a symbol of an alternative historical legitimacy. The state proclaimed on March 1, 1932, was named Manchukuo, and Puyi was installed as chief executive, not yet as emperor; the imperial title came in 1934. In the narrative of this chapter, the proclamation matters chiefly because it made the territorial consequences of the 1931 military operations explicit. Chapter 75 should explain the governing councils, financial institutions, policing, population policies, and colonial economy that followed. Here the emphasis remains on how a field initiative acquired the apparent dignity of state-making.

Japanese foreign policy did not become entirely uniform upon proclamation. Foreign ministry officials, the army, cabinet leaders, and imperial advisers continued to debate timing, recognition, and the costs of international isolation. Inukai's cabinet, for example, did not simply welcome immediate formal recognition on every proposed term. The army's position nevertheless gained practical leverage because it controlled territory and could offer—or withhold—security for the institutions it sponsored. As the government weighed recognition, it faced a question produced by prior choices: whether refusing to acknowledge the outcome could reverse occupation or merely expose deeper divisions within Japan. There was no easy solution, but that does not remove political responsibility for deciding to legitimize arrangements created by force.

The crisis also expanded beyond Manchuria. Fighting began at Shanghai at the end of January 1932, involving Japanese forces and Chinese defenders including the Nineteenth Route Army. Its origins, geography, and political setting were not identical to the Mukden operation, and it should not be collapsed into an uninterrupted campaign stretching from Mukden to Shanghai. Yet the Shanghai conflict intensified international concern and revealed how quickly regional confrontation could spread to another major Chinese center. A negotiated ceasefire eventually ended that phase of fighting, while occupation continued in Manchuria. The contrast between a localized settlement at Shanghai and the ongoing creation of Manchukuo underscores the distinct choices and pressures involved in each theater.

By spring 1932 the government in Tokyo was confronting a new political fact: military commanders had not only ignored a policy of restraint but had helped erect a rival state structure within Chinese territory. The political consequences cannot be measured solely by conquered land. Officers learned that successful defiance could earn public prestige, resources, and a role in shaping national strategy. Civilian politicians learned that military initiatives could force decisions on a timetable they did not control. Chinese leaders learned that international protest might not secure withdrawal. These lessons affected subsequent behavior, but they did not make future outcomes inevitable; they altered the costs and perceived possibilities facing every actor after 1932.

74.10 — The March and October Incidents of 1931: Conspiracy against Parliamentary Government

Radical opposition to party government did not begin with the railway explosion. In the late 1920s and early 1930s, a loose world of nationalist societies, military study circles, and conspiratorial officers criticized political parties, large financial interests, and the diplomatic agreements associated with the Washington treaty system. Some groups advocated a Shōwa Restoration: a political transformation supposedly returning authority to the emperor by sweeping away ministers and party intermediaries. The phrase sounded traditional, but the methods proposed by its advocates—military intervention, engineered emergencies, and the installation of a reorganized state—were radical. Nationalist intellectuals, military officers, and right-wing activists disagreed over both program and tactics. Those disagreements did not prevent cooperation in plots to replace parliamentary leadership through force.

The Sakurakai, or Cherry Blossom Society, emerged in 1930 among army officers including Hashimoto Kingorō. Its members criticized parliamentary corruption and entertained plans for a restructured government led by supposedly patriotic figures. The March Incident of 1931 involved a proposed seizure of political initiative that was connected to the ambitions and maneuvers of senior army figures and civilian nationalist collaborators. The scheme was not executed as originally contemplated. Its significance lies not in a successful coup—there was none—but in the willingness of commissioned officers to plan direct intervention in domestic government. The participation, tolerance, or wavering responses of more senior personnel raised questions about whether the military hierarchy could discipline officers who invoked national salvation as the rationale for conspiracy.

The October Incident followed the Manchurian Incident rather than preceding it. Conspirators again discussed a coup against the government, this time in an atmosphere electrified by Japanese successes in Manchuria. Plans were detected or disrupted, and the anticipated overthrow did not occur. Students should not treat the failed March and October attempts as identical episodes or place them together at the start of a chronological account that otherwise moves forward month by month. March preceded September 18; October followed it. The change in circumstances mattered: the field army's example gave domestic radicals evidence that action outside normal authority could produce gains and popularity. Failure to impose severe disciplinary penalties also suggested that the costs of plotting might be tolerable.

The motives and relationships among these conspirators require care. Hashimoto's role in the Sakurakai, the participation of nationalists such as Ōkawa Shūmei in the broader planning environment, and the political calculations of senior officers have been reconstructed from memoirs, interrogations, and archival records. Some later accounts place great weight on personal ideology, others on factional ambition and institutional opportunities. A rigorous narrative should avoid attributing to all participants one consistent philosophy or assuming that every leader named in retrospective testimony approved every detail. What can be established more securely is the pattern: officers crossed from criticism of policy into preparations to use military power to alter cabinet government, and the institutions expected to prevent such action repeatedly failed to establish an effective deterrent.

These events help explain why the end of party cabinets in 1932 was more than the result of one assassination. Even where plots failed, they changed the imagined range of acceptable political action. Senior officials weighed the threat of military unrest when deciding who should govern. Junior officers saw constitutional procedures as obstacles rather than obligations. Political parties were criticized not only for particular economic or diplomatic decisions but for their very claim to organize the national will. At the same time, the failures of the 1931 plots demonstrate that authoritarian victory was not automatic. Informants, officers who resisted, and leaders who canceled or blocked plans existed. The key historical issue is why these interruptions did not lead to a durable reaffirmation of civilian and military accountability.

74.11 — The Wakatsuki Cabinet, Shidehara Diplomacy, and the Failure of Political Restraint

The second Wakatsuki cabinet inherited the Manchurian crisis in a government already strained by depression and party conflict. Wakatsuki Reijirō, a Minseitō leader, had become prime minister after Hamaguchi's deteriorating health and resignation. Foreign Minister Shidehara Kijūrō favored negotiation, the maintenance of treaties, and avoidance of an extensive military confrontation with China. Their immediate response to the September 18 events was not a formal embrace of conquest. The cabinet decided upon a policy of nonexpansion, yet lacked a unified method of ensuring that field operations followed it. Army Minister Minami Jirō and other service figures occupied positions from which they could influence military reporting and cabinet discussions, and each advance created a new argument for protecting troops already deployed. The problem was one of policy, procedure, authority, and political survival at once.

The government's difficulties were cumulative rather than purely instantaneous. Cabinet ministers faced reports of Japanese soldiers in danger and domestic pressure for firm action, while foreign representatives demanded explanations and Chinese authorities protested invasion. A minister could object to unauthorized advance but be reluctant to expose public division by ordering immediate withdrawal under threat. Some operations were indeed checked for a time by directives from Tokyo, suggesting that the constitutional center retained tools. Yet enforcement depended upon willingness to use those tools repeatedly and to attach consequences to disobedience. By making exceptions, authorizing expenditures after the fact, and accepting successive military explanations, the government progressively weakened the practical credibility of its own orders. The contrast between an announcement of nonexpansion and inability to stop expansion is the principal evidence of failure, not merely the eventual resignation of the prime minister.

Party competition made concerted resistance harder. The opposition Seiyūkai had reason to attack the Minseitō government on foreign policy and economic performance, while the ruling party was divided about how far to accommodate military pressure. Politicians discussed a cooperation cabinet that might unite major parties against the crisis. Yet the proposed arrangement raised questions over leadership, portfolios, and whether it would represent a genuine constitutional coalition or an attempt to displace the existing prime minister. Home Minister Adachi Kenzō's maneuvering and exchanges among party leaders added to the political difficulties. Saionji and court advisers had to consider both cabinet stability and the principle that party leaders should remain viable candidates for office. None of these actors controlled the military situation single-handedly.

Shidehara's diplomatic approach was often derided by nationalist critics as weakness, but a historical explanation should evaluate its aims and limits on their own terms. Diplomacy could preserve lawful Japanese commercial interests without permanently occupying Chinese cities. It could also avoid international isolation and reduce the risk of an increasingly costly war. Yet its prospects declined as troops advanced, because Chinese negotiators had little reason to accept a bargain that ratified a seizure, and foreign observers became skeptical of claims that the fighting was localized. The foreign ministry could negotiate only within the space left by military and cabinet decisions. When those decisions repeatedly rewarded faits accomplis, diplomatic leverage diminished independently of the personal skill of the foreign minister.

Wakatsuki's government fell in December 1931, after unsuccessful attempts to restore political cooperation and control the policy crisis. Its collapse should not be described as the direct mechanical result of one army order. The cabinet confronted financial trouble, party rivalry, internal disagreement, and an expanding conflict abroad. There were genuine choices by ministers and senior officers, and some narrower restraints had temporarily worked. What failed was the capacity to translate those restraints into a lasting policy supported by the chain of command. That failure provided an object lesson for ambitious officers: if an operation could produce a popular success and no effective sanction followed, government leaders might ultimately resign rather than compel the field army to retreat.

74.12 — Inukai Tsuyoshi and the Last Prewar Party Cabinet

Inukai Tsuyoshi became prime minister in December 1931 as leader of the Seiyūkai, replacing the fallen Wakatsuki government. He was an experienced parliamentarian whose career reached back to the Meiji constitutional era, and his appointment demonstrated that the convention of party-led cabinets had not yet ended. The transfer of leadership from Minseitō to Seiyūkai remained intelligible within the established political system. Inukai nevertheless took office under conditions fundamentally altered by the Manchurian Incident. Japanese troops already controlled important areas of northeastern China, military commanders were pressing outward, and the diplomatic question was no longer whether to begin an intervention but whether a civilian government could restrict one already under way.

The new cabinet took consequential economic measures, including the abandonment of gold convertibility under Finance Minister Takahashi Korekiyo, but Chapter 73 treats those policies in detail. For this chapter the important point is political: policy success in one field did not restore executive control over the army. Inukai's cabinet included War Minister Araki Sadao, whose outlook differed sharply from Shidehara's diplomacy and whose prominence signaled the growing political authority of the armed services. Senior figures in the army were divided over factional questions and methods of national reform, but the cabinet could not simply order the Kwantung Army to restore the positions of September 17. Every compromise had to be assessed against the possibility of renewed insubordination or public accusations of betrayal.

Inukai did not treat the question of Manchukuo as settled on the same terms as the Kwantung Army. He explored possibilities for a negotiated arrangement and was wary of immediate formal recognition of a new state created under Japanese occupation. Later scholarship has examined contacts and proposals that might have offered alternatives to the political form being installed in Manchuria. These initiatives were constrained by the military situation and by doubts in China about Japan's willingness to withdraw. They also reveal that the cabinet was not merely a passive mouthpiece for the field army. It contained individuals who sought room for diplomacy, even as the practical means of reversing military occupation diminished. Historical assessment should therefore avoid treating every member of the final party cabinet as either a firm constitutional resister or an enthusiastic supporter of conquest.

Opposition to Inukai within radical nationalist circles developed for reasons that went beyond any single diplomatic proposal. He embodied established party politics, negotiated government, and the authority of professional politicians—qualities that young military radicals denounced as symptoms of national decline. Attacks on politicians and financiers by other conspirators in early 1932 created an atmosphere in which assassination was increasingly discussed as a route to political transformation. For some radicals, eliminating a prime minister could open the way to direct imperial rule or a military-led reform cabinet. Their imagined political future was neither unified nor fully elaborated, but they shared hostility to the party system and to leaders who appeared to obstruct decisive action.

Inukai's premiership thus illustrates a paradox of the era. Parliamentary institutions continued to appoint prime ministers and debate policies, while the most consequential decisions of war and peace were being shifted through military action outside effective cabinet control. The collapse of the cabinet on May 15, 1932, did not follow from an ordinary parliamentary defeat. It followed from the assassination of the head of government and the resulting assessment among court and party leaders that selecting another party politician might invite further violence. The distinction matters because it explains why the end of party government was an institutional defeat even though no constitutional article was immediately repealed. A practice sustained by politics and trust could be abandoned when the costs of sustaining it came to appear intolerably high.

74.13 — The Press, Public Opinion, and the Appeal of Expansionist Nationalism

Mass circulation newspapers were crucial intermediaries between events in Manchuria and Japanese readers. Reporting from the battlefield depended heavily on military announcements, official censorship, wire services, and correspondents operating under wartime constraints. Stories of threatened Japanese property, troops defending railway lines, and rapid victories could generate patriotic excitement and sell extra editions. The army itself understood the importance of such coverage, using proclamations and press briefings to make each advance appear necessary and defensive. Yet a headline is not a transparent measurement of public opinion. Newspaper editors faced commercial competition, legal restrictions, and the challenge of verifying claims arriving from far away. The original sabotage was concealed, so an audience could support an operation while remaining unaware of the deception on which its public justification rested.

Popular support for action in Manchuria was not simply invented by officials. Economic insecurity, perceptions of international unfairness, fears for Japanese settlers, pride in military achievement, and dissatisfaction with parliamentary leaders could make expansion seem attractive. Veterans' networks, patriotic associations, public rallies, and fundraising campaigns helped express and organize sympathy. Readers could distinguish their own grievances from an officer's personal motives while nonetheless applauding the result. Others opposed war because of moral objections, concerns about trade, constitutional principle, or fear that conquest would lead to isolation. The public included women excluded from national voting, industrial workers, farmers, intellectuals, students, and communities with different political experiences. One should resist the notion that a single phrase such as 'the national mood' captures all their positions.

Press coverage also shaped how responsibility was assigned. Reporting that emphasized the supposed Chinese attack treated subsequent military action as defensive. Criticism of Shidehara or Wakatsuki could then make attempts to stop the operation look like a refusal to protect Japanese lives. Where the government withheld information or issued vague statements, the military could appear more confident and direct. This imbalance gave the field army an advantage that was political as much as tactical. Ministers had to explain caution to a public hearing daily reports of achievements, whereas officers could point to newly occupied ground as tangible proof of success. Commercial reporting and official information management worked together to produce a feedback loop that magnified the effects of initial deception.

Not all Japanese commentators accepted this framing. Constitutional liberals criticized the weakening of cabinet control; socialists and other left-wing critics drew attention to the human costs and class consequences of militarism; some religious and internationalist voices warned of further violence. Their ability to influence the debate varied, and censorship or police scrutiny often limited opportunities for open dissent. A critical reader should distinguish the existence of antiwar opinion from its power to change government policy. Official narratives are abundant partly because states and large newspapers preserved them. The documentary imbalance makes the dissent of less prominent citizens harder to reconstruct, but not historically irrelevant.

Chinese public opinion was equally diverse and had its own means of action. Anti-Japanese boycotts, student demonstrations, charitable activities, newspaper criticism, and appeals for international support were not simply reactions reported by Japanese journalists. They reflected Chinese nationalist politics, economic networks, and local experiences of foreign occupation. Japanese accounts sometimes interpreted boycotts as provocation and ignored the sovereign grievances that inspired them. Historians need to compare the perspectives of both societies rather than use Japanese newspapers as the default record of Asian opinion. Differences among Shanghai, Nanjing, Mukden, and rural Manchuria should also be recognized: the news and immediate risks encountered by urban readers were unlike those facing farmers in occupied districts.

The broader lesson concerns consent under asymmetric information. Public enthusiasm could reward military initiative, but it could also be produced through selective disclosure, sensational language, social pressure, and false premises. The relationship ran in both directions: officers cultivated support and then invoked that support as a reason governments must not retreat. This does not absolve individuals or institutions of responsibility; it explains why political leaders found it increasingly difficult to impose restraint after allowing the first military explanations to circulate unchallenged. The press did not cause the constitutional collapse by itself, but its influence helped turn military gains into a source of political authority.

Section 74.14 · Division IV — Political Assassination and the End of Party Cabinets

74.14 — The Blood-Pledge Corps and the Assassinations of 1932

The Blood-Pledge Corps, or Ketsumeidan, arose within the turbulent political environment of the Depression and the Manchurian crisis. Its organizer Inoue Nisshō combined religious language, nationalist extremism, and hostility to political and financial elites. His followers presented assassination as a means of purifying the nation and attacking people they believed had betrayed imperial and popular interests. Such claims require careful treatment. They were ideological justifications by conspirators, not evidence that the targeted officials had committed the offenses alleged against them. The organization's purpose was to replace policy debate and legal accountability with exemplary violence. Its members imagined that killing prominent figures would shock the country into a political transformation, even though they lacked a coherent and democratically authorized program for governing Japan afterward.

On February 9, 1932, former finance minister Inoue Junnosuke was murdered. He had become associated in the public mind with unpopular deflationary measures and the return to the gold standard, although attributing the depression to one individual would oversimplify Japan's economic crisis. On March 5, the Mitsui executive Dan Takuma was killed. His position in a prominent business group made him a symbol of the alleged influence of big finance over party politics. The choice of targets linked economic resentments with political terrorism: an official identified with austerity and a business leader identified with concentrated private wealth. The historical contrast is significant. Citizens could legitimately criticize economic policy and financial influence, but the conspiracy transformed such criticism into the deliberate taking of lives and rejection of institutional politics.

Contemporary police and diplomatic accounts indicate that the plan contemplated additional targets beyond the two men killed. The conspiracy should therefore be studied as an organized campaign rather than as two disconnected murders. Its members differed in status and experience; their religious and nationalist associations do not justify treating all followers of any religious tradition as violent radicals. Inoue Nisshō's interpretation of Nichiren-influenced ideas was a particular political construction. The relationship between spiritual language and political action should be explained, not merely asserted as a causal formula. Historians can identify the conspirators' statements and organizational practices while recognizing that perpetrators often presented their acts in a self-serving language of sacrifice and national salvation.

The assassinations occurred while Japan's military involvement in China was widening. Some observers interpreted the killings as a warning to politicians and financial leaders who might resist nationalist demands. Others condemned them as attacks on public order. Economic hardship helped create an audience for denunciations of elites, but hardship alone cannot explain why a minority chose murder. Political organizations, personal mentors, particular ideas, and the apparent inadequacy of earlier penalties for military insubordination all mattered. The fact that most people facing rural or urban poverty did not become assassins is as important as the radicals' claim to speak for the suffering public.

The Blood-Pledge case foreshadowed the May Fifteenth Incident without being identical to it. The network, participants, targets, and immediate plans differed. Both shared the idea that destroying an elite figure could transform the political order, and both challenged the authority of courts, cabinets, and ordinary debate. The two cases should be connected through the larger political climate and patterns of extremist organization rather than conflated into a single centrally controlled plot. Their cumulative significance was the normalization of the possibility that political leaders and businessmen might be killed not for private disputes but to advance a program of national transformation. The government's ability to protect public officials and punish politically motivated violence had become a test of constitutional endurance.

Section 74.15 · Division IV — Political Assassination and the End of Party Cabinets

74.15 — The May Fifteenth Incident: The Assassination of Inukai Tsuyoshi

On May 15, 1932, groups of young naval officers, aided by associates from other military and civilian circles, carried out coordinated attacks in Tokyo. A party attacked the prime minister's official residence and murdered Inukai Tsuyoshi. Other groups targeted facilities associated with political and financial authority, including the Seiyūkai headquarters and the Bank of Japan, with the broader aim of destabilizing the existing order. The attack was not a constitutional transfer of power or a successful seizure of the whole state. Its principal immediate outcome was the death of a sitting prime minister and the creation of intense uncertainty about the next government. The conspirators' inability to install a replacement regime should not obscure the gravity of the result: a democratically connected cabinet had been shattered by political assassination.

The young officers portrayed themselves as agents of national renewal against party corruption, foreign dependence, and the influence of concentrated wealth. Their statements were a claim to legitimacy, not a mandate granted by the population. The participants did not share one detailed plan for government after the murders. Some anticipated a cabinet of national unity or an extraordinary imperial intervention, but their political hopes were much less specific than their willingness to kill. Popular retellings sometimes embellish Inukai's final exchanges with the attackers; such anecdotes should not be presented as verbatim dialogue unless supported by a contemporary, reliable record. The historical fact that matters is the murder itself and the conspiracy's deliberate use of violence against the person occupying the highest political office.

The composition of the conspiracy shows that radicalism was not confined to one service or one generation of politicians. Young naval officers acted with other sympathizers, and their networks linked military grievances to civilian nationalist circles. Yet the navy as an institution should not be equated with the assassins, any more than the whole army should be equated with the Liutiaohu plotters. Senior officers, court officials, party leaders, and journalists had varied reactions. Some publicly condemned the killing, while others showed sympathy for the motives of young men who claimed moral sacrifice. A careful account separates the perpetrators, their supporters, officials who tolerated or inadequately punished them, and institutions seeking to maintain order.

The political effects were immediate. Inukai's assassination prompted a search for a new prime minister in which the survival of the party-cabinet convention could no longer be assumed. Party leaders could still propose successors, and the constitution and Diet remained in force, but fear of another attack became a practical factor in determining who could govern. A politician selected purely because of parliamentary party position might attract nationalist hostility, while a nonparty figure with military credentials might seem better able to calm the crisis. This logic allowed violence to influence political succession even though the assassins had not overthrown the state machinery. It is an example of how terror can weaken representative government indirectly, by narrowing the choices judged politically safe or viable.

The May Fifteenth Incident should be placed alongside, but distinguished from, the Blood-Pledge murders of February and March. All three killings reflected attacks upon the established political and economic elite; the assassination of the prime minister carried a particular constitutional consequence. The end of a sequence of party-led cabinets did not mean the parties vanished, voters lost the franchise overnight, or the army controlled every ministry from the next morning. It meant that an important convention, sustained since 1924, no longer determined the selection of prime ministers. Chapter 75 will trace subsequent changes in the state; this chapter explains the pivotal event that made the retreat from regular party government unmistakable.

74.16 — Trials, Public Sympathy, and the Limits of Accountability

The trials of the May Fifteenth participants and the Blood-Pledge conspirators provide evidence of how political violence was interpreted after the crimes. Prosecutors had to establish individual responsibility, differentiate defendants, and decide which court or military tribunal had jurisdiction. The public debate concerned more than guilt. Defendants and sympathizers represented their motives as patriotic and their intended victims as corrupt or unworthy, while critics warned that treating murder as national devotion would encourage further attacks. Courts operated inside this contested political climate, although legal outcomes should be analyzed case by case rather than by claiming that all judges or institutions consciously endorsed the violence. The existence of criminal proceedings did not automatically guarantee an effective public repudiation of political terrorism.

Contemporary diplomatic documentation gives useful precision. In a dispatch dated November 15, 1933, U.S. ambassador Joseph C. Grew reported sentences announced on November 9 in the naval court-martial of ten sublieutenants involved in the May Fifteenth actions. Two received fifteen-year sentences without labor; one thirteen years; three ten years; three two years; and one one year. The four men in the last two groups received stays of execution permitting conditional liberty. These details are more informative than the general claim that defendants were 'lightly punished,' though the broader point about comparatively limited sanctions remains important. Other defendants and related conspiracies faced distinct proceedings and penalties; a single sentence range cannot describe every case.

Petitions for mercy and public expressions of sympathy formed part of the political meaning of the trials. Supporters argued that the defendants had acted from sincere concern for the emperor or for ordinary people suffering economic hardship. Sincerity, however, is not a defense against murder, and romanticizing the conspirators' sacrifice would reproduce their own political argument rather than analyze it. Public enthusiasm was uneven and cannot be measured solely from the volume of petitions. A society can contain many opponents of violence even when the loudest, most organized groups flood institutions with letters and demonstrations. Historians must therefore distinguish between the organized campaign for clemency, broader cultural acceptance of nationalist rhetoric, and the opinions of people who left no written record.

The proceedings also raise questions about military professional discipline. Soldiers and officers serving under oath had taken part in attacks on the civil leadership of the state. The military's claim to unique patriotic authority was undermined when service members adopted personal violence as a political method. Senior military leaders who treated the incidents as expressions of misguided idealism rather than as fundamental attacks on lawful command risked encouraging future insubordination. But the courts were not the only relevant institutions: personnel appointments, transfers, promotions, public statements, and the political protection of radicals also shaped incentives. Assessing accountability means looking at how states respond before plots succeed as well as after defendants are brought to trial.

These cases became warnings rather than a simple set of precedents. Later officers and nationalist activists could see that politically motivated violence might yield publicity, public sympathy, and limited punishment; opponents could see that parliamentary leaders faced threats even in the presence of formal legal institutions. Nonetheless, subsequent violence was not preordained by the 1933 sentences. The effect depended on how each new generation of officers interpreted the cases, on the response of superiors, and on the willingness of political leaders to defend constitutional procedures. The trials illuminate a broader institutional failure: the Japanese state punished certain individuals while struggling to discredit the claim that acts of unlawful violence could carry a higher patriotic legitimacy than obedience to the government.

74.17 — The Saitō Makoto Cabinet and the End of the Party-Cabinet System

After Inukai's assassination, court advisers and political leaders had to choose a successor in circumstances of extraordinary intimidation. Saionji Kinmochi, the remaining elder statesman, was central to the established process of recommending a prime minister. His preference mattered, but he could not simply recreate the political climate of the preceding decade. Party leaders sought continued recognition of parliamentary convention, while other advisers argued that a nonparty figure might ease the crisis and gain military cooperation. The selection process is therefore a revealing moment at which formal constitutional institutions remained functioning but the customary connection between election results and executive leadership weakened. Violence had not abolished the parties; it had made their claim to govern appear less secure to those responsible for appointments.

The imperial court's position was more nuanced than a portrayal of blanket support for radical militarism suggests. A May 19, 1932 memorandum preserved among the Harada Kumao Papers at the National Diet Library records Grand Chamberlain Suzuki Kantarō communicating Hirohito's wishes to Saionji. According to the library's description, the emperor wanted a person of sound character and expressly rejected a candidate with fascist leanings; he did not insist that the cabinet be drawn from one party or from a formal coalition. This evidence speaks to a documented intervention in the appointment process. It should not be stretched into proof that Hirohito was powerless, that all his preferences were followed at every stage, or that his position in May 1932 explains every subsequent imperial decision. It establishes one specific distinction between the emperor's recorded preferences and the views of some political radicals.

On May 22 Saionji recommended Admiral Saitō Makoto, and Saitō formed a cabinet on May 26, 1932. Saitō had a distinguished naval and administrative career and was not a leader of the parliamentary majority party. His government combined bureaucrats and public figures with representatives of the major political parties, a formula commonly described as national unity. That composition matters. Party members continued to hold office and influence legislative affairs, yet the premiership no longer followed the convention by which leaders of the chief parliamentary parties alternated in government. The official logic of unity during crisis displaced the norm of government through party leadership. The result was not a formally enacted abolition of the party system; it was the abandonment of a practice on which much of Taishō constitutional politics had depended.

The selection also shows the limits of simple stories about a military dictatorship beginning in May 1932. The emperor and Saionji did not appoint one of the assassins or openly proclaim an end to all representative politics. The Diet remained a legislative institution, elections continued, parties retained organizations, and cabinets still had to negotiate financial and administrative policy. Civilian bureaucrats and diplomats preserved influence in many fields. At the same time, the new political pattern gave greater weight to military acceptability and to elites outside party competition. The problem was a shift in effective power before a complete transformation of formal institutions. Students should distinguish the end of party premierships from the later abolition or forced merger of party organizations in 1940.

The Inukai assassination and the Saitō appointment were therefore connected without being identical events. One was an unlawful killing by conspirators; the other was an institutional choice made by surviving authorities under pressure. Responsibility for the first rests with those who organized and carried out the attack and those who enabled its conditions. Responsibility for the second involves the deliberations of Saionji, court officials, party leaders, and senior military figures, who judged the available paths differently. The distinction is not merely legal. It preserves the possibility that events could have unfolded otherwise. Studying the choices made under pressure is how historians explain why the political order changed so significantly while much of its outward constitutional framework remained intact.

Section 74.18 · Division V — International Consequences, Constitutional Breakdown, and Review

74.18 — The League of Nations, the Lytton Commission, and Japan's Diplomatic Isolation

China's appeal to the League of Nations brought the Manchurian Incident before an international institution whose purpose included the peaceful settlement of disputes. Chinese representatives argued that Japanese forces had occupied Chinese territory in violation of sovereignty and treaty commitments. Japanese representatives responded that their government possessed legitimate rights and obligations connected with the railway and Japanese residents and that troops had acted defensively. These were not symmetrical accounts of the origins of the conflict: the initial railway sabotage was Japanese, not Chinese. Yet international diplomacy required governments to assess claims in detail, because Japan did have established commercial and treaty interests that a settlement would have to address without legitimizing armed occupation. The distinction between legitimate prior interests and unlawful methods for enlarging them became a central question for international investigators.

The League appointed a commission chaired by the Earl of Lytton, with representatives from several countries. Its members gathered documentary testimony, interviewed officials, and traveled in China and Japan during 1932, including to Manchuria. The commission operated in a region where Japanese troops already controlled major centers and where Manchukuo had been proclaimed before the investigation was completed. That circumstance limited both access and the practical consequences of findings. The report, completed and circulated in the autumn of 1932, examined the historical background, administrative and railway disputes, military events, Chinese nationalism, and the new state's origins. Its conclusion did not simply deny all Japanese grievances. It recognized complex problems in the northeast while rejecting the claim that the Japanese campaign could be regarded as a justifiable act of self-defense and questioning the supposed independent popular origins of Manchukuo.

Japan formalized its recognition of Manchukuo through a bilateral protocol signed on September 15, 1932. The document acknowledged Manchukuo as an independent state, affirmed Japan’s treaty-related interests, and provided for continuing Japanese military cooperation and stationing of forces. Its language about the free will of inhabitants was a diplomatic claim by governments involved in the arrangement, not proof that Chinese and other local populations had freely consented to the loss of Chinese sovereignty. The protocol is significant evidence that the military-created territorial situation had become formal Japanese state policy before the League issued its final judgment. The distinction between March proclamation and September recognition also helps explain why international proposals for reversal encountered such entrenched opposition.

The report's value as a primary source rests on both its evidence and its limitations. It was an international diplomatic inquiry rather than a judicial judgment issued after full adversarial trials, and the participants worked under the constraints of the time and of the League's political rules. The investigators considered testimony supplied by interested governments, whose accounts had to be weighed critically. The maps and observations included in the commission's published report are particularly useful for examining how railway geography and troop movements affected political claims. The commission's recommendations sought a settlement that recognized China's sovereignty while dealing with Japanese interests. Students should distinguish the report's descriptions, its findings about military responsibility, and its proposals for future diplomatic arrangements.

The United States, which was not a League member, articulated a related but separate position. On January 7, 1932, Secretary of State Henry Stimson instructed that notes be sent to Japan and China rejecting recognition of situations or agreements brought about through means contrary to international obligations. The Stimson Doctrine emphasized treaty rights, China's independence and territorial integrity, and the principle that conquest should not automatically create legal title. It did not constitute a commitment to send American troops to Manchuria or to impose comprehensive sanctions. Indeed, U.S. leaders opposed military intervention and were cautious about coercive economic measures. The gap between moral or legal nonrecognition and the means of compelling withdrawal is a major reason the international response failed to reverse Japanese gains.

Japan's international position deteriorated as the army and government became more closely associated with Manchukuo. Japanese diplomats and information officials nevertheless continued to seek foreign sympathy and to present their country as responding to Chinese disorder. The diplomatic struggle was not wholly predetermined, and Western governments differed in their priorities. Their commercial interests, domestic economic troubles, commitment to collective security, and fear of larger conflict affected their decisions. China used international institutions despite doubts that they could enforce a settlement, because diplomacy could establish a record of violated sovereignty and mobilize opinion. The Manchurian question thus became a test of whether a rule-based international order could restrain a power that refused to relinquish territory conquered by force.

The most important consequence was not simply loss of reputation. International criticism exposed the contradiction between Japan's membership in diplomatic institutions and the territorial arrangements it was developing through military coercion. At home, nationalist organizations treated criticism as proof of foreign hostility, while some Japanese diplomats feared isolation and argued that continued engagement served national interests. The conflict therefore returned to Japan's domestic constitutional question: could civilian institutions choose compromise with the international system if the military and its supporters treated territorial retreat as intolerable? The League investigation made that dilemma explicit but could not solve it, preparing the confrontation that culminated in Japan's 1933 withdrawal notice.

74.19 — Withdrawal from the League and the Failure of Parliamentary Restraint

On February 24, 1933, the League Assembly adopted the report and recommendations concerning the Manchurian dispute, rejecting recognition of the territorial order created by Japan's armed intervention. The Japanese delegation, led by Matsuoka Yōsuke, rejected the result and departed from the assembly. Japan sent formal notice of its withdrawal on March 27, 1933. Under the League's rules the notification did not terminate membership immediately; the withdrawal became effective two years later, on March 27, 1935. This chronology is more than pedantic. The decision to announce departure in 1933 was a political act, while the later date marked its legal completion. Treating both as the same event obscures the ways international obligations and symbolic declarations can move on different timetables.

The diplomatic outcome did not arise only from the resolution of February 1933. For more than a year, military occupation, Japanese political accommodation, Chinese protest, foreign investigation, and public argument had transformed the dispute. Japanese officials now faced a choice between accepting international recommendations that might require retreat from established positions and rejecting an organization that challenged the legitimacy of Manchukuo. The government chose the latter path, an important victory for those who argued that Japan could not allow foreigners to determine its continental position. Yet the League was not an invading army, and the recommendations did not erase every legitimate Japanese commercial interest. It was the incompatibility between a settlement respecting Chinese sovereignty and the existing military-backed territorial project that made compromise so difficult.

At home, the withdrawal was presented by many nationalist commentators as a declaration of independence from hostile foreign opinion. The appeal of that claim was strengthened by political and economic grievances and by the military's accumulated prestige. Other Japanese voices warned that diplomatic isolation would reduce influence over security arrangements and international trade. Neither group could know the exact course of the later war, and historians should not write backwards as though leaving the League in 1933 already made the events of 1941 unavoidable. The withdrawal did, however, weaken one channel through which Japanese leaders had negotiated regional disputes and signaled an increasing willingness to defend gains obtained by force rather than accept outside adjudication.

The state at the end of this chapter remained formally complex. The Meiji Constitution continued to operate, and the Diet and parties continued to exist. Cabinets still required financial legislation and administrative cooperation, senior military figures often disagreed with each other, and the emperor's role varied with the decision under consideration. Nonetheless, the practical relationship between political responsibility and military force had changed. Field officers had staged an incident and occupied territory; some Tokyo authorities had failed to punish them and had funded or approved their actions; nationalist killers had removed political leaders; and a nonparty cabinet had succeeded the last premier drawn from the regular party sequence. These processes did not abolish constitutional forms, but they made the forms less effective at controlling the instruments of violence.

The Manchurian Incident and the domestic assassinations also affected different people in unequal ways. Chinese residents and soldiers bore the immediate costs of foreign invasion, and their country lost control of much of its northeastern territory. Koreans in Manchuria faced pressures of occupation and competing national claims. Japanese politicians experienced intimidation, institutional displacement, and sometimes murder. Ordinary Japanese voters retained formal electoral rights but found their ability to influence foreign and military policy narrowing. These changes must be analyzed together without reducing one society's suffering to another's constitutional crisis. A chapter about Japan's political breakdown would be incomplete if it treated the conquest of Manchuria only as background to cabinet negotiations.

The transition to Chapter 75 begins with a political settlement built on these altered relationships. Manchukuo's new institutions, the growth of the Kwantung Army's authority, the remaking of Japan's domestic political order, and subsequent repression require a separate, detailed treatment. Chapter 74 has established how that settlement became possible: not through one all-powerful conspiracy directing every actor, but through a sequence of calculated initiatives, institutional divisions, accommodations, failed sanctions, and choices made under pressure. The collapse of parliamentary restraint was therefore historically contingent and deeply consequential. The question students should carry forward is how institutions that had failed to check unauthorized power later adapted to, legitimized, or resisted its continued expansion.

74.20 — From Parliamentary Crisis to Militarized Authoritarianism: Synthesis and Historical Thinking Checkpoint

The history from 1930 to 1933 reveals a widening distance between the appearance of constitutional government and the power to control events. Elections, political parties, and cabinet procedures survived many years after the initial Manchurian intervention. Even in September 1931, civilian ministers and some army leaders attempted to contain military action. What changed was the reliability of those mechanisms. Once a staged attack had produced a territorial occupation, officials faced new facts that could be defended through claims of national security, troop protection, and patriotic sacrifice. Each acceptance of unauthorized action made the next challenge to authority more difficult to resist.

Constitutional structures created vulnerabilities but did not automatically cause the collapse. Separate military chains of command, imperial prerogatives, the absence of a fully parliamentary cabinet system, and the limited power of elected representatives all mattered. Yet concrete decisions mattered too: the army officers who engineered the railway explosion, the commanders who ordered deployments, the ministers who tolerated retrospective approval, the parties that placed rivalry ahead of collective defense of civilian authority, and the conspirators who murdered political and financial leaders. The constitutional system did not command any of these actors to commit sabotage or assassination. The historical explanation must therefore combine institutional constraints with human responsibility.

The same period can be interpreted from different national and social positions. Japanese constitutional politicians experienced military insubordination and the murder of a prime minister as failures of state authority; Chinese residents experienced invasion and the loss of sovereign administration; Korean settlers in the northeast faced contested allegiances and pressures generated by imperial power; and international diplomats saw a challenge to treaty-based mechanisms for peaceful settlement. Studying these perspectives reveals why a narrative focused exclusively on Japanese party politics would be inadequate. The crisis was simultaneously domestic, imperial, and international, and its consequences were distributed unevenly.

Students should also resist three misleading conclusions. First, the army was not a single actor uniformly favoring every advance; some officers attempted to impose limits, even if those efforts ultimately failed. Second, the emperor was neither an invisible spectator nor necessarily the personal planner of each incident; his documented instructions and approvals must be assessed at their particular dates and contexts. Third, the end of party cabinets in May 1932 was not the instantaneous establishment of a one-party dictatorship. The decisive change was the loss of a political convention that linked electoral party leadership to the selection of prime ministers, accompanied by an expanded role for military acceptability in political decisions.

The evidentiary record contains both unusually direct testimony and important silences. A Kwantung Army proclamation documents how aggression was publicly justified, but not the truth of its accusations. Harada Kumao's record illuminates the perceptions of court advisers, not the experiences of Chinese civilians. The Lytton Report provides broad international investigation, while the record of courts and clemency petitions reveals both legal accountability and sympathy for assassins. Students should compare the purpose and audience of each document, separate contemporary claims from later historical findings, and identify people whose experiences are least visible in official archives.

The chapter's central judgment is that parliamentary restraint failed by degrees: through an unconstitutional or unauthorized military initiative; incomplete attempts to enforce control; accommodation of the results; violent attacks on political leadership; and diplomatic rejection of externally proposed limits. This was neither a natural law of imperial Japanese politics nor an accidental outcome that no one could influence. It was a sequence of choices within institutions that possessed formal powers but increasingly lacked the will, unity, or capacity to use them effectively. Chapter 75 will examine how the balance reached by 1933 shaped the administration of Manchukuo and the subsequent militarization of the Japanese state.

Historical Debate

Why Did Parliamentary Restraint Fail?

Interpretation A emphasizes structure: direct access to the emperor, divided command and cabinet powers, and the limited constitutional status of party government made effective restraint difficult. Interpretation B emphasizes contingent decisions: officers staged the incident, superiors failed to discipline them, ministers accepted faits accomplis, and parties could not unite against violence. Continuity and Change Over Time

Constitutional Politics Before and After May 1932

Continuities: the Meiji Constitution remained in force; the Diet, parties, electoral processes, ministries, and judicial institutions continued; many administrators were still civilians.

Changes: leaders of parliamentary majority parties no longer regularly supplied the prime minister; military acceptability gained importance in appointments; foreign military initiatives increasingly predetermined cabinet choices; political terror acquired a potent public language of patriotic legitimacy.

Limits of the comparison: the 1932 end of the party-cabinet sequence must not be confused with the 1940 dissolution or merger of party organizations. Explain how institutions can survive in form while losing effective control over decisive powers.

Chapter Summary

In the late 1920s Japanese parliamentary government had developed substantial political practices but remained constitutionally insecure. Party leaders routinely became prime ministers, and universal male suffrage made electoral opinion important. Yet responsibility for military operations did not follow the same chain as civilian cabinet decision-making. Separate general staffs, direct imperial command prerogatives, and dependence on military cooperation created structural vulnerabilities, which the London Naval Treaty controversy placed under pressure in 1930.

The 1930 naval agreement was signed in April and ratified in October. Opponents used accusations of interference with supreme command to discredit the Hamaguchi cabinet even though the government ultimately secured ratification. Hamaguchi was shot that November and died in 1931. The controversy showed that a legal governmental victory could coexist with a political climate of intimidation and allegations that ministers lacked patriotic legitimacy.

Manchuria was Chinese territory in which Japan held significant railway, lease, and commercial rights. Such interests did not authorize the seizure of Chinese administration. Kwantung Army officers including Ishiwara Kanji and Itagaki Seishirō arranged the small railway explosion near Liutiaohu on September 18, 1931. The army falsely blamed Chinese troops and rapidly occupied Mukden and other railway centers. Contemporary proclamations reveal the military justification; subsequent evidence establishes the Japanese origins of the sabotage.

Wakatsuki and Shidehara sought to contain the conflict. Nonexpansion was a genuine announced policy, and military authorities in Tokyo at times attempted to restrict individual advances. Nevertheless, the unauthorized September 21 movement of Korea Army troops across the Yalu and its later regularization showed how acts already undertaken could place pressure on cabinet decisions. Successive tactical gains were represented as protection of earlier gains. By accepting some of their consequences, leaders weakened the credibility of limits they continued to proclaim.

Chinese authorities, soldiers, and civilians confronted an invasion of their homeland, not simply a dispute about Japanese cabinet discipline. Zhang Xueliang’s northeastern forces largely withdrew from some early positions, but other Chinese units and volunteers resisted. Local residents faced disrupted administration, commerce, and security. Korean settlers and activists had differing responses shaped by the experience of Japanese colonial rule. China pursued diplomatic remedies through the League and international treaty systems even as the occupation widened.

Radical plots against government also escalated inside Japan. The Sakurakai’s March and October 1931 conspiracies failed but exposed inadequate discipline. The Blood-Pledge Corps murdered Inoue Junnosuke and Dan Takuma in early 1932. Young naval officers and associates assassinated Prime Minister Inukai on May 15. Subsequent court proceedings punished some perpetrators while attracting organized public sympathy and raising troubling questions about the deterrent effects of sentences and elite responses.

The selection of Admiral Saitō Makoto in May 1932 ended the prewar practice of choosing prime ministers from the major political parties. Harada’s records and the National Diet Library’s account of the May 19 memorandum reveal a documented imperial preference against a fascist-leaning candidate. This does not settle the larger debate about Hirohito’s responsibility. The constitution, Diet, elections, and parties continued; the politically decisive loss was the effectiveness of the party-cabinet convention and the strengthened expectation that government leadership needed military acceptance.

The League of Nations investigated the Manchurian crisis through the Lytton Commission, and the United States proclaimed nonrecognition of territorial changes obtained contrary to treaty obligations. The League’s February 1933 decision rejected Japan’s account of Manchukuo’s legitimacy. Japan notified withdrawal on March 27, 1933, although its membership ceased legally in March 1935. International condemnation without effective enforcement did not restore Chinese control. The outcome connected Japan’s internal crisis of authority with an unsuccessful international effort to uphold Chinese sovereignty.

The principal interpretive conclusion is not that democracy was doomed from the beginning. The record includes officials who resisted military advances, newspapers and intellectuals who criticized expansion, and decisions that might have taken different directions. The collapse emerged when unauthorized action, inadequate accountability, violent intimidation, and political accommodation reinforced one another. Chapter 75 begins where this chapter ends: with the consequences of the new balance of power for Manchukuo and the restructuring of the Japanese state.

Chapter Reflection

The institutions of the Meiji constitutional order did not disappear in the first months after the Manchurian Incident. Their continued existence is precisely why the crisis remains historically instructive: written rules, official appointments, and electoral procedures can continue while the capacity to hold armed agents accountable declines. Japan’s experience in 1931–1933 is not a simple parable of predetermined military takeover. It is evidence of how authority can be redistributed through a series of decisions made under pressure.

The costs were never confined to Tokyo’s political elite. The first people affected by the Manchurian Incident lived in Chinese territory occupied by a foreign army. Chinese political and military choices, Korean migrants’ uncertain position, and the experiences of townspeople and farmers belong at the center of the analysis. Respect for that geography of consequence helps prevent an institutional Japanese history from erasing those subjected to the invasion.

Chapter 75 follows the developments that came afterward: Manchukuo’s structures, the further militarization of Japan, political repression, and the transformation of government. Chapter 74 concludes with the failure to enforce restraints that still formally existed. The next problem is how a state reorganized itself around the power those failures had allowed to grow.
