Part I — The Archipelago Before the Japanese State
4

Jōmon Society, Ritual, and Material Culture

Japanese History: From Jōmon Japan to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

A broken clay figure, its torso pierced and separated from its legs, lies among pottery sherds, fish bone, and charred nutshells in a refuse layer thousands of years old. It was made carefully, fired, decorated with patterned cord marks, and then — deliberately, it seems, though no one can say for certain why — broken apart. Somewhere else in the same settlement, a wooden post more than ninety centimeters across was raised upright and later left to rot in place. Somewhere else again, a body was laid to rest with a bracelet of shell beads still on its wrist. None of these acts came with an explanation attached. Most, though perhaps not every one of them, were purposeful.

Chapter 3 introduced the Jōmon world in broad outline: its long chronology, its distinctive cord-marked pottery, its settlements, and its subsistence base of hunting, fishing, and gathering across an archipelago whose environments varied enormously from Hokkaido to Kyushu. This chapter turns from that broad survey to a closer and more difficult set of questions. What did Jōmon communities believe, value, fear, and celebrate? How did they organize themselves socially — who held authority, who did specialized work, who was buried with distinction and who was not? What can the objects they made, used, broke, buried, and discarded actually tell historians about the interior life of societies that left no writing at all?

These are hard questions, and this chapter approaches them with the same evidentiary caution introduced in Chapter 2's discussion of the Fujimura fraud. Where Chapter 3 established what Jōmon communities did — how they made pottery, built settlements, and secured food — this chapter asks what those communities meant by what they did, and it is far more honest to say, at many points, that the answer is contested, partial, or simply unknown than to manufacture a confident narrative the evidence cannot support. Dogū figurines, lacquered vessels, shell-bead ornaments, elaborate burials, and long-distance exchange networks are among the richest bodies of evidence archaeologists possess for this period, and they reward careful study — but they also resist easy interpretation, and generations of archaeologists have disagreed, sometimes sharply, about what they mean.

Consider the scale of what survives. Jōmon communities produced dogū figurines by the thousands across the archipelago, the overwhelming majority of them found broken, and often broken in patterned ways — a missing leg here, a severed torso there — that seem too consistent to be the result of ordinary accident alone, though accidental breakage certainly occurred as well and not every fragment implies ritual action. They coated wooden vessels, combs, bows, and ornaments with urushi lacquer using techniques whose chemical sophistication rivals much later East Asian lacquer traditions, sometimes dyeing the lacquer a deep, deliberate red with cinnabar or iron oxide. They wore ornaments of jade, shell, bone, and antler, some of the materials carried from sources many hundreds of kilometers away. They buried their dead in a variety of positions and configurations, occasionally with grave goods, occasionally beneath large stone circles whose purpose remains debated. And they raised timber structures and stone arrangements whose scale — at sites such as Sannai-Maruyama in northern Honshu — implies organized labor well beyond what a single household could supply.

None of this proves that Jōmon societies possessed centralized political authority in the sense later chapters will describe for Yamato kingship or the ritsuryō state. The evidence instead points toward something else: durable, complex, regionally varied communities capable of sustained cooperative labor, long-distance exchange, specialized craft production, and rich symbolic and ritual life, without dependence on full-scale cereal agriculture for most of the sequence, and without writing, metal tools, or (in most interpretations) permanent hereditary rulers commanding coercive power. This combination — genuine social and symbolic complexity built on a hunting-fishing-gathering economic base, supplemented by plant management and localized cultivation — is precisely why Jōmon Japan has become internationally significant to archaeologists studying the origins of complex society worldwide, since it complicates any simple assumption that social complexity requires full-scale agriculture as its precondition.

This chapter also continues a caution introduced in earlier chapters: the temptation to read later Japanese cultural and religious practices backward into the Jōmon past. Some popular and even some scholarly writing has drawn connections between Jōmon ritual practice and later Shinto tradition, Ainu spiritual practice, or a supposed continuous "Japanese" reverence for nature. Such connections are sometimes suggestive and are actively investigated by serious researchers, but they remain hypotheses requiring careful argument and evidence, not settled conclusions to be assumed in advance. This chapter presents what the archaeological evidence itself supports, flags genuine scholarly debate where it exists, and resists the pull toward a tidy, continuous national narrative that the evidence does not by itself establish.

By the chapter's end, students should be able to describe the major categories of Jōmon ritual and material culture in detail, explain the principal scholarly interpretations offered for difficult evidence such as dogū figurines and burial practice, and understand both what a genuinely rich, non-literate society not dependent on full-scale cereal agriculture can look like in its belief and social life in the archaeological record, and where the limits of that record require historians to say, plainly, that they do not yet know.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Describe the major categories of Jōmon ritual and symbolic material culture, including dogū figurines, lacquerware, and personal ornaments.
  2. Explain the leading scholarly interpretations of dogū figurines and evaluate the evidence for and against each.
  3. Trace the development of urushi lacquer technology and assess its technical sophistication relative to other early lacquer traditions.
  4. Analyze Jōmon burial practices, including body position, grave goods, and monumental stone circles, and what they may indicate about social differentiation.
  5. Evaluate the evidence for long-distance exchange networks moving obsidian, jade, shell, asphalt, and other materials across the archipelago.
  6. Assess the evidence for craft specialization within Jōmon communities and its implications for social organization.
  7. Distinguish well-supported inference about Jōmon social organization from speculative or anachronistic claims.
  8. Explain why Jōmon Japan is significant to broader international debates about the relationship between social complexity and full-scale cereal agriculture.
  9. Identify the archaeological methods used to study Jōmon ritual sites, burials, and exchange networks.
  10. Avoid uncritical claims of direct, unbroken continuity between Jōmon spiritual practice and later Shinto or Ainu tradition.
  11. Contextualize Jōmon social and ritual complexity within international archaeological debates about non-agricultural complex societies.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. What can broken and buried objects tell historians about beliefs and practices that left no written record?
  2. What are the leading interpretations of dogū figurines, and how do archaeologists weigh competing explanations?
  3. What does the sophistication of Jōmon lacquer technology suggest about craft knowledge and its transmission across generations?
  4. What do variations in burial practice suggest about social organization and differentiation within Jōmon communities?
  5. How far did materials and, presumably, people move across the archipelago during the Jōmon period, and what does this imply about social connection between distant communities?
  6. Can a society organize large-scale cooperative labor and specialized craft production without full-scale cereal agriculture, metal tools, writing, or centralized political authority?
  7. Why must historians be cautious about projecting later Shinto or Ainu religious concepts directly onto Jōmon ritual practice?
  8. What are the limits of archaeological evidence for reconstructing belief, emotion, and social status in a non-literate society?

Historical Context

Chapter 3 established the broad chronological and environmental framework of the Jōmon period. The sequence is conventionally divided into Incipient, Initial, Early, Middle, Late, and Final phases, beginning around 16,000 years ago; the later phase boundaries and endpoint are regionally variable, with Final Jōmon ceramic traditions continuing in eastern Japan even after wet-field rice agriculture and the Early Yayoi cultural pattern had already become established in northern Kyushu, under the revised AMS chronology discussed further in Chapter 5. It also introduced the basic subsistence pattern of hunting, fishing, and intensive plant gathering — supplemented in places by early forms of plant management or cultivation that fall short of full-scale cereal agriculture — that sustained Jōmon populations for most of the sequence, prior to the cereal-based farming economies that would later define the Yayoi period.

This chapter assumes that background and turns to material the previous chapter treated only briefly: the ritual objects, ornaments, burials, exchange networks, and craft traditions that allow archaeologists to move beyond subsistence and settlement toward questions of belief, status, and social organization. The Jōmon archaeological record is unusually rich for a non-agricultural society by world standards, in part because Japan's Middle and Late Jōmon populations, particularly in central and northeastern Honshu, achieved population densities and settlement permanence considerably higher than many other hunter-fisher-gatherer societies studied elsewhere in the world, leaving correspondingly abundant material remains.

It is important to state clearly, as Chapter 3 also emphasized, that "Jōmon" describes an archaeological culture — recognized primarily through a shared pottery tradition and associated material culture — rather than a single unified people, language group, or political community. Considerable regional diversity existed across the Jōmon world, and the ritual and material practices examined in this chapter, including dogū production, lacquer use, and monumental stone circles, were not uniformly distributed; some practices were far more prominent in particular regions and periods, especially in Tōhoku, Hokkaido, and parts of central Honshu, than in others. Students should resist imagining a single, homogeneous "Jōmon religion" or "Jōmon society" and instead understand the evidence discussed here as illustrating a range of regionally and temporally varied practices connected by broad, shared technological and economic patterns.

Section 4.1

Reading Jōmon Society from Its Objects

Because the Jōmon period left no writing, everything historians can say about Jōmon belief, ritual, and social organization must be inferred from material remains: objects, their contexts of deposition, their spatial arrangement, and their relationship to other objects and features found nearby. This is a fundamentally different evidentiary situation from the periods examined in later chapters, where court chronicles, legal codes, and eventually private letters and diaries allow historians to read something closer to direct testimony. For the Jōmon period, the "text" historians read is stratigraphic and material rather than linguistic, and the interpretive leap from "this object was made and treated this way" to "this is what people believed or how they were organized" requires considerable care.

Archaeologists studying Jōmon ritual and social organization typically draw on several overlapping strands of evidence. The first is the physical context of an object's deposition: whether it was found in a domestic refuse layer, a discrete ritual pit, a burial, or a monumental structure, since the same type of object can carry different significance depending on where and how it was placed. The second is pattern and repetition: a single broken figurine might simply reflect an accident, but a consistent pattern of breakage across thousands of figurines recovered from many sites suggests deliberate, patterned behavior rather than coincidence. The third is comparison with better-documented societies, used cautiously: ethnographic and cross-cultural comparison with other hunter-gatherer and non-state societies can generate plausible hypotheses about Jōmon practice, but such comparisons cannot substitute for direct evidence and must not be treated as proof. The fourth is the changing state of scientific technique itself, including chemical sourcing of stone and pigment, radiocarbon dating of organic residues, and increasingly sophisticated microscopic and chemical analysis of lacquer, which have transformed what can be confidently said about Jōmon material culture over the past several decades.

Students should approach the sections that follow with these evidentiary principles firmly in mind. Where the evidence supports confident description — the existence and forms of dogū, the technical sophistication of lacquer, the fact of long-distance exchange — this chapter states so plainly. Where the evidence supports only plausible interpretation among several competing possibilities — the specific purposes of dogū, the precise social meaning of burial variation, the presence or absence of formalized leadership — this chapter says so as well, and presents the range of scholarly positions rather than asserting a single confident answer the evidence cannot yet fully support.

Section 4.2

Dogū: The Enigmatic Clay Figures

Dogū are fired clay figurines produced across much of the Jōmon world from very early in the sequence, with examples documented from the Incipient and Initial Jōmon periods, though the earliest known figurines are rare, small, and simple compared to the far more numerous and elaborate forms of later phases. Tens of thousands of dogū have been recovered from sites throughout the archipelago, though their frequency and elaboration vary considerably by region and period, becoming especially prominent in Tōhoku and other parts of northern and central Honshu during the Middle through Final Jōmon phases. Over the course of the Jōmon sequence, figurine styles become considerably more elaborate and regionally distinctive, culminating in well-known later forms such as the so-called "goggle-eyed" (shakōki-dogū) figurines, named for large, disc-like eye features, produced primarily in the Final Jōmon period (roughly 1000–400 BCE) in northeastern Japan.

The great majority of dogū are found broken, and the pattern of breakage has drawn considerable scholarly attention: figurines are frequently found missing limbs, heads, or torso sections, sometimes with the broken sections recovered separately, in different locations, or not recovered at all. Some archaeologists interpret this pattern as evidence of deliberate ritual breakage, arguing that fragility was built into the object's function from the outset — that dogū were, in effect, made to be broken as part of a ritual act. Other researchers caution that this reading may overstate the case: figurines are inherently fragile objects that could break through ordinary use, storage, transport, or disturbance during and after deposition, and a straightforward statistical excess of breakage compared to durable pottery vessels does not, by itself, prove ritual intent for every fragment recovered. The most careful current scholarship generally holds that at least some substantial portion of dogū breakage reflects deliberate ritual action, particularly where breakage patterns are unusually consistent or where fragments were deposited separately in ways that appear intentional, while acknowledging that not every broken example can be confidently attributed to ritual practice specifically.

The purpose or purposes served by dogū remain a subject of genuine scholarly debate, examined further in this chapter's Historical Debate feature below. Proposed interpretations include use in fertility or reproductive ritual, based on the prominent breasts, hips, and in some cases apparent pregnancy depicted in certain figurine types; use in healing or illness ritual, based on a hypothesis that breaking a figurine's afflicted body part might have symbolically transferred illness or injury away from a human patient; use in ancestor or spirit veneration; and use in rites of passage or seasonal ceremony connected to community renewal. No single interpretation has achieved universal scholarly acceptance, and it is entirely possible — indeed, likely, given the geographic and chronological range over which dogū were produced — that different dogū served different purposes in different regions and periods, rather than a single explanation applying uniformly across the entire tradition.

Historical Vocabulary
Dogū

Dogū (土偶, literally "earthen figure") refers to fired clay figurines produced across much of the Jōmon world, predominantly depicting human or humanlike forms, though some examples show more ambiguous or composite features. The term is a modern archaeological and Japanese-language category applied to this class of object; it does not derive from any surviving Jōmon-period name for these figures, since no such name is known to survive.

Students should be careful not to treat "dogū" as though it named a single uniform type of object with one settled function. The term covers a very wide range of forms produced over several thousand years and across a large geographic area, and current scholarship increasingly emphasizes regional and chronological variation in dogū form and, quite possibly, function, rather than treating the category as internally uniform.

Section 4.3

Lacquerwork and the Chemistry of Permanence

Among the more technically striking achievements of Jōmon material culture is the sophisticated use of urushi, a refined lacquer derived from the sap of the urushi tree (Toxicodendron vernicifluum), applied to wood, basketry, textile fiber, combs, bows, and ornaments to create a durable, often glossy, and sometimes vividly colored surface coating. Japan preserves one of East Asia's earliest documented lacquer traditions. Waterlogged sites with unusually good organic preservation have yielded very early evidence of urushi use: possible lacquered material from Kakinoshima B, in Hokkaido, may date to roughly 9,000 cal BP, while the earliest securely, directly dated Japanese lacquered artifacts include a comb from the Mibiki site at roughly 7,200 cal BP and a lacquered comb from the Torihama shell mound in Fukui Prefecture at roughly 6,100 cal BP. Early lacquer use is also documented on the Asian continent, including natural lacquer used as a coating and adhesive at Kuahuqiao, in China, by roughly 8,000 cal BP. The precise origins of these early East Asian lacquer traditions, and the relationship, if any, between the Japanese and continental sequences, remain an active subject of scholarly debate rather than a settled question of chronological priority in either direction.

Producing usable lacquer required specific technical knowledge: raw urushi sap contains urushiol, a compound that is strongly allergenic in its uncured state and that can still provoke reactions from lacquerware under some conditions even after curing, so successful use required the sap to be conditioned or refined for controlled, deliberate application rather than used directly in its raw form. Once applied, the lacquer cured through a slow, enzyme-mediated polymerization of urushiol that requires warm, humid conditions rather than simple open-air drying. Jōmon lacquer workers also achieved striking color effects, most notably a deep red produced by mixing lacquer with cinnabar (mercury sulfide) or with iron oxide pigments, alongside naturally darker lacquer tones. The consistent, technically demanding character of this production across multiple sites and periods indicates that lacquer-working knowledge was a genuine specialized craft tradition, transmitted deliberately across generations rather than independently reinvented by chance at each location.

The uses to which lacquered objects were put varied: some lacquered items, including combs and ornaments, appear to have served everyday or semi-ceremonial personal adornment functions, while other lacquered objects, including certain wooden vessels and, in some cases, items associated with burials, suggest a more explicitly ritual or high-status application. As with dogū, the surviving evidence does not support a single, uniform account of what lacquered objects meant to the people who made and used them; instead, it supports the more general conclusion that lacquer technology was valued, invested with considerable skill and labor, and applied across a range of everyday, ornamental, and probably ritual contexts throughout much of the Jōmon sequence.

Historical Vocabulary
Urushi

Urushi refers both to the sap of the urushi tree (Toxicodendron vernicifluum) and to the refined lacquer produced from that sap once it has been processed for use as a coating on wood, fiber, and other materials. Urushi lacquer hardens through a chemical curing process that requires specific humidity conditions rather than simple air-drying, and it produces a durable, water-resistant, and often glossy surface that can be further colored with pigments such as cinnabar or iron oxide.

The presence of sophisticated urushi lacquerwork from very early in the Jōmon sequence — with possible evidence around 9,000 cal BP and securely dated lacquerware by roughly 7,200 cal BP — is one of the clearest pieces of evidence that Jōmon material culture, though largely non-literate and, for most of the sequence, not dependent on full-scale cereal agriculture, involved considerable specialized technical knowledge, deliberately transmitted and refined across many generations.

Section 4.4

Ornament, Adornment, and the Body

Jōmon communities produced and wore a considerable range of personal ornaments, including beads, pendants, earrings, combs, and bracelets made from shell, bone, antler, stone, and, from at least the Early Jōmon period onward in some regions, jadeite. The green stone known as hisui (jadeite) was sourced almost exclusively from deposits in the Itoigawa region of what is now Niigata Prefecture; mineralogical and archaeological evidence documents working of Itoigawa jadeitite by roughly 7,000 years ago, including tools such as the Ogakuchi hammer stone associated with the Early Jōmon period, with pendants and, later, the curved bead form known as magatama following within the same jadeite-working tradition. Magatama carried considerable ritual and status significance into much later periods of Japanese history, including an eventual association with the imperial regalia discussed in later chapters. The presence of Itoigawa jadeite ornaments at sites hundreds of kilometers from the source demonstrates sustained, long-distance exchange relationships connecting geographically distant Jōmon communities, examined further in Section 4.6.

Beyond exchange-derived materials, Jōmon populations also practiced forms of bodily modification that leave direct skeletal evidence, most notably ritual tooth ablation — the patterned removal of selected incisors and/or canines, with the specific teeth extracted and the combinations involved varying by region, period, and possibly social grouping — documented at numerous Jōmon burial sites, particularly from the Late Jōmon period onward. The specific social meaning of tooth ablation remains debated: proposed interpretations include markers of age-grade or life-stage transition (such as passage into adulthood), markers of marital status, mourning practices performed by surviving kin, or markers of group or clan affiliation. As with dogū and lacquerware, more than one explanation may apply in different regional or chronological contexts, and the practice's precise social logic cannot currently be reconstructed with full confidence from the skeletal evidence alone.

Ornamentation and bodily modification together suggest that Jōmon communities used the body itself as a site of social meaning — marking identity, status, life stage, or group belonging through practices that required either access to exchanged materials or the deliberate, painful modification of the body itself. This should caution students against imagining Jōmon social life as undifferentiated or lacking meaningful internal social distinctions, even in the absence of the more familiar hierarchical markers, such as monumental tombs for named rulers, that characterize the later periods examined in Part II of this textbook.

Section 4.5

Death and Burial in Jōmon Communities

Jōmon burial practice varied considerably by region and period but has provided archaeologists with some of their richest evidence for social organization, since the treatment of the dead — body position, associated grave goods, burial location, and monumental elaboration — often reflects, however imperfectly, the social standing, group membership, or ritual significance accorded to the deceased by the living community that buried them.

A common Jōmon burial position, particularly in earlier phases, is the flexed burial, in which the body is placed with knees drawn up toward the chest, sometimes tightly bound in this position. Extended burials, with the body laid out straight, also occur, particularly in some later contexts, and secondary burial practices — in which remains were exhumed, processed, and reburied, sometimes with bones bundled or rearranged — are documented at a number of sites, indicating a more extended and multi-stage relationship between a community and its dead than a single interment event would suggest. Some burials include grave goods such as ornaments, tools, or pottery vessels, while many others contain no such items at all; the presence or absence of grave goods, along with variation in burial location — some individuals were buried within or near settlement areas, while others were placed in more separated cemetery-like zones or beneath monumental stone arrangements — has led many archaeologists to argue that Jōmon communities recognized at least some degree of social differentiation in death, even though the evidence generally does not support the kind of sharply stratified, hereditary elite burial practice documented for the much later Kofun-period tombs examined in Chapter 7.

The monumental stone circles (kanjō resseki) found at sites such as Ōyu in Akita Prefecture represent some of the most striking evidence for organized, large-scale Jōmon ritual or funerary activity. These circles, built from large numbers of stones arranged in concentric or otherwise patterned configurations, required substantial coordinated labor to construct and are associated, at several sites, with burial features, suggesting a funerary or ancestor-related function, though some researchers have also proposed calendrical, astronomical, or broader communal ritual functions that need not be mutually exclusive with a funerary interpretation. As with dogū, no single, universally accepted explanation currently accounts for the full range of evidence, and ongoing excavation and analysis continue to refine scholarly understanding of these monuments.

Section 4.6

Exchange Networks and the Movement of Materials

One of the clearest and most quantitatively well-documented bodies of evidence for Jōmon social connection across long distances comes from the chemical sourcing of raw materials found far from their points of origin. Obsidian, a volcanic glass prized for its ability to be knapped into extremely sharp cutting edges, can often be chemically "fingerprinted" to a specific geological source, such as the Kōzu-shima and Shinshū (Wada-tōge) source areas; obsidian tools sourced to these locations have been recovered at sites many hundreds of kilometers away, and in the case of Kōzu-shima, a small volcanic island off the Izu Peninsula, obsidian moved to the mainland necessarily involved a sea crossing, indicating sustained maritime transport of raw material as well as overland exchange.

Jadeite from the Itoigawa source area, discussed in Section 4.4, provides a second, comparably well-documented example: ornaments made from this material are found distributed across a very wide area of the Jōmon world, again implying sustained exchange relationships operating over long distances and, presumably, extended periods of time. Natural asphalt, used as an adhesive for hafting stone tools and repairing pottery, and marine shell, used for ornaments and, in some coastal and inland contexts, exchanged well beyond its immediate coastal source, provide further examples of materials moving through exchange networks connecting geographically and, in some cases, environmentally distinct Jōmon communities.

The existence of these exchange networks does not, by itself, prove the existence of formalized markets, currency, or specialized merchant roles in any sense comparable to later historical periods; archaeologists generally interpret Jōmon exchange as embedded within broader social relationships — including kinship ties, ritual obligation, marriage alliance, and periodic gathering or feasting events — rather than as an autonomous economic sphere separate from social and ritual life. Nonetheless, the sheer geographic scale and apparent durability of these networks, sustained across many centuries, indicates a level of intercommunity connection and cooperation considerably more extensive than a purely local, isolated village-by-village model of Jōmon society would suggest.

Section 4.7

Spirituality, Ritual Space, and Monuments

Beyond individual objects such as dogū and lacquerware, Jōmon communities in some regions invested substantial labor in constructing dedicated ritual spaces and monuments, most notably the large timber post structures documented at Sannai-Maruyama and the stone circle complexes documented at Ōyu and related sites. At Sannai-Maruyama, excavators have recovered evidence of large post holes, in some interpretations associated with a substantial raised timber structure, whose precise form and function — whether a watchtower, ceremonial platform, or some other structure — remains a matter of ongoing reconstruction and scholarly discussion, since the wooden superstructure itself did not survive and its appearance must be inferred from the surviving post arrangement and comparison with later timber construction techniques.

The scale of labor implied by such constructions, together with the long-term, apparently repeated use of specific sites for burial, ritual deposition, and monument-building over generations, suggests that at least some Jōmon communities maintained a persistent, place-based ritual life — recognizing certain locations as significant across long spans of time, worth returning to and continuing to invest in, rather than treating ritual space as purely incidental or temporary. This pattern is consistent with, though it does not by itself prove, forms of ancestor-related or place-based spiritual practice, in which specific locations accumulated ritual significance through repeated use across generations.

Students should exercise particular caution here regarding claims of direct continuity with later Japanese religious tradition. Some popular writing draws a direct line between Jōmon reverence for particular natural or constructed sites and the later Shinto concept of sacred space, or between Jōmon animistic practice and later folk religious tradition, or between Jōmon ritual and documented Ainu spiritual practice. Such connections are actively investigated by serious scholars and cannot be dismissed outright, but they remain hypotheses under active research rather than demonstrated historical continuities, given the very long span of time, substantial population and cultural change (including the Yayoi transformation examined in Chapter 5), and the absence of direct documentary evidence connecting Jōmon practice to any specific later religious tradition by name or content.

Section 4.8

Craft Production and Specialization

The technical sophistication documented in Jōmon pottery decoration, lacquerwork, and ornament production has led many archaeologists to argue that at least some degree of craft specialization existed within Jōmon communities — meaning that certain individuals or households likely devoted disproportionate time and skill to particular crafts, developing and transmitting specialized knowledge, rather than every household producing all necessary goods to an equal standard. Evidence for this includes the marked skill differential visible between the most elaborate examples of decorated pottery, dogū, and lacquerware and simpler, more utilitarian objects found at the same sites; the consistency of certain technical practices, such as lacquer processing, across multiple generations and sites, suggesting deliberate transmission of specialized knowledge; and, at some larger settlement sites, spatial evidence suggestive of workshop-like areas associated with concentrated craft debris.

It is important not to overstate this evidence into a claim of fully professionalized, full-time craft specialists comparable to later historical artisan classes. Jōmon craft specialization, to the extent it existed, most likely operated on a part-time or seasonal basis, integrated within a broader subsistence economy still centered on hunting, fishing, and gathering, rather than as a fully separate economic sector supporting individuals who did not also participate in subsistence activities. Nonetheless, the presence of specialized technical knowledge — in lacquer chemistry, in fine pottery decoration, in the working of jadeite and other hard stone — indicates a degree of social and economic differentiation within Jōmon communities that should caution against imagining Jōmon society as uniformly simple or undifferentiated merely because it was not dependent on full-scale cereal agriculture and lacked metal tools and writing.

Section 4.9

Social Organization: Kinship, Status, and Community

Drawing together the evidence examined throughout this chapter — burial variation, craft specialization, monumental construction, and long-distance exchange — most current archaeological interpretations describe Jōmon social organization as characterized by small to, in some regions and periods, moderately large communities, likely organized substantially along kinship lines, with some degree of internal status differentiation but without strong evidence for the kind of centralized, coercive, hereditary political authority that would later characterize Yamato kingship and subsequent state formation examined in Part II of this textbook.

Settlement evidence discussed in Chapter 3, combined with the burial, craft, and exchange evidence examined here, suggests that some Jōmon communities, particularly larger and longer-occupied settlements such as Sannai-Maruyama, achieved a level of demographic scale, settlement permanence, and organizational complexity substantial enough to coordinate large construction projects, maintain long-distance exchange relationships, and support at least part-time craft specialization — all without the full-scale cereal-agricultural surplus, metal tools, or writing that many older anthropological models once assumed were necessary preconditions for such complexity. This makes Jōmon Japan an internationally significant case study for archaeologists and anthropologists studying the broader relationship between subsistence economy and social complexity, since it demonstrates that complex, materially rich, and ritually elaborate societies can develop on a hunting-fishing-gathering economic base under sufficiently favorable environmental conditions, such as the rich terrestrial and marine resources available to many Jōmon communities in central and northeastern Honshu.

At the same time, historians should resist projecting fully formed "chiefdoms" or proto-states onto the Jōmon evidence. The differentiation visible in Jōmon burials and craft production appears considerably less pronounced, and less clearly hereditary, than the elite burial and status markers documented for the Yayoi and especially Kofun periods examined in Chapters 5 through 7, and no Jōmon evidence currently supports the existence of named individual rulers, dynastic succession, or centralized coercive authority extending significantly beyond a single community. The most defensible current position, and the one this chapter adopts, describes Jōmon social organization as complex, regionally varied, and marked by real but limited social differentiation — a genuine middle ground between an undifferentiated egalitarian model and a fully stratified political hierarchy.

Section 4.10

Chapter Review and Historical Thinking Checkpoint

This chapter has examined the ritual, symbolic, and social dimensions of Jōmon material culture, moving beyond the broad chronological and subsistence framework established in Chapter 3 to consider what dogū, lacquerware, ornaments, burials, exchange networks, and monumental construction can and cannot tell historians about Jōmon belief and social organization.

The core insights of this chapter are: dogū figurines, produced across much of the Jōmon world in a wide variety of forms, were frequently broken in patterns that many, though not all, archaeologists interpret as deliberate ritual action, though their specific purpose or purposes remain genuinely debated. Jōmon lacquer technology, attested from a remarkably early date, demonstrates real technical sophistication and specialized craft knowledge transmitted across generations. Personal ornamentation and bodily modification, including jadeite ornaments and ritual tooth ablation, indicate that Jōmon communities used the body as a site of social meaning. Burial practice varied considerably and, together with monumental stone circle construction, suggests a real but limited degree of social differentiation. Long-distance exchange networks moved obsidian, jadeite, and other materials across hundreds of kilometers, implying sustained connections between distant communities. And the overall picture supports describing Jōmon society as genuinely complex — capable of specialized craft production, monumental cooperative labor, and elaborate ritual life — without possessing the centralized political authority, full-scale cereal agriculture, or writing that would characterize later periods of Japanese history.

Primary Source Window

The Sannai-Maruyama Excavation Record

Creator: Aomori Prefectural archaeological teams and affiliated researchers · Date: excavations beginning in the early 1990s and continuing to the present · Type: Archaeological excavation reports and site documentation

Context: Large-scale excavation at Sannai-Maruyama began in the early 1990s in connection with planned construction at the site, which was subsequently halted and the site preserved once the scale and significance of the remains became apparent. Excavation revealed extensive dwelling pits, large post holes arranged in a distinctive pattern, refuse mounds containing enormous quantities of pottery, tools, and food remains, and burial areas, occupied across a substantial span of the Early to Middle Jōmon period.

Paraphrase of site interpretation (not a verbatim quotation): Excavators concluded that the site's scale, the presence of substantial post structures, and the volume of accumulated material remains indicated a settlement of considerable size and duration, sustained by organized cooperative labor beyond what a single household could have supplied.

Analysis: The Sannai-Maruyama excavation record illustrates how large-scale, carefully documented archaeological excavation can substantially revise scholarly understanding of a period, in this case demonstrating a level of Jōmon settlement permanence and organizational scale that earlier scholarship had generally not expected. It also illustrates the ongoing, provisional nature of archaeological interpretation: debates over the precise function of the large post structures, and over how representative Sannai-Maruyama is of Jōmon settlement more broadly, continue among specialists.

Historical Debate

What Were Dogū For?

The question: Given that dogū figurines are overwhelmingly found broken, often in patterned ways, and given the wide variation in their form across regions and periods, how should archaeologists interpret their function and meaning?

Interpretation A — Fertility and reproductive ritual: Some archaeologists argue that the prominent breasts, hips, and apparent pregnancy shown in many dogū forms indicate a primary association with fertility, reproduction, or the renewal of life, possibly used in rituals intended to promote successful childbirth, community fertility, or seasonal renewal.
Interpretation B — Healing, illness, and symbolic substitution: Other researchers propose that dogū, or at least a substantial subset of them, functioned as symbolic stand-ins for afflicted individuals, with the deliberate breaking of a specific body part corresponding to a specific illness or injury, intended to transfer or dispel harm away from the person the figurine represented.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on the same body of dogū finds and their patterns of breakage and deposition, but differ in how they weigh the figurines' physical features (favoring the fertility hypothesis) against the specific, sometimes body-part-targeted pattern of breakage (favoring the healing/substitution hypothesis). Some researchers argue the two interpretations are not mutually exclusive and that different dogū, in different regions or periods, may have served either or both functions, alongside other purposes not captured by either hypothesis.

Why it matters: How historians and archaeologists interpret dogū shapes broader claims about the presence, nature, and diversity of ritual and spiritual practice in Jōmon society, and cautions against assuming a single, simple explanation for a tradition that spanned thousands of years and a large geographic area.

Student task: Identify one piece of evidence discussed in this chapter that supports each interpretation, and explain whether you think a single unified explanation is more or less plausible than a regionally and temporally varied set of functions.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Ritual Life Across the Jōmon Sequence

What Changed

  • Dogū forms grew markedly more elaborate and regionally distinctive from the Early through Late/Final Jōmon periods
  • Monumental stone circle construction expanded significantly in the Late Jōmon period in parts of northern Japan
  • Settlement scale and permanence, as at Sannai-Maruyama, increased substantially during parts of the Early and Middle Jōmon periods before later contraction
  • Final Jōmon ceramic traditions persisted in eastern Japan even as wet-field rice agriculture and the Early Yayoi cultural pattern became established in northern Kyushu, foreshadowing the transformation examined in Chapter 5

What Stayed the Same

  • Continued reliance on hunting, fishing, and gathering as the basic subsistence foundation throughout the Jōmon sequence
  • Sustained long-distance exchange of obsidian, jadeite, and other materials across many centuries
  • Ongoing production of lacquerware and ornamented pottery as valued craft traditions
  • Persistent evidence of ritual attention to the body, in both ornamentation and burial practice

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both change and continuity within the Jōmon period itself prevents students from treating "Jōmon Japan" as a single static condition lasting many thousands of years, when in fact it encompassed substantial internal development and regional diversity.

Cause and Effect

Environmental Abundance and Social Complexity

Long-term causes

The stabilization of a warmer, more forested Holocene environment, described at the end of Chapter 2, provided rich and relatively predictable terrestrial and marine food resources across much of central and northeastern Honshu.

Short-term causes

Locally abundant resources in particular regions allowed some communities, such as that at Sannai-Maruyama, to sustain larger, more permanent settlements than a purely mobile hunter-gatherer pattern would typically support.

Effect

Larger, more settled communities generated the labor capacity, accumulated skill, and social organization needed for craft specialization, monumental construction, and sustained long-distance exchange.

Long-term consequence

Jōmon Japan became internationally significant as a documented case in which social and ritual complexity developed on an economic base not dependent on full-scale cereal agriculture, informing broader anthropological debate about the relationship between subsistence strategy and social complexity worldwide.

Historical complexity: Environmental abundance created possibilities for social complexity but did not mechanically produce it; regions with comparably rich resources did not all develop identical settlement scale or ritual elaboration, indicating that social choices and historical contingency also shaped the outcome.

Everyday Life and Environment

The Texture of Jōmon Ritual and Domestic Life

Alongside the more dramatic evidence of dogū, lacquerware, and stone circles, Jōmon daily life involved the more routine activities of managing a pit-dwelling household, processing and storing gathered plant foods (including nuts such as chestnut and walnut, sometimes intensively managed near settlements), preparing fish and shellfish, and maintaining pottery, stone, bone, and antler tools essential to everyday survival. Ritual and craft activity did not occur separately from this domestic routine but was interwoven with it, taking place within and around the same settlements where food was prepared, tools were made and repaired, and children were raised.

Seasonal rhythms likely shaped much of this activity, with different resources — nuts in autumn, fish runs at particular times of year, shellfish gathering tied to tidal and seasonal cycles — structuring the community's labor calendar across the year, much as later agricultural societies would structure their labor around planting and harvest cycles, though Jōmon communities relied on managed wild and semi-managed resources rather than field agriculture. Understanding this everyday texture helps prevent the more striking ritual evidence examined in this chapter from overshadowing the ordinary, sustained labor of subsistence that made Jōmon community life, craft specialization, and ritual elaboration possible in the first place.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the ritual, symbolic, and social dimensions of Jōmon material culture, building on the chronological and subsistence framework established in Chapter 3 to ask what Jōmon communities believed, valued, and how they organized themselves socially, insofar as the archaeological evidence can support such conclusions.

Dogū figurines, produced in large numbers across much of the Jōmon world and especially prominent in Tōhoku and other parts of northern and central Honshu, were overwhelmingly found broken, in patterns many archaeologists interpret as deliberate ritual action connected to fertility, healing, or other purposes not yet fully understood, and quite possibly varying by region and period. Urushi lacquer technology, attested from a remarkably early date, demonstrates specialized technical knowledge transmitted deliberately across generations, applied to both everyday and ritual objects. Personal ornamentation, including jadeite beads sourced from Itoigawa and distributed across long distances, together with practices such as ritual tooth ablation, indicate that the body itself served as a site of social and ritual meaning.

Burial practice varied considerably across the Jōmon sequence, and the presence or absence of grave goods, along with variation in burial location and the construction of monumental stone circles such as those at Ōyu, suggest a real, if limited, degree of social differentiation within Jōmon communities. Long-distance exchange networks, documented through chemical sourcing of obsidian, jadeite, and other materials, connected communities across hundreds of kilometers and, in some cases, across open water, implying sustained social and economic relationships extending well beyond individual settlements.

Evidence for craft specialization, drawn from the marked skill differential visible in fine pottery, lacquerware, and worked stone, together with the scale of cooperative labor implied by sites such as Sannai-Maruyama and the Ōyu stone circles, supports describing Jōmon society as genuinely complex — capable of specialized production, monumental construction, and long-distance exchange — while stopping well short of the centralized, hereditary political authority that would characterize later periods of Japanese history examined beginning in Part II of this textbook.

The chapter has repeatedly emphasized the limits of what can be confidently known from this evidence. The specific purposes of dogū, the precise social meaning of burial variation, and the presence or absence of formalized leadership all remain subjects of genuine, ongoing scholarly debate rather than settled fact, and this chapter has presented competing interpretations honestly rather than asserting false certainty. The chapter has also cautioned against uncritically projecting later Shinto, Ainu, or broader "Japanese" religious concepts directly onto Jōmon evidence, treating such connections as hypotheses under active investigation rather than demonstrated historical continuities.

Jōmon Japan's combination of a subsistence economy not dependent on full-scale cereal agriculture with genuine ritual, craft, and organizational complexity makes it an internationally significant case study for broader anthropological and archaeological debates about the origins and preconditions of complex society, a significance this chapter has situated within the textbook's larger themes of evidentiary caution and historical nuance introduced in Chapter 2.

These findings prepare students directly for Chapter 5, which examines the Yayoi transformation — the introduction of wet-rice agriculture, metallurgy, and new forms of social and political organization that would, over succeeding centuries, substantially reshape the technological, economic, and social patterns this chapter has described, even as certain Jōmon-period technologies, exchange relationships, and possibly ritual practices persisted into and influenced the societies that followed.

Students who understand Jōmon Japan as a genuinely complex, materially rich, and ritually elaborate society — while remaining honest about the real limits of what its surviving evidence can establish — are well prepared to evaluate the significant social and economic changes examined in the chapters that follow.

Key Terms

Dogū
Fired clay figurines, predominantly humanlike in form, produced across much of the Jōmon world and frequently found broken; deliberate ritual breakage has been proposed for many examples, but cannot be assumed for every fragment.
Urushi
Refined lacquer derived from tree sap, applied to wood, fiber, and ornaments; attested in Japan from a remarkably early date.
Magatama
A curved bead form, often made from jadeite, produced from the Jōmon period onward and later associated with elite and imperial regalia.
Kanjō resseki
Large circular stone arrangements associated with Late Jōmon ritual or funerary activity, as at Ōyu.
Shell mound (kaizuka)
An accumulated deposit of shell, bone, pottery, and other refuse, a major source of evidence for Jōmon diet and material culture.
Pit-dwelling
A common Jōmon house form built over a shallow excavated pit, typically with a wooden and thatch superstructure.
Flexed burial
A burial position with the body's knees drawn up toward the chest, common in earlier Jōmon phases.
Extended burial
A burial position with the body laid out straight, documented particularly in some later Jōmon contexts.
Secondary burial
A burial practice in which remains were exhumed, processed, and reburied, sometimes with bones rearranged or bundled.
Grave goods
Objects placed with a burial, whose presence or absence and character are used by archaeologists to infer social status or ritual significance.
Craft specialization
The concentration of particular technical skills and production in specific individuals or households rather than uniformly across a community.
Exchange network
A system of relationships through which raw materials and goods moved between communities, often over long distances.
Chemical sourcing
Scientific analysis used to trace a material such as obsidian or jadeite to its specific geological point of origin.
Obsidian
A volcanic glass valued for sharp cutting edges, chemically traceable to specific sources and widely exchanged in Jōmon Japan.
Jadeite (hisui)
A green stone sourced almost exclusively from the Itoigawa region and worked into ornaments distributed widely across the Jōmon world.
Cinnabar
A red mercury sulfide pigment used, among other applications, to color Jōmon lacquer.
Ritual deposition
The deliberate placement of an object in a specific context as part of a ritual act, as distinct from ordinary discard.
Fertility hypothesis
The interpretation that dogū figurines were primarily connected to fertility or reproductive ritual.
Ancestor veneration
Ritual practice honoring deceased forebears; proposed, though not conclusively demonstrated, as one dimension of Jōmon ritual life.
Animism
A belief system attributing spiritual significance to natural objects, places, or phenomena; sometimes hypothesized, with caution, in connection with Jōmon practice.
Ritual tooth ablation
The deliberate removal of specific teeth, documented at Jōmon burial sites and interpreted as marking age, status, or group affiliation.
Non-agricultural complexity
The archaeological and anthropological concept that social and ritual complexity can develop without dependence on a full-scale cereal-agricultural economic base, exemplified by Jōmon Japan.
Sannai-Maruyama
A large Early-to-Middle Jōmon settlement site in Aomori Prefecture providing key evidence for settlement permanence and cooperative labor.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has carried the Jōmon world introduced in Chapter 3 into its ritual and social dimensions, showing that a society not dependent on full-scale cereal agriculture, and lacking metal tools and writing, could nonetheless produce technically sophisticated craft traditions, sustain long-distance exchange relationships, organize substantial cooperative labor, and maintain rich and varied ritual practices. This complexity should not be mistaken for the centralized political authority that later chapters will describe for Yamato kingship and subsequent state formation, but it equally should not be underestimated or flattened into a simple, undifferentiated picture of prehistoric life.

The chapter's treatment of dogū, burial variation, and monumental construction matters beyond its immediate subject. It demonstrates that historians and archaeologists can build a genuine, evidence-based account of belief and social organization even from a non-literate society, while remaining honest that such accounts often involve real, unresolved debate rather than settled certainty. Students should carry this same evidentiary caution into later chapters, particularly those addressing early court chronicles, later mythological tradition, and other periods where the temptation to read confident narratives into ambiguous or contested evidence will recur.

The chapter has also reinforced an important theme carried from Chapter 2: the danger of projecting later identities and traditions backward onto earlier periods. Just as Paleolithic populations should not be described as early "Japanese people," Jōmon ritual practice should not be assumed, without careful argument, to be directly continuous with later Shinto or Ainu tradition, however suggestive some parallels may appear. This caution will remain relevant as the textbook proceeds to examine the Yayoi transformation, the emergence of Yamato kingship, and the gradual construction of Japanese religious, political, and cultural traditions over many subsequent centuries.

Students who understand Jōmon society as genuinely complex, materially rich, and ritually elaborate — while remaining alert to the real limits of the evidence and the dangers of anachronistic projection — are well prepared to move forward into Chapter 5, where the introduction of wet-rice agriculture, metallurgy, and new social and political patterns will substantially transform, though not entirely erase, the foundations this chapter has described.
