Part XI — Empire, Militarism, and Total War
81

Surrender, Repatriation, and the Collapse of Imperial Japan

Japanese History: From Jōmon Japan to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

At noon on August 15, 1945, much of the population of Japan, and many Japanese, Koreans, Taiwanese, and others living under Japanese rule, heard the voice of the emperor on the radio for the first time. The announcement never used the word "surrender." For listeners in Tokyo it ended a war; for a farm family in Manchuria, a colonial teacher in Seoul, or a soldier in Java, it was the beginning of a process whose outcome no one could yet see.

This chapter follows that process. Chapter 80 described the destruction of Japan's cities, the Battle of Okinawa, and the bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki. This chapter asks how a government that had committed itself to fighting for the homeland came to accept defeat, how that acceptance was converted into the actual cessation of fighting across an empire stretching from the Kuril Islands to New Guinea, and what happened to the millions of people whose lives had been organized around that empire.

Four processes are easy to confuse and must be kept distinct: the decision to surrender, taken by Japan's leaders between August 9 and 14; the cessation of combat, which was uneven and in some places continued for days after August 15; the formal surrender of military forces, signed on September 2 and carried out in regional ceremonies for weeks afterward; and the dissolution of the overseas empire, a political, legal, and human process that unfolded over years. Treating any one of these as the whole story produces a misleading history.

Japan's empire had placed roughly 6.6 million Japanese soldiers and civilians overseas by the time of defeat, according to the demobilization and repatriation research collection of the Japan Center for Asian Historical Records (JACAR). The same empire had governed tens of millions of Koreans, Taiwanese, Chinese, Southeast Asians, and Pacific islanders. Its collapse therefore meant disarmament and flight for some, liberation and political struggle for others, captivity for hundreds of thousands, and, for many, a prolonged uncertainty about nationality and belonging.

The chapter's central argument is that Japan's surrender ended the empire's war but did not instantly end the empire. Military units had to be disarmed; colonial administrations were replaced by very different successor authorities (Soviet, American, Chinese Nationalist, British, Dutch, French, and indigenous governments or movements); and millions of people moved, were moved, or were trapped. The political geography of East Asia was transformed, while questions of citizenship, responsibility, and memory remained unresolved. Asian nationalist movements, in Korea, Indonesia, and Vietnam especially, acted on their own initiative and should not be treated as mere beneficiaries of Japanese defeat.

The chapter must also hold together experiences that postwar memory has often separated. Japanese settlers who fled Manchuria and Japanese prisoners who died in Siberian camps suffered terribly; those histories cannot be told honestly without the prior history of Japanese colonization, land seizure, forced labor, and military occupation treated in Chapters 62, 75, 76, 78, and 79. The people over whom the empire had ruled experienced its end in their own ways, and their histories are part of this one.

The evidence is varied and uneven. It includes the Potsdam Declaration, the imperial rescript, the Instrument of Surrender and General Order No. 1; cabinet and imperial-conference records, diaries, and memoirs (with the caution that many were written after the war to explain or defend choices); Allied and Japanese military records; port, ministry, and relief-agency documents; newspapers and radio; oral histories and memoirs of repatriates and internees; and museum collections such as the Maizuru Repatriation Memorial Museum. Numbers, especially for deaths and for people moved, are estimates that depend on who counted, when, and under what definitions; this chapter gives ranges and attributes them.

The chapter ends in the transition from Part XI to Part XII. Chapter 82 examines how the Allied occupation actually governed Japan; Chapters 83 to 90 treat the trials, constitutional reform, scarcity, social life, and reconstruction of the occupation years. Here the aim is narrower and prior: to understand how the war ended and what the end of empire meant for those who lived through it.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Distinguish the decision to surrender, the broadcast of August 15, the cessation of combat, the formal surrender of September 2, and the dissolution of the empire as separate processes.
  2. Explain the strategic dilemma facing the Suzuki cabinet in mid-1945, including Ketsu-Gō planning, the search for Soviet mediation, and the Potsdam Declaration.
  3. Evaluate the roles of the atomic bombings, Soviet entry, the imperial institution, and military and political divisions in the surrender decision, using documentary evidence.
  4. Reconstruct the Supreme War Council deadlock, the imperial interventions of August 10 and 14, and the Kyūjō Incident.
  5. Compare how imperial rule ended in Korea, Manchuria, Taiwan, southern Sakhalin, the Kurils, Okinawa, and Southeast Asia.
  6. Explain why the 38th parallel became a dividing line and how General Order No. 1 allocated surrender responsibilities.
  7. Trace the administrative machinery of demobilization and repatriation and the different fates of soldiers and civilians.
  8. Analyze the Manchurian settler catastrophe and Soviet internment as consequences of both defeat and earlier Japanese policy.
  9. Assess the experiences of Koreans, Taiwanese, Chinese, and other former colonial subjects, including those who remained in Japan.
  10. Interpret statistics on overseas populations, returnees, and detainees, recognizing differing endpoints and definitions.
  11. Explain how nationality, residence, and political belonging were redefined after 1945 and why continuity of the Japanese state matters.
  12. Evaluate primary sources for their creators, purposes, and limits, and identify whose voices they omit.

Historical Context

By spring 1945 Japan had lost its outer perimeter in the Pacific, much of its merchant shipping, and its air defense. Strategic bombing had destroyed or damaged most major cities, and the Battle of Okinawa (Chapter 80) was consuming the forces that might have defended the main islands. The Koiso cabinet resigned in April, and the retired admiral Suzuki Kantarō formed a new government on April 7. The Allied powers, meanwhile, had agreed at Yalta in February that the Soviet Union would enter the war against Japan within a few months of Germany's defeat, in return for territorial concessions; Japanese leaders did not know these terms.

The empire that was about to collapse was a layered structure. Taiwan (from 1895) and Korea (from 1910) were colonies with Japanese-dominated governments-general; southern Sakhalin (Karafuto) was administered as part of the Japanese state; Manchukuo (1932) was a nominally independent state controlled by the Kwantung Army; large parts of China, and after 1941 Southeast Asia and the Pacific, were under military occupation, in some places with collaborating or allied governments. Okinawa and Hokkaido, treated in earlier chapters, were Japanese prefectures whose populations had their own histories of incorporation. Each layer would end differently.

Japanese policy had also sent very large numbers of its own people abroad: officials, police, teachers, merchants, and industrial employees across the empire, and, especially in Manchuria, state-sponsored agricultural settlers. At the same time Koreans, Chinese, Taiwanese, and others had been brought to Japan or mobilized within the empire as laborers and soldiers (Chapter 79). In August 1945, therefore, people were stranded in both directions: Japanese outside Japan, and colonial subjects inside it.

Finally, the Japanese state would not disappear. Under the Instrument of Surrender the authority of the emperor and government was made subject to the Supreme Commander for the Allied Powers, but Japanese ministries and officials continued to administer the country and, initially, to organize demobilization and repatriation under Allied supervision. That continuity is essential to understanding both the speed and the limits of what followed.

Section 81.1 · Division I — The Decision to Surrender and the End of the War, July–September 1945

Japan's Final Strategic Dilemma: Soviet Mediation, the Potsdam Declaration, and the Defense of the Homeland

The Suzuki Kantarō cabinet took office on April 7, 1945, with a mandate that its own members understood differently. Suzuki, a retired admiral and former grand chamberlain, was trusted by the court; Tōgō Shigenori at the Foreign Ministry sought a negotiated end to the war; Anami Korechika at the Army Ministry, Umezu Yoshijirō and Toyoda Soemu as the army and navy chiefs of staff, and Yonai Mitsumasa at the Navy Ministry brought the services' views into the small Supreme Council for the Direction of the War (the "Supreme War Council"). The cabinet's public posture was resistance; its private discussions were more divided.

Military planning centered on Ketsu-Gō, a strategy of concentrating forces and resources, above all in Kyushu and the Kantō plain, to inflict heavy losses on an Allied landing and so improve Japan's bargaining position. It relied on massed suicide aircraft and boats, coastal defenses, and the mobilization of civilians, formalized in June in the Volunteer Fighting Corps. Planners hoped that a bloody defense would make the Allies willing to offer terms preserving the existing political order. Whether Ketsu-Gō could have succeeded is doubtful; what matters historically is that it gave the army's leadership a reason to resist terms it regarded as unconditional.

The central political question was the kokutai, the "national polity" defined around the emperor's sovereignty. Leaders who favored peace, including Tōgō and the court circle around Lord Keeper of the Privy Seal Kido Kōichi, worried that Allied terms might abolish the imperial institution or place the emperor on trial. Hardliners worried about the same thing and also about disarmament, occupation, and the prosecution of officers. On June 22 the emperor asked the six council members to consider ending the war alongside the plans to continue it, and the government pursued the one channel it imagined might work: the Soviet Union, still bound to Japan by the 1941 neutrality pact.

That approach was misconceived. The Soviet government had given notice in April that it would not renew the pact, had committed at Yalta to enter the war, and had no interest in mediating a settlement that would deny it gains in Manchuria, Sakhalin, and the Kurils. Japanese attempts to send the former prime minister Konoe Fumimaro to Moscow in July were met with delay, and Ambassador Satō Naotake in Moscow warned that vague proposals would fail. Allied code-breakers read much of this diplomatic traffic.

On July 26 the United States, Britain, and China issued the Potsdam Declaration from the conference in progress there. It demanded Japan's surrender, called for the elimination of the authority of those who had led Japan into war, announced occupation of designated points, limited Japanese sovereignty to the four main islands and minor islands to be determined, and promised disarmament, war-crimes trials, democratic tendencies, and eventual withdrawal of occupation forces once a peacefully inclined, responsible government had been established. It was silent on the emperor. The Soviet Union was not a signatory.

The cabinet's reply is a famous source of confusion. On July 28 Prime Minister Suzuki told reporters the government would mokusatsu the declaration, a word that can mean to treat with silence, to withhold comment, or to dismiss. Historians debate how much the word expressed a decision and how much an intention to wait for a Soviet response; the Allies and most of the world read it as rejection. The episode is a reminder that surrender was shaped by what the leadership could not say publicly as much as by what it could.

Primary Source Window

The Potsdam Declaration, July 26, 1945

Creator: The governments of the United States, the United Kingdom, and China (the Republic of China concurring by cable) · Date: July 26, 1945 · Type: Public proclamation of surrender terms · Provenance: English text reproduced in the National Diet Library's "Birth of the Constitution of Japan" collection and in Allied documentary compilations. · Original English proclamation

Context: The declaration was released while the Allied leaders met near Berlin and was broadcast and leafleted to Japan. It followed the Cairo Declaration (1943) and the German surrender and was issued without Soviet participation.

Editorial paraphrase, not a verbatim translation:The declaration warned that Japan faced overwhelming force and "prompt and utter destruction" unless its armed forces surrendered unconditionally. It called for removal of those who had misled the people into war, occupation of designated points, limitation of Japanese territory to the four main islands and minor islands to be decided, disarmament, punishment of war criminals, the removal of obstacles to democratic tendencies and respect for fundamental human rights, and withdrawal of occupation forces once these aims were met and a peacefully inclined and responsible government was established.

Analysis: The text shows what the Allies publicly offered; it does not show how Japanese leaders read it. Its silence on the emperor was the central ambiguity: it neither guaranteed nor threatened the institution, which let both peace and war factions project their fears and hopes onto it. It was also a propaganda document aimed at the Japanese public and at Allied audiences, and it says nothing of the atomic bomb, which was not yet used. The source cannot tell historians how Tokyo debated it; cabinet minutes, diaries, and memoirs are needed for that.

Questions for students:

  1. What does the declaration demand of Japan, and what does it promise?
  2. Who issued it, and who was missing from the list of signatories?
  3. What can historians learn from the declaration about Allied war aims, and what can they not learn about Japanese deliberations?
  4. Why might the omission of the imperial institution have been read differently by different Japanese leaders?
Section 81.2 · Division I

The August Crisis: Hiroshima, Soviet Entry, and Nagasaki

Chapter 80 describes the destruction of Hiroshima on August 6 and Nagasaki on August 9. This section asks a different question: how did those events and the Soviet declaration of war alter the calculations of the people who decided policy?

Information from Hiroshima reached Tokyo slowly and in fragments. Army and navy investigators were sent; the Army initially doubted that the weapon was atomic, even after President Truman's public statement of August 7. The decisive political reaction seems to have come not from the army's assessment but from the emperor and the foreign minister. On August 8 Tōgō reported to Hirohito, who is recorded as saying that the war must be ended quickly and that the opportunity should not be lost in disputes over terms. The cabinet, however, did not meet to decide until the next day.

On August 8 (Moscow time), Foreign Minister Molotov informed Ambassador Satō that the Soviet Union would consider itself at war with Japan from the next day. The Soviet offensive in Manchuria, known in Soviet histories as the August Storm, began in the early hours of August 9 local time, with well over a million troops advancing against a Kwantung Army that had been stripped of its best units and equipment for the defense of the home islands and the Pacific. The offensive collapsed organized resistance in much of Manchuria within days and opened the possibility of Soviet attacks on Korea, Sakhalin, and the Kurils.

Soviet entry destroyed two assumptions at once. The diplomatic assumption was that Moscow might mediate; Moscow was now an enemy. The strategic assumption was that Japan could use the continent as a base of resistance and negotiate from that position; the Kwantung Army was being broken. The Nagasaki bombing on the morning of August 9 added a second atomic attack and made it plausible that further bombs would follow.

It is therefore difficult to separate the factors cleanly. The atomic bombs gave civilian leaders a powerful argument that continued fighting was futile and gave the emperor a reason to intervene; Soviet entry removed Japan's last diplomatic option and endangered Japanese positions on the continent and the home islands' northern flank; and the army leadership, which had expected to fight for another stage on the main islands, was forced to confront conditions it had hoped to avoid. Historians disagree about the weight of each and the Historical Debate in Section 81.3 returns to the question.

The two shocks were not received in Tokyo through identical channels. Hiroshima was initially a communications and scientific problem: reports from a devastated city, an American announcement, and military investigators' assessments had to be fitted into existing assumptions about bombing and defensive operations. Soviet entry was immediately a diplomatic fact as well as a battlefield emergency. It rendered further approaches through Moscow pointless and compelled the foreign ministry to explain why the channel it had pursued in July had failed. This difference in the form of information can matter as much as the later debate over which event was 'more important': ministers argued with incomplete reports, and the military's institutional priorities filtered what each report appeared to imply.

The crisis also shows the limits of what the surviving evidence establishes. Contemporary records of the Supreme War Council and cabinet meetings, diaries such as Kido Kōichi's, and later memoirs by participants provide the main basis for reconstruction, and many of those memoirs were written to justify their authors' roles. Students should treat them as evidence about perceptions and arguments rather than as a transparent record of why decisions were made.

Section 81.3 · Division I

The Supreme War Council, Imperial Intervention, and the Acceptance of Surrender

On the morning of August 9 the six members of the Supreme War Council met to consider the Potsdam Declaration. Tōgō proposed acceptance on the sole condition that the declaration not prejudice the emperor's position as sovereign. Anami, Umezu, and Toyoda argued for three additional conditions: that Japan itself disarm and demobilize its forces, that it conduct its own war-crimes trials, and that there be no Allied occupation or only a minimal one. Suzuki and Yonai were inclined toward Tōgō's position. The council deadlocked three to three, and a cabinet meeting that followed was no more decisive.

Suzuki then took an unusual step. Late on August 9 he convened an imperial conference in the palace air-raid shelter, with the Privy Council president Hiranuma Kiichirō attending, and after the same disagreement was repeated he asked the emperor to resolve the question. In the early hours of August 10 Hirohito expressed his support for Tōgō's proposal. This is the "sacred decision" (seidan) of Japanese historiography. It was constitutionally irregular: the emperor ordinarily did not decide such questions, and the procedure worked only because the leaders had run out of institutional alternatives.

On August 10 Japan transmitted its offer to the Allies through Switzerland and Sweden, accepting Potsdam on the understanding that it did not prejudice the emperor's prerogatives as sovereign. Secretary of State James Byrnes's reply of August 11 did not guarantee the institution. It stated that from the moment of surrender the authority of the emperor and the government would be subject to the Supreme Commander, and that the ultimate form of Japan's government would be established by the freely expressed will of the Japanese people.

The reply reopened the argument. Tōgō and the court read it as compatible with the preservation of the institution; Anami, Umezu, and Toyoda read the phrase "subject to" as a threat to the kokutai and argued that Japan should ask for clarification or continue the war. The Supreme War Council deadlocked again on August 13, and the army's leadership was under pressure from officers who wanted to fight on. Allied aircraft dropped leaflets carrying the Japanese offer and the Allied reply, which made the exchange public and increased the pressure on the government.

On August 14 Hirohito convened a second imperial conference, attended by the cabinet and the military leaders, and again announced that he accepted the Allied reply, asking for a rescript that would explain his decision to the nation. The cabinet formally approved the rescript later that day. The army minister negotiated changes in the draft wording but did not prevent it.

The sequence of meetings shows why the cabinet, the Supreme Council for the Direction of the War, and the imperial conference cannot be treated as the same institution. The six-member council concentrated the decisive political and service leaders, but did not itself replace the cabinet's collective responsibility. Resort to the emperor allowed a deadlocked group to present acceptance as a command from the throne rather than as one faction defeating another. This preserved a route for officers to obey without publicly conceding their earlier judgment. It also exposed the weakness of a governing arrangement in which the army minister and the military chiefs could each block an agreement that other leaders believed essential. Anami ultimately helped transmit the decision while refusing to endorse the reasoning of his opponents.

The documentary record requires careful handling of dates and retrospective testimony. Private diaries, the intercepted exchanges with neutral governments, the text of the Byrnes reply, and post-surrender interrogations illuminate different parts of the decision. A recollection recorded after Allied occupation began is not worthless, but its author had reason to allocate credit and responsibility strategically. Hirohito's postwar account, prepared in an environment where his future and possible prosecution were at issue, is especially revealing about self-presentation. Students should compare contemporaneous diplomatic texts with later narrative recollections rather than assuming that any one source discloses the private intention of every participant.

What the evidence shows about imperial influence is both substantial and limited. The emperor's two interventions were decisive in resolving a deadlock that no formal body could break, and the government's acceptance was formulated as obedience to his will, which the army could not easily reject. But he acted within a process shaped by others, after the bombings and Soviet entry, in support of a position that the foreign minister, the prime minister, the Navy Minister, and the court had already prepared. The main sources are diaries by Kido and others, memoirs by Tōgō and Suzuki's secretary, and the "monologue" the emperor dictated to his staff in 1946, which was published in 1990; because the latter was produced in the context of the coming Tokyo trials, it must be read with caution.

Historical Debate

Why Did Japan Surrender When It Did?

The question: Which developments in early August 1945 best explain the decision to accept the Potsdam terms: the atomic bombings, the Soviet declaration of war, the imperial institution, or a combination?

Interpretation A — The atomic bombs as decisive shock: Some historians, among them Asada Sadao, emphasize that the bombings, especially Hiroshima, changed the terms of debate within the leadership, provided the emperor and peace advocates with a reason to act, and offered the army a face-saving explanation for abandoning the plan to fight on. They point to the timing of the emperor's first intervention and to the rescript's explicit reference to the new weapon.
Interpretation B — Soviet entry as decisive: Hasegawa Tsuyoshi and others argue that the Soviet declaration of war was the greater shock because it destroyed the mediation strategy and the continental position and raised the prospect of Soviet occupation. They note that leaders' own statements emphasized the Soviet threat and that Japan had already endured massive conventional bombing without surrendering.
Interpretation C — Emphasis on the emperor and the kokutai: Other historians, including Herbert Bix, stress that the central issue for Japan's leaders was preserving the imperial institution and the political order around it, and that the decision was shaped by the leadership's efforts to protect the emperor and its own position. Richard Frank and others emphasize the cumulative effect of the shocks combined with the diminishing prospects of Ketsu-Gō.

Evidence: Cabinet and council records, diaries, memoirs, intercepted diplomatic cables, Allied intelligence assessments, and the text of the rescript. Participants' memoirs often serve later purposes.

Why it matters: The answer shapes how historians interpret the use of atomic weapons, Soviet policy, the role of the emperor, and the political memory of the end of the war in Japan and elsewhere. Many scholars now treat the factors as interacting rather than as rivals.

Section 81.4 · Division I

The Kyūjō Incident, the Imperial Rescript, and the August 15 Broadcast

The decision of August 14 was followed immediately by an attempt to reverse it. A group of army officers in the Military Affairs Bureau, led by Major Hatanaka Kenji, believed that surrender could be prevented if the Imperial Guards were used to isolate the palace, seize the emperor's announcement, and force a continuation of the war. On the night of August 14 to 15 the conspirators sought the support of senior officers, and when Lieutenant General Mori Takeshi, commander of the First Imperial Guards Division, refused, they killed him and used his seal on forged orders.

Guards units occupied the palace grounds, cut communications, and searched for the recordings of the emperor's announcement made that night by NHK technicians; they did not find them, because palace officials had hidden them. The plot depended on the cooperation of senior army commanders, which it did not obtain. The commander of the Eastern District Army, Tanaka Shizuichi, went to the palace in the early morning of August 15 and ordered the troops to stand down. Hatanaka and another conspirator died by suicide later that morning. Elsewhere that night extremist officers attacked the residence of Prime Minister Suzuki and other targets, and Army Minister Anami died by suicide before dawn on August 15, leaving a note that, in the usual reading, expressed apology to the emperor.

At noon on August 15 NHK broadcast the recording. The emperor's voice, in formal court Japanese, was unfamiliar to most listeners, who had never heard it, and the classical diction was hard to follow; announcers explained the content afterward. The rescript itself had been signed on August 14, and the broadcast was made on August 15; the dates should not be conflated. The text did not use the word "surrender," referred to the enemy's "new and most cruel bomb," described the war aims as self-preservation and the stability of East Asia, disclaimed any intent to infringe on others' sovereignty or seek territory, and asked the people to endure hardship and to preserve the national polity.

Public reactions varied. Contemporary diaries and later memoirs record grief, shock, relief, anger, and numbness; some people, including officers, died by suicide, while others turned at once to practical concerns of food and shelter. Outside the main islands the broadcast meant something else. In Korea and Taiwan many people heard of the end of colonial rule with joy as well as uncertainty; Japanese settlers in the colonies and soldiers at the front often heard it in circumstances where the announcement did not end their danger.

The attempted seizure of the recordings demonstrated a final practical problem for the government: even the emperor's explicit decision could be obstructed by armed subordinates if senior commanders hesitated. The coup's failure owed less to unanimous agreement with surrender than to the refusal of crucial commanders to lend their authority to forged orders. Army discipline, personal loyalty to the throne, fear of civil conflict, and recognition of defeat operated together. Later dramatizations often reduce the night to a contest between a handful of heroes and fanatics; a historian must also ask how a wartime system dependent on military autonomy permitted a comparatively small group to come so close to interrupting a national announcement.

The announcement was also not the end of fighting. The Imperial General Headquarters issued a cease-fire order on August 16, and members of the imperial family were dispatched to carry the instruction to field commanders in China, Southeast Asia, and Korea. Some officers committed suicide rather than comply; the navy's Vice Admiral Ōnishi Takijirō, who had been associated with the special attack forces, died on August 16, and Vice Admiral Ugaki Matome led a final sortie after the broadcast. Where Soviet forces were advancing, combat continued: southern Sakhalin and the Kurils saw fighting for several days after August 15. The Suzuki cabinet resigned on August 15, and Prince Higashikuni Naruhiko formed a new cabinet on August 17 to carry out the surrender.

Section 81.5 · Division I

From Cease-Fire to Formal Surrender: Allied Arrival and September 2, 1945

Surrender required orders, communications, and negotiation. A delegation led by Lieutenant General Kawabe Torashirō flew to Manila on August 19–20 to receive instructions from General MacArthur's headquarters on the procedure for the occupation and the surrender. Advance parties of American personnel reached Atsugi airfield near Tokyo on August 28, and MacArthur landed there on August 30, with Japanese authorities having arranged transport and security. The first stage of the occupation of Japan itself was thus carried out through the cooperation of the Japanese government and military, a feature of the surrender that shaped what followed.

The formal surrender was signed on September 2 aboard USS Missouri in Tokyo Bay. Foreign Minister Shigemitsu Mamoru signed for the government and General Umezu Yoshijirō for Imperial General Headquarters, and MacArthur accepted for the Allied powers, with representatives of the United States, the Republic of China, the United Kingdom, the Soviet Union, and other Allied countries also signing. The instrument committed Japan to carry out the Potsdam terms, ordered all Japanese forces to cease hostilities and to surrender, placed the emperor's and the government's authority under the Supreme Commander, and required the government to preserve property and to carry out the Allies' instructions.

The same day, General Order No. 1 specified which Allied commander would receive the surrender of which Japanese forces. Japanese commanders in China (except Manchuria), Taiwan, and northern French Indochina would surrender to Chiang Kai-shek; those in Manchuria, northern Korea, Sakhalin, and the Kurils to the Soviet commander in the Far East; those in Southeast Asia to Admiral Mountbatten's South East Asia Command or Australian forces; and those in Japan, the Philippines, southern Korea, and the Ryukyus to U.S. commands. The order was in the first instance an Allied document implemented by Japanese headquarters.

A string of local ceremonies followed. Yamashita Tomoyuki, commander in the Philippines, surrendered in early September; Japanese forces in the Ryukyus surrendered at Kadena on September 7; the Japanese governor-general in Korea signed with American commanders on September 9; Okamura Yasuji surrendered the China Expeditionary Army at Nanjing on September 9; the surrender in Southeast Asia was signed in Singapore on September 12; and the surrender in Taiwan came on October 25. Each ceremony had its own politics and legal character. In places where Allied forces were few or arrived late, Japanese units kept their weapons and administrative functions for weeks, and sometimes longer, because there was no one to relieve them.

The administrative work of capitulation began before the ceremony in Tokyo Bay and varied sharply between locations. Where American or British forces could arrive rapidly, Japanese headquarters delivered records, marked minefields, helped recover prisoners, and surrendered installations under close supervision. Elsewhere commanders remained responsible for supplies, policing, and preventing the movement of armed units until a receiving force arrived. The Allied demand that Japanese commands preserve existing equipment and records did not mean that they retained political sovereignty: their authority was provisional, constrained, and vulnerable to immediate alteration. In China and Southeast Asia, instructions to await particular Allied representatives made questions about who could lawfully receive a surrender part of the struggle among rival political forces.

The September 2 instrument did not settle every question. It dealt with the armed forces and the government's subordination to the Supreme Commander, but it did not by itself determine the future sovereignty of Taiwan, Korea, Sakhalin, the Kurils, or the Ryukyus, the legal status of the colonial populations, or the treatment of those held by the Allies. Those questions would be resolved, or left unresolved, in later agreements. Imperial Japanese Army and Navy headquarters were abolished in the autumn of 1945, and the two services' ministries were replaced by demobilization ministries in December, as Section 81.10 explains.

Historical Vocabulary
Historical Vocabulary: Surrender, Armistice, Cease-Fire, and Demobilization

Surrender is the act by which a state or its forces submit to an enemy under agreed or imposed terms. In 1945 it included a government decision (August 14), a public announcement (August 15), orders to forces (from August 16), a signed instrument (September 2), and many local acts of disarmament. Armistice normally means a negotiated suspension of hostilities between belligerents who remain legally at war; the 1945 settlement was different, because the Potsdam terms demanded unconditional surrender of Japan's forces and the Allies did not negotiate the rest. A cease-fire is a halt to combat, which in this case did not occur simultaneously everywhere.

Demobilization is the release of military personnel from service and the dissolution of units; it is distinct from repatriation, the return of people to their country, because a soldier could be discharged while remaining overseas, and a civilian could be repatriated without ever having been in uniform. The English word "surrender" also masks a Japanese distinction: the government's term for the event was "ending of the war" (shūsen), which emphasized termination rather than defeat, while "defeat" (haisen) became the term for the postwar memory of the war. Which word a speaker used often signaled how they understood responsibility.

Section 81.6 · Division II — The Collapse of Japanese Colonial and Occupation Authority across Asia

Korea's Liberation and the Division of the Peninsula

For most Koreans, August 15, 1945 meant the end of thirty-five years of colonial rule that had begun with annexation in 1910 (Chapter 62). The colonial state had mobilized Korean labor, soldiers, and resources for the war, pressed assimilation policies, and relied on a large police and administrative apparatus; roughly 700,000 Japanese civilians lived in Korea at the end of the war. Its authority did not disappear instantly. In the days before the broadcast, Governor-General Abe Nobuyuki's administration sought to arrange an orderly transfer of authority with a Korean figure acceptable to local opinion, in part to protect Japanese residents and to prevent disorder. The nationalist Yeo Un-hyung (Lyuh Woon-hyung) accepted conditions and on August 15 organized the Committee for the Preparation of Korean Independence.

Across the peninsula, local committees and "people's committees" took over police, administration, and local order. In early September delegates in Seoul proclaimed a Korean People's Republic. These bodies reflected a wide range of political opinions, from nationalists to socialists and communists, and they were Korean initiatives, not creations of the occupying powers. Other nationalists, including leaders of the Korean Provisional Government who returned from China and figures such as Syngman Rhee who returned from the United States, disputed their claims to leadership.

The division of the peninsula was decided elsewhere. In mid-August American staff officers, with very little time, proposed the 38th parallel as the line separating the zones where Japanese forces would surrender to Soviet and American commanders; the Soviet government accepted it and its forces had already begun entering the north. General Order No. 1 incorporated the line. It was an Allied military convenience and not a boundary chosen by Koreans or reflecting any prior political division, although it soon hardened into a political one. American troops under General John Hodge landed at Incheon on September 8, and the Japanese governor-general signed the surrender in Seoul on September 9.

American policy initially retained some Japanese officials and police to maintain order, a decision that provoked bitter Korean protest and was reversed within days. The U.S. Army Military Government in Korea declined to recognize the People's Republic and in the south relied on conservative Korean politicians and administrators, many of whom had served in the colonial government, a continuity that remained politically explosive. In the north the Soviet command worked with local committees and with Korean communist figures, including Kim Il Sung, whose guerrilla and Soviet army connections later made him the leading figure.

Local Korean initiatives reveal why the peninsula's history cannot be described simply as a transfer from one foreign army to another. Organizers in cities and rural districts sought to release prisoners, stabilize food supplies, control police stations, and replace colonial officials. Their committees often drew on networks of labor activism, nationalist education, and anti-Japanese resistance that preceded August 1945. Their membership and authority differed by locality, and they were divided over the balance between social transformation and immediate restoration of a unified government. The Korean People's Republic proclaimed in September reflected one claim to legitimacy; it did not possess universal acceptance, and it was not equivalent to the Democratic People's Republic of Korea established in 1948.

Occupation policy introduced a second and increasingly decisive political timetable. Soviet and American officers arrived with different military assignments, strategic assumptions, and relationships with Korean political organizations. In the south the American military government confronted strikes, demands for land reform, shortages, and disputes over the continued employment of people associated with colonial rule. In the north Soviet authorities supported the reorganization of local institutions while shaping the balance of parties and the security apparatus. Both zones had active Korean political actors, but neither allowed them a free and unified settlement of the peninsula's future. The boundary at the 38th parallel began as an instruction about Japanese surrender, then became a line controlling travel, supplies, institutions, and political opportunity.

The December 1945 Moscow conference of the Allied foreign ministers proposed a trusteeship for Korea, which produced fierce Korean opposition and political polarization in the south. A joint U.S.–Soviet commission failed, and in 1948 separate states were established: the Republic of Korea in August and the Democratic People's Republic of Korea in September. Liberation, in short, was not the same as independence or unity. Students should distinguish the end of colonial rule in 1945 from the creation of two governments three years later and from the Korean War that followed in 1950.

Section 81.7 · Division II

Manchukuo, the Kwantung Army, and the End of Japanese Power in Northeast China

The Soviet offensive that began on August 9 struck Manchukuo from three directions. The Kwantung Army, nominally some 700,000 men, had been hollowed out by transfers of units to the Pacific and by a hasty mobilization of reservists and settlers in the summer of 1945; its heavy equipment and training were inadequate against mechanized Soviet forces. Resistance was concentrated in pockets and, after August 15, in places where commanders had not yet received or did not at once accept the cease-fire. By the time formal surrender negotiations were completed, Soviet forces held the principal cities and rail lines.

Manchukuo, founded in 1932 and presided over by Puyi, had depended on Japanese officials and the Kwantung Army at every level (Chapter 75). Its collapse was therefore swift. Puyi abdicated at Tonghua on August 18, and Soviet forces captured him when he attempted to fly to Japan. He later testified at the Tokyo trial and was returned to China in 1950. Senior Manchukuo officials were arrested or dispersed, and Japanese personnel in the state's administration, industry, and railways lost their posts and, often, their freedom.

The Soviet occupation of Manchuria had its own aims. Soviet authorities removed industrial equipment as war booty and reparations, an extensive dismantling of plants built under Japanese rule, and detained large numbers of Japanese soldiers and civilians, as Section 81.14 explains. They also operated within a diplomatic framework: the Sino-Soviet Treaty of August 14, 1945 recognized the Nationalist government as China's legitimate authority. In practice, Soviet forces facilitated the entry of Chinese Communist forces and delayed the arrival of Nationalist troops, and Soviet withdrawal in the spring of 1946 left Manchuria as the principal region of the Chinese Civil War.

The rapid military defeat also exposed how uneven the state of Manchukuo had always been. Japanese administrators and industrial managers operated alongside local elites, migrant workers, farmers, and the subjects of a nominally sovereign court, but real coercive resources remained concentrated in the Japanese army and police. When that military guarantee disappeared, the institutions built around it could not command obedience on their own. Railway junctions and industrial cities became prizes not merely because they were symbolic capitals of empire but because they controlled transportation, machinery, food distribution, and communications across a region larger than many European states.

The Chinese political contest should not be collapsed into a simple Soviet transfer of territory to one side. Nationalist and Communist authorities both asserted claims to Manchuria; local associations, workers, and village communities pursued their own immediate concerns amid hunger, reprisals, and the disruption of commerce. Some Japanese technical specialists were kept to operate railways, hospitals, and industrial plant because new administrations lacked equivalent trained personnel. Retention did not mean that the Japanese empire continued legally, nor that all specialists stayed voluntarily. It meant that personnel once employed in a colonial system could be reassigned, sometimes coercively, during the construction of successor regimes.

Chinese local populations experienced the end of Japanese power as liberation from occupation and also as disorder. Many had suffered land expropriation, forced labor, and violence under Japanese rule, and some Chinese attacked Japanese settlers and looted settlements in the weeks after surrender, while other Chinese families sheltered and adopted Japanese children. A number of Japanese technicians, doctors, railway workers, and others were retained, sometimes under pressure, by Chinese Nationalist or Communist authorities because their skills were needed. Section 81.13 returns to the experience of Japanese settlers; this section emphasizes that the end of Manchukuo was a sequence of political and military changes in which several Chinese, Soviet, and Japanese actors competed, not a single hand-over.

Section 81.8 · Division II

Taiwan, Southern Sakhalin, the Kurils, and Okinawa: Different Outcomes of Imperial Collapse

Taiwan had been a Japanese colony since 1895. On October 25, 1945, in a ceremony in Taipei, Governor-General Ando Rikichi signed the surrender to Chen Yi, the Republic of China's representative, and the Nationalist government announced that it was resuming sovereignty over Taiwan. The Cairo Declaration of 1943 and the Potsdam Declaration had stated that Taiwan would be returned to China, and the Allies' General Order No. 1 assigned the surrender of Japanese forces in Taiwan to Chiang Kai-shek. Many Taiwanese welcomed the end of Japanese rule, and some 200,000 Taiwanese had served the Japanese military as soldiers or civilian employees. About 300,000 or more Japanese civilians and a large military garrison remained and were repatriated in 1946, with a number of technical personnel retained for a time.

The Nationalist administration quickly alienated many Taiwanese through corruption, economic mismanagement, and discrimination, and the island erupted in the February 1947 uprising and its violent suppression. Legally, the handover of administration was clear in practice but the question of sovereignty was not closed. Japan renounced its claim to Taiwan in the 1951 San Francisco treaty without naming a recipient, and the Republic of China and the People's Republic of China, as well as other scholars and governments, have since offered different legal interpretations of the island's status. Students should distinguish the military surrender of 1945, the administrative takeover, the treaty renunciation, and the contested question of sovereignty.

Southern Sakhalin (Karafuto) and the Kurils followed a different course. Soviet forces crossed the 50th parallel into Karafuto on August 11 and continued to fight after August 15, including battles on the southern coast; an often-recounted episode is the suicide of a group of female telephone operators at Maoka in the last days of the fighting. In the Kurils, fighting on Shumshu on August 18 to 23 was followed by Soviet occupation of the island chain, completed in early September, including the southern islands that Japan today claims as the Northern Territories. The Soviet government annexed the territories in 1946; Japanese residents, some hundreds of thousands in Karafuto and about 17,000 in the Kurils, were removed through unofficial flight and then organized repatriation between 1946 and 1949. Koreans who had been brought to Sakhalin as laborers were largely left behind. Japan renounced Karafuto and the Kurils in 1951, again without naming a recipient, and the dispute over the southern Kurils has continued.

Okinawa was different again. It was a Japanese prefecture, and the Battle of Okinawa (Chapter 80) had ended in June. American forces had already established military government there, and Japanese forces in the Ryukyus surrendered at Kadena on September 7. Okinawans were held in camps, and the islands passed under U.S. military administration, in which Japan retained what Washington called "residual sovereignty" until the reversion of 1972 (Chapter 90). Okinawan history from 1945 therefore diverges from that of the main islands, with long-term consequences for bases, land, and identity.

A useful comparison is between military control, civil administration, and territorial title. In Taiwan the Republic of China took over governing functions after receiving Japanese surrender, while postwar treaty instruments later addressed Japan's renunciation of the island without providing a simple, universally accepted answer to every sovereignty argument. In southern Sakhalin and the Kurils, Soviet soldiers established control through operations begun in August, and Soviet institutions replaced Japanese ones. In Okinawa, the United States retained separate military administration despite Okinawa's long-standing status as a Japanese prefecture. These transitions were geographically simultaneous in 1945 but rested on different combinations of battlefield control, Allied orders, later diplomacy, and competing legal claims.

The societies affected were not interchangeable. Taiwanese families could view the end of Japanese colonial schools and police with relief while rapidly discovering new forms of administrative coercion. Residents of southern Sakhalin included Japanese settlers and Koreans who had been brought there under Japanese rule; their postwar mobility and nationality options were not the same. Okinawans had endured catastrophic fighting and experienced the postwar occupation under conditions distinct from those of the Japanese main islands. For that reason, a map shaded simply 'lost to Japan in 1945' hides more than it reveals unless students ask who governed each place, who could leave, and which treaties later defined its status.

The four cases illustrate why the end of empire cannot be summarized as a single transfer. A military handover (Taiwan, October 1945), effective administration (the Soviet Union in Karafuto and the Kurils), a formal treaty renunciation (1951–52), and a settled sovereignty are different things, and for some territories they did not coincide.

Section 81.9 · Division II

Southeast Asia after Japanese Defeat: Independence Movements and Returning Colonial Powers

In Southeast Asia, Japanese occupation (from 1941–42) had destroyed the prewar colonial order and mobilized nationalist movements, sometimes cooperating with them and sometimes repressing them. The end of the war created a vacuum before returning colonial powers could reestablish control, and nationalists acted inside it.

In the Dutch East Indies, Indonesian nationalists had prepared for independence under Japanese sponsorship, but on August 15 younger activists pressed Sukarno and Mohammad Hatta to proclaim it without waiting for Japanese approval. After the Rengasdengklok episode of August 16, they proclaimed the independence of Indonesia in Jakarta on August 17. British forces arrived late in September, and the Netherlands sought to restore its rule, leading to the Battle of Surabaya in November and a revolution that lasted until 1949. Under British orders, Japanese units remained armed for a time to maintain order and were classified as Surrendered Japanese Personnel, and a number of Japanese soldiers deserted to fight with the Indonesians.

In Vietnam, the Viet Minh seized power in the August Revolution after the Japanese coup of March 1945 had overthrown the French administration. Emperor Bảo Đại abdicated on August 25, and Hồ Chí Minh declared independence in Hanoi on September 2. The surrender of Japanese forces was assigned by General Order No. 1 to Chinese Nationalist troops north of the 16th parallel and British-led forces south of it. In the south, British and Indian troops used Japanese troops at times to restore order in Saigon, and the French returned; the war that followed began from these conditions.

Burma's path differed. Aung San's Burma National Army had turned against the Japanese in March 1945, and after the Japanese surrender the British returned, negotiated with nationalist leaders, and granted independence in January 1948. In Malaya, the largely Chinese anti-Japanese guerrillas gave way to a British military administration, and the surrender in Singapore was signed on September 12. The Philippines, liberated by American and Filipino forces in a campaign that devastated Manila, saw the dissolution of the Japanese-sponsored republic and independence from the United States on July 4, 1946. Thailand, nominally allied with Japan, was treated by the Allies as a special case.

In Indonesia, Japanese military surrender coincided with the proclamation by Sukarno and Hatta, but the republican state still needed local support, functioning administration, access to arms, and recognition. Young activists pressed established nationalist leaders to move quickly and disputed how Japanese authorities should be treated. British forces initially arrived to accept surrender and recover prisoners and internees, while Dutch authorities attempted to restore colonial power. The resulting struggle was not simply a continuation of Japanese policy: Indonesian political organizations and fighters made independent decisions in a situation where Allied commands themselves disagreed about immediate priorities.

In Vietnam, the August Revolution brought Việt Minh organizations to power in Hanoi and other centers as Japanese authority broke down, and Hồ Chí Minh announced independence on September 2. North and south then came under different Allied arrangements for disarming Japanese forces; Chinese Nationalist troops entered the north and British-led forces entered the south, where French authority was reintroduced amid conflict. In Burma, the anti-Japanese resistance and the politics of independence developed on yet another path; in the Philippines, a commonwealth government was being restored after a devastating occupation. These contrasts demonstrate that the timing of a Japanese withdrawal alone cannot explain what followed. Local wartime politics, preexisting colonial institutions, international military arrangements, and the capacity of nationalist movements were decisive variables.

Allied prisoners of war and civilian internees were also liberated across the region after months or years of captivity, often in poor health; their experiences are treated in Chapters 78 and 79. For Southeast Asians, the end of Japanese occupation marked the end of a brutal regime and the beginning of struggles over who would govern. Historians therefore reject the idea that Japanese defeat simply "handed" independence to Asian nationalists: the movements had their own leaders, strategies, and constituencies, and Japanese policy affected them in contradictory ways.

Section 81.10 · Division III — Demobilization, Repatriation, Captivity, and the Return of Imperial Populations

Disarming the Imperial Army and Navy: Demobilization at Home and Abroad

Demobilization began in Japan under Japanese administration but Allied orders. Imperial General Headquarters was abolished in the autumn of 1945, and in December the Army and Navy Ministries were replaced by a First and a Second Demobilization Ministry whose task was to wind up the services, settle accounts, and manage the discharge and return of personnel. Inside the home islands, where several million men were under arms, units were disarmed, weapons and equipment collected, and men discharged with small payments, rations, and railway passes. The process was rapid, but it was also messy: large stocks of military supplies disappeared into private hands or black markets, and the diversion of such goods became a public scandal (Chapter 85).

Abroad, demobilization meant something else. Japanese units there were disarmed in place by the Allied command responsible for the area, concentrated in camps or assigned areas, and held until transport could be arranged or a decision was made about their disposition. The Allies distinguished prisoners of war, who had rights under the Geneva Convention, from categories such as "Surrendered Japanese Personnel" (used in the South East Asia Command) and "Japanese Enemy Personnel," which allowed them to be kept in military formations and used for labor and administration. In Southeast Asia, Japanese troops were used to guard installations, repair infrastructure, clear mines, and maintain order, sometimes for more than a year.

In China, the China Expeditionary Army under General Okamura Yasuji, numbering more than a million men, surrendered to Nationalist authorities. The Nationalist government, anxious to reoccupy cities and rail lines ahead of the Communists and short of its own forces, kept some Japanese units armed and on guard duty for a time, and some individuals and small groups remained with Nationalist or Communist forces as technicians, instructors, or soldiers. In Shanxi province, for instance, a Japanese foreign-ministry historical study identifies approximately 2,600 former First Army officers and soldiers who remained with Yan Xishan after surrender; wider secondary estimates use different categories and should not be treated as equivalent. See the source register for the ministry study and specialist research by Gillin and Etter and by King and Muminov. These cases are exceptional, but they show that surrender did not mean an immediate return to civilian life.

At home, discharge did not always take place in a single ceremony. Units needed to identify personnel, account for weapons and supplies, distribute papers, and direct men toward railway routes and relatives who might themselves have been displaced. Military employment had organized pay, food, rank, and medical attention for millions; the transition shifted those needs toward civilian households and overburdened local authorities. War injuries or prolonged illness complicated the process, and former officers were not necessarily treated in the same way as conscripts. Thus the end of the armed services could be swift as an institutional act and slow as an individual reentry into ordinary life.

Abroad, retention under arms had several meanings and must not be interpreted as a uniform continuation of Japanese military policy. The British used surrendered Japanese personnel under supervision for temporary security duties in parts of Southeast Asia, while in China disarmament was shaped by the armed contest between Nationalists and Communists. Some Japanese soldiers remained attached to local Chinese forces or were deliberately retained for their expertise; others were held as prisoners, or merely awaited shipping. A study by Donald G. Gillin and Charles Etter, and subsequent archival research by Amy King and Sherzod Muminov, documents Japanese military and civilian personnel who stayed in China after surrender. The ministry’s narrower count for Shanxi therefore cannot be substituted uncritically for some much larger estimates in later scholarship. Their history must not be used to suggest that most surrendered troops fought on.

Distinguishing release from physical return is essential. A soldier discharged from service in Singapore, Mukden, or Manila might remain in a labor camp for months or years; a soldier captured by the Soviet Union might be taken to Siberia; a soldier in Korea or Taiwan might be among the first to leave. Allied priorities, the availability of shipping, and the political character of each zone determined the sequence. The following sections describe the machinery that moved them and the civilians who accompanied or preceded them.

Section 81.11 · Division III

The Machinery of Repatriation: Shipping, Ports, Quarantine, and Government Administration

The Allied powers made the return of Japanese forces and civilians a matter of policy, partly to disarm Japan's forces and partly to relieve the occupying armies of supervising them. In the autumn of 1945 the Allied command issued directives governing the movement, and the Japanese government, which lacked ships, fuel, and authority abroad, set up agencies to receive and process returnees. A repatriation relief agency under the Ministry of Health and Welfare coordinated the work, relying on prefectural governments, local officials, and volunteer groups.

The Japanese state designated a number of ports of entry; the Maizuru Repatriation Memorial Museum reports eighteen receiving ports. Among the most important were Hakata and Sasebo in Kyushu, Senzaki in Yamaguchi, Kure, Maizuru on the Japan Sea coast, Hakodate in Hokkaido, and Uraga near Tokyo. Each became the site of a reception center. Shipping was the central constraint. Most of Japan's merchant fleet had been sunk, and the Allies lent American ships, including landing ships and Liberty ships, and permitted the use of surviving Japanese vessels. Many returnees traveled in crowded, unsanitary conditions, with limited food and water.

On arrival, returnees underwent a standardized process. Medical staff examined them, and quarantine procedures including disinfection with insecticide powder were applied, with ships from some areas held offshore because of disease. Cholera and typhus outbreaks, especially in 1946, made quarantine a serious public-health matter. Personnel recorded names and destinations, and authorities distributed food, a small amount of cash (the amount of money and baggage that returnees could bring from abroad was strictly limited by Allied rules), and railway tickets for onward travel. Local groups provided meals and shelter, and volunteers helped with lost children and missing relatives.

The first administrative challenge was to distinguish who was waiting to return and who could be transported immediately. Military headquarters compiled unit rolls and surrendered-personnel lists; civilian offices attempted to register households, dependents, deaths, and missing relatives. These registers were necessarily incomplete after bombing, flight, and the destruction of records. Allied commands controlled ocean transport and movement across former battle zones, while Japanese institutions handled much of the reception work at home. A vessel assigned to carry troops from one theater could not always be diverted to evacuate families in another, and decisions about priority reflected military security as much as humanitarian urgency. The contrast between the speed of return from some American-controlled areas and the prolonged Soviet internment illustrates why one nationwide average obscures radically different experiences.

Reception ports functioned as intersections of maritime logistics and public health. After disembarkation, returnees were counted, questioned, screened for contagious disease, and sometimes quarantined, disinfected, or referred for hospital treatment. Officials issued basic assistance and organized onward movement by railway, but could not guarantee a surviving home or a relative waiting at the destination. Families might become separated again when illness delayed one member's release. A child's identity could be difficult to establish if parents had died and papers were missing. The physical geography of Maizuru, Sasebo, Hakata, and other ports thus shaped an administrative record: the documents created there tell historians much about ships and processed persons, but less about those who died en route or never reached an embarkation point.

Transportation capacity was constantly renegotiated. Japanese merchant shipping had been devastated, coastal waters required minesweeping, and the movement of occupation forces and prisoners of war competed with the movement of civilians. An American diplomatic report from 1946 describes how repatriation schedules from northern China had to conform to port capacities and available vessels, rather than merely to the wishes of Japanese families. Quarantine and weather could postpone an onward voyage even when a ship existed. Museums later memorialized arrivals at particular ports, but their totals must be read as local cumulative records. The approximately 660,000 arrivals at Maizuru through 1958, for example, overlap with broader repatriation statistics and do not represent an additional population to add to the national total.

The scale of the operation was extraordinary. JACAR estimates that approximately 6.6 million Japanese military personnel and civilians were overseas at the end of the war and that about 6.24 million had returned by the end of 1947. Shipping, port capacity, and Allied priorities set the pace: in some areas, such as Taiwan and much of southern Korea, large numbers left in a few months, while in Manchuria and Soviet-held areas the return was slower or blocked. The system was administratively impressive and it should not be romanticized; it was also a managed process in which individuals had very little choice about when and by what route they traveled.

Section 81.12 · Division III

Japanese Civilians Leaving Korea, Taiwan, China, and Other Former Colonial Territories

The Japanese civilians in the colonies were not a uniform group. Colonial officials, police, teachers, company employees, merchants, small shopkeepers, and farmers had occupied very different positions in the colonial order, and they encountered very different circumstances at its end. Many had lived their whole lives in Korea or Taiwan; some were born there. Their property, jobs, and administrative privileges were lost, and the colonial ties of language, community, and status were dissolved.

In southern Korea, departures began soon after the surrender, and most Japanese civilians left within months, in a process organized by Japanese residents' associations and the American military government. Many were able to leave relatively quickly, with limited belongings. In northern Korea, the situation was harder. Soviet authorities restricted movement, many Japanese were housed in makeshift camps, and in the winter of 1945–46 hunger and typhus killed many; thousands tried to walk south across the parallel before the border was closed. In Taiwan, the transfer of administration was comparatively orderly and Japanese civilians were repatriated by the spring of 1946, though some technical staff were retained.

In China, the return of Japanese civilians from the Nationalist-controlled cities, Shanghai, and the north was organized by Chinese and American authorities, with large numbers leaving through ports such as Shanghai, Tianjin, and Qingdao in 1946. Southeast Asia saw civilians interned or placed in camps before shipment home. In Karafuto, where Soviet authorities held the territory, people left first by their own means in small boats and then in organized transport after the 1946 U.S.–Soviet agreement.

For Japanese settlers in Korea, the practical end of colonial privilege could be abrupt. Land, housing, enterprises, and schools that had depended on imperial authority became contested, and residents faced new Korean officials and neighbors whose experiences of colonial rule differed sharply from their own. Departure policies were affected by American or Soviet occupation arrangements as well as by local conditions. Some residents sold goods at reduced prices, exchanged possessions for transport money, or left property behind because movement was restricted. Families with young children or elderly relatives encountered different risks from officials traveling with unit or agency documents; no single story of 'evacuation from Korea' captures the range.

Taiwan presents a contrasting administrative sequence. The Republic of China took over Japanese offices in late 1945, and repatriation occurred chiefly during 1946 under negotiations involving Chinese and American authorities. JACAR's anniversary exhibit summarizes Japanese relief-office records indicating that roughly 300,000 civilians were returned in the principal spring 1946 movement, with further departures later, while other official tallies include military personnel and use different cutoff dates. The figures should not be treated as a precise snapshot of everyone living on the island in August 1945. Some technicians and medical personnel were retained temporarily to operate essential services. Residents' departures therefore reflected administrative decisions about which work could be transferred and which could not simply be interrupted.

In occupied mainland China, the route out depended on where a family lived and on the increasingly contested railway and port system. Japanese residents of Shanghai or northern treaty-port cities were not in the same position as agricultural migrants stranded far inside Manchuria. Chinese authorities controlled many embarkation points, and Allied shipping arrangements imposed limits on passengers and possessions. An American diplomatic memorandum of May 1946 reported that organized civilian returns from Manchuria had not yet begun, while substantial numbers had already left other Chinese regions. Such dated reports allow historians to test claims that 'the Japanese' were returned promptly: the answer depends on location, political authority, available ships, and the date at which the question is asked.

The categories used by historians matter here. Organized repatriation refers to the movement of people under Allied and Japanese arrangements; evacuation refers to emergency movement during or just after hostilities; expulsion refers to forced removal by a local authority; individual migration refers to people who left on their own, sometimes before official channels existed. Japanese colonial residents experienced all of these. What they shared was the loss of the position they had held in the empire, which was built on the subordination of the people among whom they lived; recognition of that fact does not diminish the hardship of the journey, but it frames the history of departure in the context of colonial rule.

Historical Vocabulary
Historical Vocabulary: Repatriation (Hikiage), Nationality, and Displacement

Repatriation in English implies return to one's homeland. The Japanese term hikiage (literally "drawing back") was used by the government for the return of both soldiers and civilians, and was distinguished from fukuin (demobilization). The term hikiagesha came to designate the returnees as a social category. The English word can mislead, because some of those "returned" had been born abroad and had never lived in Japan.

Nationality is a legal relationship between a person and a state; in the empire, colonial subjects of Japan were Japanese nationals in some senses but not in others, with differing rights in citizenship, suffrage, and registration (koseki). Displacement describes forced or constrained movement from home, including by flight, deportation, and detention. Historians use these three terms to distinguish who moved, why, and under whose authority, and the same person could be described by more than one.

Section 81.13 · Division III

The Manchurian Settler Catastrophe: Flight, Death, Separation, and Children Left Behind

More than a million Japanese civilians lived in Manchuria in 1945, including some 270,000 members of state-sponsored agricultural settlement groups, the kaitakudan. The settlement program, expanded after 1936 with the aim of sending a million households in twenty years, had been an instrument of colonization: the land on which settlers were placed had often been acquired from Chinese and Korean farmers at low prices or by pressure, and the settlements were intended to serve both economic and strategic purposes (Chapter 75). In the summer of 1945, as the army mobilized reserves, many settler men were conscripted, and the settlements were left with women, children, and the elderly.

When the Soviet offensive began, authorities and the Kwantung Army gave priority to military and official evacuations, and many settlers received little warning. Accounts, including those of survivors and later scholarship, describe settlement groups walking for weeks toward cities and rail lines under attack by Soviet troops and by local people, in conditions of hunger, exhaustion, and disease. Some groups chose collective suicide. Others were attacked or plundered. Many were taken to camps in cities such as Changchun, Harbin, and Mukden, where in the winter of 1945–46 typhus and starvation killed large numbers. Estimates of Japanese civilian deaths in Manchuria in 1945–46 vary with the definition and the source, with estimates varying widely because some studies count only agricultural settlers and others count all Japanese civilians and later mortality. Published estimates cannot be compared without identifying the population and period used.

Families were separated in the chaos. Parents who could not feed or carry their children sometimes sold or gave them to Chinese families, and Chinese households sometimes took in abandoned or orphaned Japanese children and raised them. Young Japanese women, in order to survive, married or lived with Chinese men. These people, known in Japanese as zanryū koji (children left behind) and zanryū fujin (women left behind), remained in China for decades. Normalization of relations between Japan and China in 1972 and later programs beginning in the 1980s allowed many to visit or resettle in Japan, though they faced difficulties with identification, language, citizenship, and social support, and later litigation over government responsibility.

The central evacuation of Manchuria began in 1946. Under an arrangement involving the Chinese Nationalist government and the American military, more than a million Japanese passed through the port of Huludao that year and were shipped to Japan. The operation brought many to safety but it came after a winter in which thousands died.

Settlement policy helps explain why this disaster was so severe. During the 1930s and war years the Japanese state encouraged farming colonies in parts of Manchuria, presenting them as opportunities for impoverished rural households and as instruments of imperial expansion. The settlements occupied contested agricultural land and existed within a coercive political order imposed on Chinese and other local populations. Their residents included adults who made choices under powerful economic pressures and children who made none. Colonial authorities advertised villages as protected by the army; yet military priorities in 1945 concentrated on defending strategic lines and on withdrawing key personnel, not on providing reliable transport for every isolated settlement. Mariko Asano Tamanoi's research on settlers and children documents the mismatch between the promises of protection and the actual circumstances of flight.

The most dangerous journeys were shaped by distance and information. Rumors of a Soviet advance might reach one settlement before any authoritative evacuation instruction arrived. Trains and carts were insufficient, bridges and roads could be blocked, and groups walking with children could not move at military speed. Reports of attacks and sexual violence are well documented in some localities, but testimony collected afterward cannot automatically be generalized to every village. Hunger, exposure, and disease were additional major causes of death. Families sometimes arranged for Chinese households to take children in the hope that a child could survive when adults could not. Such acts created enduring bonds, but also difficult questions of name, language, adoption, and eventual return.

Death estimates require a defined denominator. Studies alternately count agricultural settlers, all Japanese civilians in Manchuria, or civilians who died during flight and the months before organized evacuation; they also use different registration sources. Some published estimates for the broader civilian catastrophe exceed one hundred thousand, whereas narrower studies of specific settler groups give different figures. Rather than silently combining them, students should identify whether a number refers to registered settlers, all residents, an individual prefectural colony, or postwar mortality over several years. What remains secure is the catastrophic scale of civilian loss and the responsibility of the colonial settlement system for placing vulnerable families in territories whose protection the Japanese state could not sustain.

Interpreting this history requires several things at once. The settlers' suffering was real and the abandonment by the military and administrative authorities was a major reason for it; scholars have debated how far this constituted deliberate abandonment and how far it reflected the collapse of the army. At the same time, the settlers were part of a colonial project, and Chinese communities were its victims before they were the actors who sometimes harmed, and sometimes saved, Japanese settlers. Postwar Japanese memory has often emphasized settler suffering while the prior colonization has received less attention; this chapter tries to hold the two together.

Section 81.14 · Division III

Soviet Internment: Captivity, Forced Labor, and Delayed Repatriation

Soviet forces took large numbers of Japanese prisoners in Manchuria, northern Korea, Karafuto, and the Kurils. In August 1945 the Soviet leadership decided to use Japanese prisoners as labor, and the Japanese soldiers were not sent home but transported by rail to camps across Siberia, Central Asia, the Far East, and Mongolia. Estimates of the number deported vary, generally in the range of 575,000 to 600,000 from Japanese and Russian sources, and some civilians were included. The men were told at first that they were going home or to nearby work.

The camps were built on sites with extreme cold and limited supplies. Prisoners felled timber, built railways and roads, mined coal and other minerals, and worked in construction. Rations were low and clothing inadequate, and in the first winter many died of cold, malnutrition, and disease. Estimates of deaths range from about 55,000 to over 60,000, with some Japanese estimates higher; the figures remain debated because of differing definitions and records. The Japanese term Shiberia yokuryū (Siberian internment) has come to cover this entire experience, even though the camps extended well beyond Siberia.

Camp life included political re-education. Soviet authorities promoted a "democratization movement" among prisoners, with study groups and a Japanese-language newspaper published in Khabarovsk, and they rewarded cooperation with better rations and earlier release while punishing those labeled reactionary. Conflicts among prisoners over denunciation and ideology left scars. Communication with families was restricted at first, and families in Japan often knew nothing of whether their men were alive.

Repatriation began after a December 1946 agreement between the United States and the Soviet Union, and the main flows occurred in 1947–1949, with prisoners shipped from Nakhodka to Japanese ports, above all Maizuru. In 1950 the Soviet government announced that repatriation had been completed except for a number held as war criminals or convicts, a claim the Japanese government and families disputed. Returns resumed after Stalin's death and were concluded after the Japan–Soviet Joint Declaration of 1956, with some arrivals at Maizuru continuing into 1958. These late returns kept the war's consequences present in Japanese life through the 1950s.

The process of detention was not reducible to one moment of capture. Soviet forces processed surrendered Japanese soldiers and military-associated civilians in Manchuria and other occupied areas, assembled them in collection points, and transported many by rail to labor camps distributed across Siberia, Central Asia, and Soviet-controlled territories. Camp assignments and mortality depended on season, work site, disease, food availability, and the physical condition of prisoners upon arrival. The commonly repeated figure of roughly 575,000–600,000 detainees refers to a particular reconstructed population and is not interchangeable with every Soviet count of captured personnel. Andrew E. Barshay's history of the Siberian internment describes a somewhat larger wartime surrender and captivity population and emphasizes the limits of retrospective administrative accounting.

Conditions were especially dangerous during the initial winters, when inadequate clothing, sanitation, and nutrition combined with exhausting forestry, mining, construction, and other work. Mortality estimates commonly run to tens of thousands, often stated as approximately 55,000–60,000 or more, but methods differ over the inclusion of deaths outside camp registers and the handling of missing persons. The history is not solely one of deprivation. Japanese inmates organized forms of mutual support and conflict; Soviet political education sought to reshape their loyalties, while some prisoners were pressed to denounce former officers. Later memoirs described both the coercion of these programs and disagreements among the Japanese themselves. Camp poetry and artwork preserve experiences that official labor reports scarcely record.

Releases unfolded in stages conditioned by Soviet policy, Japanese demands, and Cold War diplomacy. The U.S.–Soviet repatriation agreement of December 1946 enabled large-scale return, but releases were uneven and some prisoners were held for many additional years. Maizuru became especially associated with later arrivals because its harbor, rail connections, and reception arrangements were used as other receiving ports closed. For families waiting in Japan, the absence of a letter or an official name on a transport list did not necessarily distinguish death from detention. Historians must therefore read Soviet administrative records alongside Japanese household petitions, survivors' accounts, and cemetery documentation, accepting that some individual fates remain uncertain.

Soviet detention has often been described in Japan as a violation of the terms of the Potsdam Declaration, which promised Japanese soldiers the chance to return home to peaceful lives. Historians note that the Soviet use of prisoner labor followed a common wartime pattern, including Germans held in the Soviet Union and the Japanese treatment of prisoners and conscripted workers (Chapter 79), and that the principal responsibility for the camps lay with Soviet authorities. The experience is part of the history of imperial collapse because the soldiers were held for what remained of the Japanese empire's war.

Section 81.15 · Division III

Koreans, Taiwanese, Chinese, and Other Former Colonial Subjects: Return, Remaining, and Belonging

Just as Japanese were stranded overseas, former colonial subjects were stranded in Japan. At the end of the war about two million Koreans lived in Japan, according to estimates cited by Stanford University's Freeman Spogli Institute, including hundreds of thousands brought as mobilized laborers (Chapter 79). Many wished to return home, and in the first months after the surrender large numbers did so, often through the ports of Hakata and Senzaki, in overcrowded ships and with severe limits on what they could carry. Shipping was scarce and some boats were lost; the sinking of the Ukishima Maru in Maizuru Bay on August 24, 1945, killed hundreds of Koreans and others on board, with the cause and the number of victims still disputed between Japanese official counts and Korean and other estimates.

Estimates indicate that between one million and 1.4 million Koreans left Japan after the war, while approximately 600,000 remained by 1948. The reasons for remaining varied. Economic collapse and political instability in Korea made return uncertain, and Koreans who had lived in Japan for decades had established households, jobs, and families. Rules limiting the money and goods they could carry made return costly. Political divisions in the peninsula, and later armed conflict, made the future uncertain. The Korean population in Japan formed associations, including the left-led League of Koreans in Japan (Chōren) and, from 1946, Mindan, associated with a contrasting Korean nationalist orientation, and established ethnic schools; conflicts over these schools in 1948 led to confrontations with Japanese and occupation authorities.

Taiwanese, Chinese, and others had parallel histories. Chinese wartime laborers were also repatriated from Japan following a coercive transport-and-labor program whose death toll and surviving records became central to later legal disputes. Taiwanese in Japan and across the empire were returned to Taiwan, where many soldiers and workers faced uncertain status. Koreans in Sakhalin, numbering tens of thousands, were largely excluded from repatriation by both the Soviet and Japanese governments and many remained for decades. Okinawans and others also faced displacement after the war.

Korean return migration involved decisions made under extreme constraint. Wartime conscription and labor recruitment had brought many Koreans to Japan under coercive conditions, but some had lived there for years, formed families, or built community institutions. Liberation prompted numerous departures, yet damaged shipping, restrictions on money or belongings, and economic and political uncertainty in Korea impeded others. Parents weighed the prospects of employment, schooling, and reunification differently from single workers. Estimates often cite roughly two million Koreans in Japan near the end of the war and approximately 600,000 remaining by 1948, but different registers, definitions of departure, and return trips prevent simple subtraction from producing an exact total.

The sinking of the Ukishima Maru on August 24, 1945 is a particularly revealing case. The vessel was carrying Koreans from northern Honshu toward Korea when it sank near Maizuru. The official count recorded 549 deaths, including 524 Korean passengers and 25 Japanese personnel; survivor testimonies disputed the passenger total and consequently the mortality count. Jonathan Bull and Steven Ivings have examined the survivors' statements and the occupation-era investigation. Their work shows why a number documented by an authority must still be considered alongside the circumstances of testimony, the limited inquiry, and demands by bereaved families. It does not justify presenting every disputed reconstruction as established fact.

Chinese forced laborers faced a different journey out of Japan. A Japanese Foreign Ministry investigation recorded 38,935 Chinese people transported for wartime labor and 6,830 deaths, a statistical record later discussed in Japanese litigation. Repatriation could be supervised by Allied authorities, but returning workers did not simply receive restitution for coercion or lost wages. Taiwanese residents and former soldiers likewise faced overlapping questions about movement and legal identity after the Republic of China assumed administration of Taiwan. Comparing these populations makes clear that ships traveled in both directions after surrender, while the coercion, rights, and documentary classifications attached to each journey were profoundly unequal.

The legal framework for their status was unsettled. The Allies treated Koreans as liberated people but also, for some purposes, as Japanese nationals; Japanese authorities treated them as subjects of the former empire whose status was undefined. The next sections take up the legal consequences. The important point here is that the same events that sent six million Japanese home also created a postcolonial population in Japan itself.

Section 81.16 · Division IV — Families, Reintegration, Citizenship, and the Unfinished Consequences of Defeat

Women, Children, Missing Persons, and Family Separation

Imperial collapse had gendered consequences. In the flights from Manchuria and northern Korea, women were often responsible for children and the elderly, and they faced hunger, disease, and violence. Sexual violence by soldiers, especially Soviet troops, and coercion in conditions of disorder are documented in survivors' testimony and scholarship, and survivors’ testimonies describe episodes of sexual coercion and survival arrangements in particular communities. Scholarly research on the relief system also documents medical interventions, including abortions, involving some repatriated women. Because personal testimony was often recorded years later under conditions of stigma, historians distinguish documented local cases from blanket claims about women’s experiences; the detailed medical and administrative evidence is discussed below.

Children's experiences were similarly extreme. Some died on the journey; others were separated from their parents and survived through adoption or fostering by Chinese families; others arrived in Japan alone. A national 1948 Ministry of Health and Welfare survey classified more than 123,000 children as koji in multiple administrative categories. The categories, which are not identical to narrowly defined combat orphans, are examined below. Some orphans lived on the streets of Japan's cities, a theme taken up in Chapter 85.

Families that stayed in Japan faced a different uncertainty. Millions of households waited for news of husbands and sons who might be alive in a camp or dead in an unknown place. The government published lists of repatriates; radio programs, notably NHK's "Seeking Persons" (Tazune bito) broadcasts, read out names and appeals for missing relatives; and people gathered at ports and stations with photographs and notices. Women, as household managers, bore much of this work of searching and waiting. Widowed women, without husbands' wages and, after 1946, without military survivor pensions, faced severe poverty.

Flight magnified inequalities already built into wartime family life. Adult men were often serving in the military, detained by Soviet forces, or assigned to industrial work far from their households, leaving women responsible for protecting children and elderly relatives. In Manchuria and Korea, the task of finding food and accommodation could fall to mothers with little access to transport or official information. Sexual assault occurred amid military occupation and disorder, yet testimony reflects unequal willingness and ability to report abuse. Survivors could face pressure to conceal experiences when they returned to communities that stigmatized victims. Gender history therefore requires attention not only to what happened but to how institutions recorded—or deliberately did not record—women's experience.

Medical relief could itself become a site of state power over women's bodies. In her study of organized abortions involving repatriated women between 1945 and 1948, historian Yoko Matsubara examines the Japanese Ministry of Health and Welfare, receiving-port consultation systems, national hospitals, and ambiguous interpretations of abortion law before the Eugenic Protection Law of 1948. Her evidence shows government-directed medical programs in which some abortions were requested and others may have been involuntary; the stated rationale mixed concern for health with social and eugenic assumptions. It would be inaccurate to say that every repatriated woman underwent a procedure, that all such pregnancies resulted from sexual assault, or that every reception center operated identically. The case instead exposes the coexistence of humanitarian assistance and coercive reproductive governance.

Children separated from adults were difficult for postwar authorities to classify. A 1948 Health and Welfare Ministry survey counted 123,511 children in several categories of koji, including 28,248 classed narrowly as war orphans, 11,351 as repatriated orphans, 81,266 as general orphans, and 2,647 as abandoned or lost children; those reported category subtotals differ by one from the stated overall figure. Mariko Asano Tamanoi notes that the categories overlap wider experiences of wartime loss and were not a complete census of all children bereaved by war. The totals as reproduced in later scholarship should be treated as administrative categories, not as an exact count of children whose parents were killed by enemy action. Children remaining with Chinese families often fell outside these Japanese reception records altogether. Their eventual identification and possible repatriation required individual inquiries years after the main transport program ended.

The law shaped the process. A late-1950s statute allowed the government to treat those who had not returned as legally dead after a long period, closing cases for many families and ending the status of missing persons for administrative purposes. For families who had held out hope, such closure was sometimes unwelcome. The history of the missing thus extends the chapter's chronology and shows how the empire's end continued to shape households long after the surrender.

Section 81.17 · Division IV

Former Soldiers and Veterans Returning to Civilian Life

Japan's armed forces had numbered several million at their peak, and by the end of 1945 the homeland army was largely demobilized. Veterans returned to households that were often destroyed or crowded, to cities with severe shortages, and to a society whose attitude to soldiers shifted from reverence to ambivalence. Some were received as heroes by their families; others encountered resentment from civilians who blamed the military for the war and its destruction, and some felt guilt, confusion, or silence about what they had done abroad.

Practical difficulties were severe. Discharge payments were small and inflation was high, and employment depended on personal networks and on an economy that could not absorb millions of men. Occupation policy abolished military pensions in 1946 and barred many former officers from public office through the purge (Chapter 82), and veterans' organizations were dissolved. Disabled veterans, some of whom appeared in public in white hospital clothes and begged at stations, became a visible symbol of the state's withdrawal from its wartime promises. Pensions and relief were restored for Japanese nationals in 1952–53 after the occupation ended.

Many veterans also carried responsibility for war crimes and atrocities committed overseas (Chapter 79), and Allied trials prosecuted some. Most returned without being charged, and the question of how to remember what they had done was left to individuals, families, and later historians. The silence of many veterans, and the later testimony of a minority, form part of the evidence for the Asia-Pacific War.

A military discharge certificate did not guarantee employment or a receptive household. Men who had spent years in units organized by rank faced workplaces where prewar positions had disappeared and where the prestige once attached to military service had collapsed. Physical wounds, malaria, tuberculosis, and nutritional deprivation could limit a veteran's ability to work; psychological consequences were no less real even though contemporary Japanese medical language rarely categorized them in modern terms. Some veterans entered factories or farming, while others joined informal trading or moved between relatives. A local welfare office might evaluate them as needy civilians even while a former officer regarded assistance as an entitlement earned through service. These conflicting understandings of obligation shaped the emerging politics of veterans' relief.

The relationship between responsibility and reintegration was equally uneven. Ordinary conscripts included perpetrators, witnesses, victims of military discipline, and men with little direct combat experience. Their accounts of war were affected by shame, fear of criminal investigation, loyalty to comrades, and the expectations of relatives trying to rebuild household life. Researchers cannot infer innocence from silence, but neither can they assign equal responsibility to everyone in uniform. War-crimes trials, documents left by units, testimony by Asian survivors, and the recollections of Japanese servicemen all illuminate different dimensions of military experience. The postwar distinction between the soldier as a victim of state policy and the soldier as an agent of imperial violence became one of the enduring tensions in Japanese memory.

Colonial military service added a legal dimension to these disputes. Koreans and Taiwanese had been mobilized through the imperial system but later faced the state's insistence that people recorded in former colonial family registers were no longer Japanese nationals. That classification affected pension eligibility and the ability to obtain assistance for disability or bereavement, even when wartime orders and service records were indisputable. Some former colonial personnel were prosecuted by Allied tribunals for actions performed in Japanese service, while relief rules that applied to Japanese veterans excluded them. The asymmetry shows why the end of the empire cannot be measured solely by the number of men discharged from the Japanese army.

Former colonial soldiers were in a different position. Hundreds of thousands of Koreans and Taiwanese had served the Japanese army and navy as soldiers, laborers, or civilian employees, and some, including more than a hundred Koreans who had worked as prison guards, were tried by Allied courts as war criminals. After 1952 Japanese relief laws restricted benefits by nationality, excluding former colonial soldiers who were no longer Japanese citizens, a long-standing grievance. Taiwanese veterans won one-time payments only in the late 1980s. Their experience shows that the legal consequences of the empire's end fell very unevenly.

Section 81.18 · Division IV

Repatriates in a Changed Japan: Reception, Housing, Work, and Community

The journey ended at a reception port, but not at home. Repatriates arrived with few possessions, little money, and often illness, and many found their homes destroyed or occupied by others, their relatives dead or scattered, and their prefecture unable to provide housing. Reception centers offered temporary shelter, food, and transport; local governments and private groups offered further assistance, but resources were scarce. Repatriates from Manchuria, Korea, and elsewhere were dispersed across the country, many to rural relatives, many to the cities.

Some repatriates were resettled in what the government called reclamation (kaitaku) projects, which assigned them marginal land in Hokkaido, Tōhoku, and the highlands to grow food and absorb the unemployed, a policy that placed former colonial settlers in new frontier communities with poor soil and little infrastructure; many of these settlements struggled or were abandoned. Repatriates also entered the postwar labor market, small businesses, and black markets, and the Ministry of Health and Welfare and local agencies provided a modest range of support. Detailed treatment of the food crisis and the informal economy belongs to Chapter 85.

The first problem after reaching the home islands was deciding where 'home' actually was. Some repatriates had been born in Korea, Taiwan, or Manchuria and had never lived in the prefecture named in a parent's household register. Others had ancestral relatives in rural districts but no enforceable right to occupy a house or obtain employment there. Transport documents provided a destination without providing shelter. A reception center might send a family to a prefectural office that could offer only a temporary barracks room, a crowded school building, or a request to seek relatives. The category hikiagesha designated an administrative problem as well as a lived identity: it identified people entitled to some assistance, while distinguishing them from households already struggling inside Japan.

Official repatriation relief had to work through several scales of authority. Central ministries established eligibility categories and arranged receiving facilities; prefectures and municipalities distributed immediate aid; private religious organizations, neighbors, employers, and former associates sometimes provided material assistance that official agencies could not. Documentation created complications. A person with missing registration papers might need to reconstruct identity and family relationships before receiving assistance, applying for work, or entering school. Women who arrived without husbands and children whose parents had died faced especially difficult claims on households and family registers. Moreover, people transferred to medical treatment or temporary camps could disappear from simple narratives of 'arrival' even though their reintegration had barely begun.

Agricultural reclamation was promoted partly because it appeared to convert idle labor into food production. Former settlers sometimes had farming experience from the colonies, but that did not make land in upland Hokkaido or Tōhoku economically equivalent to the farms they had left. Poor soils, harsh weather, inadequate tools, and insufficient roads limited productivity, and some projects demanded additional labor simply to make fields usable. Families had to construct housing while cultivating land, often without capital or reliable markets. The rhetoric of a fresh start could conceal the transfer of economic risk from the central government to the household. Failure to sustain a settlement should therefore be investigated through policy, geography, and resources rather than attributed casually to insufficient effort by returnees.

Urban reintegration was different. Former civil servants and teachers sought new credentials or posts; technicians sometimes found work in industries that could use their skills; merchants tried to rebuild customer networks without the protected markets of colonial rule. In cities damaged by bombing, shortage of living space and competition for work generated tensions with long-term residents. Some returnees had savings and connections that eased adaptation, while others lost nearly all their assets and depended on relief. The experience differed further by gender and age: a boy whose schooling had been in Japanese colonial Manchuria did not face the same social adjustment as a widow responsible for several children. Household correspondence, municipal petitions, and repatriate association publications provide evidence that port statistics alone cannot convey.

Commemoration developed unevenly after the immediate crisis. Repatriate associations worked to secure recognition, benefits, and memorials and accumulated photographs, diaries, and names of the missing. Such sources can preserve indispensable testimony, but they also reflect what association members chose to emphasize. Lori Watt argues that the return of empire's Japanese population helped recast the postwar national community, often highlighting suffering and obscuring former settler privilege. The point is not that Japanese suffering was invented; it is that public narratives tended to separate the pain of return from the institutions that had organized colonial settlement. A balanced history follows both strands and asks what former colonial subjects would have recognized in the same account.

Reintegration was also social. Returnees were sometimes treated with sympathy and sometimes with suspicion, as competitors for scarce resources or as people who had enjoyed colonial privilege or been tainted by life abroad. Children raised in Manchuria or Korea found schools and neighbors unfamiliar. Lori Watt's study of the process argues that the category of hikiagesha helped Japan redefine itself: by treating the people who had been the empire's agents as victims of war who had come home, postwar society could separate the nation from the empire, while leaving unexamined what the empire had done. Associations of repatriates, memoirs, and museums later preserved their stories.

Everyday Life and Society

Maizuru and Ise Hashino: Waiting for the Ships

This feature relies on the documentation of the Maizuru Repatriation Memorial Museum and distinguishes contemporary evidence from later commemoration. It invents no scene or dialogue.

Maizuru, on the Japan Sea coast of Kyoto Prefecture, was a former naval base that became one of the principal reception ports; the museum records that about 660,000 repatriates passed through it between 1945 and 1958. Among the thousands who met the ships was a woman from Tokyo, Hashino Ise, whose son Shinji had served overseas. According to the museum's account, she traveled repeatedly to Maizuru in the hope that he would be among the men returning from Soviet custody. Reports that he had died did not end her visits.

Her story became widely known through a popular song of the 1950s, "Mother of the Quay" (Ganpeki no haha), and the image of a mother searching a crowd of arriving soldiers became a symbol of waiting families. Students should separate three kinds of evidence: the surviving records of her visits and her son's service; the song and later retellings, which dramatize and simplify; and the larger social reality of which she was one example. The case shows that for families with missing members, surrender began a long wait, and that the end of empire was measured in years at a port, not in a broadcast.

Section 81.19 · Division IV

The End of Empire and the Problem of Political Belonging

The Japanese empire collapsed in 1945, but the Japanese state did not. Under the Instrument of Surrender the emperor and government were subordinated to the Supreme Commander, yet the Higashikuni cabinet (August 17 to October 9) and then the Shidehara cabinet continued to run ministries, police, and local government, and to implement Allied directives. This arrangement, often described as indirect rule, is the subject of Chapter 82. It meant that the personnel and institutions that had administered the empire handled much of its dissolution.

The meaning of nationality changed gradually and, in several cases, without clear public decisions. Japanese nationals returning from the colonies were not in doubt, although their social position was. For Koreans and Taiwanese, the position was ambiguous. In December 1945 the revised election law that gave women the vote suspended the voting rights of people registered in the colonial household registers, and on May 2, 1947, the day before the new constitution took effect, an imperial ordinance on alien registration treated Koreans and Taiwanese in Japan as foreigners "provisionally." The loss of nationality was confirmed administratively around the time the San Francisco treaty took effect in April 1952, without individual choice, a step that left many Koreans stateless in practice and excluded from pensions and other benefits. Scholars including John Lie have explored how this history shaped Zainichi identity.

Elsewhere, political belonging depended on place. People in Taiwan became, in the Republic of China's view, Chinese nationals in 1945–46; people in Korea came under Soviet and American military occupation authorities in 1945, with separate Korean republics not established until 1948; people in the Ryukyus lived under American military rule while remaining, in Washington's formula, under residual Japanese sovereignty; people in Karafuto and the Kurils came under the Soviet Union, with the Japanese population removed. Children left in China, Koreans on Sakhalin, and Japanese settlers in Korea or Manchuria all lived with questions of identity for decades.

Legal chronology is essential. The ending of Japanese administration in Korea and Taiwan in 1945 did not automatically settle the citizenship of every person from those territories who was then living in the home islands. The December 1945 election-law revision effectively excluded persons entered in colonial household registers from the new franchise; the 1947 alien-registration rules treated Koreans and Taiwanese as foreigners for registration purposes on a provisional basis. Neither administrative practice should be described as identical to the 1952 nationality policy. In a circular issued just before the San Francisco peace treaty took effect on April 28, 1952, Japanese authorities declared that Koreans and Taiwanese would lose Japanese nationality when the treaty entered into force. The treaty itself did not expressly establish a comprehensive individual choice procedure, a point emphasized in later legal debates.

The consequences were not purely symbolic. Nationality affected the possibility of political participation, employment in certain occupations, travel documents, welfare, pensions, and eventual rights of residence. A Korean born in Osaka, a former Taiwanese serviceman living in Tokyo, and a Japanese settler arriving from Korea could all have held documents issued under the same empire but faced dramatically different postwar classifications. Household registers, alien-registration files, and the wording of ministry notices show how categories were constructed administratively. Students should ask who had an opportunity to consent, appeal, or choose among national attachments—and who did not. These questions form part of the wider history of Zainichi communities without assuming that all residents shared the same political allegiance.

For Korea specifically, the 38th-parallel line governed the surrender of Japanese forces and then occupation zones in 1945; it did not confer citizenship in two Korean republics that did not yet exist. The Republic of Korea and Democratic People's Republic of Korea were established in August and September 1948. On Taiwan the Republic of China advanced its nationality claims as it took over administration, while legal debates concerning territorial sovereignty continued. Okinawa remained under a distinct American system, and Sakhalin Koreans who had been mobilized under Japanese rule could not assume the same return rights as departing Japanese settlers. These cases explain why the empire's end must be narrated using precise institutional dates rather than a single broad statement that everyone 'became a citizen' in 1945.

These cases show that the end of empire was a legal and personal process as well as a political one. It did not follow a single timetable, and many of the decisions that shaped it were made in the following years in occupation directives, treaties, and domestic legislation. Multiple Perspectives

Defeat and the End of Empire: Five Positions

These perspectives summarize documented positions in general terms; they do not quote individuals, and the groups did not hold equal power or bear equal responsibility.

A Japanese cabinet official: Concerned above all with the survival of the imperial institution and the orderly transfer of the armed forces, he saw the surrender as the end of a catastrophe that had to be managed. Postwar accounts by such officials tend to emphasize necessity and the emperor's decision, and say less about the empire's victims.
A Korean independence activist: For her, August 15 was liberation after decades of colonial rule, followed at once by competing plans for self-government and then the shock of foreign occupation and a dividing line she had not chosen.
A Japanese agricultural settler in Manchuria: Part of a state-sponsored colonizing program, she was left with children after the men were conscripted, and suffered hunger, disease, and violence; her account must be read with awareness that the land she farmed had been taken from others.
A Soviet-held internee: A soldier or civilian taken to a labor camp, he faced cold, hunger, and political re-education, and his family at home had no information about his fate for years.
A former colonial resident in Taiwan or Korea living in Japan: Brought to Japan as a worker or having migrated for work, he faced a choice between an uncertain return and an uncertain stay, and discovered that his legal status was being redefined without his consent.
Cause and Effect

From Military Defeat to Political Collapse, Displacement, and Changing Citizenship

Long-term causes

Decades of imperial expansion that placed millions of Japanese abroad and brought colonial subjects into the metropole; a political system that allowed the military to pursue expansion; the war with China and the Pacific War; the attrition of Japan's economy and shipping.

Short-term causes

Strategic bombing and the Okinawa campaign; the atomic bombings; Soviet entry; the Potsdam terms and the deadlock over the imperial institution; the imperial decisions of August 10 and 14.

Event or transformation

Surrender and the dissolution of the empire: disarmament, zone-by-zone transfer of authority, and mass population movement.

Immediate effects

Liberation, division, or renewed colonial conflict across Asia; return of about 6 million Japanese by 1947; Soviet captivity; the deaths of settlers and prisoners; the stranding of Koreans and others in Japan.

Long-term consequences and historical complexity

Divided Korea, the Soviet–Japanese territorial dispute, the status of Taiwan, Okinawa under U.S. rule, the Zainichi population, postwar memory of victimhood, and decades of late repatriation. The outcomes were not determined by the surrender alone: they depended on Allied decisions, local actors, and the choices of millions of individuals.
Continuity and Change Over Time

Imperial Subjects, National Belonging, and Postwar Migration

What Changed

  • The territorial empire ended, and with it the colonial administrations of Korea, Taiwan, Karafuto, and Manchukuo.
  • Millions of Japanese moved from the empire to the home islands.
  • Koreans, Taiwanese, and others gradually lost the status of imperial subjects.
  • The military ceased to exist as an institution.

What Remained Continuous

  • Japanese ministries, bureaucrats, and local government continued to operate.
  • The imperial institution remained, though its status would change (Chapter 84).
  • Colonial-era populations remained in Japan as a postcolonial minority.
  • Many colonial economic and political elites kept positions in successor states.

Who experienced change most directly? Settlers, soldiers, and colonial officials abroad, and colonial populations whose governments changed. Who experienced greater continuity? Metropolitan officials and ministries, and many Japanese households in places that were not destroyed. Why the pattern matters: It shows that the end of empire was both a rupture and a continuation, and that it did not affect all groups in the same way.

Section 81.20 · Division V — Historical Synthesis and Review

Surrender, the End of Empire, and the Human Consequences of Defeat: Synthesis and Review

The surrender of Japan was not one event but a chain of linked decisions and processes that unfolded on different schedules. In Tokyo, the decision came between August 9 and 14, after a deadlock among the leaders was broken by the emperor, who twice intervened in support of acceptance, and after an attempted coup showed how much the army's leadership feared the loss of the institutions it defended. The decision was announced to the Japanese people on August 15, in a rescript that never used the word "surrender" and attributed the outcome chiefly to a new weapon. Fighting nonetheless continued where Soviet forces were advancing, and the surrender of Japan's forces required orders, delegations, and ceremonies from September 2 into the autumn.

The collapse of Japanese authority produced widely different outcomes. In Korea it meant liberation from colonial rule, followed by a division fixed by Allied military convenience; in Manchuria, a Soviet occupation and a contest between Chinese Nationalists and Communists; in Taiwan, an administrative handover whose legal significance remained contested; in southern Sakhalin and the Kurils, Soviet annexation and a territorial dispute that has not been resolved; in Okinawa, American military rule; and in Southeast Asia, independence movements that clashed with returning colonial powers. The empire's end was not a single transfer.

The human consequences were vast. Roughly 6.6 million Japanese were overseas, and about 6.24 million had returned by the end of 1947; many remained outside Japan after 1947, including detainees in Soviet camps whose repatriation proceeded on a different timetable into the 1950s. Settlers in Manchuria faced catastrophe, in part because the colonial state that had settled them failed to protect many communities during the military retreat in defeat; Japanese prisoners endured labor and political re-education in the Soviet Union; women and children bore the burdens of flight, violence, and separation. At the same time, hundreds of thousands of Koreans and others remained in Japan, their nationality redefined without their consent, while Chinese families took in Japanese children and Chinese laborers returned from Japan. These experiences should be understood together, not as competing claims to suffering.

Students should therefore be able to distinguish four meanings of imperial collapse. Politically, the empire ended when its government accepted defeat and lost the authority to rule overseas. Militarily, it ended when its forces were disarmed, a process that lasted from August to the end of 1945 and beyond. Geographically and legally, it ended as territories passed to successor authorities, some by treaty in 1951–52 and some without final resolution. Demographically and personally, it ended as people moved or stayed and found, or did not find, a political home, a process that stretched into the late 1950s and beyond. These meanings overlapped but did not coincide, and which one a historian emphasizes affects the date and the story.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced the end of the Japanese empire from the strategic dilemma of the summer of 1945 to the long aftermath of repatriation. Its central argument is that Japan's surrender ended the empire's war but not instantly the empire itself: the dissolution of colonial authority, the disarmament of forces, and the movement of millions of people unfolded over years and with very different outcomes in different places.

In July 1945 the Suzuki cabinet faced a contradiction between the army's plan for a decisive homeland battle (Ketsu-Gō) and the diplomatic effort, through Moscow, to find an acceptable end. The Potsdam Declaration offered terms but said nothing of the emperor, and the cabinet's ambiguous response, the mokusatsu remark, was read by the Allies as rejection.

The events of August 6 to 9, the bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki and the Soviet declaration of war and invasion of Manchuria, transformed the situation. The Supreme War Council deadlocked over whether to attach conditions to acceptance; the emperor's interventions on August 10 and 14 resolved the question, and the Allied reply of August 11 left the future of the institution to the Japanese people while subordinating the emperor's authority to the Supreme Commander. Historians disagree about the relative weight of the atomic bombs, Soviet entry, and the imperial question, and many now stress their interaction.

The attempted coup of August 14–15 failed, and the emperor's recorded rescript was broadcast at noon on August 15. The announcement did not end combat everywhere, and the surrender of forces was a process of orders, delegations, and ceremonies culminating on September 2 aboard USS Missouri. General Order No. 1 allocated surrender responsibilities among Allied commands and thereby shaped the geography of the empire's end.

The end of Japanese rule diverged by territory. Korea was liberated and divided at the 38th parallel into American and Soviet zones; Manchukuo collapsed under Soviet attack and became the arena of Chinese civil conflict; Taiwan passed to Republic of China administration; southern Sakhalin and the Kurils were occupied by the Soviet Union; Okinawa came under American rule; and Southeast Asia saw nationalist declarations and renewed colonial wars.

The disarmament and return of Japan's forces and civilians constituted one of the largest population movements of the century. Roughly 6.6 million Japanese were overseas, and about 6.24 million had returned by the end of 1947. The administrative apparatus of reception ports, quarantine, and relief handled the flow but could not prevent disaster in places where authority had collapsed.

The Manchurian settlers suffered the worst of it: flight, disease, death, and the separation of families, with children left behind in China. The suffering of the settlers followed from a colonial policy that had dispossessed Chinese farmers and from the failure of authorities to protect them. Hundreds of thousands of Japanese soldiers and civilians were taken to Soviet camps, where tens of thousands died; returns extended into the late 1950s.

Koreans, Taiwanese, Chinese, and others had a parallel history. Many returned to their homelands; about 600,000 Koreans remained in Japan by 1948, and their legal status was redefined, culminating in the loss of Japanese nationality around 1952. Excluded from many benefits, they became a postcolonial minority within Japan.

Returnees entered a country in ruins and were integrated unevenly, often with prejudice and hardship. Women and children bore particular burdens, and the missing remained a presence in public life. Veterans met ambivalence, and their colonial counterparts met exclusion from relief.

Japan's empire ended, but the Japanese state continued under Allied supervision. The chapter prepares the way for Chapter 82, which examines how that supervision worked, and for the chapters that follow on trials, reform, scarcity, culture, and reconstruction.

Tracing a Route of Return

This project asks you to follow one group or route of return or detention and place it in its regional and political setting.

  1. Choose a subject. Examples: Japanese civilians leaving northern Korea; settlers from a named Manchurian settlement; Soviet internees returning through Maizuru; Koreans leaving through Hakata; Chinese laborers returning from Japan.
  2. Build a map and timeline. Map the places of departure, transit, and arrival, noting that modern borders differ from those of 1945.
  3. Identify sources. Locate at least four sources (documents, memoirs, oral histories, museum or archive collections, scholarly studies) and identify the creator, audience, purpose, and limits of each.
  4. Compare interpretations. Summarize how at least two scholars interpret your subject, and note where they agree and differ.
  5. Write your findings. Prepare a 5–7 page essay or equivalent presentation, using careful language and attributing estimates to their sources.

Library resources to use: JACAR, the Maizuru Repatriation Memorial Museum, the National Diet Library, scholarly studies such as Lori Watt's When Empire Comes Home, and the MGU History Library's reference collection.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows that the end of a war and the end of an empire are different things. The broadcast of August 15 gave Japanese listeners a single moment to remember, but the dissolution of the empire took place across a continent and over more than a decade, in surrender ceremonies, camps, ports, and legal offices, and in the lives of millions.

It also shows how much of postwar East Asia was shaped by decisions made in the weeks of surrender: the 38th parallel, the Soviet occupation of Manchuria and the Kurils, the American administration of Okinawa, the transfer of Taiwan, and the returns of colonial powers to Southeast Asia. Later chapters on the Korean War, the San Francisco settlement, and Okinawa trace those consequences.

The chapter connects the archipelago to its neighbors in a different way from earlier chapters. The people who returned to Japan, and those who remained in Japan, linked the home islands to the peoples of the former empire, and the memory of repatriation, settler suffering, and Soviet captivity became central to postwar Japanese identity, sometimes at the expense of attention to what Japan had done abroad. Holding both histories together is one of the tasks of this textbook.

Students who understand surrender as a sequence, and the empire's end as a process with several meanings, are prepared for Part XII, which turns to the occupation of a defeated Japan: its institutions, its reforms, and the lived experience of a society rebuilding amid scarcity.
