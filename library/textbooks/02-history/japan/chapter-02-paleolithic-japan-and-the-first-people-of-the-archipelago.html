Part I — The Archipelago Before the Japanese State
2

Paleolithic Japan and the First People of the Archipelago

Japanese History: From Jōmon Japan to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

Tens of thousands of years before there was a Jōmon world, a Yayoi village, or a Yamato court, small bands of people were already walking, paddling, and living across the landscape that would one day become the Japanese archipelago. They left no writing, no named leaders, and no chronicles. What they left instead was stone — flaked, chipped, and shaped with increasing skill over tens of thousands of years — scattered through soil layers that archaeologists have spent the past century learning to read.

Imagine a group of hunters moving along a river valley in what is now central Honshu, more than thirty thousand years ago. The coastline they know is not the coastline on a modern map: sea levels are lower, more land is exposed, and travel by foot between what will later be separate islands is, in some places, possible in a way it no longer is today. The climate is colder and drier than today's humid archipelago, and the animals they track — some now extinct, including large deer and, for several more millennia yet, Naumann's elephant (Palaeoloxodon naumanni), whose population overlapped with human presence in the archipelago for perhaps four to six thousand years before its extinction — move through forests and grasslands unlike the landscape of later Japanese history. These hunters carry stone tools worked with real technical sophistication: blades struck from carefully prepared cores, points hafted onto wooden shafts, tools sharp enough to butcher game and process hides. By reasonable analogy with Homo sapiens elsewhere in this period, they likely speak a language, organize themselves into social groups, share food, and care for their young and, perhaps, their elderly — and they may well hold beliefs about the world around them — but these are general inferences about the species, not conclusions Japanese Paleolithic archaeology can independently demonstrate, and archaeology can recover almost none of this directly from the archipelago's own evidence. What survives is stone, and occasionally the sediments, ash layers, and rare organic remains that surround it.

This chapter is about that world: the Japanese Paleolithic, the long period of human presence in the archipelago before the appearance of the earliest Jōmon pottery in the Late Pleistocene, several millennia before the beginning of the Holocene. It is a period defined almost entirely by stone tools and geology rather than by named individuals, political events, or even secure cultural identities. It asks difficult but answerable questions: When did people first reach the archipelago? By what routes did they arrive? What tools did they make, and how did those tools change over tens of thousands of years? What did they eat, how did they live, and what can — and cannot — be known about their social lives? And it asks one further, methodologically urgent question that this field of Japanese archaeology has had to confront directly: how do historians and archaeologists protect themselves against being misled by fraudulent or mistaken evidence, a lesson driven home by a real and consequential scandal in the history of Japanese Paleolithic archaeology itself.

It is essential from the outset to state plainly what this chapter is not. It is not a history of "the Japanese people" in any ethnic, linguistic, or national sense. The term "Japanese" describes a much later cultural and political formation that had not yet come into being, and it would be a serious historical error to imagine the Paleolithic inhabitants of the archipelago as early "Japanese" ancestors possessing a fixed identity that persisted essentially unchanged into later periods. The people discussed in this chapter did not call themselves anything that survives in the historical record; they belonged to small, mobile communities whose relationship to later Jōmon, Yayoi, Ainu, or other populations remains, in many respects, a live subject of ongoing genetic, archaeological, and linguistic research rather than settled fact.

This chapter also continues a theme introduced in Chapter 1: the archipelago's geography was not a fixed backdrop but an active, changing stage. During the Pleistocene epoch — commonly called the Ice Age, though it in fact consisted of many alternating cold glacial and milder interglacial phases — global sea levels rose and fell repeatedly as vast quantities of water were locked into and released from continental ice sheets. At their lowest points, these sea-level changes exposed land connections in some parts of the region while other passages, especially those linking the archipelago's southern and central zones to the Asian mainland, appear to have remained crossable only by deliberate sea voyage even under the lowest sea levels of the era. Understanding how people arrived, therefore, is inseparable from understanding how the physical geography of the region itself was changing beneath their feet — and, in some cases, beneath the waves.

Finally, this chapter introduces students to a set of evidentiary methods that will recur throughout the textbook, particularly in its treatment of periods before or with limited written record: stratigraphy (the study of soil and sediment layers), typology (the classification of stone tools by shape and manufacturing technique), radiocarbon dating and its limitations for very old material, tephrochronology (dating using layers of volcanic ash), and the cross-checking of independent lines of evidence against one another. These methods matter not only for understanding the Paleolithic but as a model of how historians and scientists evaluate any claim about the deep past — including claims that later turn out to be mistaken or, in one well-documented case in Japanese archaeology, deliberately fabricated.

By the chapter's end, students should understand the Japanese Paleolithic neither as a simple, static "prehistoric" backdrop to later chapters, nor as a settled and uncontroversial body of knowledge, but as an active field of scientific inquiry with real debates, real uncertainties, and a well-documented cautionary episode about the dangers of unverified claims — a fitting foundation for the historical skepticism and evidentiary care this textbook asks of its students throughout.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Describe the current archaeological evidence for the earliest human presence in the Japanese archipelago and the uncertainties surrounding its dating.
  2. Explain how Pleistocene glacial cycles altered sea levels, coastlines, and possible routes of human movement into the archipelago.
  3. Distinguish land-bridge migration from deliberate maritime crossing, and evaluate evidence for each in different parts of the archipelago.
  4. Trace the development of Japanese Paleolithic stone-tool technology, including changes in core and blade technique over time.
  5. Analyze the environmental and faunal conditions of Ice Age Japan and their implications for subsistence strategies.
  6. Evaluate the significance of major Paleolithic sites and the methods used to date and interpret them.
  7. Explain the Fujimura Shinichi archaeological fraud case and its consequences for the field.
  8. Assess what can and cannot be inferred about Paleolithic social organization, belief, and daily life from the surviving archaeological record.
  9. Describe the transition from Paleolithic technology and lifeways toward the Jōmon cultural pattern.
  10. Identify the scientific methods — stratigraphy, typology, radiocarbon dating, and tephrochronology — used to reconstruct this period.
  11. Avoid anachronistic claims that project a later "Japanese" ethnic or national identity onto Paleolithic populations.
  12. Contextualize the Japanese Paleolithic within the broader pattern of early human dispersal across East Asia and the Pacific.

Historical Context

Chapter 1 established the physical stage on which this chapter's human history unfolds: an archipelago formed over tens of millions of years of tectonic activity, whose coastline and connection to the Asian mainland shifted repeatedly with Pleistocene sea-level change. This chapter picks up that geological story at the point where humans enter it. The broader pattern of early human dispersal across Asia — the spread of Homo sapiens out of Africa, its interactions and interbreeding with other hominin populations such as Neanderthals and Denisovans, and its gradual movement eastward and southward across the Asian continent — forms the necessary backdrop for understanding how and when people could have reached the Japanese archipelago specifically. This textbook does not attempt a full global history of human origins, which lies outside its scope, but students should understand that the archipelago's human settlement was part of a much larger process of movement across Eurasia and into the Pacific rim, rather than an isolated or separate event.

Archaeological evidence for the Japanese Paleolithic has been recovered from thousands of sites across the archipelago, ranging from Hokkaido to Kyushu and the Ryukyu Islands, though the density, quality, and dating security of these sites vary enormously by region and period. Japan's acidic volcanic soils generally preserve stone tools well but destroy bone, wood, and other organic material far more readily than the alkaline soils of some other regions, meaning that direct evidence for diet, clothing, or the human skeletal remains themselves is comparatively rare for most of this period compared to stone tools. This preservation bias should be kept firmly in mind throughout the chapter: the disproportionate survival of stone artifacts does not mean that stone tools were the only, or even the most important, technology Paleolithic people used, only that they are what has most reliably survived to be found.

Because this chapter addresses a period with no writing of any kind, its evidentiary foundation differs fundamentally from later chapters that can draw on court chronicles, provincial gazetteers, or documentary sources. Readers should treat the absence of names, political events, or securely reconstructed language and belief systems not as an oversight but as an honest reflection of what this kind of evidence can and cannot support. The chapters that follow — on the Jōmon world, whose earliest ("Incipient") phase begins within the Late Pleistocene and continues across the transition into the Holocene (Chapters 3 and 4), and the Yayoi transformation that followed (Chapter 5) — will return repeatedly to the technological, environmental, and social patterns introduced here as a foundation for understanding later developments.

Major Sites, Peoples, and Features

Reference — Sites, Regions & Concepts

Iwajuku Site (Gunma Prefecture)
A site excavated beginning in 1949 by amateur researcher Aizawa Tadahiro and archaeologist Sugihara Sōsuke, whose discovery of stone tools beneath a volcanic ash layer previously believed to be culturally sterile is widely credited with establishing the existence of a Japanese Paleolithic as a recognized field of study.
Hoshino Site
A site whose claimed very early stone artifacts have been the subject of genuine, independent scholarly debate since at least 1971, entirely separate from the Fujimura fraud discussed below; its status remains disputed and under ongoing investigation rather than either confirmed or discredited.
Ryukyu Islands Paleolithic sites (including Yamashita-chō Cave and Pinza-Abu Cave, Okinawa)
Sites yielding some of Japan's oldest Pleistocene human remains, dated largely through associated stratigraphic and contextual materials such as overlying charcoal rather than direct dating of the bones themselves; later finds at Shiraho-Saonetabaru Cave on Ishigaki Island provide direct radiocarbon dates on human skeletal collagen, in the range of roughly 16,000–20,000 years ago, important for debates over early maritime crossing into the southern archipelago discussed further in Chapter 32's later treatment of Ryukyuan history.
Hokkaido microblade sites
A cluster of sites reflecting close technological connections with Siberia and the Asian mainland via the northern land-bridge routes discussed in Section 2.3.
Land bridges (Pleistocene)
Temporary land connections exposed during periods of low sea level, most securely documented in the north connecting Hokkaido to Sakhalin and the Asian continent; other proposed connections elsewhere in the archipelago remain more uncertain or contested.
Homo sapiens dispersal into East Asia
The broader process by which anatomically modern humans spread across the Asian continent beginning tens of thousands of years before their documented arrival in the Japanese archipelago, forming the necessary background for this chapter's narrower regional focus.
Fujimura Shinichi
An amateur Japanese archaeologist whose fabricated "discoveries" at dozens of sites between the 1970s and 2000 were exposed as fraudulent, discussed in detail in Section 2.6.
Tephra layers (regional markers)
Volcanic ash deposits from major eruptions, used throughout Japanese archaeology (and introduced in Chapter 1) to date and correlate stone-tool layers across widely separated sites.
Microblade technology

"Incipient Jōmon"
A term used by some archaeologists for the transitional period, beginning roughly 16,000–15,500 years ago within the Late Pleistocene, in which some of the world's earliest pottery appears alongside continuing Paleolithic-style stone tool traditions, examined further at the start of Chapter 3.

Section 2.1

Before the Archipelago Was an Island Chain

As Chapter 1 explained, the Japanese archipelago's present shape is a relatively recent geological development. During the Pleistocene epoch, which lasted from roughly 2.6 million to 11,700 years ago, the planet experienced repeated glacial cycles in which enormous volumes of ocean water were locked into continental ice sheets, lowering global sea levels by as much as 120 meters below present levels at the coldest points. When ice sheets melted during warmer interglacial phases, sea levels rose again, sometimes close to or above modern levels. These oscillations occurred repeatedly over hundreds of thousands of years, and the landscape encountered by the earliest human arrivals in the region that became the Japanese archipelago therefore looked substantially different from the archipelago familiar today.

At the lowest points of the last glacial cycle, more of the continental shelf surrounding the archipelago lay exposed as dry land, though the degree and location of this exposure varied considerably across the archipelago's length. In the north, the connection between Hokkaido, the Russian island of Sakhalin, and the Asian mainland narrowed dramatically and, at times, closed entirely, creating an intermittent overland corridor. Elsewhere, however — particularly across the straits separating Kyushu from the Korean Peninsula, and the waters separating the Ryukyu Islands from Taiwan and the Asian mainland — even the lowest Pleistocene sea levels appear to have left substantial water gaps that could only have been crossed by boat or raft, a point of considerable importance for understanding how people reached different parts of the archipelago, discussed further in Section 2.3.

The climate of Ice Age Japan also differed markedly from the humid, monsoon-influenced climate described in Chapter 1. Average temperatures were generally cooler, precipitation patterns differed, and vegetation across much of the archipelago during colder phases consisted of more open coniferous forest, grassland, and steppe-like environments in places that would later become the dense mixed and broadleaf forests of the Holocene. These environmental differences shaped what animals lived in the archipelago and, in turn, what resources were available to the human communities who arrived to hunt, gather, and eventually settle there.

Section 2.2

Who Were the First People, and When Did They Arrive?

The question of when humans first arrived in the Japanese archipelago has been the subject of considerable archaeological effort and, at times, controversy. Securely dated and widely accepted evidence — meaning stone-tool assemblages found in reliable stratigraphic context, associated with datable materials, and independently verified by multiple researchers — currently places the beginning of confirmed human presence in the archipelago at around 38,000 years ago (calibrated). This figure reflects the overlapping Bayesian model ranges of the two oldest currently accepted sites, Ishinomoto (roughly 38,430–36,800 cal BP) and Idemaruyama (roughly 38,420–36,530 cal BP), rather than evidence that initial colonization itself spanned this entire interval; the ranges should be understood as statistical estimates of each site's age, not as a description of how long the process of first settlement took. Importantly, these earliest secure assemblages are characterized primarily by flake-based stone-tool production rather than the more standardized blade technology often associated with the Japanese Paleolithic in popular summaries; blade-based industries and the distinctive edge-ground stone axes discussed in Section 2.4 appear slightly later in the sequence, roughly 37,000 to 36,000 years ago. Some researchers have proposed slightly earlier dates based on particular sites and dating methods, and the precise threshold continues to be refined as new sites are excavated and existing sites are re-dated using improved Bayesian statistical techniques, so students should understand these figures as the current, well-supported estimates rather than permanently fixed numbers.

It is important to note that considerably earlier dates — in some cases extending back several hundred thousand years into what would have been the "Lower" and "Middle" Paleolithic periods by international terminology — were once widely reported in Japanese archaeology and even textbooks, based primarily on the work of one prolific amateur excavator. As Section 2.6 explains in detail, the great majority of these earlier claims were later shown to have been the product of deliberate fabrication and were formally repudiated by the Japanese Archaeological Association following an investigation beginning in 2000. This episode means that, unlike in some other regions where the Paleolithic archaeological record has been built up gradually and cumulatively with comparatively few major revisions, the currently accepted chronology of the Japanese Paleolithic reflects a significant and well-documented correction. It is equally important, however, not to overstate how completely this closes the question of possible pre-38,000-year-old occupation: a small number of sites with disputed pre-Fujimura histories, such as Hoshino, discussed further in Section 2.6, remain the subject of independent, ongoing archaeological investigation entirely separate from the Fujimura affair, even though none currently commands anything close to the broad evidentiary acceptance enjoyed by the roughly 38,000-year-old horizon. Students encountering older sources — including some encyclopedia entries, popular books, and even some educational materials — should in any case be alert to outdated claims about very early "Lower Paleolithic" dates in Japan tied specifically to the discredited Fujimura finds.

The people who left this evidence were almost certainly anatomically modern humans, Homo sapiens, part of the broader wave of human dispersal that had already reached much of the Asian continent by this period. There is no confirmed evidence in the Japanese archipelago of earlier hominin species such as Homo erectus or Homo floresiensis, in contrast to some other parts of island Southeast Asia where such earlier hominins are documented; the Japanese Paleolithic record, as currently understood, begins with modern humans rather than earlier hominin populations. This does not mean earlier hominins definitely never reached the archipelago — the absence of evidence is not necessarily evidence of absence, particularly given the acidic soils that make organic preservation difficult in various regions, even though these same soils generally preserve stone tools relatively well — but it does mean that no confirmed evidence for pre-sapiens hominin occupation currently exists.

Section 2.3

Routes of Arrival: Land Bridges and Sea Crossings

Understanding how people reached the archipelago requires distinguishing between different regions and different possible routes, since no single explanation accounts for the whole of the Japanese islands. In the north, the intermittent land connection between Hokkaido, Sakhalin, and the Asian mainland — sometimes called the "Sōya" or northern route in Japanese archaeological literature — unquestionably existed as a genuine overland corridor during the coldest, lowest-sea-level phases of the Pleistocene, allowing people (and animals, including species such as woolly mammoth whose remains have been found in Hokkaido) to walk into the northern archipelago without crossing open water, at least at certain periods. It is important, however, to distinguish the existence of this corridor from the currently favored interpretation of how Hokkaido was first colonized by humans: the island's earliest known stone-tool assemblages closely resemble contemporary industries from Paleo-Honshu to the south, suggesting that Hokkaido's initial human settlement, around 30,000 years ago, may have come from the south rather than directly overland from the continent. Clearer evidence of northern, continental connections becomes visible somewhat later, in the blade and especially microblade industries discussed in Section 2.4, rather than in the very earliest phase of Hokkaido's settlement.

Further south, the picture is different and, in some respects, considerably more striking. The strait separating Kyushu from the Korean Peninsula — the Korea/Tsushima Strait discussed in Chapter 1 — appears to have remained a genuine water barrier throughout the Pleistocene, even at the lowest sea levels of the Last Glacial Maximum, though narrowed compared to today and broken by the intervening island of Tsushima. Reaching Kyushu from the Korean Peninsula, therefore, likely required some form of watercraft, even if a relatively simple raft or dugout canoe, rather than a simple walk across exposed land.

The most striking case concerns the Ryukyu Islands. Archaeological sites across the Ryukyu chain indicate human presence by roughly 35,000 to 30,000 years ago, even though the sea gaps separating these islands from Taiwan and the East Asian mainland remained substantial water crossings under any reconstructed Pleistocene sea-level scenario. Reaching the Ryukyus at this date therefore necessarily involved deliberate maritime crossing, though the exact watercraft, navigational methods, and starting points used by these early voyagers remain unknown, since no boat remains have survived from this period. This evidence has led some archaeologists to argue that early Homo sapiens in this region possessed more capable maritime technology and navigational skill at a surprisingly early date than was once assumed. In 2019, Japanese researchers associated with the National Museum of Nature and Science tested this possibility directly: a team built a 7.5-meter dugout canoe using tools and methods appropriate to the Paleolithic and paddled it across the Kuroshio Current from Taiwan toward Yonaguni Island, the westernmost of the Ryukyus, successfully completing the crossing. The results of this and related experiments were published in the journal Science Advances in 2025. The experiment demonstrated that such a crossing was physically feasible using Paleolithic-appropriate technology and skill; it does not, by itself, prove that ancient voyagers used precisely this method, this route, or this type of vessel, only that the deliberate-crossing hypothesis is consistent with what human effort and craft could plausibly achieve.

Taken together, the evidence suggests that human arrival in the archipelago was not a single migratory event but a series of distinct movements into different regions, using different routes, over an extended period, likely involving multiple source populations from different parts of the Asian mainland and possibly Southeast Asia. This regional complexity is a recurring theme the textbook will return to, since it cautions against imagining a single, unified "first Japanese" migration comparable to a national origin story.

Section 2.4

Stone-Tool Traditions and Technological Change

Because organic materials rarely survive in the Japanese Paleolithic archaeological record, stone tools form the primary evidence for technological change over this long period. Archaeologists classify Paleolithic stone tools by their manufacturing technique (typology) and study the sequence in which techniques appear and change over time, cross-checked against stratigraphic position and, where possible, absolute dating methods.

The earliest securely dated Japanese Paleolithic assemblages, dating to around 38,000 years ago (with the Ishinomoto and Idemaruyama sites modeled at roughly 38,430–36,800 and 38,420–36,530 cal BP respectively), are characterized primarily by flake-based stone-tool production: knappers struck usable flakes directly from a stone core using comparatively simple techniques, producing knives, scrapers, and points without the more standardized preparation associated with true blade technology. A shift toward blade technology — in which a core is carefully prepared so that long, parallel-sided blades of consistent shape can be struck off repeatedly — appears slightly later in the sequence, becoming clearly established by roughly 37,000 to 36,000 years ago. These blades could be further shaped into a variety of tool types and could be hafted — mounted onto wooden or bone handles or shafts — to create more effective composite tools. The distinction between this earlier flake-based horizon and the subsequent blade-based horizon has become clearer only through the kind of careful Bayesian re-analysis of radiocarbon dates that recent research has applied to sites such as Ishinomoto and Idemaruyama, and it revises a once-common simplification that treated blade technology as characteristic of the Japanese Paleolithic from its very beginning.

A particularly notable feature of the early Japanese Paleolithic is the appearance of edge-ground stone axes, tools whose cutting edge was ground smooth against an abrasive stone rather than left in its flaked state. The earliest securely modeled examples of this technology date to roughly 37,000 to 36,000 years ago, appearing alongside the emerging blade tradition rather than characterizing the very earliest, flake-based phase of Japanese Paleolithic occupation. Even at this slightly later date, edge-grinding of this kind is remarkably early compared to similar technology elsewhere in the world, where fully ground stone tools generally do not become common until much later, closer to the Neolithic period in many regions. This has led some archaeologists to regard the early Japanese Paleolithic as a technologically significant and, in some respects, precocious regional tradition, though the specific functions of these early edge-ground axes — whether primarily for woodworking, as some evidence suggests, or for other tasks — remain a subject of ongoing research.

Later in the Paleolithic, a distinct microblade technology spread across the archipelago, closely paralleling similar technologies found across Siberia, Mongolia, and other parts of northeast Asia, though its timing varied significantly by region. In Hokkaido and the broader Paleo-Sakhalin–Hokkaido–Kurile landmass, microblade assemblages appear considerably earlier than once assumed, extending from roughly 26,000 down to about 15,000 years ago and continuing into the terminal Paleolithic. In central Japan, by contrast, prominent microblade traditions developed somewhat later in the sequence, generally within the range of about 20,000 to 16,000 years ago. Microblade technology involved carefully preparing small, wedge-shaped or conical cores from which tiny, extremely standardized blades were struck; these microblades were then set in rows into grooved bone, antler, or wood shafts to create composite cutting or piercing tools. This technology offered practical advantages for highly mobile hunting groups: individual microblades could be manufactured quickly from portable cores and replaced easily when damaged or dulled, without discarding an entire tool. The earlier and more sustained appearance of microblade technology in Hokkaido illustrates a continued and substantial technological and, quite possibly, demographic connection between the archipelago's northern regions and the broader northeast Asian mainland throughout much of the later Paleolithic, complementing the evidence discussed in Section 2.3 that Hokkaido's earliest settlement may have come from the south — a reminder that different lines of evidence can point to different, and not necessarily contradictory, connections at different points in time. Long-distance movement of obsidian and other raw materials across sea passages, evidenced at various sites, further complicates any picture of the archipelago as isolated even at this very early period.

Historical Vocabulary
Paleolithic

The term Paleolithic ("Old Stone Age") describes the long period of human technological development characterized by chipped, or "knapped," stone tools, as distinct from the later Neolithic ("New Stone Age") period associated with ground stone tools, agriculture, and settled village life in many world regions. In Japanese archaeology, the term is applied to the period from the earliest confirmed human presence in the archipelago, around 38,000 years ago, until the appearance of pottery and the associated Jōmon cultural pattern beginning within the Late Pleistocene, roughly 16,000–15,500 years ago.

Students should note that this periodization does not translate in a simple, one-to-one way across different world regions: the Japanese Paleolithic's remarkably early edge-ground stone tools blur the conventional Paleolithic/Neolithic technological boundary used in many other parts of the world, illustrating why period labels should be treated as regionally specific analytical tools rather than universal categories.

Section 2.5

Ice Age Environments and Subsistence

The animals and plants available to Paleolithic communities in the archipelago differed considerably from those familiar in later Japanese history, though the picture is more one of gradual overlap and change than of a single sharp shift. Fossil evidence indicates that large mammals, including giant deer (Sinomegaceros) and Naumann's elephant (Palaeoloxodon naumanni), inhabited parts of the archipelago across much of the Late Pleistocene. Recent refined dating indicates that Naumann's elephant persisted until roughly 35,000 to 33,000 years ago, meaning that the earliest confirmed human populations, arriving around 39,000 to 38,000 years ago, likely coexisted with these large herbivores for perhaps four to six thousand years before the species' extinction. Current evidence provides little support for the once-common assumption that human hunting was the principal cause of this extinction, and climatic or ecological factors may have played a larger role, though the question remains under active investigation. As Naumann's elephant and other megafauna disappeared over the following millennia, the animals hunted by Paleolithic communities came to more typically include deer, wild boar, and a range of smaller game, along with fish and shellfish in some coastal areas.

Vegetation likewise shifted with the glacial cycles: colder phases supported more open coniferous forest and grassland vegetation across substantial portions of the archipelago, while warmer interglacial phases supported denser and more temperate forest cover. These shifts affected the availability of plant foods, firewood, and raw materials for tools and shelter, and required Paleolithic communities to adapt their subsistence strategies as conditions changed across millennia — a much longer and slower process of adaptation than the seasonal adjustments familiar from later historical periods, but a form of environmental adaptation nonetheless.

Because organic remains preserve poorly in most of Japan's acidic soils, direct evidence for diet — animal bones with cut marks, plant remains, or residue on tools — survives only at a limited number of exceptionally well-preserved sites, and archaeologists must often infer subsistence practices indirectly from tool types, site location, and comparison with better-preserved sites elsewhere in the world. In certain Ryukyu Island contexts especially, coastal sites indicate that aquatic-resource use, including shellfish gathering, was already part of some Late Pleistocene communities' subsistence strategy well before the fuller development of the much more extensive Jōmon shell-mound tradition described in Chapters 3 and 4; this regionally specific evidence suggests an element of continuity in some coastal areas, even though the large-scale, systematic shell-midden record that characterizes the Jōmon period elsewhere in the archipelago becomes considerably clearer and more widespread only in that later period.

Section 2.6

Sites, Excavations, and the Problem of Fraud

The modern study of the Japanese Paleolithic effectively began in 1949 at Iwajuku, in present-day Gunma Prefecture, where amateur researcher Aizawa Tadahiro discovered stone tools in a layer of reddish volcanic soil (the Kantō loam) that had previously been assumed, based on prevailing geological opinion, to predate any human presence in the archipelago. Aizawa's discovery, confirmed and formally excavated with archaeologist Sugihara Sōsuke, demonstrated for the first time that a Paleolithic period existed in Japan at all, and Iwajuku remains a landmark site in the history of Japanese archaeology as a discipline, illustrating how a single well-documented find can overturn an entire field's prior assumptions.

In the decades following Iwajuku, Japanese archaeologists identified thousands of additional Paleolithic sites across the archipelago, gradually building up the chronological and technological picture described in Sections 2.2 through 2.5. This process included genuine, good-faith scholarly debate over a small number of difficult sites: at Hoshino, for example, archaeologists disputed as early as 1971 — long before the events described below — whether certain stone objects recovered from very early strata were genuinely human-made tools or naturally fractured stones, a legitimate scientific controversy entirely independent of the fraud discussed next, and one that the Japanese Archaeological Association's research reviews have continued to revisit in subsequent decades.

This cumulative, largely legitimate process of debate and revision suffered a severe and well-documented setback of a very different kind, however, connected to the activities of one individual: Fujimura Shinichi, an amateur excavator whose apparent discoveries at dozens of sites across northern Japan, over a period of about twenty-five years from roughly 1976 to 2000, had progressively pushed back the claimed date of the earliest human presence in the archipelago to several hundred thousand years — claims that, had they been genuine, would have placed the Japanese Paleolithic among the oldest and most significant early hominin records in the world.

In November 2000, a Japanese newspaper published photographs, taken secretly, showing Fujimura burying stone objects at a site in Kamitakamori, Miyagi Prefecture, the night before he "discovered" them during an excavation the following day. Confronted with this evidence, Fujimura admitted to fabricating this and numerous earlier finds. The Japanese Archaeological Association formed a special investigative committee in 2001 to examine the sites associated with Fujimura's excavations, and published its final report in 2003, concluding that the great majority of his claimed "Lower" and "Middle Paleolithic" discoveries, spanning dozens of sites and roughly a quarter century of work, were fraudulent. The Association formally withdrew recognition of nearly all of these Fujimura-associated sites, and Japanese school textbooks and museum displays that had incorporated his claimed findings had to be revised. This process was entirely separate from the ongoing, legitimate scholarly debate over sites such as Hoshino described above; students should take care not to conflate a small number of genuinely disputed early sites with the much larger set of sites that were specifically and deliberately fabricated by Fujimura.

The Fujimura case has become a widely studied cautionary episode in the history of archaeology internationally, not merely a scandal specific to Japan. It illustrates several points of enduring importance for historical and scientific method: the danger of according excessive authority to a single prolific researcher without adequate independent verification; the importance of professional skepticism even toward findings that confirm a scholar's or a field's hopes (in this case, the hope of establishing a very deep antiquity for human presence in Japan); and the value of institutional mechanisms — peer review, independent excavation, and, in this case, investigative journalism — for catching fraud that might otherwise persist for years. It is also a reminder that Japanese archaeology, following the exposure, undertook a serious and largely successful process of self-correction, re-examining its own prior claims and revising the accepted chronology accordingly — a process that itself illustrates historical method functioning as intended, even though the underlying episode was a serious failure of professional trust.

Section 2.7

Social Life in Paleolithic Communities

Reconstructing the social organization, beliefs, and daily concerns of Paleolithic communities in the archipelago is far more difficult than reconstructing their tool technology, since social life leaves far less direct physical evidence. Archaeologists can make some reasonable, evidence-based inferences: the small size and dispersed distribution of known sites suggest that Paleolithic populations lived in small, mobile social groups, moving seasonally in response to the availability of game and plant resources; while such groups plausibly involved family or kin relationships, as is common among mobile hunter-gatherer populations generally, the surviving Japanese evidence does not by itself demonstrate the specific structure of these groups. The presence of raw stone materials at some sites that must have originated from sources many kilometers away indicates either long-distance movement by the groups themselves or some form of exchange network connecting different communities, though direct evidence for organized "trade" in any formal economic sense is not available for this period.

Evidence for symbolic behavior, ritual, or belief in the Japanese Paleolithic record is limited compared to some other Paleolithic archaeological traditions elsewhere in the world, such as the cave art traditions of Ice Age Europe. This scarcity likely reflects, at least in part, the same preservation challenges discussed above — perishable materials such as wood, hide, plant fiber, and pigment on organic surfaces would not survive in most Japanese soil conditions even if they had once existed — rather than necessarily indicating an absence of symbolic or ritual life among Paleolithic communities in the archipelago. Archaeologists must therefore be especially careful, in this area more than most, to distinguish between "no evidence has survived" and "this behavior did not occur," a distinction with obvious relevance to the broader historical-methods lessons this textbook emphasizes.

Given these limits, this chapter deliberately avoids speculative reconstructions of Paleolithic religion, family structure, gender roles, or political organization that cannot be supported by direct evidence. Where later chapters describe such matters for the Jōmon period, based on richer archaeological evidence including burials, ritual objects, and settlement patterns, students should understand that this reflects a genuine improvement in the quality and quantity of available evidence beginning in the Jōmon period, not an assumption that social complexity itself began only at that point.

Section 2.8

The End of the Paleolithic and the Transition Toward Jōmon

The Japanese Paleolithic did not end abruptly but gave way gradually, over the course of several thousand years, to the cultural pattern historians and archaeologists call Jōmon, examined in full in Chapters 3 and 4. The most commonly cited marker of this transition is the appearance of pottery: fragments recovered from several sites in Japan, including the Odai Yamamoto site in Aomori Prefecture, have been dated to as early as roughly 16,000 to 15,500 years ago, making them among the oldest known pottery in the world, predating the development of pottery in many other world regions by thousands of years.

This early pottery appeared alongside stone-tool traditions that, in many respects, still closely resembled late Paleolithic technology, leading some archaeologists to describe this transitional period using the term "Incipient Jōmon," distinguishing it from the fuller "Initial Jōmon" and later Jōmon phases discussed in Chapter 3, during which pottery became more widespread, varied, and integrated into settled or semi-settled ways of life. Incipient Jōmon, on most current chronological schemes, begins well within the Late Pleistocene, roughly 16,000 to 15,500 years ago — several thousand years before the Pleistocene-Holocene boundary itself — and continues across the Pleistocene-to-Holocene transition described below, commonly extending to around 11,300 years ago by some definitions. Students should therefore understand the beginning of the Jōmon cultural pattern not as something that starts "at the beginning of the Holocene," a common but imprecise shorthand, but as a gradual development that begins within the Late Pleistocene and continues through the environmental transition into the new epoch. The reasons for the early appearance of pottery in this region remain a subject of active research; possible explanations discussed in the scholarly literature include changing subsistence needs associated with warming climate and shifting plant and animal resources, and the value of pottery for processing or storing certain foods, though no single explanation has achieved unanimous scholarly agreement.

The broader end of the Pleistocene and beginning of the Holocene epoch, conventionally dated to roughly 11,700 years ago, brought the retreat of glacial conditions, a substantial and fairly rapid rise in global sea levels, and a general warming and increase in forest cover across much of the archipelago. These changes, discussed further at the opening of Chapter 3, established the broadly modern coastline and climate pattern within which the fuller, developed Jōmon cultural pattern took shape on top of the Incipient Jōmon foundations already several millennia old by that point. Students should understand this transition as a gradual, regionally variable process rather than a single sharp break, and as a continuation of the very same long-term human adaptation to a changing physical environment that this chapter has traced from the earliest confirmed arrival of people in the archipelago.

Section 2.9

Evidence, Method, and the Limits of Knowledge

The Japanese Paleolithic record is reconstructed almost entirely through methods drawn from archaeology and the earth sciences rather than from any documentary source, since writing does not appear in the archipelago until many thousands of years later. Stratigraphy — the careful recording of which soil and sediment layers artifacts are found in, and in what order those layers were deposited — provides the basic relative chronology on which Paleolithic archaeology depends: artifacts found in deeper, older layers generally predate artifacts found in shallower, more recent layers, absent disturbance from later digging, erosion, or other processes.

Typology, the classification of stone tools by manufacturing technique and form, allows archaeologists to group similar tools together and trace how tool-making traditions changed over time and varied across different regions, providing a relative chronological and cultural framework that can then be tested against absolute dating methods. Radiocarbon dating, which measures the decay of a radioactive isotope of carbon present in organic material, provides absolute age estimates for material such as charcoal, bone, or shell, though its reliability decreases for very old samples approaching or exceeding roughly 40,000–50,000 years, near the outer limit of the method's usefulness — a limitation of direct relevance to dating the earliest phases of the Japanese Paleolithic discussed in Section 2.2.

Tephrochronology, introduced in Chapter 1, plays an especially important role in Japanese Paleolithic archaeology because the archipelago's frequent volcanic activity has deposited numerous, chemically distinct ash layers across wide areas, each associated with a specific, often independently dated eruption. When a stone-tool layer can be shown to lie above or below a securely dated tephra layer, archaeologists gain a valuable, independent check on the site's age that does not depend solely on radiocarbon dating or typological comparison. The combination of these methods — stratigraphy, typology, radiocarbon dating, and tephrochronology, cross-checked against one another — provides considerably more reliable dating than any single method used alone, a methodological lesson made especially vivid, and especially urgent, by the Fujimura fraud case discussed in Section 2.6, in which inadequate independent verification allowed fabricated claims to stand unchallenged for years.

Historical Vocabulary
Tephrochronology

Tephrochronology is the method of dating and correlating geological or archaeological layers using tephra — the ash, pumice, and rock fragments deposited by a volcanic eruption. Because a single eruption can deposit a chemically distinct ash layer across a wide area almost instantaneously in geological terms, and because many of the archipelago's major eruptions have themselves been independently dated, a tephra layer functions as a natural, widely traceable "marker bed" that archaeologists can use to check whether stone-tool layers at distant sites are older or younger than one another, and roughly how old they are in absolute terms.

This method is especially valuable for Japanese Paleolithic archaeology precisely because the archipelago, as Chapter 1 explained, sits at a highly active volcanic junction: frequent eruptions have left numerous datable tephra layers embedded in the soil across much of the country. Cross-checking stratigraphy, stone-tool typology, radiocarbon dates, and tephrochronology against one another, rather than relying on any single method alone, is the methodological practice most directly responsible for the currently accepted Japanese Paleolithic chronology described in this chapter — and, as Section 2.6's discussion of the Fujimura fraud illustrates, the practice whose absence allowed fabricated evidence to escape detection for so long.

Section 2.10

Chapter Review and Historical Thinking Checkpoint

This chapter has introduced the earliest documented human presence in the Japanese archipelago, tracing the arrival, technology, environment, and eventual transformation of Paleolithic communities into the Jōmon cultural pattern examined beginning in Chapter 3. Before proceeding, students should be able to demonstrate understanding of these foundational concepts.

The core insights of this chapter are: confirmed human presence in the Japanese archipelago currently dates to around 38,000 years ago (based on the overlapping model ranges of the Ishinomoto and Idemaruyama sites), associated first with flake-based industries and, slightly later, with blade technology, a chronology subject to ongoing refinement and, notably, to a major historical correction following the exposure of fabricated evidence in 2000. People appear to have reached different parts of the archipelago by different routes — overland in the north during periods of low sea level (though Hokkaido's earliest settlement may itself have come from the south), and by deliberate sea crossing further south, including into the Ryukyu Islands, where the evidence for early maritime capability is especially striking. Stone-tool technology changed considerably over tens of thousands of years, from early flake- and blade-based industries and remarkably early edge-ground axes to later microblade traditions connected to the broader northeast Asian mainland, with Hokkaido developing microblade technology notably earlier than central Japan. Evidence for Paleolithic environments, diet, and social life is real but limited by poor organic preservation in much of Japan's soil, requiring historians and archaeologists to distinguish carefully between well-supported inference and unsupported speculation. The Fujimura fraud case stands as a landmark lesson in archaeological verification and professional self-correction, distinct from legitimate, ongoing scholarly debate over a small number of independently disputed sites. And the Paleolithic gave way gradually, beginning with some of the world's earliest pottery around 16,000–15,500 years ago within the Late Pleistocene, to the Jōmon world examined in the chapters that follow.

Primary Source Window

Excavation Reports and the Fujimura Scandal

Creator: Japanese archaeological press and, following exposure, the Japanese Archaeological Association investigative committee · Date: excavation claims spanning the 1970s–2000; exposure and investigation, 2000 · Type: Archaeological site reports, contemporary newspaper investigation, and professional association findings

Context: Between the 1970s and 2000, excavation reports describing Fujimura Shinichi's finds at numerous sites in northern Japan were published and, for many years, broadly accepted within Japanese archaeology and reflected in some textbooks and museum exhibits, given his prolific record of apparent "discoveries" that appeared to push the date of the earliest Japanese human presence back several hundred thousand years. In October and November 2000, a Japanese newspaper conducted a covert investigation, photographing Fujimura burying objects at the Kamitakamori site the night before he publicly "found" them. The Japanese Archaeological Association subsequently formed an investigative committee to review sites associated with Fujimura's work.

Paraphrase of the Association's 2003 findings (not a verbatim quotation): The Association's investigation concluded that the overwhelming majority of Fujimura's claimed "Lower" and "Middle Paleolithic" finds, spanning dozens of sites over roughly twenty-five years, lacked scientific validity and should be withdrawn from the accepted archaeological record, a conclusion Fujimura did not dispute after his public confession.

Analysis: This case illustrates how excavation reports and even professional publications can, for a time, embed fabricated evidence within a field's accepted body of knowledge, particularly when a single individual's findings are not independently replicated or verified with sufficient rigor. It also illustrates the eventual capacity of professional and journalistic institutions to identify and correct such fraud, though only after real damage had been done to public understanding, museum displays, and educational materials describing Japan's deep prehistory. Historians should note that the source of exposure was investigative journalism working alongside archaeological expertise, not internal archaeological review alone, underscoring the value of independent scrutiny from multiple directions.

Questions for students:

  1. Why might a single prolific excavator's claims have gone unchallenged for so many years before being exposed?
  2. What does the method of exposure — covert photography by journalists — suggest about the limits of purely internal professional review?
  3. How should historians and archaeologists change their practices to reduce the risk of similar fraud in the future?
  4. What does this case suggest about the relationship between exciting or "significant" findings and the need for rigorous, skeptical verification?
Historical Debate

How and When Did People First Reach the Archipelago?

The question: Even after the Fujimura fraud eliminated many previously claimed early sites from consideration, questions remain about the precise date of earliest arrival, the number and direction of migratory movements into different parts of the archipelago, and the level of maritime technology available to the earliest arrivals. How should historians and archaeologists weigh incomplete and unevenly distributed evidence in addressing these questions?

Interpretation A — A cautious, minimal-claims approach: Some archaeologists argue that historians should rely only on the most securely dated and independently verified sites, accepting a relatively conservative timeline (around 38,000 years ago) and remaining skeptical of claims for earlier dates, additional migratory routes, or more sophisticated technology than the direct evidence clearly supports, particularly given the field's history with the Fujimura case.
Interpretation B — An inference-open approach emphasizing indirect and experimental evidence: Other researchers argue that experimental archaeology (such as the dugout-canoe voyages testing Ryukyu crossing feasibility), genetic evidence from later populations, and comparative evidence from other early Homo sapiens dispersals elsewhere in the world justify somewhat bolder inferences about early maritime capability and possible earlier or more complex migratory patterns, even where direct site-based confirmation remains incomplete.

Evidence: Both positions draw on the same body of confirmed sites, but differ in how much additional weight to give experimental, comparative, and indirect genetic evidence when direct archaeological confirmation is sparse or regionally uneven.

Why it matters: How historians resolve this question shapes how confidently later chapters can describe the relationship between Paleolithic populations and the Jōmon, Ainu, Ryukyuan, and other populations discussed later in this textbook, and shapes how cautiously students should treat popular claims about Japan's "oldest" human history.

Student task: Identify one piece of evidence discussed in this chapter that supports a cautious approach and one that supports a more inference-open approach, and explain which standard of evidence you think is more appropriate for a topic with the Japanese Paleolithic's particular history of fraud and correction.

Multiple Perspectives

Imagining Paleolithic Life: What Can and Cannot Be Said

Note: because no named individuals or first-person testimony survive from this period, the perspectives below describe categories of experience reconstructed from archaeological evidence, not quotations or biographies of specific people.

A mobile hunting group in Ice Age central Honshu: Subsistence depended on tracking game across a colder, more open landscape than later periods, requiring detailed knowledge of animal behavior, seasonal movement, and the careful curation of blade and hafted-tool technology that could be repaired or reworked rather than easily replaced.
A community reaching the Ryukyu Islands by watercraft: A single crossing could conceivably have resulted from an accidental drift voyage rather than deliberate navigation, and this possibility cannot be entirely excluded for any one event. However, the successful, apparently repeated colonization of the Ryukyu Islands across a persistent, substantial water gap is harder to explain by chance alone, and most researchers studying this region take it to imply a real and repeatable maritime competence — boatbuilding skill, the ability to navigate or at least to survive and successfully land after open-water travel, and the willingness to undertake a genuinely dangerous crossing.
A northern group moving via the Hokkaido–Sakhalin land corridor: Movement into the northern archipelago during colder phases occurred alongside the movement of large game animals such as mammoth and other megafauna, linking human subsistence strategies directly to the same environmental and migratory patterns shaping animal populations across the wider Northeast Asian region.
A modern archaeologist evaluating a newly reported "discovery": Professional practice, especially since the Fujimura case, requires independent verification, multiple lines of dating evidence, and transparent publication of excavation methods before a claimed find can be accepted as reliable evidence for the historical record.
Continuity and Change Over Time

From Paleolithic to Jōmon

What Changed

  • Stone-tool technology shifted from an initial flake-based horizon to blade and edge-ground-axe traditions, followed by regionally varied microblade technologies and, eventually, ground and polished Jōmon-style tools
  • The environment warmed and became more forested as the Pleistocene gave way to the Holocene, altering the animals and plants available for subsistence
  • Pottery appeared for the first time around 16,000–15,500 years ago, within the Late Pleistocene, enabling new methods of food processing and storage
  • Rising sea levels gradually separated the archipelago more fully from the Asian mainland, altering the routes and ease of continued contact

What Stayed the Same

  • Continued reliance on hunting, gathering, and, in coastal areas, fishing and shellfish collection as the basis of subsistence
  • Continued mobility and small-group social organization, at least into the early Jōmon period
  • Ongoing technological and, likely, demographic connections to the broader Northeast Asian mainland
  • Continued adaptation of tool technology and subsistence strategy to a changing physical environment, rather than a single fixed way of life

Why the pattern matters: Treating the Paleolithic-to-Jōmon transition as gradual and regionally variable, rather than as a sudden break, prevents students from imagining Japanese prehistory as a series of disconnected episodes rather than a continuous process of human adaptation.

Cause and Effect

Climate Change and Human Movement

Long-term causes

Pleistocene glacial cycles repeatedly lowered and raised global sea levels over tens of thousands of years, periodically narrowing or exposing land connections between the Asian mainland and parts of the archipelago.

Short-term causes

Across the Late Pleistocene, changing sea levels repeatedly altered migration routes and the width of sea gaps; during the Last Glacial Maximum these geographic effects became especially pronounced, although the initial colonization of Paleo-Honshu (around 38,000 years ago) and of parts of the Ryukyu Islands (roughly 35,000–30,000 years ago) occurred substantially earlier, well before the Last Glacial Maximum itself.

Effect

Human populations became established in different parts of the archipelago at different times, using different routes and, in some cases, different technologies suited to local conditions.

Long-term consequence

The archipelago's Paleolithic populations, whose relationship to later Jōmon, Ainu, Ryukyuan, and other populations remains a subject of ongoing genetic and archaeological research, laid the technological and adaptive groundwork later built upon by Jōmon communities beginning within the Late Pleistocene and continuing across the transition into the Holocene.

Historical complexity: Climate change did not mechanically "cause" migration in a simple, deterministic sense; it altered the range of possibilities and constraints available to human communities, who then made their own decisions about where and how to live within those changing conditions.

Everyday Life and Environment

The Texture of Paleolithic Daily Life

Although direct evidence is limited, archaeologists can reasonably reconstruct some elements of daily experience for Paleolithic communities in the archipelago. Daily life likely revolved around securing food through hunting and gathering, maintaining and repairing stone and, presumably, organic tools that have not survived, and moving camp in response to seasonal resource availability, within relatively small, closely interdependent social groups. By general analogy with mobile hunter-gatherer societies elsewhere, such groups probably also involved some degree of shared caregiving for children and for elderly or injured members, though this specific inference rests on broader knowledge of Homo sapiens behavior rather than on direct Japanese archaeological evidence, which cannot itself demonstrate such practices. Shelter likely took the form of temporary structures — perhaps simple hide or brush shelters, or the use of natural rock shelters and caves where available — appropriate to a highly mobile way of life, though direct architectural evidence rarely survives.

Fire, evidenced at some sites by hearth features and burned material, would have provided warmth, light, protection, and a means of cooking food, representing a basic but essential technology whose significance is easy to overlook given how little direct evidence survives compared to stone tools. Understanding these everyday dimensions of Paleolithic life, even in outline, helps prevent this early chapter from reducing tens of thousands of years of human experience to a bare list of stone-tool types, and reminds students that the people who left this evidence lived full, socially embedded lives whose richness exceeds what the surviving archaeological record alone can fully capture.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has introduced the earliest documented human presence in the Japanese archipelago, a period known as the Japanese Paleolithic, extending from the currently accepted earliest confirmed arrival around 38,000 years ago to the appearance of pottery and the beginning of the Incipient Jōmon phase within the Late Pleistocene, around 16,000–15,500 years ago. This period is documented almost entirely through archaeological and geological evidence rather than any written source, requiring historians to rely on stratigraphy, typology, radiocarbon dating, and tephrochronology to reconstruct its chronology and content.

People appear to have reached different parts of the archipelago by different routes at different times. In the north, intermittent land connections linking Hokkaido to Sakhalin and the Asian mainland, exposed during periods of low Pleistocene sea level, provided a genuine overland corridor. Further south, the persistence of a water gap between Kyushu and the Korean Peninsula, and a still more substantial water gap separating the Ryukyu Islands from Taiwan and the mainland, indicate that at least some populations possessed the maritime technology and skill necessary for deliberate sea crossing at a surprisingly early date, a conclusion supported by both archaeological evidence and experimental voyage reconstructions.

Stone-tool technology developed considerably over this long period, from an initial flake-based industry to blade-based industries and remarkably early edge-ground stone axes appearing within a few thousand years, and eventually to the later spread of microblade technology connected to broader Northeast Asian traditions — a spread that began notably earlier in Hokkaido than in central Japan. Environmental conditions shifted substantially across the glacial and interglacial cycles of the Pleistocene, affecting the animals and plants available to Paleolithic communities, including a multi-millennial period of overlap between human populations and now-extinct megafauna such as Naumann's elephant, and requiring ongoing adaptation in subsistence strategy.

The chapter has also addressed, directly and in some detail, the Fujimura Shinichi fraud case, in which fabricated "discoveries" spanning about twenty-five years were exposed in 2000, forcing a major correction of previously accepted claims about the deep antiquity of human presence in Japan. This episode serves as both an important corrective to the historical record and a valuable case study in archaeological method, professional verification, and the risks of according excessive, insufficiently scrutinized authority to a single researcher's claims.

At the same time, the chapter has been careful to distinguish well-supported archaeological inference from unsupported speculation, particularly regarding Paleolithic social organization, belief, and daily life, for which direct evidence remains limited by poor organic preservation across much of the archipelago's soils. The chapter has consistently avoided describing Paleolithic populations as early "Japanese people" in any ethnic or national sense, since such a description would project a much later cultural and political identity backward onto populations whose relationship to later Jōmon, Ainu, Ryukyuan, and other groups remains, in important respects, a subject of ongoing scholarly investigation rather than settled certainty.

Finally, the chapter has traced the gradual transition from Paleolithic technology and lifeways toward the Jōmon cultural pattern, marked most clearly by the appearance of some of the world's earliest pottery and by the environmental transformations accompanying the shift from the Pleistocene to the Holocene. This transition sets the stage directly for Chapters 3 and 4, which examine the Jōmon world in full historical and cultural depth.

These findings are not incidental background to later Japanese history. The technological ingenuity, environmental adaptability, and demonstrated maritime capability of the archipelago's earliest inhabitants establish patterns of human adaptation to a demanding physical environment that will recur, in new forms, throughout this textbook's treatment of the Jōmon, Yayoi, and later periods.

The chapter's methodological lessons — the careful use of multiple, cross-checked lines of evidence, and the vigilance required to detect and correct fraudulent or mistaken claims — are equally important preparation for the historical reasoning skills this textbook will continue to develop throughout its remaining ninety-eight chapters.

Students who understand the Japanese Paleolithic as a genuine, evidence-based field of historical inquiry — with real debates, real uncertainties, and a well-documented history of professional self-correction — are well prepared to approach the richer, but still carefully evaluated, evidence of the Jōmon world examined next.

Key Terms

Paleolithic
The "Old Stone Age," characterized by chipped stone-tool technology; in Japan, the period from around 38,000 years ago to the appearance of pottery around 16,000–15,500 years ago within the Late Pleistocene.
Pleistocene
The geological epoch, roughly 2.6 million to 11,700 years ago, marked by repeated glacial and interglacial cycles.
Holocene
The geological epoch beginning about 11,700 years ago, whose warmer postglacial conditions accompanied the further development and expansion of Jōmon lifeways already established during the Late Pleistocene.
Glacial / interglacial cycle
Alternating cold (glacial) and warmer (interglacial) phases within the Pleistocene, driving major changes in global sea level and climate.
Last Glacial Maximum
The period of lowest global sea level in the most recent glacial cycle, roughly 26,000–19,000 years ago.
Land bridge
A temporary overland connection exposed during periods of low sea level; documented most securely in Japan between Hokkaido, Sakhalin, and the Asian mainland.
Flake technology
A stone-toolmaking technique in which usable flakes are struck directly from a stone core using comparatively simple methods; characteristic of the earliest securely dated Japanese Paleolithic assemblages, around 38,000 years ago.
Blade technology
A stone-toolmaking technique producing long, parallel-sided flakes (blades) from a carefully prepared core, becoming established in Japan slightly after the earliest flake-based horizon, roughly 37,000–36,000 years ago.
Knapping
The process of shaping stone tools by striking or pressure-flaking a stone core.
Edge-ground stone axe
A stone tool whose cutting edge was ground smooth rather than left in its flaked state; remarkably early examples appear in Japan roughly 37,000–36,000 years ago, alongside the emergence of blade technology.
Microblade
A small, standardized stone blade produced from a specially prepared core, set in rows into composite tools; appears in Hokkaido by roughly 26,000 years ago and continues to about 15,000 years ago, with prominent central Japanese traditions developing somewhat later, roughly 20,000–16,000 years ago.
Hafting
The process of mounting a stone tool onto a wooden, bone, or antler handle or shaft.
Stratigraphy
The study of layered soil and sediment deposits, used to establish the relative chronological order of archaeological finds.
Typology
The classification of artifacts, especially stone tools, by manufacturing technique and form.
Radiocarbon dating
A method of estimating the age of organic material by measuring the decay of a radioactive carbon isotope; less reliable for material older than roughly 40,000–50,000 years.
Tephrochronology
The dating of archaeological or geological layers using chemically distinct volcanic ash (tephra) deposits from known eruptions.
Megafauna
Very large animal species, some now extinct, that inhabited parts of the archipelago during earlier phases of the Pleistocene.
Palaeoloxodon
An extinct genus of elephant whose fossil remains have been found in Japan, associated with earlier, more open Pleistocene land connections to the continent.
Homo sapiens
The species of anatomically modern humans responsible for the confirmed Paleolithic archaeological record in the Japanese archipelago.
Denisovan / Neanderthal
Other hominin populations known from the broader Eurasian fossil and genetic record, relevant background to the broader story of human dispersal, though not confirmed within the Japanese archipelago itself.
Incipient Jōmon
A term for the transitional period beginning roughly 16,000–15,500 years ago, within the Late Pleistocene, in which early pottery appears alongside continuing Paleolithic-style stone-tool traditions.
Watercraft hypothesis
The proposal that early Homo sapiens populations used boats or rafts to cross open water into parts of the archipelago, including the Ryukyu Islands, supported by archaeological and experimental evidence.
Archaeological fraud
The deliberate fabrication of archaeological evidence, most notably documented in Japan through the Fujimura Shinichi case exposed in 2000.
Anachronism
The error of applying a later concept, identity, or category to an earlier period in which it did not yet exist or apply; relevant here to describing Paleolithic populations as "Japanese."
Iwajuku
The Gunma Prefecture site whose 1949 excavation established the existence of a Japanese Paleolithic as a field of study.

Major Sites, Peoples, and Features

Identification & Significance

Iwajuku Site
The Gunma Prefecture site excavated from 1949 that established the existence of a Japanese Paleolithic period as recognized field of study.
Ryukyu Islands cave sites (e.g., Yamashita-chō, Pinza-Abu)
Sites yielding early human skeletal remains dated largely through associated stratigraphic materials rather than direct dating of the bones; important to debates over Paleolithic maritime crossing capability.
Shiraho-Saonetabaru Cave
An Ishigaki Island site that has produced directly radiocarbon-dated human skeletal remains, roughly 16,000–20,000 years old, among the most securely dated Pleistocene human remains in the Ryukyus.
Hoshino Site
A site subject to genuine, long-running scholarly debate over the authenticity of its very early stone artifacts, independent of and predating the Fujimura fraud.
Hokkaido microblade sites
Sites reflecting close technological ties between northern Japan and the broader Siberian/Northeast Asian mainland.
Kamitakamori Site
The Miyagi Prefecture site where Fujimura Shinichi was photographed burying artifacts in 2000, precipitating the exposure of his fraud.
Odai Yamamoto Site
An Aomori Prefecture site that has yielded some of the world's earliest known pottery fragments, dated to roughly 16,000–15,500 years ago, within the Late Pleistocene.
Fujimura Shinichi
Amateur archaeologist whose fabricated discoveries, exposed in 2000, forced a major revision of the accepted Japanese Paleolithic chronology.
Aizawa Tadahiro
Amateur researcher whose 1949 discovery at Iwajuku established the existence of the Japanese Paleolithic.
Sugihara Sōsuke
Archaeologist who confirmed and formally excavated the Iwajuku site alongside Aizawa.
Japanese Archaeological Association
The professional body that investigated and formally repudiated the Fujimura-associated "discoveries" following the 2000 exposure.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has carried the physical foundations introduced in Chapter 1 into their first human chapter: the long, largely anonymous story of the archipelago's earliest inhabitants. It has shown that even without names, chronicles, or political events, historians and archaeologists can reconstruct a genuine and evidence-based account of human life, technology, and adaptation stretching back tens of thousands of years — while also being honest about the real limits of what that evidence can support.

The chapter's treatment of the Fujimura fraud case matters beyond its immediate subject. It demonstrates that even a field built on careful scientific method can be led astray by insufficiently verified claims, and that professional self-correction, however painful, is a genuine strength of serious historical and scientific inquiry rather than a sign of the field's unreliability. Students should carry this lesson of evidentiary caution into every subsequent chapter of this textbook, particularly those addressing periods with their own contested chronologies, competing interpretations, or politically charged questions of memory and identity.

The chapter has also planted an important seed for the rest of the book: the recognition that the archipelago's human population did not arrive as a single, unified group with a fixed identity, but through multiple movements, by different routes, over an extended period, adapting technologically and environmentally along the way. This complexity anticipates the textbook's continuing insistence — introduced in Chapter 1 and developed throughout — that "Japan" and "the Japanese people" are historical constructions built over many centuries, not timeless categories that can be projected back onto the archipelago's deep past.

Students who understand the Paleolithic as a genuine field of ongoing inquiry, shaped by both remarkable discoveries and a serious, well-documented episode of fraud, are well prepared to move forward into Chapters 3 and 4, where the richer, though still carefully evaluated, evidence of the Jōmon world reveals a rapidly diversifying set of societies building on the technological and adaptive foundations this chapter has described.
