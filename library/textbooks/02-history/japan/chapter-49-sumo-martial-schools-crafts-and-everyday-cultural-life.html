Part VII — Tokugawa Japan

Sumo, Martial Schools, Crafts, and Everyday Cultural Life

Japanese History: From Jōmon Japan to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

Tokugawa peace did not end the importance of physical skill, craftsmanship, or inherited custom. It changed the institutions, markets, audiences, and everyday settings in which they were learned, performed, produced, and transmitted.

In a city such as Edo, a crowd might gather for a fundraising sumo tournament while, a few streets away, a samurai instructor taught a lineage of swordsmanship and a carpenter trained an apprentice. These scenes belong to the same society: one in which generations without large domestic wars redirected martial skill, urban commerce expanded markets for crafts, and households organized work and consumption within a system of status.

The chapter asks how Tokugawa society transformed older martial skills, inherited crafts and local customs into institutions, products and practices that shaped everyday life for different groups. It is not a catalogue of timeless Japanese customs. Sumo, martial arts and crafts had earlier roots, but their Tokugawa forms differ from modern professional sumo, kendo, judo and “traditional crafts,” and the chapter keeps that difference visible.

Four forces recur. Peace changed what martial skill was for. Urban and regional demand created audiences and markets, from sumo spectators to buyers of porcelain, lacquer and cotton. Hereditary skills and household labor, including the work of women and children, sustained workshops, farms and shops. And inequality of status, wealth, gender and region meant that “everyday life” was many lives, not one.

A further dimension is geography. The practices discussed here were most visible in Edo, Osaka and Kyoto and in major castle towns, but they also depended on forests, iron-producing mountains, cotton fields, indigo districts, ports and pilgrimage routes. Villages, domains and regions experienced them differently, and societies such as the Ainu and the Ryukyuan kingdom, discussed in Chapter 48, had their own institutions and customs. The chapter therefore avoids treating the customs of one large city as the culture of the whole archipelago.

Evidence includes printed ranking sheets, school scrolls, guild and household records, houses, utensils, textiles, prints, travel diaries and archaeological finds. Museum collections are valuable but favor prosperous households and notable makers, and printed sources favor literate urban audiences, so the chapter repeatedly asks whose lives the sources make visible. It builds on Chapters 43–48 and leads into Chapter 50, which turns to learning, scholarship, reform and the pressures on the late shogunate.

Historical Context

Chapter 42 described the bakuhan system that kept domestic order, and Chapter 43 the status categories and household (ie) that organized obligation and inheritance. Chapter 44 showed how villages produced taxes, by-employment and protest, and Chapter 45 how castle towns, highways, merchants and markets connected regions. Chapter 46 treated theater, prints and pleasure districts, Chapter 47 religious institutions and festivals, and Chapter 48 controlled external trade, which supplied some goods and markets for Japanese crafts, including Hizen porcelain.

This chapter builds on those foundations without retelling them. Its subject is the physical and material side of Tokugawa society: bodies trained for combat or performance, hands that made goods, homes and meals that sustained households, and the leisure and travel through which people met beyond their neighborhoods. Overlaps are unavoidable, since sumo relied on shrine and temple fundraising, crafts on merchant networks, and festivals combined religion with recreation; the chapter explains these links while concentrating on everyday cultural function.

Readers should also keep in mind the political setting. Domains competed for prestige and revenue, the shogunate periodically issued reform edicts, and population, urbanization and commercial agriculture changed over two and a half centuries. Practices described in one section may therefore refer to different decades or places, and the text flags where the evidence is local or uncertain.

Section 49.1

From a Warrior Society to a Culture of Peace

The military settlement that followed Sekigahara (1600) and the siege of Osaka (1614–1615), examined in Chapters 41 and 42, did not abolish the warrior class. It gave warriors a different job. Samurai households remained hereditary, stipended and legally privileged, but for most of the seventeenth, eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries they served as administrators, ceremonial attendants, accountants and castle-town residents rather than as fighters. The Shimabara Rebellion of 1637–1638 (Chapter 47) was the last large-scale campaign for generations, and even it was fought largely by retainers who had little recent experience of battle. By the late eighteenth century, few active samurai had ever seen combat, and many had little direct knowledge of the battlefield techniques their lineages claimed to preserve.

Official language framed this role. The Buke shohatto, the laws for warrior houses first issued in 1615, urged warriors to cultivate both literary and martial arts, a pairing often summarized as bunbu, and it framed martial competence as part of the duty of a warrior household rather than as an emergency skill. In practice domains interpreted the ideal differently: some invested in schools and halls for training, while others, burdened by debt and by stipends that failed to keep pace with prices, treated martial display as an expense to be trimmed. The condition of samurai finances (Chapter 43) therefore shaped how much training was possible. The Buke shohatto thus set an ideal rather than a training program: it told warrior houses what to value while leaving domains and lineages to decide how martial skill would actually be taught.

What, then, was the purpose of martial training? Historians generally answer that its meanings multiplied. For samurai, skill with sword, bow, spear and horse was a mark of status and a form of education, expected of a warrior household even when never tested in war. For domains, martial training demonstrated readiness to the shogunate and maintained a hierarchy of retainers. For instructors, a school was a livelihood and a lineage. For other groups, physical prowess could become entertainment, as in sumo, or a path to local standing, as some wealthy villagers and townspeople later took up swordsmanship. Peace did not end the importance of physical skill; it changed who valued it, why, and in what settings it was learned and displayed.

Peace also altered who could be considered skilled. Fighting ability could not be tested in war, so reputations rested on lineages, licenses, demonstrations and the judgment of peers, and instructors competed for pupils and patrons. This made martial education a social marketplace as well as a military one. The question of readiness returned in the middle of the nineteenth century, when foreign pressure (Chapter 51) led the shogunate to found a martial training institute, the Kōbusho, in the mid-1850s; that late effort suggests how far the earlier tradition had moved from field preparation toward education and ceremony, though the details of the institute fall outside this chapter.

Evidence for these changes comes from school scrolls, domain regulations, printed rankings, prints, craft objects and household records, and each kind favors particular groups. Martial documents mostly come from samurai institutions, sumo evidence from urban publishers and audiences, and craft evidence from workshops and collectors. The chapter therefore treats Tokugawa society as a set of overlapping social worlds rather than a single “Edo culture,” and it avoids reading modern sumo, kendo or judo back into the period.

Section 49.2

The Development of Tokugawa Sumo

Wrestling had a long pre-Tokugawa history. Court records from the Nara and Heian periods describe sumai performed as part of annual ceremonies, and the Nihon Shoki includes a legendary wrestling match whose figures belong to myth and later tradition rather than documented history. In the medieval and Sengoku periods, bouts were also staged among warriors, and some commanders, including Oda Nobunaga, are reported to have organized matches. These accounts show that wrestling had ritual, military and entertainment functions, but they do not describe a professional institution. Tokugawa sumo developed in a different setting: a society with growing cities, cash markets, large audiences and tight concern for public order.

The central institution was kanjin-zumō, tournaments held to raise funds (kanjin) for the construction or repair of temples and shrines. Fundraising gave wrestling a religious justification and a legal pretext for public performance. Authorities in Edo, Kyoto and Osaka licensed these events, and regulation often followed disorder: masterless warriors and the retainers of lords sometimes quarreled at or around matches, and the shogunate periodically restricted or banned street wrestling. Over the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, kanjin-zumō evolved from occasional benefit events into recurring seasonal tournaments run by professional organizations, with officials, referees and rules for rank and payment. In Edo, venues included the grounds of Tomioka Hachimangū in Fukagawa and later Ekōin in Ryōgoku, which tradition associates with regular tournaments from the later eighteenth century and as the principal venue from the early 1830s; histories describe the exact dates of these arrangements differently.

Tournaments were typically short, often ten days, and weather-dependent because early venues were outdoors or only partly covered; the Tokyo Metropolitan Library’s sumo exhibition captures this with an Edo phrase about a fine fellow who lives a whole year on twenty days of work. That phrase refers to the roughly two annual Edo tournaments, and it reminds us that wrestlers’ income also depended on patrons, retainers’ stipends and off-season activity. Authorities in different cities also differed, so Osaka and Kyoto sumo had distinct organizations and rankings from Edo’s, and the unified national structure of modern professional sumo belongs to a later era.

Sumo’s institutions depended on people as well as venues. Retired wrestlers who held the status of elders (toshiyori) organized tournaments, trained recruits and managed the wrestlers’ associations, and young men entered through the households of established masters, a pattern that anticipates later training stables but should not be assumed identical to them. Ranks developed within the East–West ranking system, and the title of ōzeki long signified the highest working rank before the yokozuna honor acquired its modern prominence. Tournament operations also required referees, ring officials and ticket sellers, which made sumo an employer of many people beyond the wrestlers themselves.

Rules about who could take part and who could watch reveal social boundaries. The sacred ring was closed to women, and professional sumo was a male institution, though historians have documented performances by women wrestlers and by other novelty groups at fairs and entertainment districts, which authorities at times restricted. Wrestling by the blind and by unusual or very large performers also appeared as spectacle. The boundary between sport, ritual and show was therefore blurred, and the line that modern sumo draws between respectable ritual and mere entertainment was not fixed in the Edo period. In Kyoto and Osaka, tournaments with their own rankings and customs coexisted with Edo’s and sometimes drew wrestlers from one city to another.

Section 49.3

Wrestlers, Rankings, Patrons, and Popular Spectacle

Printed rankings (banzuke) were central to sumo’s public life. According to the Tokyo Metropolitan Library, banzuke were originally written on boards and posted at venues. As kanjin-zumō organization matured, publishers began issuing woodblock-printed versions so that many people could learn wrestlers’ names and ranks. These sheets listed East and West sides, ordered by rank, with wrestlers’ names and often home regions or affiliations. Their popularity inspired mitate banzuke, parody rankings of hot springs, restaurants, entertainers and scholars; the library’s exhibit cites a famous rich-men’s ranking of 1846, which shows that banzuke culture spread beyond sumo itself.

Patronage structured wrestlers’ careers. Many top wrestlers were retained by daimyō houses, receiving stipends, status and the right to represent a lord’s domain, while others relied on tournament income and sponsors. Tanikaze Kajinosuke (1750–1795), associated with the Sendai domain, and Raiden Tameemon (1767–1825), associated with Matsue, became the most celebrated champions of the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries. Records of rank and bouts, printed lists, and prints support their prominence, but stories about their size, strength and rivalries grew in later retellings, and statistics reported in popular books should be treated cautiously. The honorary yokozuna title, later seen as the sport’s highest rank, developed through licensing by Yoshida lineage authorities and was not a fixed rank in the modern sense during this period.

Commercial celebrity and warrior patronage reinforced each other: a retained wrestler brought prestige to his lord’s house, while public rank and fame raised his value to promoters. Ōzeki rank, patronage and licensing are best seen as parts of one system of reputation, not separate honors.

Audiences included townspeople, samurai and visiting travelers, and prints and handbills make wrestlers early celebrities. Sacred ritual elements—entering the ring, salt, and the exclusion of women from the ring—reflect the religious setting of kanjin events, yet the spectacle also depended on commerce: ticketing, seating, vendors and publishers. Sumo therefore shows how a religious fundraising device and a samurai-patronage system became a commercial entertainment, linked to but distinct from the kabuki and print culture discussed in Chapter 46.

The system of ranks and honors also offers evidence about how fame worked. The yokozuna title, awarded through a licensing arrangement associated with the Yoshida family, began as a privilege to wear a special rope at ring-entering ceremonies; the traditional list of early holders includes figures whose existence or achievements are uncertain, and modern lists were compiled later. Tanikaze and another wrestler are commonly counted among the first to receive the licence in 1789, but students should treat the exact sequence cautiously because sources and later lists differ. The point for this chapter is that rank and honor were being codified at the same time that sumo was becoming a public business.

The audience that gave the wrestlers their value left traces in print and in recorded events. Woodblock prints depicting wrestlers, handbills, illustrated books and parody rankings show enthusiasm among townspeople, and the shogun’s viewing of a specially staged tournament, traditionally dated to 1791, shows that sumo’s appeal extended to the highest levels of the warrior elite. Even so, spectators, bettors and fans appear mainly through the works that sold to them, and the voices of poorer attendees are largely missing. Wrestlers themselves are visible chiefly through rank sheets, prints and anecdote, so their working conditions, earnings and injuries must be reconstructed with great care.

Section 49.4

Martial Schools and the Education of Warriors

The characteristic institution of Tokugawa martial education was the ryūha, a lineage claiming descent from a founder and transmitting a defined body of techniques. Many ryūha originated in the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, for example schools associated with Katori and Kashima, and more appeared in the seventeenth century. They taught swordsmanship, spearmanship, archery, horsemanship, jūjutsu, firearms and other arts, usually in combinations. Teaching relied on kata, prearranged forms, repeated under an instructor’s supervision, and on written scrolls that recorded principles, techniques or genealogy.

Several schools illustrate the range. Some, such as the Katori Shintō-ryū associated with an early founder of the fifteenth century, claimed long lineages and survive as living traditions today, but historians distinguish the school’s claims about its origins from its documented history, and the later label “koryū” (old school) is a modern category. Others specialized in horsemanship, such as the Ōtsubo-ryū, which was linked to the shogunal court, or in archery, spearmanship, firearms and jūjutsu. A samurai might therefore study several arts, and the schools overlapped in membership while keeping their teachings distinct.

Credentials structured transmission. Students progressed through levels, often described as initial, middle and deep transmission, and could receive a license (menkyo) certifying competence or the right to teach. The highest license, often called menkyo kaiden, conferred full transmission. Such documents mattered because they protected a lineage’s authority and income, and some were issued for fees or gifts; historians read them as evidence of school organization, not simply of skill. Instructors worked in several settings: they might be hired by domains as retainers, maintain private dōjō in castle towns or Edo, or teach itinerant students.

Admission and transmission were governed by ritual and obligation. Students often swore written oaths of secrecy and loyalty, paid entry gifts and fees, and received teachings gradually, which protected the school’s income and its claims to authority. The secrecy also shaped what survives: scrolls were produced for initiated students, so many surviving documents represent a school’s official presentation of itself rather than the daily experience of training. Women of samurai households sometimes learned polearm techniques such as the naginata, though evidence about their training varies by domain and school. The diversity of schools, the variety of arts and the dependence on patronage show a martial culture closer to a network of professional teachers than to a uniform system.

Domain schools (hankō) increasingly incorporated martial instruction alongside Confucian study, especially in the later eighteenth century, when reform-minded officials worried about samurai softness. Instruction varied widely by domain and rank: elite retainers and heirs often received systematic schooling, while lower-ranking samurai and commoners had uneven access. Some well-off villagers and townspeople sought training, sometimes in the same schools, which complicates the picture of martial arts as a strict samurai monopoly, though regional and school practices differed and status distinctions remained significant.

Access varied with status and money. Commoners who joined schools paid fees and gifts and might be admitted only to lower levels or to schools willing to accept them, so one lineage could offer very different experiences to different students. Domain schools standardized instruction for retainers, private dōjō sold it to paying students, and lineage transmission reserved the deepest teachings for a few. Licenses also marked authority: a teaching license could allow a graduate to open his own dōjō or teach on a lineage’s behalf, so schools could grow by branching, but a lineage head might withhold the highest level to keep control of the school’s name and income.

Historical Vocabulary
Ryūha and menkyo

A ryūha is a martial lineage or school transmitting a body of techniques; a menkyo is a license certifying a student’s level of mastery or right to teach. Modern “styles” and ranks should not be assumed to match them. Related terms: kanjin-zumō (fundraising sumo), banzuke (ranking sheet), shokunin (craftsperson), machiya (urban townhouse), minka (farmhouse).

Section 49.5

Swordsmanship, Strategy, and Martial Ideals

Two lineages illustrate how martial schools connected to government. The Yagyū Shinkage-ryū, a branch of the Shinkage tradition, enjoyed shogunal patronage, and Yagyū Munenori’s Heihō kadensho (1632) linked sword practice to statecraft and discipline. Ittō-ryū and its branches also served shogunal and domain patrons. Such texts addressed an audience that needed to justify warrior status in peacetime, and they stress composure, readiness and judgment more than battlefield tactics.

Other lineages show alternative paths. Ono Jirōemon Tadaaki, associated with Ittō-ryū, served as a sword instructor to the shogunate, and branches of the school spread through domains, while the Yagyū family’s Shinkage-ryū also had an Owari line. Miyamoto Musashi spent his final years in the Kumamoto domain of the Hosokawa family, and the Gorin no sho is structured as five scrolls on earth, water, fire, wind and void. These cases show that famed swordsmen depended on domain patronage and that their ideas were shaped by lordly service as much as by independent wandering, which popular fiction tends to emphasize.

Miyamoto Musashi (d. 1645) is known to historians through the Gorin no sho, a treatise attributed to him in his final years, and through a few other sources; the Musashi of novels, plays and films is largely a later creation, so biographical details should be separated from legend. Texts attributed to the Zen monk Takuan Sōhō linked sword practice with Buddhist ideas of an unattached mind, and treatises by Yamaga Sokō and Daidōji Yūzan offered competing ideas of warrior conduct, while Hagakure, compiled in the early eighteenth century from Yamamoto Tsunetomo’s recollections, expresses a particular domain-retainer’s outlook rather than a universal code.

These authors offered different answers. Munenori treated swordsmanship as a means of governing oneself and others, Musashi addressed individual strategy in the language of a master who had served a domain, writings associated with Takuan stressed an unattached mind, and the Saga retainer behind Hagakure emphasized unconditional devotion to a lord and domain. Yamaga Sokō, by contrast, set out a Confucian vocation for the warrior as moral exemplar. Their disagreement shows a class debating what responsibility meant when it no longer fought, and reading them against one another is more informative than reading any as “the samurai code.”

The Hagakure offers a cautionary case about reception. Compiled in the early eighteenth century from the recollections of Yamamoto Tsunetomo, a retainer of the Saga domain, it reflects a specific retainer’s grievances and ideals, and it did not circulate as a general warrior manual in the Edo period; its fame as a statement of samurai values grew much later. Reading it alongside other writers shows that Edo thinkers disagreed about whether loyalty, learning, ritual or technical skill defined a good warrior. The shared concern was legitimacy: how should a hereditary military elite justify itself when it no longer fought?

These writings show that “bushidō” was not a single, timeless code in the Tokugawa period. Different authors proposed Confucian, Buddhist and domain-specific ideals, often addressing the problem of what samurai were for in peacetime. The unified “bushidō” familiar from the early twentieth century was assembled in the Meiji period and later (see Chapter 58). Students should therefore read Edo martial writings as arguments about conduct, authority and identity, and not as descriptions of what samurai universally did. Readers should also remember who wrote and who read these works: authors were mostly warriors addressing other warriors, and their prescriptions reveal elite anxieties more directly than everyday behavior.

Section 49.6

Training, Competition, and the Transformation of Martial Practice

The All Japan Kendo Federation’s historical summaries provide a useful chronology for one line of development: protective equipment and bamboo-sword sparring. Naganuma Shirōzaemon Kunisato, associated with Jikishinkage-ryū, is credited with developing or popularizing equipment-based practice around 1711–1715. Nakanishi Chūzō Tsugutake, in an Ittō-ryū lineage, advanced the use of equipment and the bamboo sword in the middle decades of the century, around 1751–1764. These developments were not simultaneous and did not spread uniformly: many schools kept to wooden swords and kata, and some resisted the new methods as unsafe to the integrity of technique.

From roughly 1789 to 1801, inter-school contests became increasingly prominent, and the nineteenth century saw further changes including the four-slat bamboo sword, improved torso protection and dōjō that attracted students from many domains. The late Edo period also saw some masters travel and exchange practice, creating networks of schools beyond individual domains. These networks anticipated later national organizations but did not constitute them, and rules, scoring and equipment remained school-specific.

The adoption of equipment was contested. Advocates argued that protective armor and the bamboo sword allowed students to strike with full force and learn timing, distance and spirit against a resisting opponent, while critics, especially in schools built around kata, feared that contests would reward quickness over principle and distort the school’s teaching. The result was a variety of practices: some schools forbade contests, some combined kata with sparring, and some embraced open exchanges. Debates over the length and construction of the bamboo sword show that standardization was incomplete well into the nineteenth century.

Kata and sparring coexisted because they served different educational purposes. Kata transmitted a school’s principles and kept its repertoire intact, protecting a lineage’s identity and authority, while sparring trained speed, nerve and timing against a resisting opponent and drew students and reputation. Many instructors used both. The modern kendo comparison is limited because later kendo standardized equipment, rules and scoring across schools and carried a national educational mission that Edo schools did not share. Instructors had practical reasons to adopt or resist each method, since students, fees and reputation depended on what a school could promise, and the choice shaped both the school’s identity and its income.

By the later period, prominent urban dōjō, including those associated with Chiba Shūsaku, Saitō Yakurō and Momoi Shunzō in Edo, drew students from many domains and helped create wider networks of exchange. These networks matter because they connected samurai and sometimes commoners across regions and prepared the ground for political associations at the end of the shogunate. After 1868 the abolition of the samurai order and the prohibition on wearing swords in 1876 (Chapter 54) disrupted the economic basis of many schools, and later reorganization produced modern judo, kendo and other budō. Jūjutsu schools such as Tenjin Shin’yō-ryū and Kitō-ryū were among those on which Kanō Jigorō later drew, which is why the Edo period is best understood as ancestry rather than as an early version of the modern arts.

The transformation matters because it shows peacetime martial culture as innovative, not merely conservative. Protective equipment made repeated, relatively safe competition possible, expanding the pool of practitioners, while kata-based schools preserved other values. Modern kendo, with standardized rules and national federations, emerged in the Meiji and later twentieth century, and judo was created by Kanō Jigorō in the 1880s from jūjutsu schools. Edo practices should therefore be understood on their own terms.

Section 49.7

The Artisan’s World: Workshops, Households, and Apprenticeship

Artisans (shokunin) occupied the third rung of the idealized four-status order described in Chapter 43, below peasants and above merchants. That ranking said little about daily life. Skilled urban craftsmen such as carpenters, plasterers, dyers, lacquerers and metalworkers often earned more than poor samurai and many villagers, while less-skilled day laborers and peddlers were far poorer. Status also differed from legal residence: most Edo artisans lived as tenants in row houses (nagaya) and were subject to the town administration, and wealth, reputation and demand shaped their standing more than the status label.

The workshop was also a household. A master (oyakata) lived and worked with his family, apprentices and sometimes hired workers, and the household economy depended on the labor of wives, children and others. Apprentices, often boys sent from the countryside or poorer families around their early teens, lived in the master’s house, performed domestic and workshop chores, and learned by observation and repetition. Terms of service varied widely by trade, place and the age at which a boy entered; contracts running from several years to around a decade or more are reported, often followed by further service to repay training, and written agreements with guarantors were common in towns, though the evidence is uneven and no single term was typical. The system provided training and subsistence but also bound young workers to harsh discipline and unpaid labor, so it should not be romanticized.

Apprentices normally received food, lodging and clothing rather than wages, sometimes with small gifts at festivals, and they were subject to the master’s discipline; guarantors answered for their conduct, and runaways and disputes appear in records. This arrangement transferred the cost of training to the apprentice’s family and the benefit of cheap labor to the master. Women shaped the system as well: wives managed accounts, meals and customers, daughters and widows sometimes continued trades, and the household’s reproduction depended on their labor.

Relations with merchants and patrons varied. Some artisans sold directly from workshop-shops; others produced for wholesalers who advanced materials or credit and set prices, which made them dependent on merchant capital; and some served temples, daimyō houses or the shogunate through commissions or appointments. These ties explain why craft status, wealth and independence varied so much within the same trade.

Trades were organized in associations that regulated membership, training and prices. Merchant wholesalers’ associations (kabunakama) were recognized and licensed by the shogunate from the early eighteenth century, while many artisan and occupational associations existed with varied legal standing. The Tenpō reforms moved against monopolistic wholesalers in 1841, a broader dissolution order followed in 1842, and associations were again authorized in 1851, though not every earlier privilege returned. Associations controlled entry, could restrict competition and also provided mutual assistance, ritual and dispute settlement. Authorities issued wage and price ordinances, particularly for construction labor in Edo, which suggests both the importance of skilled labor and officials’ concern about costs.

Rural production differed from urban crafts. Many villages combined farming with by-employment such as spinning, weaving, paper-making, sake brewing, oil pressing and pottery, and these tasks were often done by women, children and older family members during agricultural slack seasons. Merchants frequently supplied materials and purchased finished goods through putting-out arrangements, linking village households to urban markets (see Chapter 45). Historians use guild records, tax and census registers, contracts, workshop account books, prints and surviving tools to study artisan life, but these sources favor larger workshops, male masters and urban centers, and give little direct testimony from apprentices or women.

Section 49.8

Metalworking, Swordsmithing, Woodworking, and Construction

Iron production underpinned much of Tokugawa metalworking. In the mountains of the Chūgoku region, tatara furnaces smelted iron sand and charcoal into a large bloom (kera) of mixed quality, from which skilled workers selected high-carbon pieces as tamahagane and sorted other iron and steel for tools, and iron moved through markets to smiths across the archipelago. Sword production continued after the wars ended, but its market changed: smiths worked for warrior households, for gift exchange and for the status display that peace made more important. Makers working after the 1590s are classed by connoisseurs as shintō (“new swords”), and Osaka and Edo smiths produced well-known blades; the later shinshintō revival belongs to the late eighteenth and nineteenth centuries. These categories are collecting terms, not Edo names.

A single smelt lasted several days and consumed many tons of iron sand and charcoal; figures from modern reconstructions of the process, which should not be read as documented Tokugawa yields, report a bloom of roughly two to three tons from which only part, often under half, was suitable as tamahagane, with the rest used as lower-grade steel and iron or discarded. Izumo (modern eastern Shimane) was a leading producing region, domain-licensed ironmasters such as those serving the Matsue domain managed production, and by the later Edo period the Chūgoku mountains supplied a large share of Japan’s domestic iron. Not all iron from a furnace was tamahagane, and swordsmiths often combined selected steel with softer iron within the blade. The same iron supplied farm tools, kitchenware and fittings, which shows how closely warrior and everyday markets were linked.

A finished sword was the product of divided labor: smiths forged and tempered the blade, polishers prepared the surface, and other specialists made scabbards, hilts and decorative fittings such as sword guards (tsuba), which workshops decorated in many styles. Families such as the Gotō produced fittings for shogunal and other elite patrons, and by the later period urban metalworkers made a wide range of decorative pieces for samurai and wealthy townspeople. However, most smiths made tools: hoes, sickles, axes, kitchen knives, nails, kettles and fittings. Regional specializations existed, such as the multi-tined hoes associated with Bitchū, and village blacksmiths served local farmers, which shows that metalworking was mostly an agricultural and household industry, not simply a martial one.

Carpenters (daiku) built houses, temples, shrines and castle structures, but their methods varied by building type and region. Temple and shrine carpentry used complex bracketing and joinery, while townhouses and farmhouses used different framing and materials. Builders often used modular proportional systems (kiwari) and standardized components that allowed rapid assembly; head carpenters (tōryō) managed workers, materials and patrons, and families sometimes held hereditary roles for major institutions. Manuals and drawings circulated within carpentry lineages, though Edo practice cannot be reduced to a single Japanese building method. Carpenters learned through apprenticeship in workshop households and worked with saws, planes, chisels and adzes.

Construction was bound up with disaster and resources. Fires repeatedly destroyed large parts of Edo, notably the Meireki fire of 1657, and rebuilding created enormous demand for timber, tiles, plaster and labor. Authorities tried to improve fire resistance through measures including fire lanes, tile roofs and storehouses, though adoption varied by neighborhood and wealth. Timber came from managed forests, and domains such as Owari regulated cutting in the Kiso mountains to protect supply, which shows early resource management. Domain and shogunal commissions offered stable patronage to some builders, while most carpenters worked for commercial clients, so the trade combined artisanal skill, state regulation and market demand.

Section 49.9

Ceramics, Porcelain, Lacquerware, and Decorative Crafts

Ceramic production expanded during the Tokugawa period, with kilns that served both everyday and elite markets. The most consequential development was Hizen porcelain. Korean potters brought to Kyushu in the aftermath of Hideyoshi’s invasions (Chapter 40) contributed significantly to ceramic technology in western Japan, and tradition credits the Korean potter Yi Sam-pyeong with discovering kaolin at Izumiyama near Arita in the 1610s. Historians treat that account cautiously: it may simplify a process involving several potters, earlier stonewares, local entrepreneurs and domain patronage, and archaeology of the Arita kilns supports a developing industry rather than a single event. The Nabeshima domain regulated production and made high-quality wares for official gifts.

Distribution turned Hizen porcelain into a major commodity. Wares were shipped from the port of Imari, so “Imari” became a commercial label rather than a kiln location, and the Dutch East India Company began exporting Hizen wares in quantity from 1659, during the temporary disruption of Chinese export production, a trade discussed in Chapter 48. Domestic buyers also purchased Arita porcelain, with differences between fine overglaze-enameled pieces, such as Kakiemon types, and plain blue-and-white wares for ordinary tables. Collectors later created categories (“Old Imari,” “Ko-Kutani”) that differ from how makers and merchants labeled goods, so the chapter distinguishes manufacturing centers, commercial names and modern collecting terms.

Domain involvement shaped the industry. Nabeshima authorities supervised porcelain production within the Saga domain and, from the later seventeenth century, developed an official kiln at Ōkawachiyama (established as an official operation by 1675) that made fine wares for official use, while Arita’s many privately operated workshops divided labor among throwers, painters, kiln loaders and merchants who financed firings. Dutch demand grew after 1659, when the temporary collapse of Chinese export production between about 1650 and 1680 opened a market, and the Japanese export trade is dated from 1659 to about 1740. Its decline had several causes: the Qing refurbishment of Jingdezhen, the Dutch company’s return to cheaper Chinese porcelain, and European imitation at lower cost, so domestic demand became relatively more important. Merchants, domain officials and consumers each steered the industry in different ways. Participants in this trade ranged from kiln owners and skilled decorators to laborers who dug and prepared clay and fired the kilns, and their working conditions are far less documented than the finished wares. Domestic demand, the Dutch trade and gift exchange pulled production toward different grades of ware.

Other centers had distinct profiles. Kyoto potters such as Nonomura Ninsei and Ogata Kenzan produced decorated wares for elite and urban customers, and Kiyomizu workshops served a large market. Kutani wares are associated with Kaga domain, but scholars disagree about where the early “Ko-Kutani” style was made: much porcelain in that style is now attributed to Arita kilns, while kilns in the Kutani area are documented more securely from an early-nineteenth-century revival. Seto in Owari, long a stoneware center, began making porcelain in the early nineteenth century, drawing on Hizen techniques. These cases show that ceramic centers competed and borrowed methods rather than following one path.

Lacquerware (urushi-nuri) used the sap of the lacquer tree, applied in many layers to wood, often reinforced with cloth and finished with polishing or gold decoration such as maki-e. Production required specialists in woodworking, base coating, lacquering and decoration. Wajima in Noto and Aizu, whose domain promoted lacquer production, became well-known centers. Archaeology demonstrates that inexpensive lacquerware was widely used in ordinary households, so luxury pieces in museums should not be taken as typical. Surviving objects, kiln-site finds and export records allow historians to distinguish elite and everyday goods, though documentation of lower-priced work and of workers’ lives remains thin.

Section 49.10

Textiles, Clothing Production, Dyeing, and Fashion

Textile production was one of the largest crafts and one of the most important for women’s work. Hemp and ramie were long common fibers for commoners, and silk was a prestige cloth. Cotton cultivation spread from the sixteenth century and, with improved planting and processing, became important in the Kinai, Mikawa and other regions; cotton was warmer, easier to dye and affordable, changing clothing for many commoners. Households typically ginned, spun and wove cotton, but it also fueled a commercial market, with fertilizer such as dried fish and oil cakes (Chapter 44) tying cotton farming to wider commodity networks.

Silk weaving was concentrated in Kyoto’s Nishijin district, which supplied fine cloth to court, warrior and wealthy merchant customers, while regional centers such as Kiryū and Ashikaga developed weaving later. Imported Chinese raw silk was important in the seventeenth century, but domestic sericulture expanded, and restrictions on imports encouraged domestic production in areas such as Kōzuke and Shinshū. Late in the period, some places moved toward larger workshops gathering many weavers, although putting-out arrangements remained common. Regional specialties such as Echigo hemp cloth, Awa indigo and Kyoto’s resist-dyed yūzen textiles illustrate how local resources and skills created distinctive products.

Preparation of cotton involved ginning, carding, spinning and weaving, much of it done by women within households, sometimes for family clothing and sometimes for sale to merchants who supplied materials and bought cloth. This blurred the line between household and paid labor: a woman’s weaving could be unpaid family work, by-employment for a merchant, or both, and records usually credit the household head. In Nishijin, weavers likewise worked within household workshops under wholesalers who set designs and prices, so that silk, like cotton, linked agriculture, craft specialization and urban capital, though Chapter 45 treats the wider commercial system.

Textile work took several forms that should not be collapsed. Some households wove only for their own clothing, others sold surplus cloth locally, others worked under putting-out arrangements for wholesalers who supplied thread and paid by the piece, and some weavers worked in larger workshops or as hired hands in towns. Women’s labor appears in each form, but whether it was paid or unpaid depended on the arrangement, and wholesalers tended to control design, quality and prices. Differences in setting mattered as well: urban workshops answered quickly to fashion and merchant orders, while rural households fitted weaving around planting and harvest.

Indigo dyeing was an important craft, and Awa (modern Tokushima) became famous for fermented indigo (sukumo) supplied to dyers elsewhere. Dyers used resists, stencils and paste techniques to make patterns, and many wore blue work clothes. Fashion responded to urban markets and printed pattern books, with changing designs and colors; by the later period, subtle tones and discreet linings allowed wealthy commoners to display taste within official limits.

Sumptuary laws repeatedly restricted luxury fabrics, colors and decoration for commoners, with major campaigns during reform eras such as Kyōhō, Kansei and especially Tenpō (1841–1843). Enforcement depended on local officials, and repetition of an edict shows official concern, not how widely it was ignored; evidence of compliance has to come from enforcement records, accounts and surviving goods (see §49.17). The laws reveal officials’ concern with status boundaries and moral discipline as well as the market power of merchants. Women’s labor in spinning, weaving and dyeing supported much of this economy, though male masters and merchants dominate the records. Everyday dress and grooming are discussed in §49.13.

Section 49.11

Houses, Interiors, Furnishings, and Domestic Space

Housing varied sharply by status, wealth, region and climate. Samurai lived in residences (buke yashiki) allocated by their lords or the shogunate; senior retainers and daimyō had large compounds, while lower-ranking warriors lived in modest row dwellings within the castle town or Edo. Chapter 45 described the layout of cities; here the concern is what domestic space looked like and how it was used. Samurai houses often included reception rooms reflecting rank, with guest areas separated from family and service spaces, though the details differed by rank and domain.

Urban commoners lived in two principal types of dwelling. Merchant and artisan townhouses (machiya) stood along streets with narrow frontages, combining a shop or workshop at the front with living space and often a storehouse (kura) at the rear, linked by an earthen-floored passage (doma). Much of the urban population, however, rented tiny units in tenement row houses (nagaya) on back lanes. A conventional model often cited for Edo back-lane tenements describes a unit of about nine shaku by two ken (roughly 2.7 by 3.6 meters), with a small earthen entrance and one raised room; it is best read as a typical plan drawn from descriptive sources and prints, not a statistical average, and actual sizes varied. Residents shared wells, latrines and drainage, and the night soil was typically sold to farmers as fertilizer, linking city sanitation to rural agriculture (Chapter 44). Edo’s water supply, drawn from rivers and conveyed through underground wooden channels to wells, made water available to many neighborhoods, though not equally.

Farmhouses (minka) differed even more by region. Common features included an earthen-floored area (doma) for cooking and work, a raised floor with a sunken hearth (irori), thatched or wooden roofs and framing suited to local climate. Heavy snow regions, such as the Shirakawa-gō area, developed steep roofs, while southern and coastal houses addressed heat, wind and typhoons. Wealthier farm families, including village heads, had larger houses with guest rooms, while poor farmers lived in small dwellings with limited furnishings. Tatami was originally a feature of elite rooms and spread more widely in wealthier commoner homes over time, whereas many ordinary households used boards, straw mats or earthen floors.

Space was allocated flexibly rather than by function. In farmhouses the earthen floor served for cooking and work, family members slept and ate on the raised floor, and a better room (zashiki) was kept for guests and ceremonies; privacy was limited and rooms changed use by time of day and season. Merchant houses combined shop, living quarters and storehouse, with servants and apprentices housed on the premises, so household size and rank were visible in the arrangement of rooms. The more modest dwellings of the poor have left few surviving buildings, which is why surviving houses give a skewed picture.

Furnishings were relatively few and movable. Sliding partitions (fusuma, shōji) allowed flexible use of rooms, and bedding (futon) was stored during the day so rooms could serve multiple purposes. Heating came from hearths, charcoal braziers (hibachi) and later kotatsu; lighting depended on oil lamps burning rapeseed or other vegetable oils, while wax candles were costly. Kitchens had hearths, pots, tubs, wooden utensils and storage jars, and household goods connect to the crafts of §§49.8–49.10. Fire was a constant danger: storehouses, tile roofs and neighborhood fire brigades were partial answers, but crowded wooden neighborhoods burned repeatedly. Surviving buildings and inventories disproportionately represent prosperous families, and poorer dwellings are known mainly from archaeology, prints and regulations.

Section 49.12

Food, Cooking, Markets, and Eating Practices

Rice was the central commodity of Tokugawa taxation and prestige, as earlier chapters discuss, but its role in the diet depended on income and region. Urban residents, especially in Edo, ate relatively large quantities of polished white rice, and some historians connect that preference with beriberi, sometimes called an “Edo disease,” though the explanation was not understood at the time. In many villages, farmers who grew rice for tax sold much of it and ate barley, millet, buckwheat, other grains, vegetables and root crops, often mixed with rice. Regional patterns varied: northeastern and upland communities depended more on millet and other grains, while other areas had more access to seafood. The sweet potato became a significant famine-resistant crop in some regions in the eighteenth century.

Everyday meals commonly combined a staple with soup, pickles and a small side dish. Fermented foods were fundamental: miso, soy sauce and pickled vegetables preserved nutrients and added flavor, while tofu supplied protein. Soy sauce production became a major industry, with centers such as Chōshi and Noda supplying the Edo market, and the cookbook Tōfu hyakuchin (“One Hundred Tofu Dishes”), usually dated to 1782, illustrates how widely the ingredient was discussed. Seafood, dried fish and bonito flakes (katsuobushi) provided flavor and protein, and salted or dried foods allowed long storage. Cooking used hearths, iron or ceramic pots, wooden steamers and a limited range of tools, making charcoal and firewood important household costs.

Food depended on labor and fuel. Women in most households carried water, tended hearths and prepared meals, and wood or charcoal was a significant cost, particularly in cities. Edo drew vegetables from surrounding farms, fish from nearby waters and sake and other goods from the Kansai, so urban eating depended on rural production and shipping. Salting, drying and pickling extended seasonal supplies. Contemporaries recognized the illness beriberi (kakke) and associated it with certain seasons and places but did not understand its cause; modern nutrition links it to thiamine deficiency in diets heavy in polished rice. Cookbooks, prints and archaeological remains add evidence, but each favors particular groups.

Cities developed a rich food market. Edo had large fish and produce markets, many vendors who sold from stalls or carried goods to customers, and eating places selling noodles, tempura, eel and other quick foods. Because many tenement residents lacked full kitchens, buying cooked food was common. Sushi was different from the modern dish: older fermented forms existed, and the hand-pressed nigirizushi sold in Edo emerged in the early nineteenth century, with later tradition naming particular inventors whose claims historians treat cautiously. Historians generally describe a gradual shift from two to three daily meals in many places over the early modern period, but timing varied by region, occupation and class, and the generalization rests on limited evidence.

Elite dining differed in ingredients, service and occasion. Samurai and wealthy merchants hosted banquets with multiple dishes and sake, and published recipe collections and restaurants (ryōtei) mainly reflect urban elite tastes. Ordinary households ate what harvests, markets and wages allowed. Food access was shaped by agricultural conditions, taxation and prices; in famine years (Chapter 44) people relied on substitutes and wild foods, while urban rice price rises threatened poorer residents. Sources for diet include cookbooks, archaeological food remains, village accounts, travel diaries and prints, but written recipes disproportionately represent literate and prosperous households.

Did Tokugawa Peace Weaken Martial Culture or Transform It?

The question: Did two centuries of peace erode martial skill, or reshape what martial skill was for?

Interpretation A — Decline: Contemporary critics lamented samurai softness, very few active samurai had fought, and some schools emphasized forms and rituals more than battlefield effectiveness. On this view martial culture became formalized and detached from combat.
Interpretation B — Transformation: Schools multiplied, domains incorporated martial training into formal education, equipment-based sparring and inter-school contests developed, and martial arts took on educational, ethical and performative functions. On this view the practices were reinvented, not lost.

Evidence: A depends on critics’ writings, domain complaints and the absence of campaigns; B on school scrolls, domain school regulations, the eighteenth-century chronology of protective equipment, and the spread of training to some non-samurai. What could distinguish them: measures of training time and participation, records of actual skills tested, and comparisons across domains. Why it matters: It determines whether Edo martial arts are read as survivals or as new institutions. Section 49.13

Clothing, Grooming, Hygiene, and Bodily Customs

Clothing worked as a social language. Samurai were distinguished by the pair of swords and formal wear such as hakama and crested outer garments, while commoners wore a range of kimono, work wrappers, aprons and leggings according to occupation and season. Farmers and laborers often used straw raincoats and hats, and footwear included straw sandals (waraji), clogs (geta) and thonged sandals (zōri), with tabi socks for those who could afford them. Style, color and fabric indicated age, marital status, occupation and wealth, but wearers adapted rules through fashion and secondhand markets; old-clothes dealers (furugiya) supplied a large share of urban clothing, and garments were repaired, recut and recycled many times. Manufacturing is covered in §49.10; this section concerns use.

Hair and body practices marked social identity. Adult men generally wore a topknot (chonmage) with the pate shaved, and its form varied with status and fashion. Women arranged their hair in many styles that changed with age, marital status and fashion, and hair dressers (kamiyui) provided styling. Married women in some communities blackened their teeth (ohaguro) and women might shave eyebrows after marriage or childbirth, though customs varied by region and period. These practices should be understood as historically specific norms, not universal habits.

Everyday care of clothing and body also marked differences. Some garments were unstitched, washed as flat lengths of cloth and sewn together again, a technique known as araibari that is documented in surviving garments and in later accounts of kimono care; how widely it was practiced in the Tokugawa period is not established. Where it was used, it made cloth easier to clean but added labor, which fell largely to women. Samurai wore formal dress at official functions and carried swords, urban commoners had a wider range of garments and fashions than villagers, and farmers often worked in short, simple clothing in hot weather. Cleaning teeth with frayed willow twigs and tooth powders sold in cities is reported for Edo and other towns, though evidence for villages is thin. These details show that standards of appearance depended on occupation, resources and setting, and printed images of fashionable townspeople should not be taken as typical of village life.

Hair, clothing and grooming also expressed social position. Children’s hairstyles and clothing marked stages of growth, and the appearance expected of a married woman or an apprentice differed from that of a youth, so grooming signaled place in the household and community as much as hygiene.

Bathing was widespread, but conditions differed. In cities, commercial bathhouses (sentō) were neighborhood institutions for washing, conversation and social contact, and tenement residents who lacked private baths depended on them. Authorities periodically tried to regulate mixed bathing and bathhouse morals, including in the Kansei reforms of the late eighteenth century, with uneven results. Rural households might bathe in tubs at home, share baths or use hot springs, but fuel and water were costly. Hot springs also served therapeutic visits (tōji), often seasonal. Bathhouse owners, fuel suppliers, attendants and barbers made up service occupations tied to cleanliness and grooming.

Hygiene depended on infrastructure and income. Laundry was done in wells, streams or tubs, and cleansing relied on ash, bran and soap-like substances. Disease nonetheless spread, including smallpox, measles and cholera in the later period, and urban crowding created risk. Evidence includes sumptuary decrees, prints, guidebooks, household accounts and archaeological finds, but prints often idealize or satirize appearance. Regional climate and occupation produced diverse practices, and evidence for poorer households and rural areas remains limited.

Section 49.14

Household Labor, Gender, Childhood, and the Life Course

The household (ie) was the basic unit of production, property and succession, as Chapter 43 explained. Work was divided by gender, age and status, but not rigidly. In farm households, men typically managed fieldwork and heavy tasks, while women participated in planting, harvesting, spinning, weaving, silkworm rearing, food preparation, child care and household management. In merchant and artisan households, wives often handled accounts, customers and household staff, and in some trades women’s labor was essential to the business. In samurai households, women managed domestic budgets and servants within the constraints of status and stipend.

Marriage was usually arranged between households and tied to property and succession. Inheritance often favored a single heir, commonly a son, but practices varied; when no suitable son existed, families adopted heirs, sometimes bringing in a son-in-law, a practice that prioritized household continuity over biological descent. Divorce was not rare in some communities. A husband’s brief letter of divorce (traditionally associated with a formula of three and a half lines) could end a marriage, but women had recourse through families, community mediation and, in some cases, temple refuges such as Tōkeiji. Historians stress that these practices varied by class and region.

Succession and service shaped the life course differently across groups. Samurai heirs generally needed domain approval, and adoption was widely used to continue houses, sometimes bringing in sons from other warrior families. Customs of inheritance differed among farming households and regions, and not every household passed property to the eldest son. Many boys and girls from poorer families entered apprenticeship or domestic service, often for fixed terms with contracts negotiated by parents, so their labor supported both their own households and others. Education reached some girls as well as boys through temple schools and private tutors, with literacy generally higher in cities, though local evidence differs widely.

Women’s economic decision-making varied with property and trade. In merchant and artisan households they could manage shops, accounts and credit, and widows sometimes headed households and trades, while in samurai families status and stipends passed through male heads. Records show women as petitioners and property holders in some cases, which cautions against reading conduct literature as a rule of absolute subordination.

Prescriptive literature such as conduct books for women, including Onna daigaku (usually dated to the early eighteenth century and attributed to Kaibara Ekken in tradition, though scholars question the attribution), stressed obedience and subordination. Such texts reveal elite and Neo-Confucian ideals and a market for moral instruction, but they are not accurate records of behavior. Household registers, village diaries, contracts and legal records show women as laborers, property holders in some situations, and agents in family decisions, even if men held formal authority.

Childhood was shaped by household economy. Children helped with chores, farm work and craft production, attended temple schools (terakoya) in some places, and were often apprenticed or placed in service as adolescents. Ceremonies such as the seven-five-three celebration marked growth, though they were not universal practices. Families also limited household size in difficult times, and historians discuss practices such as infanticide (mabiki) in some regions as part of strategies for survival and succession, with debate over its frequency. Old age brought retirement (inkyo) for household heads and dependence on children or relatives, and poor elderly people faced hardship. Evidence is uneven: registers and village records survive in many areas, while the voices of women, children and the poor are often indirect.

Section 49.15

Games, Recreation, Festivals, and Seasonal Celebrations

Leisure in Tokugawa Japan ranged from professionally organized competition to household pastimes. The board games go and shogi were played by samurai, merchants and some villagers. In the early seventeenth century the shogunate patronized professional go players, and four go houses, Honinbō, Hayashi, Inoue and Yasui, held stipends and trained successors; their annual game before the shogun, known as castle go, was a formal event. Shogi also had professional lineages under official patronage. Elite competition differed from ordinary play, since most players were amateurs who met in homes, shops and temples, and publishers and board makers served a wider market. Historians must therefore distinguish the documented institutions of the go and shogi houses from the many unrecorded games of everyday life.

Children’s play and household games left less documentation, but toys, prints and popular books show a lively culture. Kites, spinning tops, shuttlecock battledore (hanetsuki) with decorated paddles (hagoita), dolls, picture-matching and poem-card games (karuta), and the dice-and-board game sugoroku were widely enjoyed. Girls and boys often played different games, and seasonal events such as Girls’ Day and Boys’ Day involved dolls or banners, though practices varied by region and income. Toy makers, game-card printers and vendors formed small industries, and authorities sometimes regulated kite flying or gambling for safety and order. Cards used for gambling were repeatedly restricted, so games and moral regulation intersected.

The calendar structured communal recreation. New Year, Setsubun, the Star Festival (Tanabata), the summer Bon season, and autumn moon viewing marked the year, and people gathered for cherry-blossom viewing, summer cooling on riverbanks and fireworks. In Edo, tradition dates the Sumida River fireworks display to 1733, linked to commemoration after famine and epidemic, while the planting of cherry trees at sites such as Asukayama is associated with shogunal initiatives of the early eighteenth century; both are traditional attributions. These occasions created demand for food, drink, boats, fireworks makers and vendors, showing that recreation was an economic activity. Their religious aspects, including shrine festivals, belong to Chapter 47, and theater and pleasure districts to Chapter 46.

Age, gender and community organization shaped participation. Town neighborhoods and villages often organized festivals and dances, with young men’s associations reported in many villages handling processions and mutual aid, though their roles varied and the evidence is strongest for the later period, while women and children took part in seasonal customs such as Obon dances or Girls’ Day, though who could attend and how varied by place. Leisure was also limited by work: festival days and agreed rest days were among the few regular breaks for farm households, and authorities sometimes tried to restrain the expense of celebrations. Entertainment vendors, toy makers and food sellers depended on these seasonal peaks, so recreation shaped incomes as well as habits. Festival expenses also fell unevenly, since wealthier households and neighborhood leaders often bore larger shares of the costs than poorer residents.

Participation differed by place and means. Wealthy townspeople hosted gatherings and hired entertainers, while villagers celebrated at local shrines, in work breaks and in seasonal dances, often under community organization. Women’s participation varied with the event, and travelers could join pilgrimage-linked festivals (§49.16). Contemporary descriptions of festivals come from guidebooks, diaries, prints and local records, and they favor lively urban scenes. Modern festivals sometimes preserve Tokugawa elements but have been reorganized by later institutions, tourism and preservation movements, so they should not be treated as unchanged survivals.

Section 49.16

Travel, Local Customs, and Encounters Beyond the Household

Travel took people out of household routines and into unfamiliar settings. Chapter 45 explained roads, post stations and the political infrastructure; here the focus is on experience. Travelers included pilgrims, merchants, artisans seeking work, samurai on official duty, entertainers, and ordinary people visiting temples or hot springs. Regulations required identification and checks at barrier stations (sekisho), but enforcement differed by place and by traveler: officials were particularly concerned with weapons and with women leaving Edo, and women’s travel often required documents. Yet women did travel, on pilgrimages, family visits and business, and the picture of total restriction is inaccurate.

Pilgrimage was a major form of mobility. Ise Shrine attracted enormous numbers of visitors, and mass “thanksgiving” pilgrimages (okage mairi) in 1705, 1771 and 1830 drew very large crowds, supported in part by local donations to pilgrims; some who joined them left home without permission, a practice distinguished as nukemairi. Pilgrimage associations (kō) pooled savings so that representatives could travel, and shrine and temple priests (oshi) arranged lodging and services. Circuits such as the Shikoku pilgrimage involved walking long distances with specific clothing and local hospitality. These movements exposed participants to regions beyond their villages, though they also remained shaped by religious institutions discussed in Chapter 47.

Authorized pilgrimage normally involved household or village permission and documents, and household heads and local officials decided who could leave. Unauthorized departures such as nukemairi drew on devotion to Ise but also on young people’s wish to escape workloads, and communities along the road often supplied food and shelter, so religious feeling, household authority and mobility restrictions interacted. Women and servants appear among such travelers, though numbers cannot be established.

Women’s experience of travel shows the limits of generalization. Women went on pilgrimages in groups or with family, and documented travel diaries by women exist, although they often needed documents to pass certain barriers and faced greater suspicion at some checkpoints. Guidebooks listed sights, hot springs and inns, and commercial travel promoted places such as Konpira and Zenkōji, so pilgrimage and tourism blended. Travelers’ expectations and the choices of guidebook publishers shaped which places became famous.

Inns provided varied accommodation: official inns (honjin) for high-ranking travelers, ordinary inns (hatago), and cheap lodging (kichinyado) where guests brought food or paid for firewood. Teahouses and roadside shops offered rest and food. Cost shaped who could travel and how: pilgrims on tight budgets chose cheap lodging, joined savings associations (kō) or relied on donated meals, while wealthier travelers paid for better inns, porters or horses, so the same road offered very different journeys. Travelers wore sedge hats, leggings and straw sandals and carried small bundles, and they purchased regional specialties and souvenirs for family and neighbors. They encountered local foods, dialects, architecture and customs that could differ strongly from home, and misunderstandings or hardship were common. Guidebooks and illustrated gazetteers such as famous-places compendia and comic travel fiction, including Jippensha Ikku’s Tōkaidōchū hizakurige (from 1802), turned travel into entertainment, and print series of the Tōkaidō by Hiroshige expanded its imagery.

Sources require caution. Diaries record travelers’ own perspectives, often of literate and relatively prosperous people, while guidebooks and prints present selective or idealized views for buyers. Fiction exaggerates for humor. Records of the poor, of women travelers and of people who experienced travel as forced movement or hardship are thinner. Together, evidence suggests that travel increased contact among regions and encouraged shared expectations about famous places, products and manners, without erasing local distinctiveness.

Section 49.17

Inequality, Regulation, and Diversity in Everyday Life

Everyday life in Tokugawa Japan cannot be reduced to a single pattern. Wealth, status, occupation, gender, age and place shaped what people ate, wore, owned and enjoyed. A prosperous Osaka or Edo merchant might have a large townhouse, storehouse, servants and access to restaurants, entertainment and travel, while a poor tenant in a back-lane row house depended on daily wages or small trade and bought cooked food by the portion. Samurai varied just as much: senior retainers had large households and stipends, while lower-ranking warriors often lived in strained circumstances and took side work such as craft production or tutoring, as historians have shown for several domains. Status labels therefore do not predict consumption.

Rural contrasts were equally significant. Landholding farmers and village leaders had larger houses, access to literacy and, sometimes, regional trade, while tenants, day laborers and household servants (hōkōnin) had fewer resources. Regions differed: commercial agriculture and by-employment in the Kinai and parts of central Japan supported more cash income, while northeastern communities were more vulnerable to cold-weather crop failure (Chapter 44). Marginalized groups, including people classed as eta and hinin, performed tasks such as leather work, execution-related duties and cleaning, and faced discrimination and legal restrictions (Chapter 43). Ainu and Ryukyuan societies had distinct economies, institutions and customs, discussed in Chapter 48, and should not be absorbed into generalizations about “Tokugawa” households.

Urban poverty and rural hardship were different forms of vulnerability. Day laborers and small vendors in cities lived from daily earnings, and famine years drove rural people toward towns (Chapter 44). Official responses were limited and uneven: the shogunate authorized a public medical facility in Koishikawa in 1722, for example (institutional histories place its establishment at the garden site in early 1723), which suggests concern for the poor but reached only a small number of people. Servants (hōkōnin) were bound by contracts of service whose terms varied, and outcast communities faced restrictions on residence and occupation. Differences between regions mattered as well, since commercialized areas of central Japan and colder northeastern farming regions offered different risks and resources.

Legal status and economic position must be kept apart. A wealthy merchant could be formally of lower status than a poor samurai yet consume far more, and a village head could be legally a commoner while holding great local influence. Enforcement records, merchant accounts, household inventories and surviving goods show what people owned and how they adapted, but each covers only some households.

Authorities attempted to regulate consumption, occupation, dress, housing and entertainment through repeated edicts, especially in reform periods. Regulations aimed to preserve status distinctions, limit extravagance and promote discipline, but enforcement relied on local officials and varied by place and time. Repeated edicts show that officials kept returning to the problem, but repetition alone cannot establish how widely rules were broken; compliance has to be judged from enforcement records, merchant accounts, household inventories and surviving goods, and occasional crackdowns could impose real penalties. Sumptuary law shows official priorities and anxieties; what people actually wore, owned and did must be established from enforcement records, inventories, accounts and surviving objects.

Historians reconstruct ordinary lives from uneven sources. Museum collections preserve objects from wealthy owners and notable makers, household inventories survive mostly from prosperous families, village registers and account books record taxation and population but rarely emotions, archaeology recovers discarded goods from everyday life, and prints and literature present selective, often urban, perspectives. The most visible lives are urban, male, literate and propertied. Students should ask who produced each source, who preserved it, and whose experience is missing, then compare multiple kinds of evidence to correct each one’s bias.

Section 49.18

The Cultural Legacy of Tokugawa Everyday Life

Many practices now described as traditional Japanese culture took recognizable form in the Tokugawa period, but their later meanings often differ from their original ones. Peace, urban growth and commerce shaped sumo’s tournaments and rankings, martial lineages and equipment-based training, regional craft specialization, and household routines. This was not simple preservation of earlier customs: it was creation and reorganization under new political and economic conditions.

Sumo illustrates the pattern. Edo kanjin-zumō depended on licenses, temple fundraising and separate organizations in Edo, Kyoto and Osaka. The later professional structure emerged in the modern era: a permanent hall, the Kokugikan, opened in Tokyo in 1909, the incorporated Dai-Nippon Sumo Association was established in December 1925, and the Tokyo and Osaka organizations merged in January 1927, with the sport increasingly presented as a national tradition. Martial arts changed too. Edo ryūha and kata transmission continued in some lineages, but modern budō was shaped by Meiji and later institutions: Kanō Jigorō founded the Kōdōkan in 1882 and created judo from jūjutsu schools, the Dai Nippon Butokukai promoted martial arts from 1895, and kendo’s modern rules and federations developed later, including after the postwar prohibition and revival. Edo martial culture was an ancestor, not an identical form.

Crafts likewise acquired new roles. Workshop knowledge, regional specializations and apprenticeship continued, but industrialization, imported goods and world’s fairs altered markets, and the folk-craft (mingei) movement and later heritage laws recast everyday objects as cultural treasures (Chapter 72). Household practices changed, too: Edo-period housing, food and dress were modified by Meiji reforms, new technologies and Western influences, and later representations often idealized a simplified “traditional Japanese house” or diet that obscures regional and class variation.

The Meiji and later period reshaped everyday Tokugawa practices in ways that complicate any claim of continuity. As Western-style clothing spread gradually and unevenly, especially for men in public life, kimono came increasingly to be seen as traditional dress, though many women wore it as everyday clothing for longer. Modern tourism, schooling and national ceremonies promoted selected Tokugawa-era customs as national heritage, a process scholars describe as the invention or reinvention of tradition. Heritage protection predated 1945, but the Cultural Properties Protection Law of 1950, followed by 1954 measures on important intangible properties, extended recognition to skilled craftspeople and performers as bearers of heritage, which gave artisanal traditions new institutional status. The result is that familiar traditions often combine Edo-period roots with modern organization and meaning.

Standardization changed what counted as tradition: modern associations, national rules, schooling and heritage laws gave Tokugawa practices uniform forms that differed from the local variation of the period. Later periods thus gave older practices new meanings without erasing their Edo roots, so continuity and reinvention must be traced together.

The chapter’s unifying argument is that Tokugawa peace did not end the importance of physical skill, craftsmanship or inherited custom; it changed the institutions, markets, audiences and settings in which they were learned, performed and transmitted. Sumo turned religious fundraising into commercial spectacle, martial schools turned warrior skill into education and competition, artisans responded to urban and regional demand, households organized work and consumption within status limits, and leisure and travel created shared experience. Inequality shaped all of this, and surviving sources overrepresent the prosperous. Understanding tradition as historically constructed, selectively remembered and repeatedly reshaped prepares students for Chapter 50’s study of learning, reform and pressure on the shogunate.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Tokugawa Practice and Modern “Tradition”

What changed? Institutions, rules, scale and public meanings: sumo organization, equipment and rules in martial arts, markets for crafts, and housing and diet. What remained continuous? Lineage transmission, apprenticeship, household labor, seasonal rhythms and many technical skills. Who experienced change most directly? Wrestlers, swordsmiths and martial instructors whose patrons and audiences shifted after 1868. Who experienced greater continuity? Many rural households, whose work rhythms changed more slowly. Why it matters: It shows that tradition is built from selective continuity and reorganization.

Chapter Summary

Tokugawa peace changed the purposes of martial skill without ending its importance. Samurai remained a hereditary warrior class but served mostly as administrators, so martial training took on roles as education, status display and, increasingly, structured competition.

Sumo shows the same pattern in a commercial setting. Rooted in earlier ritual and warrior wrestling, it became organized public entertainment through kanjin-zumō, fundraising events for temples and shrines licensed by authorities. Tournament seasons, printed banzuke, patronage by daimyō houses and the fame of wrestlers such as Tanikaze and Raiden created a spectacle linked to urban audiences. The 1860 Ekōin banzuke is one surviving object of that culture.

Martial schools organized skill through lineages, kata, scrolls and licenses. Domains hired instructors and incorporated martial training into domain schools, while writings such as the Heihō kadensho and the Gorin no sho addressed the problem of what warriors were for in peace. “Bushidō” was not a single timeless code, and modern kendo and judo are products of later institutions. Eighteenth-century equipment-based sparring and inter-school contests show innovation within a peaceful society.

Artisans worked in household workshops, trained apprentices over years and belonged to associations whose privileges the shogunate granted and later suppressed. Their official status did not predict wealth. Rural households combined farming with by-employment, often drawing on women’s and children’s labor and on merchants who supplied materials and purchased products.

Metalworkers, carpenters, potters, lacquerers and textile makers responded to different markets. Swordsmiths served status display, while most smiths made tools; carpenters used varied techniques and rebuilt fire-prone cities; Hizen porcelain, other kilns, lacquerware and textiles reached domestic and sometimes overseas buyers. Luxury and everyday goods existed side by side, and sumptuary rules show official anxiety about consumption.

Households differed in space, food, dress and routine. Samurai residences, urban machiya and nagaya and rural minka had distinct layouts and resources. Diets depended on region and income; urban markets and vendors supplied cooked food. Clothing, hairstyles and bathing marked age, gender, status and occupation, but wearers adapted and recycled goods. Household labor, marriage, adoption and childhood were organized around the continuity of the ie, and conduct literature should not be mistaken for practice.

Recreation and travel extended social life beyond the household. Go, shogi, toys, seasonal events, fireworks and flower viewing created shared experiences, and pilgrimages, inns and guidebooks expanded encounters with other regions. These activities also supported vendors, makers and service trades.

Inequality ran through all of this. Merchants, tenants, samurai of different ranks, landholding farmers, servants, women, children and marginalized groups lived differently, and Ainu and Ryukyuan societies had distinct institutions. Surviving sources overrepresent prosperous, urban, literate and male perspectives, so historians must compare multiple kinds of evidence.

The chapter’s central argument is that peace changed the institutions, markets, audiences and settings through which skill, craft and custom were learned and transmitted. Many practices later called traditional took recognizable form in this period but were reorganized by Meiji and later institutions, which is why tradition should be understood as historically constructed. Chapter 50 turns to learning, scholarship and reform.

  6. Assess limits. Explain how survival, collecting and display may overrepresent prosperous owners or notable makers, and what is missing.
  7. Write and cite. Write a 3–5 page comparison with a bibliography and note every source consulted; do not invent catalog numbers or quotations.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter shows that the Tokugawa peace was not a quiet interval but a generative period. Warriors without wars, wrestlers without battlefields, craftspeople serving expanding markets and households managing work and consumption together created institutions and habits that shaped life for generations. Understanding Tokugawa society requires attention to bodies, hands and homes as well as to governments and ideas.

The chapter also shows how unequal these experiences were. A samurai instructor, a retained wrestler, a master carpenter, a merchant household, a tenant farmer and a woman weaving cloth stood in different relations to power, markets and records, and the surviving evidence reflects that inequality. Habits of reading sources critically, asking who left them and who did not, are among the chapter’s most important lessons.

Many of the practices examined here later came to be described as timeless Japanese traditions. The chapter has argued instead that tradition is made: Tokugawa forms were products of peace, commerce and regulation, and Meiji and later institutions reorganized them. That insight prepares students for later chapters on modern state formation, mass culture and the politics of heritage.

It connects status and household (Chapter 43), villages (44), cities and merchants (45), the floating world (46), religion (47) and controlled international trade (48). Chapter 50 turns from daily life to learning, Dutch studies, reform and the pressures that strained the late shogunate, and the habits of inquiry described here will carry into that discussion.
