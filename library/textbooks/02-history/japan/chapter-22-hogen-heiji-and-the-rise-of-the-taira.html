Part IV — The Rise of the Samurai and Kamakura Japan
22

Hōgen, Heiji, and the Rise of the Taira

Japanese History: From Jōmon Japan to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

In the summer of 1156, the capital at Kyoto witnessed something that living aristocrats had never seen: a succession quarrel among the highest members of the court was settled in a single night by armed men attacking a residence within the city. The people who carried out the attack were not rebels against the court. They were warriors summoned by court leaders to serve one faction against another.

The event is known as the Hōgen Conflict (Hōgen no ran), named for the era name Hōgen (1156–1159). It began after the death of the retired sovereign Toba, the most powerful figure at court, and it pitted a reigning emperor, Go-Shirakawa, against his elder brother, the former emperor Sutoku. It also split the leading Fujiwara family, set fathers against sons and brothers against brothers among the Minamoto and Taira warrior houses, and ended with executions and exiles on a scale that contemporaries found shocking. Three years later, in the winter of 1159–1160, the Heiji Conflict (Heiji no ran) followed: a coup, a countercoup, and fighting in the streets of Kyoto that destroyed the Minamoto leadership and left one warrior, Taira no Kiyomori, in a position no warrior had ever held.

This chapter traces how those two conflicts, and the political decades that followed them, changed the way power worked at the center of the Japanese court state. Chapter 21 explained how durable military households and networks formed in the provinces and in the service of the capital. Chapter 22 asks what happened when those networks were drawn directly into succession disputes and factional struggles at the heart of the court. Its central question is how Hōgen and Heiji transformed warriors from servants of court factions into indispensable participants in central politics, and how Taira no Kiyomori turned military service into unprecedented court power.

The argument of the chapter is deliberately more subtle than the older phrase "the samurai overthrew the aristocracy." Warriors did not break into high politics against the court; they broke in by serving aristocratic and imperial factions. Kiyomori's success came because he learned to operate inside the court order, through office, rank, marriage, patronage, imperial connections, and wealth, as well as through military strength. By the late 1160s he had held the highest ministerial post in the state, and by the 1170s his family was woven into the imperial house itself. This is the story of the militarization of court politics. It is not yet the creation of warrior government, which belongs to Chapter 24, and it is not yet the Genpei War, which belongs to Chapter 23.

Historians know this period through an unusual combination of sources. Aristocrats kept diaries, some of which survive in whole or in part, and these give brief, contemporary, and often anxious glimpses of the events. Buddhist temples, estates, and court offices generated documents. And within a few generations, storytellers and writers turned the conflicts into dramatic war tales, above all the Hōgen monogatari and Heiji monogatari, which shaped how later centuries imagined the age. Students must learn to read these sources against one another, since the most vivid scenes in the tradition are often the hardest to verify.

The chapter also insists that this was a Kyoto-centered crisis with consequences far beyond Kyoto. The Taira drew wealth from provincial governorships, the maritime traffic of the Inland Sea, and trade with Song China through Kyushu and, increasingly, the harbor at Ōwada no tomari near Kiyomori's base at Fukuhara. The Ōshū Fujiwara in the northeast, the merchant and craft communities of Hakata, and the island communities on the southern trade routes all appear at the edges of this story. The Taira ascent was not only a courtly drama but part of a wider East Asian commercial world.

By the chapter's end, students should understand why this period is a turning point without treating it as a clean break. Aristocrats, retired emperors, temples, and warriors remained tightly interconnected. What changed was who possessed credible coercive power inside aristocratic politics, and that change made the outbreak of civil war in 1180 possible.

Historical Context

By the mid-twelfth century, the political structure that Chapters 17 through 20 described had taken a distinctive form. Sovereigns still reigned from Heian-kyō (Kyoto), the capital founded in 794, and the ritsuryō administrative framework of ranks and offices still supplied the vocabulary of government. But real decision-making had moved away from the formal channels. Fujiwara regents and chancellors (sesshō and kanpaku) had long governed in the name of young or compliant emperors. From the late eleventh century a second layer was added: emperors who abdicated but retained authority as "cloistered" or retired sovereigns (in), running government through their own private household offices. This arrangement is known as insei, and it gave the retired emperor control of appointments, patronage, and a growing portfolio of estates.

Economically, the court and the great temples depended on shōen (estates) and on provincial governorships (zuryō) whose holders sent revenue to the capital. Provincial governors of middling aristocratic rank, and their military followers, became key intermediaries. As Chapter 20 explained, estates and provinces generated both wealth and conflict, and the court leaned increasingly on provincially rooted military households for policing, escort, and the suppression of disorder. Chapter 21 traced how such households, especially those descended from branches of the Minamoto and Taira, developed kinship networks, mounted-archery traditions, and ties of service to noble patrons. Two families were especially important around the capital: the Seiwa Genji Minamoto, linked with the Fujiwara regents and with the east, and the Kanmu Heishi Taira, closely tied to the retired sovereigns and to the west.

The court's political culture was a culture of ritual precedence, rank, genealogical position, and diary-keeping. Aristocrats recorded ceremonies and appointments with obsessive care, because correct precedent was itself a form of political capital. Disputes were normally contested through these tools: who was appointed to which office, who stood where at a ceremony, who was exiled to a provincial post, and whose child was placed on the throne. Violence, and above all executions, were rare in the capital, and the court's own self-image emphasized purity and avoidance of pollution from bloodshed. That is why the events of 1156 registered so strongly on contemporaries.

Outside Kyoto, the archipelago was not a single political community. In the northeast, the Ōshū Fujiwara ruled from Hiraizumi over a wealthy frontier region connected by trade to northern communities in Ezo (Hokkaido and the northern islands), including populations associated with Satsumon and Okhotsk-derived traditions that contributed to the later formation of Ainu culture; goods from these northern networks reached the capital through intermediaries. In Kyushu, the Dazaifu administration and the port of Hakata mediated contact with Song China. In the southern island chain, communities on Kikai Island and elsewhere took part in trade networks that linked Kyushu and the Ryukyuan islands to the continent. This chapter's central drama occurred in Kyoto, but its economic underpinnings and its consequences extended across this wider world.

Section 22.1

A Court Divided: Succession, Regents, and Retired Emperors

To understand why a crisis of the 1150s could erupt into fighting, students need only the essentials of the preceding decades. The retired emperor Shirakawa, who abdicated in 1086 and ruled from retirement until his death in 1129, had made cloistered rule the dominant political form. His successor as senior retired sovereign was his grandson Toba, who had himself been enthroned as a child and pressed to abdicate in 1123 in favor of his own son, Sutoku. Toba lived until 1156, and for roughly a quarter century after Shirakawa's death he was the senior authority in the court. Toba controlled patronage, held large estate portfolios, and cultivated close ties with Taira and Minamoto military households, whom he used as guards and enforcers.

Succession created the fault line. Sutoku was Toba's eldest son and reigned from 1123 to 1142, but Toba favored a younger son, born of his consort Bifukumon-in, and pressed Sutoku to abdicate in favor of that child, who became Emperor Konoe. Later tradition, appearing in literary and anecdotal sources, added that Toba doubted Sutoku's paternity; historians treat this as a later story of uncertain reliability rather than as reliable evidence. What contemporary evidence does show is that Sutoku's line lost the throne and its future prospects, and that resentment followed. When Konoe died young in 1155 without an heir, the choice of successor was disputed. The winner was not Sutoku's son but Sutoku's younger brother, who became Emperor Go-Shirakawa. In effect, Sutoku's line was passed over a second time. Rumors circulated that Konoe's early death had been caused by curses, and the suspicion fell on Yorinaga, whose ties to Sutoku were close.

The Fujiwara were similarly divided. Fujiwara no Tadazane, the elder statesman of the regent line, had favored his younger son Yorinaga over the elder Tadamichi, who held the office of kanpaku. Yorinaga, a learned and ambitious minister of the left, had been passed over in various appointments and stood on the side of Sutoku. Tadamichi and his allies stood with Toba's chosen line. This meant that the Fujiwara, whose regency had for generations been the stabilizing counterpart to the throne, could no longer supply a unified counsel. Their split reveals a broader pattern: in an era of retired-emperor rule, the throne and the regency both depended on personal favor from the sovereign in retirement, and when the retired sovereign died the arrangement lost its keystone.

The result was a divided political center. Two factions were forming around two claimants: a reigning emperor, Go-Shirakawa, supported by Tadamichi and by ministers such as the scholar-official Fujiwara no Michinori (Shinzei); and a former emperor, Sutoku, supported by Yorinaga and by certain disaffected warriors. Each faction had a claim to legitimacy, and each needed guarantees of physical security. That need would draw military households into the conflict, and Section 22.2 explains why this represented a change.

Historical Vocabulary
Insei (cloistered rule)

Insei literally means "rule by the retired sovereign's cloister (in)." An emperor abdicated, often taking Buddhist vows in name, but continued to direct government through his private household office (in no chō), which issued documents that carried real authority. Retirement freed the senior sovereign from the ceremonial duties and the constraints of the regent, allowing him to appoint favorites, grant estates, and control succession by placing his chosen heir on the throne.

The term should not be read as the retired emperor "ruling instead of" the emperor in a legal sense. The reigning emperor remained the formal center of the ritual and legal order, and regents and ministers continued to function. Insei was an informal but powerful overlay on the ritsuryō structure. It matters here because Toba's death in 1156 removed the person who held that overlay together, leaving competing claims to authority in a political system built on personal ties.

Section 22.2

Why Court Factions Needed Warriors

Armed men had always existed in the capital. Palace guards, police officials (kebiishi), and the private retinues of aristocrats and temples provided protection and enforcement. From the late eleventh century, military households of Minamoto and Taira descent had served the retired emperors as guards, as pursuers of bandits, and as commanders in campaigns against provincial disorder and temple protesters. Chapter 21 explained that such service gave warriors ranks and offices and brought them into regular contact with court patrons. What was new in 1156 was not the presence of warriors in Kyoto but the willingness of court leaders to use them as decisive instruments in a dispute over the throne itself.

Why was that a major escalation? Earlier disputes at court had been managed largely through the instruments the court itself controlled. An unwelcome rival might be dismissed from office, exiled to a provincial post, or outmaneuvered in a ceremonial appointment. Rivals also used religious tools, such as appeals to temple protest or claims of spiritual pollution, but rarely pitched battle inside the city. Even the great temple protests, in which armed clerics carried sacred symbols to the capital, were governed by conventions about how such pressure would be applied and answered. The violence of 1156 sidestepped those conventions. Once one side moved warriors into position, the other had to answer in kind or lose.

The military households had their own reasons for participating. Warriors depended on court patrons for offices, provincial appointments, and recognition of their landholdings and titles. Taking the right side in a succession dispute could bring rewards, and taking the wrong side could bring ruin. But the households were also fractured internally. The Minamoto, in particular, were divided between the leader Tameyoshi, aligned with Yorinaga and Sutoku, and his eldest son Yoshitomo, who had links to Go-Shirakawa's circle. The Taira were similarly split: Kiyomori aligned with Go-Shirakawa, while his uncle Tadamasa backed Sutoku. Consequently, the warrior households did not appear as unified blocs pursuing warrior interests. They appeared as families whose members judged, in different ways, how to survive and advance within court patronage.

This point is central to the chapter's interpretation. It would be misleading to imagine the Hōgen Conflict as a moment when a self-conscious warrior class turned on the court. The evidence suggests rather that court leaders, seeking a decisive advantage, drew on military households as instruments, and that those households had not yet developed a distinct, institutionalized political voice. The importance of the conflict lies in what followed: having seen that force could settle a succession quarrel, aristocratic factions would be tempted to use it again, and warriors would learn that they were indispensable.

Section 22.3

The Hōgen Conflict of 1156

Toba died in the summer of 1156. In the days that followed, Go-Shirakawa's supporters acted to secure the capital, reportedly moving to control the movements of Yorinaga and Sutoku's allies and to prevent the gathering of armed men. The Sutoku side, for its part, assembled at the Shirakawa palace (Shirakawa-den), a residence in the eastern suburbs of Kyoto, where Sutoku had taken refuge. Yorinaga joined him, and Minamoto no Tameyoshi and several of his sons, including the formidable archer Tametomo, brought their followers. Taira no Tadamasa, Kiyomori's uncle, also joined. On the opposing side, at the Takamatsu palace, were Go-Shirakawa, Tadamichi, and the counselor Shinzei, together with the warriors Minamoto no Yoshitomo, Taira no Kiyomori, and others.

Tradition credits a debate on the Sutoku side over how to proceed. Tametomo is said to have urged a night attack on the emperor's palace, but Yorinaga is said to have rejected a strike that would appear as rebellion against the throne. Whether or not this account is literally accurate, and it comes from the later war tales, it captures a genuine tension in how the participants understood their own actions. On the night of the eleventh day of the seventh month, Go-Shirakawa's side launched its own attack on the Shirakawa palace. Their force is described in the war tales as numbering several hundred men, though such numbers are not reliable. Fighting was short. The palace was set on fire, Sutoku's defenders scattered, and Yorinaga, struck by an arrow, fled toward Nara and died of his wound within days. Sutoku surrendered soon afterward.

The conflict was not a warrior rebellion against the court. Both sides claimed imperial legitimacy and both sides were led by members of the court elite. The warriors on each side fought in the service of aristocratic and imperial principals. Yoshitomo and Kiyomori were commanders serving the reigning emperor's faction, and Tameyoshi and Tadamasa served Sutoku and Yorinaga. In that sense the conflict is best understood as a court civil conflict fought partly through warrior households.

Historians reconstruct the conflict from a small set of sources with different strengths, and it is worth pausing to distinguish them. The contemporary diary Heihanki, kept by the courtier Taira no Nobunori, and other diaries such as Sankaiki by Nakayama Tadachika, offer short entries written near the events, recording who moved where, which appointments were made, and how aristocrats reacted. The documentary record also includes orders and regulations issued by Go-Shirakawa's government in the aftermath. Against these stand the Hōgen monogatari and the later Heiji monogatari, war tales composed, in versions that scholars believe took shape in the early thirteenth century, decades after the events. These tales are rich in dramatic scenes, speeches, and heroic single combat, and they were shaped by literary conventions of the warrior era in which they were written. They are indispensable for understanding how later generations remembered the conflict, but they are not stenographic records of the 1150s.

Section 22.4

Violence in the Capital and the Consequences of Hōgen

The fighting was brief, but the aftermath made the deeper impression. Sutoku was exiled to Sanuki Province on Shikoku, where he remained until his death in 1164. Yorinaga's family and estates were penalized, and the confiscated holdings passed largely into the hands of Go-Shirakawa's government. Members of the defeated warrior families were rounded up. Tameyoshi was executed on the order of Go-Shirakawa's side, and Yoshitomo, who had fought for the victors, carried out or oversaw the killing of his own father and several of his brothers. Kiyomori, for his part, executed his uncle Tadamasa. Tametomo, spared execution, was reportedly maimed and sent to exile on Izu Ōshima, though later tradition embroidered his fate heavily.

These executions were politically and culturally significant because capital punishment had, according to traditional accounts, largely fallen out of use in the capital for several centuries, an interpretation historians continue to qualify since the court had other forms of lethal punishment in practice. What matters is that contemporaries treated the reintroduction of executions of leading figures as a break with the restraint the court associated with its own traditions. Shinzei is often associated in the sources with pressing for severity, though attribution of individual responsibility for decisions in these years is difficult. The later war tales dwell on the horror of a son forced to kill his father and use the scene as a moral commentary on the age.

The political consequences were immediate. Go-Shirakawa's court used the moment to assert control: a set of regulations issued in 1156, sometimes referred to as the Hōgen regulations, and the re-establishment of a records office (kirokujo) sought to regularize control over shōen and reassert central authority over estate proliferation. The aristocracy's problems, it seemed, could be managed by a stronger, more centralized court. But this outcome contained an irony. The regime that seemed to restore order had done so with the help of armed force, and it now depended on the same warriors whose services had been decisive.

Contemporary and later observers understood that something had changed. The monk-historian Jien, writing in the early thirteenth century, looked back at Hōgen as a moment when the world entered a new phase in which the rule of warriors became prominent. Whether one accepts that periodization, and Section 22.10 presents a debate about it, the concern that violence had entered high politics was widely felt. The dead were also memorialized in ways that reveal anxiety. Sutoku, who died in exile, came to be regarded in later tradition as a vengeful spirit (onryō), and the court reportedly took steps in the 1170s, after disasters in the capital, to honor him and Yorinaga posthumously. The belief that the unquiet dead could unleash calamities belonged to the religious world of the court, and it shows how the violence of 1156 continued to shape political anxiety for decades.

Primary Source Window

Jien's Gukanshō and the War Tales on the Meaning of Hōgen

Creator: Jien (1155–1225), a Tendai monk from the Fujiwara regent family, and anonymous tale-tellers who produced the Hōgen monogatari and Heiji monogatari · Date: Gukanshō c. 1220; war tales in versions of the thirteenth century · Type: Historical interpretation and literary war tales, composed decades after the events

Context: Jien came from the highest reaches of the aristocracy and wrote his historical meditation at a time when the Kamakura bakufu already existed and tension between the court and the bakufu was mounting, shortly before the Jōkyū War of 1221. He interpreted history as a sequence of ages shaped by moral and spiritual principle (dōri). The war tales, by contrast, were performed and copied within a warrior-era culture that valued martial valor and the memory of lost heroes.

Paraphrase (not a verbatim quotation): Jien treated the conflicts of the mid-twelfth century as the point after which the disorder of warriors became a lasting feature of the age, marking a break with earlier patterns of government, and he sought to explain this as part of a larger historical decline and transformation.

Analysis: The Gukanshō shows that within a lifetime or two of Hōgen and Heiji, aristocratic observers were already reading those events as a turning point. That retrospective judgment is itself historical evidence, but it reflects hindsight: Jien knew that the warriors' government had been created and that the court's position had weakened. The war tales likewise contain memorable scenes and speeches that no court diarist recorded and that later narrators shaped for dramatic and moral effect. Neither source represents the views of ordinary residents of Kyoto, of provincial warriors' families, or of women, whose experiences appear only indirectly. Historians therefore use the diaries and documents to establish sequence and participants, and the literary works to understand memory and meaning.

Section 22.5

Between Hōgen and Heiji: Kiyomori, Yoshitomo, and Court Factions

The three years between the two conflicts are easy to skip, but they matter because they show that the aftermath of Hōgen was not a simple contest between two warrior houses. Multiple political networks operated at once. Go-Shirakawa, having survived the succession crisis, reigned only until 1158, when he abdicated in favor of his son Emperor Nijō and took up the role of retired sovereign. This looked like a continuation of insei, but the arrangement produced a new rivalry. Nijō and his supporters, including senior courtiers with their own ambitions, sought to assert the reigning emperor's authority against the retired father. A court that had once been split between two brothers now risked splitting between a father and a son.

Within Go-Shirakawa's own circle, Shinzei was the dominant minister. A scholar and reformer, he influenced appointments, organized the reconstruction of the palace, and pursued efforts to reassert central control. Because he was not from the highest Fujiwara lines, his rise generated resentment. Fujiwara no Nobuyori, a courtier whom Go-Shirakawa favored, resented Shinzei's dominance and sought greater rank and office. Elite rivalry of this kind, and not a rivalry between Minamoto and Taira as such, was the immediate driver of events.

Kiyomori and Yoshitomo had both served the victorious camp in 1156, but their rewards diverged. Kiyomori held provincial governorships and a post at the Dazaifu in Kyushu, an appointment that gave him access to the trade at Hakata. He forged a marriage tie with Shinzei's circle, as tradition and some documentary evidence suggest, betrothing a child to one of Shinzei's sons, though such marriage arrangements were later disrupted by events. Yoshitomo, by contrast, received a more modest position and reportedly felt slighted, having been forced to kill his father and brothers for a reward he regarded as inadequate. Whether or not his resentment was as sharp as the war tales suggest, it is clear that he had drifted toward Nobuyori, who was likewise dissatisfied with Shinzei's ascendancy.

The point is that the two warrior leaders were not a fixed pair of rivals. They were clients of different patrons, ranked differently, and worried about different things. The step that turned discontent into violence was a decision by a courtier, Nobuyori, to use military force to solve a political problem, and a decision by Yoshitomo to help him. The lesson of Hōgen had been absorbed by the court's own factions.

Section 22.6

The Heiji Conflict of 1159–1160

In the twelfth month of Heiji 1 (winter 1159–1160), Kiyomori left Kyoto on a pilgrimage to the Kumano shrines, accompanied by some of his sons and retainers. Nobuyori and Yoshitomo seized the opportunity. They attacked the Sanjō palace, where Go-Shirakawa was residing, set it ablaze, and took the retired emperor into custody. They also secured the person of Emperor Nijō. Shinzei, learning of the attack, fled the capital but was hunted down and died, whether by his own hand or that of his pursuers according to differing traditions. His head was displayed in the capital. Nobuyori then arranged appointments for himself and for Yoshitomo, so that for a brief interval the coup leaders controlled the sovereign, the palace, and the appointments process.

The coup is the first clear case in which military leaders decided who physically controlled the throne. In Hōgen the warriors had struck an enemy residence at the direction of the winning faction. In Heiji, Yoshitomo's forces, in alliance with a court noble, seized the sovereign himself. This was new. Military leaders were no longer merely auxiliary muscle. Their decisions could determine who held the palace and the capital.

But the coup had a weakness: it lacked broad aristocratic support, and it left Kiyomori, the other principal military leader around the capital, unchecked and free to return. Court nobles who disliked Nobuyori, including Fujiwara no Tsunemune and Fujiwara no Korekata, associated with the Nijō faction, worked to arrange a countercoup. Kiyomori, hearing of events, returned to Kyoto and, by prior arrangement or opportunistic realignment, was received as a defender of order. Nijō was smuggled out of the palace, according to tradition in disguise, and taken to Rokuhara, the Taira compound. Go-Shirakawa also escaped confinement and found refuge in a temple. With the sovereign and the retired emperor in safe hands, Kiyomori's side could present itself as the legitimate government and the Nobuyori-Yoshitomo group as rebels.

Fighting followed in Kyoto. Yoshitomo's forces, cut off from legitimacy and outnumbered, were defeated in and around the capital. Yoshitomo fled eastward with a few followers, hoping to reach the Minamoto's supporters in the Kantō, but he was killed in the province of Owari by a local ally who had sheltered him or by men who turned on him, according to the war tale tradition. Nobuyori was captured and executed. The event was recorded by contemporaries and dramatized in the Heiji monogatari and in a later picture-scroll tradition (emaki), including a well-known scroll depicting the burning of the Sanjō palace. Those images, made in the Kamakura period, show us how later viewers wanted to see the violence, with mounted warriors, flames, and helpless courtiers, and should be used as evidence of memory rather than eyewitness depiction.

The Heiji Conflict shows once more that warriors acted within court politics. The countercoup succeeded because Kiyomori had the confidence of the Nijō faction and could present himself as restoring the proper order. The result was that a warrior leader became the principal protector of the sovereign, a political fact with major implications.

Section 22.7

The Destruction and Survival of Warrior Networks

For the Minamoto, Heiji was a catastrophe. Their leader Yoshitomo was dead, and many of his followers had been killed, captured, or dispersed. The Seiwa Genji line's presence at court, which had been substantial in the decades before, collapsed. The Taira no longer faced a comparable rival in the capital. That shift, more than any single battle, altered the balance among warrior houses.

Yoshitomo's sons were treated in different ways. The eldest surviving son, Minamoto no Yoritomo, was about thirteen by Japanese reckoning when captured. Tradition says that Kiyomori's stepmother, Ike no Zenni, pleaded for his life, and Kiyomori commuted the sentence to exile in Izu Province in 1160. Yoshitomo's concubine Tokiwa and her young sons, including the child who would later be known as Yoshitsune, were also spared according to the war tales, though the specific details, including the choices that Tokiwa is said to have made, belong to a literary tradition that later generations embellished. The historical point is that the Taira, at the moment of triumph, chose not to exterminate the Minamoto line. Some historians see this as a sign of the court-centered political culture in which Kiyomori operated, one that favored exile and clemency for children over wholesale killing. Others emphasize that Kiyomori had no way to foresee the consequences.

Yoritomo should therefore remain, in this chapter, a future possibility. In 1160 he was a boy in exile in the eastern province of Izu, under watch by local officials. Nothing in the evidence for the 1160s suggests that he was regarded as a threat. His later reconstruction of Minamoto power and the world it created belong to Chapters 23 and 24. What matters here is that the Minamoto network was broken, not destroyed: members of the family survived in the provinces, in temples, and in the households of other patrons, and the ties of service that connected eastern warriors to Minamoto lords did not vanish.

For other warriors, the outcome was a lesson in the risks of aligning with a court faction. Households that had backed Yoshitomo had to find new patrons or lose position. The Taira, by contrast, expanded their network. They distributed governorships and other offices to relatives and allies, and cultivated ties with provincial warriors, especially in the west. The warrior world, once composed of multiple competing lines around the capital, tilted toward a single dominant household.

Section 22.8

Kiyomori's Rise Inside the Court

Kiyomori was born in 1118 into a family whose fortunes had been built by his father Tadamori and grandfather Masamori through service to retired emperors, especially as provincial governors and as enforcers. Tadamori was granted the privilege of attending the sovereign in the palace (shōden) in 1132, a rare honor for a man of such lineage. Later tradition, reflected in the Heike monogatari, claimed that Kiyomori was in fact the illegitimate son of the retired emperor Shirakawa; modern historians regard this as an uncertain story and note that Kiyomori's success can be explained without it.

After Heiji, Kiyomori's rise was rapid but methodical. He held a series of provincial posts, including the position of vice-governor-general of the Dazaifu (Dazai-no-daini), which gave him a connection to Kyushu and its trade. In 1160 he was appointed a councillor (sangi), the first member of a warrior household to sit among the senior officials of the state. In the mid-1160s he moved up through the ranks, becoming an inner minister (naidaijin) in 1166. In the second month of 1167 he was appointed grand minister (daijō-daijin), the highest office in the ritsuryō system, though he held it only a few months before resigning. Resignation did not mean withdrawal. In 1168 he took Buddhist vows, a step often taken by senior aristocrats in ill health or as they neared retirement, and he became known as a lay monk (nyūdō) while continuing to exert influence, including through his sons Shigemori and Munemori.

The interpretation matters. Kiyomori did not abolish aristocratic government and establish a shogunate. He became extraordinarily powerful through aristocratic political institutions. His rise depended on a combination of factors. First, he retained military credibility, since the Taira were the strongest armed force around the capital and had proved decisive in 1156 and 1160. Second, he cultivated ties with both Go-Shirakawa and the Nijō faction, positioning himself between them and gaining favors from both at different times. Third, he took on the manners of the court elite and participated in its rituals, its poetry and religious patronage, and its rank and office competitions. Fourth, he built a network of Taira relatives and allies who held provincial governorships across the archipelago, so that the family held a striking share of offices, with some accounts claiming roughly half of the provinces at the peak, a figure that later sources may have exaggerated.

Kiyomori's Rokuhara compound in eastern Kyoto served as a base for this network. It housed retainers, stored weapons, and acted as an administrative center that was distinct from the palace and the retired emperor's household yet integrated with them. Neighbors and contemporaries understood Rokuhara as a center of power, and its name would later be attached to a Kamakura bakufu institution.

Historical Vocabulary
Kugyō (senior court nobles)

The kugyō were the small group of officials of the highest ranks, including ministers, counselors, and councillors, who sat in the council of state (jin no sadame) and shared in major decisions. Membership was reserved mostly for men of the aristocratic families that had monopolized these posts for generations. Reaching the kugyō was the entrance to formal high politics.

Kiyomori's entry into the kugyō as a councillor in 1160 and his later promotions did not overturn the ritsuryō system. They showed that a warrior could occupy the very positions that had defined aristocratic status, and that the aristocratic monopoly, though not abolished, had been broken.

Section 22.9

Taira Wealth, Religion, Maritime Connections, and Song Trade

Kiyomori's power cannot be reduced to battlefield success. Taira wealth rested on provincial governorships, particularly in western Japan and around the Inland Sea, where the family and its allies exercised authority over ports, shipping, and estate revenues. Governors could tax, appoint local subordinates, and reward followers. The Taira also held or controlled shōen, whose income supported households and retinues. Their patronage network extended to local elites who valued the connection to Rokuhara and the capital.

The Inland Sea was the artery of this world. It connected the capital region to Kyushu and to the continent, and control over the harbors of Settsu Province gave the Taira influence over traffic bound for Kyoto. Kiyomori developed a base at Fukuhara (in the Kobe area) and supported improvements to the harbor at Ōwada no tomari. Later tradition attributes great engineering feats to him, including the creation of an artificial island, Kyōgashima, and the cutting of a channel at Ondo Strait; some of these accounts embellish, but the family's interest in port facilities is well supported by the pattern of their patronage.

Trade with Song China grew in this period. Merchants from the Song empire operated at Hakata, in northern Kyushu, and their goods, such as copper coins, ceramics, books, and Buddhist texts, entered Japan, while Japan exported items such as gold, sulfur, and timber. Chinese copper cash would in later decades become a monetary presence in Japan, a development in which the Taira's port policies played a role, though the full monetization of the economy came later. In 1170, the diary Gyokuyō, kept by Kujō Kanezane, records that Go-Shirakawa met visitors or merchants from Song China at Kiyomori's Fukuhara residence, an encounter that some aristocrats regarded as a breach of court propriety. The episode shows that Kiyomori was willing to bend court norms on foreign contact for commercial and political advantage.

The maritime network reached beyond Kyushu. Archaeological work on Kikai Island in the Amami group has uncovered the large Gusuku site complex, which appears to have been a center of exchange linking Kyushu, the southern islands, and the continent in the eleventh and twelfth centuries. Sulfur and other southern goods traveled these routes. The island would later appear in the story of the Shishigatani exiles, when the priest Shunkan was sent to a place remembered in tradition as Kikai-ga-shima. These connections remind us that Ryukyuan and Amami communities were participants in the same trading world, not merely a periphery of the court.

Religion reinforced Taira position. Kiyomori became a major patron of Itsukushima Shrine on an island in the Inland Sea, commissioning building programs and, in 1164, the Taira family presented an elaborate set of decorated sutras (the Heike Nōkyō) to the shrine. He also funded the Rengeō-in, commonly known as Sanjūsangen-dō, a hall in Kyoto built for Go-Shirakawa in 1164. Such gifts were tools of alliance. They honored the retired emperor, affirmed Taira devotion, and tied the family to institutions and sacred sites of the maritime world. Buddhist and Shinto patronage were, for Kiyomori as for other aristocrats, part of a single strategy of legitimation and connection.

Section 22.10

The Taira at the Center of Government

The Taira did not challenge the structure of government; they entered it. The most important instrument was marriage. The Fujiwara had for centuries married their daughters to emperors so that their grandsons would sit on the throne. Kiyomori applied the same method. His wife Tokiko was the elder sister of Taira no Shigeko (Kenshunmon-in), Go-Shirakawa's consort and the mother of Emperor Takakura, who was enthroned in 1168. Kiyomori's daughter Moriko married the regent Konoe Motozane, tying the Taira directly to the regent house; after Motozane's death in 1166, Moriko became an important conduit for its estates and resources. Another of Kiyomori's daughters married Motozane's son Motomichi. And in 1171 his daughter Tokuko entered the palace as consort to Takakura, later holding the title of empress and being known as Kenreimon-in. In 1178 she gave birth to a son, who became Emperor Antoku in 1180 as a child, making Kiyomori the emperor's maternal grandfather (gaisofu).

These marriages gave the Taira the status of maternal relatives of the imperial house (gaiseki), the same position that had underwritten Fujiwara power. Taira leaders also adopted other Fujiwara-style mechanisms: officeholding, household networks, estate income, and proximity to the sovereign. The distinctive element was that the Taira combined these tools with a military capability that no aristocratic family could match.

The concept at the heart of this section is the weakening of the aristocratic monopoly on political power. Aristocrats did not disappear. Fujiwara regents continued to serve, court nobles kept diaries and held offices, and the retired emperor remained a central figure. What changed was that a family with a military household as its core could now compete for the same offices and could bring armed force to bear on disputes. The court's traditional aristocratic families found that their political skills, though still valuable, no longer guaranteed control. Family, faction, and force had become inseparable.

This transformation also had a social dimension. Taira relatives and dependents received offices and ranks that earlier would have gone to aristocratic families, provoking resentment. Later tradition, in the Heike monogatari, attributes to a Taira relative a boast that those outside the Taira did not count as people. Whether or not the words were spoken, the sentiment expresses how resentful contemporaries came to view the family's dominance.

Historical Debate

Did Hōgen and Heiji Begin the Age of the Warrior?

The question: Jien and many later writers regarded Hōgen as the moment when warriors' rule began. Modern historians disagree about how far that judgment holds. Should the conflicts be seen as a breakthrough for warrior power, or as a stage in the evolution of aristocratic government?

Interpretation A — A warrior breakthrough: Some historians emphasize that after 1156 and 1160, military leaders became indispensable to court politics. The events demonstrated that the court could not settle its disputes without armed force, and the Taira regime that resulted was the first in which a warrior household stood at the center of government. On this view, the conflicts opened a new political era.
Interpretation B — The militarization of an aristocratic order: Others argue that the Taira regime remained an aristocratic government operating through court offices, marriages, and estates, and that the true institutional transformation came later with the Kamakura bakufu. On this view, Hōgen and Heiji show the militarization of court politics but not yet the creation of a distinct warrior government, and Jien's periodization reflects the hindsight of a later age.

Evidence: Both positions draw on diaries, documents on appointments and estates, and the war tales. They differ in how much weight to assign to the novelty of military intervention as against the continuity of offices, marriage politics, and imperial legitimacy.

Why it matters: How historians understand the period shapes how they interpret the Kamakura bakufu. If warrior government began in 1156, Kamakura extended a trend; if the Taira remained an aristocratic regime, Kamakura was a more decisive institutional break.

Section 22.11

Resistance to Taira Dominance and the 1179 Coup

Taira dominance produced opposition from several directions. Go-Shirakawa, who had accepted Taira support, was not content to be a client. He cultivated his own circle of favorites and used cultural and religious patronage, including his famous compilation of popular songs, the Ryōjin hishō, to build a court of his own. The retired emperor and the Taira cooperated in some periods, but their interests diverged. Aristocratic families passed over for promotion, temples that resented Taira interference, and provincial families dependent on other patrons added to the discontent.

In 1177, tensions became open. A dispute in Kaga Province involving the retinue of Go-Shirakawa's favorite Saikō (Fujiwara no Morotsune) and a temple affiliated with Enryaku-ji escalated into a protest by the great Tendai monastery. The court's handling of the dispute strained relations between Go-Shirakawa's circle and the Taira, who were called upon to deal with the temple's armed clerics. Soon afterward came the Shishigatani affair, in which a group of Go-Shirakawa's associates, including Saikō, Fujiwara no Narichika, and the priest Shunkan, were accused of plotting against the Taira at a villa in the Shishigatani hills. Kiyomori acted decisively. Saikō was executed, Narichika was exiled and died, and Shunkan and others were exiled to the southern islands. Go-Shirakawa was not punished, but his position was weakened. That same year a great fire, the Angen fire, devastated parts of Kyoto, an event that contemporaries interpreted in religious as well as political terms and which the court linked, in some accounts, to the need to appease unquiet spirits such as that of Sutoku.

The critical turning point came in 1179. Kiyomori's son Shigemori, a moderating figure who had maintained good relations with Go-Shirakawa, died that year. Go-Shirakawa then took steps to seize properties associated with Shigemori and to promote his own favorites. In the eleventh month, Kiyomori marched into Kyoto with troops, dismissed dozens of officials, including the regent, replaced them with allies, and confined Go-Shirakawa, in effect ending his cloistered rule. This coup was not a rebellion against the court. It was the seizure of the levers of the court by a Taira regime that used military force to reorganize offices. In that sense, it demonstrates that coercion had been integrated into high politics.

Yet the coup also displayed the regime's fragility. The Taira could remove opponents, but they could not eliminate the sources of opposition, including a retired emperor with prestige, temples with armed followers, and aristocrats resentful of Taira dominance. The regime also made enemies among the provincial warriors who felt excluded from the benefits of Taira patronage, a subject that Chapter 23 will take up.

Section 22.12

On the Threshold of Civil War

In early 1180, Emperor Takakura abdicated in favor of the young Antoku, Kiyomori's grandson. The Taira had reached the height of their power. A Taira woman was the mother of the reigning emperor, Kiyomori's family held key ministerial posts and numerous governorships, and Go-Shirakawa was neutralized. In a sense, the Taira had duplicated the Fujiwara achievement of controlling the throne through maternal kinship, while adding the advantage of military force.

The transformation from 1156 to the eve of 1180 was substantial. In Hōgen, warriors fought for competing court factions. In Heiji, warrior leaders determined which faction physically held the sovereign. By the late 1170s, Kiyomori's military household had itself become one of the dominant political forces at court. The change did not come from a warrior ideology or a plan to end aristocratic government. It came from the accumulation of opportunities created by court disputes and from Kiyomori's skill in exploiting them.

Yet the Taira still depended on imperial legitimacy, aristocratic offices, marriage alliances, and traditional institutions. This is the central contradiction of the regime. Its strength came from its integration into the court order, but that same integration made it vulnerable to the accusations of the court's other members and left it dependent on a sovereign who was, in the end, a child. It also created a gap between the Taira and provincial warriors, who did not share in the benefits of the regime and who owed their positions to different patrons, including exiled or dispersed Minamoto connections. Discontent of this kind would need a leader and a claim to legitimacy, and in 1180 both would appear. That story, the Taira-Minamoto war and the collapse of the Taira order, belongs to Chapter 23. The making of a durable warrior government, and the role of Yoritomo, belong to Chapter 24.

For students, the significance of the period is that it shows how a political system can be transformed from within. The ritsuryō offices, the regency, and the retired emperor's household continued to exist, but the meaning of holding them changed once coercive power became a normal element of court politics.

Everyday Life and Environment

Kyoto Under Threat

Kyoto in this period was a city of aristocratic mansions, temples, markets, and crowded neighborhoods, largely built of wood and vulnerable to fire. When palaces burned in 1156 and 1159, the damage was not limited to the elite. Fire could spread, rebuilding required labor and materials from the surrounding countryside, and the courts' financial demands fell on estates and provinces. The great fire of 1177 destroyed a large part of the city, and contemporaries read it in religious terms.

Religion permeated the response to crisis. Aristocrats commissioned rituals and sutra copying, patronized temples, and worried about the anger of the dead. Popular culture also flourished: the retired emperor's interest in imayō songs and in the entertainers who performed them, including women performers known as shirabyōshi, shows a court culture that mixed elite and popular forms. The Taira commissioned splendid religious art, and Kiyomori's family sponsored performances and festivals. Ordinary people, though rarely named, sustained this world as porters, servants, boatmen, craftspeople, and farmers whose labor and taxes underwrote it.

Cause and Effect

The Militarization of Court Politics and Taira Dominance

Long-term causes

The growth of insei and regency politics that concentrated power in personal networks; the rise of provincial military households and their service to capital patrons; the accumulation of estates and governorships as bases of wealth.

Short-term causes

The contested succession of 1155; the death of Toba in 1156; the Fujiwara split; personal rivalries among courtiers such as Nobuyori and Shinzei; Kiyomori's absence from Kyoto in 1159.

Event or transformation

The Hōgen and Heiji conflicts, followed by Kiyomori's rise to the highest offices and the Taira integration into the imperial house.

Immediate effects

Executions and exile; destruction of Minamoto leadership; consolidation of a Taira network; a weakened aristocratic monopoly on offices.

Long-term consequences

A regime dependent on both force and court legitimacy, generating opposition that would erupt in 1180; a precedent for warrior participation in central government that the Kamakura bakufu would institutionalize.

Historical complexity

No single actor caused the transformation. Succession disputes, factional rivalry, military capacity, marriage strategy, and commercial resources each mattered, and the outcome was not inevitable.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced the political turning point in which warrior households, whose networks Chapter 21 described, were drawn into the center of court politics. The setting was a court divided by succession disputes, the rule of retired emperors, and the split within the Fujiwara regent line. By the death of Toba in 1156, the political center itself had fractured, and factions sought security and advantage from military households.

The Hōgen Conflict of 1156 was fought between the supporters of Emperor Go-Shirakawa and those of the former emperor Sutoku, with Minamoto and Taira warriors on both sides. It was not a rebellion by warriors against the court; it was a court conflict in which warriors served competing principals. Its speed and its aftermath, including executions and exile, shocked contemporaries and were remembered as a breakdown of older restraint.

The years between the conflicts show that court rivalry, not a simple Taira-Minamoto contest, drove events. Go-Shirakawa's abdication, the rise of Shinzei, and the ambitions of Nobuyori created tensions that Yoshitomo and Kiyomori navigated according to their own patronage ties. The Heiji Conflict of 1159–1160 escalated the pattern: military leaders seized the sovereign and the palace, and a countercoup by Kiyomori restored the Nijō and Go-Shirakawa circle and destroyed Yoshitomo's faction.

The consequences for the Minamoto were severe but not final. The leader was dead and the network broken, yet survivors, including the young Yoritomo, remained. For the Taira, the outcome was opportunity. Kiyomori advanced through governorships, ranks, and offices to become daijō-daijin in 1167, and he did so by working within the court system, not against it.

Taira power rested on more than force. Provincial governorships, the Inland Sea, Kiyomori's base at Fukuhara and the nearby harbor at Ōwada no tomari, trade with Song China, patronage of Itsukushima Shrine and Kyoto temples, and marriage ties to the imperial house all contributed. The Taira became maternal kin of the throne, imitating the strategy of the Fujiwara but supplementing it with military capability.

The chapter's central interpretive point concerns the weakening of aristocratic political monopoly. Aristocrats, retired emperors, temples, and regents remained important. What changed was that a warrior household could occupy the offices and use the marriage strategies that had defined aristocratic power, and could bring credible coercive power to disputes. The result was the militarization of court politics.

Regional and international dimensions were woven through the story. The Taira's western and maritime base, the Dazaifu and Hakata trade, the southern island connections at Kikai, the exile sites in Izu and Sanuki, and the independent northeastern power of the Ōshū Fujiwara show that events in Kyoto were embedded in a wider archipelago and East Asian world.

The chapter has also emphasized source criticism. Contemporary diaries and documents establish sequence and participants, while the war tales, Jien's Gukanshō, and later picture scrolls reveal how memory shaped the story. Dramatic episodes, such as speeches, single combats, and the details of Yoritomo's rescue, should be treated as literary tradition unless independently supported.

By 1177–1179, resistance to Taira dominance surfaced in the Shishigatani affair and Kiyomori's coup against Go-Shirakawa and his allies. The coup showed how coercion had become integrated into high politics, while also revealing the regime's fragility, since it still depended on imperial legitimacy and could not eliminate opposition.

By the eve of 1180 the Taira stood at the height of their power with their grandson on the throne. Their regime was a paradox: strong because it was embedded in the court order, and vulnerable for the same reason. That paradox set up the civil war of Chapter 23 and, ultimately, the durable warrior government of Chapter 24.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a fundamental change in political power can occur without a dramatic break in institutions. The ritsuryō offices, the regency, the retired emperor's household, and the imperial line all continued. What changed was the presence of credible armed force within the competition for those offices. The people who benefited most were warriors who understood how the court worked.

The chapter also reveals a process that recurs in Japanese history and elsewhere: groups initially serving elites may become indispensable and then claim a place at the center. The Taira did not seek to abolish aristocratic government. They joined it, and in doing so changed it. That pattern helps explain why the Kamakura bakufu, later, would define itself in relation to the court and not simply against it.

Finally, the period connects Kyoto to a wider world. The Taira drew on the Inland Sea, on Kyushu, and on Song China, while northern and southern communities pursued their own connections. The story of the capital was therefore not the story of the archipelago as a whole, yet it was shaped by, and shaped, those wider networks. Students who understand this chapter are prepared for Chapter 23, in which the Taira regime confronts rebellion and civil war, and for Chapter 24, in which a new kind of warrior government emerges.
