Part III — Nara and Heian Japan

Provinces, Frontiers, and Peoples Beyond the Court

Japanese History: From Jōmon Japan to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

Every bundle of cloth, every measure of rice, every dried fish or salt cake that reached the storehouses of Heijō-kyō first had to be grown, woven, boiled, dried, packed, labeled, and carried—often on the backs of the very people who had produced it. The Nara state that Chapters 14 and 15 examined from the capital looked very different from a village in Shimōsa, a district office in Izumo, or a wooden fort on the plains of Mutsu.

Among the many objects recovered from the ancient capital and from provincial sites are narrow wooden tablets, called mokkan, that were tied to bundles of goods traveling to the court. Some record a province, a district, a settlement, a taxpayer's name, the kind of product, and a date. They are humble objects—strips of wood, often discarded after use—but they capture something the great chronicles rarely do. They show that the machinery of the ritsuryō state depended on countless individual acts of production and transport carried out by named people in places whose names still survive in the archaeological and documentary record. When a tablet reads "province, district, village, person, item," it records the point at which a household in the countryside met the demands of a court hundreds of kilometers away.

This chapter reverses the viewpoint of the two chapters before it. Chapter 14 asked how the Nara state was organized around its capital; Chapter 15 asked how Buddhist institutions became part of that state. Here the question is what that state looked like to people living in the provinces and at its contested edges. How did registers, taxes, governors, district officials, roads, temples, forts, and military expeditions actually reach rural communities? How did local elites—families that had wielded authority in their regions long before the court claimed to rule them—mediate, exploit, and sometimes resist that authority? And why did the northeastern expansion of imperial institutions into Mutsu and Dewa produce not simply conquest, but negotiation, alliance, exploitation, incorporation, and armed resistance together?

The chapter's central argument is that Nara rule was real but uneven. Court authority did extend far beyond the Kinai, the home region around the capital. Households in distant provinces were registered, assigned land, and taxed; governors arrived from the capital; roads, post stations, and provincial headquarters linked distant places to Heijō. But the court did not govern every village directly. It relied on hereditary district magistrates drawn from powerful local families, on household production, on negotiated compliance, and on regional networks that predated the state. At the northeastern frontier, imperial claims were even further ahead of imperial capacity. The court's category "Emishi" covered diverse communities whose relationships to the state ranged from trade, tribute, and alliance to incorporation and armed resistance.

It is therefore a serious mistake to describe the Nara period's northeastern expansion as the "unification of Japan," as though a single nation were steadily filling out its natural borders. The people of the northeast were not empty land awaiting settlement, nor were they a single tribe or a single "ethnic enemy." The state that pressed northward was itself a young institution, unfinished and internally strained. The expansion changed both sides, created new social groups, generated wealth and inequality, and left conflicts that the next generation would inherit. This textbook's commitment to keeping regional and frontier populations visible—rather than allowing the history of the central court to stand for the whole archipelago—is especially important here.

The evidence for this chapter's subject is uneven and often difficult. Court chronicles, especially the Shoku Nihongi (completed in 797), and the ritsuryō law codes describe the provinces from above and often in the language of the court. Archaeology, wooden tablets, surviving household registers from the eighth century, provincial gazetteers called fudoki, inscribed stone monuments, poetry, and the remains of provincial headquarters, roads, temples, and forts allow historians to reconstruct provincial experience, though only imperfectly. Almost no writings survive from communities the court called Emishi; historians must read court sources against the grain and combine them with archaeology. This chapter therefore asks constantly not only what happened, but who is telling us, and whose voice is missing.

Chapter 16 thus completes a three-chapter movement: from the state as seen from Heijō, to religious institutions within the state, to the state as it was experienced, mediated, and contested beyond the court. It ends by handing Chapter 17 a political order that is powerful but strained, ruling diverse regions imperfectly, as the generation of Emperor Kanmu confronts the decision to leave Nara. It also anticipates later chapters on warriors, estates, and the transformation of provincial society, which will build on the local elites, military obligations, and frontier relationships introduced here.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how the Nara state extended registers, taxation, and administration into rural communities, and why implementation was uneven.
  2. Describe the household as the basic unit of rural economic and social life, including differences in labor by gender and status.
  3. Analyze the tax and labor system from the perspective of a taxpaying household rather than the capital, and assess what "centralization" cost ordinary people.
  4. Distinguish the roles of provincial governors (kokushi) and district magistrates (gunji), and explain why the court depended on locally rooted elites.
  5. Trace how provincial headquarters, roads, post stations, markets, ports, and temples created regional networks—and how exchange also existed beyond direct state control.
  6. Define "frontier" as a shifting zone of interaction rather than a fixed border, and locate Mutsu, Dewa, and Tagajō within it.
  7. Evaluate why the term "Emishi" cannot be treated as the name of a single ethnic group, polity, or the later Ainu, and identify the kinds of evidence historians use.
  8. Explain the mechanisms of northeastern expansion—forts, settler transfers, alliances, ranks, and gifts—and how they complicated a simple binary of court versus Emishi.
  9. Interpret the career of Iji no Azamaro and the revolt of 780 as evidence about incorporation and resistance within the same frontier system.
  10. Assess the limits of court power and the ways Nara authority varied across the Kinai, the provinces, Kyushu, and the northeast.
  11. Read court-produced sources critically, distinguishing administrative categories, ideological labels, and evidence about actual events.
  12. Connect frontier developments to the wider Nara world, including Baekje-descended officials, Balhae contacts, Hayato in southern Kyushu, and the southern islands.

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  2. What did "centralization" cost a rural household, and to what extent did legal prescriptions about land, taxes, and labor match actual practice?
  3. Why did the court need hereditary local elites, and how did those elites use their position between the state and the villages?
  4. In what ways were provincial regions connected to one another and to the capital, and where did networks operate independently of the state?

Historical Context

By the early eighth century, the court in Yamato had spent roughly two centuries expanding its influence over regional powers. Earlier chapters traced how Yamato rulers built alliances with powerful chiefs across the archipelago (Chapters 7 and 8), how continental models of writing, law, and Buddhism arrived (Chapters 9 and 10), and how the reforms of the seventh century and the law codes of 701 and 718 laid out an ambitious administrative order (Chapter 13). Chapter 14 described that order as it operated from Heijō-kyō: provinces (kuni) subdivided into districts (gun) and settlements (sato, later gō), household registers, land allotment, taxation in grain, cloth, local products, and labor, and a bureaucracy ranked and salaried by the state. Chapter 15 showed how state-sponsored Buddhism, culminating in the provincial temple system ordered in 741, linked religious and political authority. This chapter takes those institutions as given and asks how they worked at a distance.

The court's own image of its realm was shaped by continental political ideas. The ritsuryō state modeled itself in part on the Tang empire, with an emperor at the center of a civilized realm and "barbarian" peoples at its margins. In this vision, the peoples of the southern part of Kyushu, called Hayato, and the peoples of the northeast, called Emishi, were groups who lived beyond the full reach of civilization and were to be brought into the state's order through tribute, submission, or force. This language tells historians about court ideology. It is not a neutral ethnography, and it should not be mistaken for a description of how those communities understood themselves.

The geography of the archipelago mattered enormously. Most of the population lived in river valleys, coastal plains, and basins where irrigated rice could be grown, from the Kinai and the Inland Sea to the Kantō plain in the east and northern Kyushu in the west. Mountains divided one region from another, and travel by sea often mattered as much as travel by road. In the northeast, rice agriculture was less dominant and its spread was itself part of the frontier story. Long before the ritsuryō state claimed the region, communities there had developed their own ways of farming, hunting, fishing, gathering, trading, and organizing political leadership, in contact with peoples in Hokkaido and across the Sea of Japan.

Finally, the demographic and environmental conditions of the eighth century shaped what the state could accomplish. The population of the archipelago is often estimated at five to six million people in this period, though such figures are approximations. Famine and epidemic were recurrent; the smallpox epidemic of 735–737 was especially devastating and provoked policy responses described in Chapter 15. The court's ambitious systems of registration and allotment were designed for a stable, counted, settled population, and the reality of migration, illness, and evasion repeatedly disrupted that design. Understanding this gap between design and practice is central to the chapter.

Section 16.1

Beyond Heijō: Seeing the Provinces from the Ground

To understand the Nara state, it is tempting to begin where the sources are richest: in the capital, with its palace, its ranked officials, its great temples, and its chronicles. Yet the capital was, in an important sense, a consumer. Its population of aristocrats, officials, artisans, monks, and laborers was fed, clothed, housed, and equipped by goods and labor that flowed in from the provinces. A large proportion of the people who made this possible never saw Heijō. They lived in settlements along rivers, on plains, on coasts, and in hills, and their daily lives were organized around planting and harvest, weaving, fishing, and the obligations imposed by governors and district officials.

The word "province" in this period requires care. In court documents, the archipelago was divided into provinces (kuni) grouped into circuits or "roads" (dō) along which officials and messages traveled. In practice, a province was an administrative frame laid over landscapes that already had their own histories of settlement and political organization. The boundaries and names of provinces changed during the eighth century; new provinces such as Dewa (712) were created, others were divided or merged. Nor did the name of a province mean that the court controlled everything within it equally. A province near the capital might be densely administered; a province on the far side of a mountain range or a strait might be governed largely through the cooperation of powerful local families. Modern prefectural boundaries should not be projected onto these earlier units.

The Problem of Evidence

Reconstructing provincial life requires combining several kinds of sources, each with its own limitations. The court chronicles, especially the Nihon Shoki (720) and the Shoku Nihongi (completed 797, covering 697–791), record imperial decrees, appointments, campaigns, epidemics, and famines from the standpoint of the court. They are invaluable for dates and official policies but tell us little about ordinary villages. The ritsuryō codes describe what the state intended, not necessarily what it achieved. Prescriptions for land allotment and taxation tell us the ideal; they do not tell us how much was actually collected.

More direct evidence comes from documents produced as part of the administration itself. Fragmentary household registers from the early eighth century survive in the collections of the Shōsōin, the treasure repository associated with Tōdaiji; they were preserved largely because official paper was reused and stored. Wooden tablets (mokkan), found in very large numbers at the site of the capital and at provincial and district sites, include tax tags attached to shipments, records of personnel, and messages. In 713, the court ordered provinces to compile gazetteers (fudoki) recording place names, natural products, the fertility of the land, and local legends. Only a few survive in substantial form, including that of Izumo (compiled in 733 under the province's administration and associated with its powerful local lineage), and partial or fragmentary texts for other provinces such as Harima and Hitachi. They reflect local memory as filtered through the requirements of a court order.

Inscribed stone monuments, poetry, and archaeology add further dimensions. The Man'yōshū, the great anthology of Japanese poetry compiled in the later eighth century, preserves poems by conscripted eastern frontier guards and reflections on rural poverty, though these too were selected and edited by elite compilers. Archaeology has revealed the layouts of provincial headquarters, district offices, roads, storehouses, temples, kilns, and settlements. Excavation of forts and fortified settlements in the northeast provides evidence about the frontier that written sources alone cannot. Historians must therefore triangulate, using texts to date and label, and archaeology to see patterns of settlement, production, and movement that texts overlook.

Whose voices are missing? Farmers, women, laborers, unfree persons, and most northeastern communities did not write about themselves in ways that survive. What we possess are traces: names on registers and tags, objects in the ground, and court descriptions of them. Reading these traces carefully—without romanticizing rural people as passive victims or treating the court's categories as neutral facts—is the methodological challenge of this chapter.

Section 16.2

Rural Households, Villages, and Agricultural Life

The overwhelming majority of people in the Nara-period archipelago lived in rural communities and worked on the land or the water. There was no single "peasantry" with a uniform experience. A household in a fertile basin of the Kinai growing irrigated rice differed from a household in the eastern mountains raising millet and hemp, from a coastal household that combined fishing, salt boiling, and small-scale farming, and from a northeastern community whose economy might combine horse raising, hunting, fishing, gathering, and farming. The court's legal categories treated households as equivalent taxpaying units; lived reality was far more varied.

Agriculture and Regional Production

Wet-rice agriculture, introduced during the Yayoi period (Chapter 5), was the foundation of the state's fiscal system, and rice fields were the central object of land allotment. But rice was not the only crop. Households grew millet, barley, beans, and vegetables in dry fields, and cultivated hemp for cloth and mulberry for silk where climate and skill allowed. Lacquer trees, fruit trees, and timber added to household resources. Livestock, especially horses and cattle, were significant in some regions, and in eastern provinces the state maintained pastures for horses. The state's regional demands reflect these differences: tribute goods (chō) in the form of textiles, iron, salt, dried and salted fish, abalone, seaweed, and other local specialties are attested in the tags and documents. Salt was produced at coastal locations, iron in regions with ore and fuel, and ceramics in kiln complexes that supplied local and distant markets. Such production meant that rural communities were not merely agricultural; they were nodes in networks of specialized production.

Households and Labor

The household was the basic unit of both production and taxation. Surviving registers show households that were often larger than a modern nuclear family, sometimes including several related adults, children, elderly relatives, and unfree dependents. Historians debate how far the registered "households" corresponded to actual residential groups; the state had incentives to create administratively convenient units, and households may have been reorganized on paper to facilitate taxation and conscription. The registers nonetheless offer a rare window into ordinary rural families, including their size, composition, and legal status.

Labor was gendered but not simply divided. Men were the primary subjects of the most burdensome obligations, including military service and labor service, while women were expected to perform the textile work that generated cloth for tax and household use. Women's labor in spinning, weaving, and sewing was essential to the household economy and to the state's textile revenue, even where the law recorded taxes primarily in men's names. Women also received allotted fields, in principle at a smaller share than men, and some registered households were headed by women. Registers also record unfree persons (nuhi), held by the state, temples, elites, or wealthy households; their legal allotments were smaller still. The legal categories of free and "base" status shaped life chances, though the boundaries were not always clear in practice.

Seasonal Rhythms and Vulnerability

Rural life followed the agricultural calendar: plowing, transplanting, weeding, harvest, and storage, interwoven with the demands of tax labor, which could take household members away at critical moments. Vulnerability was structural. A poor harvest, a flood, a drought, or an epidemic could push households into debt or flight. The smallpox epidemic of 735–737 was catastrophic; scholars propose that it killed a substantial share of the population, with some estimates in the range of a quarter to a third, although such figures are uncertain and must be treated cautiously. Epidemics disrupted planting, reduced the labor available for fields and tax service, and altered the registers on which the state's fiscal system rested.

Rural communities also had religious and ritual lives that the state did not fully control. Local shrines honoring the kami of fields, mountains, and rivers structured village ritual, and agricultural festivals marked the seasons. Buddhist temples, some founded by local elites and later joined by state-sponsored provincial temples, added new institutions, though popular Buddhist practice and its relation to older cults developed unevenly. The fudoki preserve many local tales linking place names to deeds of kami, showing that provincial communities thought of their landscapes in terms of local memory and religious meaning as well as in terms of tax and administration.

A Registered Household

Imagine a household in an eastern province, entered in the register as a single unit under a male head. It includes the head and his wife, a grown son and his wife, several children, an elderly widowed relative, and perhaps a dependent of unfree status. The register records each member's age category and sex because these categories shaped obligations. The household owes the grain tax (so) on its allotted rice fields, while taxable males bear personal obligations including chō, yō, labor service, and military conscription. Women, children, and the elderly generally face lighter or different formal obligations. Land has been allotted according to the household's members, though the allotted fields may be scattered, of varied quality, and not always actually cultivated by the recipient.

The household's year is defined by tasks. In spring they prepare seedbeds and fields; in summer they weed and manage water; in autumn they harvest and thresh. Between these tasks, the women spin and weave hemp or silk, the men repair tools, cut wood, and perhaps travel to fulfill labor duties. When the district office demands tax goods, the household must gather grain, cloth, and specific local products, often packaged and labeled with tags. Some of these goods might be carried to the provincial center; others might be sent onward to the capital by household members responsible for transport. A year of poor harvest, an epidemic, or the loss of an adult man to conscription or death could leave the household unable to meet its obligations. In that situation it might borrow grain from the provincial loan system, sell or pledge assets, seek the protection of a powerful local family, or leave.

Section 16.3

Land, Registers, and the Tax Burden

Chapter 14 described the institutional design of the ritsuryō land and tax system. This section asks a different question: what did "centralization" cost a household? The answer requires distinguishing between legal prescription and implementation, and between obligations in principle and burdens in practice.

The Register and the Allotment

The system began with registration. In principle, the state compiled household registers at regular intervals, recording the members of each household with their ages, sex, and status. An earlier nationwide register, the Kōgo-year register of 670, established an important precedent. The Kōin-year register of 690, compiled under the Asuka Kiyomihara system, became the starting point for the regular six-year cycle of household registration later codified under the ritsuryō state. Registers were the basis for the allotment of rice fields (the handen system): in principle, each registered person over a certain age received a plot on loan from the state for life, with allotments smaller for women and for unfree persons, and the plots were to be returned and reassigned at intervals. Allotment created the legal basis for taxation, since those who received land owed taxes, and gave the state a claim to control the distribution of land.

Historical Vocabulary
Koseki (household register)

A koseki was a household register compiled by local authorities under the ritsuryō system. In the Nara period, registers recorded household members by name, age category, sex, and status, and served as the basis for land allotment, taxation, and conscription. The term also names the later Japanese family register system, but the eighth-century registers were tools of a specific administrative order and should not be confused with modern household registration.

Surviving eighth-century registers are valuable but must be read critically. They were produced by local officials for the state; historians note irregularities, such as surprisingly high proportions of women in some registers, that may reflect tax avoidance, since women's obligations were lighter. The registers therefore show both the state's ambitions and the strategies households used to shape what the state saw.

The Taxes and Their Weight

The tax system consisted of several categories. The so, a grain tax on allotted rice land, was nominally modest and largely retained in the province for local storage and use. The chō was a levy in textiles or local products, and the yō a corvée-exemption levy commonly paid in cloth or other goods; both were personal obligations of taxable males and were sent toward the capital for the use of the court and bureaucracy. The zōyō was an additional compulsory labor obligation, up to a fixed number of days each year, often imposed for provincial projects such as roads, irrigation, and construction. Beyond these, households were subject to military obligations: adult men could be drafted into provincial military units (gundan), and certain men were sent as frontier guards (sakimori) to Kyushu and the islands of the strait. The provincial government also lent grain to households at interest (suiko), which began as a form of relief and seed loan but came to function as a quasi-tax when borrowing and repayment became compulsory or customary.

The nominal rate of the grain tax was low by later standards, but the system's true burden lay in the combination of goods and labor, and especially in transport. Households were responsible for delivering chō and yō goods to the capital, in many cases by having members carry them on foot or by pack animal over long distances, at their own expense in provisions and time. Men who traveled to the capital had to return home on their own. Some died on the road. The Man'yōshū includes a poem, traditionally attributed to Yamanoue no Okura, that depicts a poor household in which the village headman arrives demanding taxes; whatever its literary features, it captures how the system was perceived from below. The burden of the zōyō and conscription was unequal: adult men in their prime carried the heaviest loads, and a household with few adult men was exposed to catastrophic strain if one was drafted or died.

The eastern provinces (Tōgoku) bore particular military burdens. Eastern conscripts were sent to Kyushu as sakimori for terms of years; poems collected by Ōtomo no Yakamochi in 755 preserve the voices of some of these men and their families, lamenting departure and separation. The system of sending eastern soldiers to the far west was suspended and revived at different times in the eighth century, reflecting both its cost and the court's continuing concern for defense. Eastern households also supplied labor and materials for frontier building in the northeast, as the second half of this chapter shows.

Evasion, Mobility, and the Erosion of the Allotment Ideal

The allotment system presumed a settled, countable, and cooperative population. Reality intruded. Households sought to reduce burdens by underreporting members, especially adult men, and by overrepresenting women and the elderly. Some people fled their registered home to escape taxes and labor service; the sources call such people furō (wanderers) or tōbō (fugitives). Fugitives sometimes found protection with powerful local families, monasteries, or newly opened fields, where they could work outside the direct oversight of the district office. The court repeatedly tried to reclaim such people and stabilize registration, with mixed success.

The court also faced land pressure. Population loss from epidemic and migration left allotment fields uncultivated in some places, while pressure to open new fields grew elsewhere. To encourage reclamation, the government adopted measures such as a law in 723 allowing families to hold newly opened land for a limited number of generations, and a further decree in 743 that permitted the perpetual private holding of newly reclaimed land. These measures encouraged elites and temples with labor and capital to expand their holdings. They did not abolish the state's system overnight, but they opened a path toward the estates (shōen) that will be examined in Chapter 20. It is important not to imply that every obligation was collected uniformly everywhere or that the ritsuryō tax system ended abruptly. Rather, the state's fiscal reach was strong in some places and weak in others, and local officials and elites had considerable discretion in how demands were met.

Money added another layer of unevenness. The court issued copper coinage beginning in 708 and tried to promote its use, including by rewarding those who accumulated it. But in most rural regions, cloth, rice, and other goods continued to serve as everyday media of exchange, and coinage circulated most actively in and around the capital. The fiscal system was thus, for most rural households, a system of goods and labor rather than of cash.

Section 16.4

Provincial Governors, District Officials, and Local Elites

Chapter 14 introduced the distinction between provincial governors (kokushi) and district officials (gunji). That distinction is the key to understanding how the Nara state worked in practice. Governors came from the center; district administration depended on locally rooted hereditary families. Provincial society should therefore be understood as co-governed and mediated, not as a capital-based bureaucracy reaching down to passive villages.

Kokushi: Officials from the Center

Each province was headed by a governor (kami) assisted by lesser officials, collectively called kokushi, who were appointed by the court for limited terms. They were drawn from the ranked officialdom of the capital, and a provincial appointment was a step in a career that might lead to higher office. Governors supervised tax collection, the registers, the storehouses, conscription, public works, and the administration of justice. Their headquarters (kokufu) were planned centers with government offices, storehouses, and residences, and archaeology has revealed many such sites laid out in imitation of the capital's style. But governors served for short periods, often did not know the region, and needed local knowledge and cooperation to accomplish their tasks. Some were diligent and some exploited their posts for private gain, and the court's supervision of them was imperfect.

Gunji: The Locally Rooted Elite

The districts, by contrast, were administered by officials called gunji, who were chosen largely from families that had wielded authority in the region for generations. Many were descended from the holders of the older title kuni no miyatsuko (often read kokuzō), regional rulers who had been incorporated into the Yamato political order in earlier centuries (Chapter 8). The ritsuryō system transformed such families into district magistrates, and in practice the office often remained within a family for generations, though the law framed it as an appointment based on ability. Former kuni no miyatsuko lineages sometimes retained ritual roles, for example in the priesthoods of major regional shrines, alongside their administrative positions.

Historical Vocabulary
Gunji (district magistrate)

A gunji was an official responsible for the administration of a district (gun), a subdivision of a province. The office was filled mainly by members of established local families, many of whom had ancestors who were regional rulers before the ritsuryō system. Gunji supervised registration, taxation, labor service, and local order, and they often held additional religious and economic roles in their communities.

The term should not be confused with kokushi, the governors sent from the capital. The relationship between the two was central to provincial administration: governors depended on gunji for local knowledge and cooperation, while gunji depended on the court for rank and legitimacy. The gunji offered the state something it could not easily create for itself, namely, established social authority in the countryside.

Why did the court need these families? They possessed knowledge of local resources, people, and boundaries; they commanded the labor of households through kinship, patronage, and custom; they had wealth and often literacy; and their prestige made their cooperation more effective than coercion by outsiders. A district could not easily be governed against the will of its leading family. The state secured their cooperation with offices, ranks, office-fields, and the prestige of connection to the capital. In return, gunji families gained a new source of authority backed by the law, opportunities to advance in rank, and access to court networks. Some patronized Buddhist temples and constructed private temples in their districts, expressing status and connecting to the new religion. Inscribed stone monuments in Kōzuke and Shimotsuke Provinces, including the Tago stele of 711 recording the creation of a new district from households of neighboring districts, preserve the voices and ambitions of local elites who chose to memorialize their families and their relationship to state institutions in stone.

The relationship was not without tension. Gunji had opportunities to manipulate registers, shield dependents from taxation, use public labor for private ends, and expand their landholdings, and governors had reasons both to prevent this and to overlook it in exchange for cooperation. Rival local families competed for district offices and for influence with governors. The court attempted to regulate these matters by issuing warnings and reforms, and it monitored the appointment of gunji, but its ability to do so was limited. Provincial elites should therefore not be portrayed solely as agents of the court or solely as victims of it. They were both beneficiaries and negotiators, and their interests sometimes aligned with those of the state and sometimes diverged.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Local Elite Mediation, from Yamato Alliances to the Ritsuryō Provinces

What Changed

  • Regional rulers who had once negotiated alliances with Yamato kings became office-holders within a written legal and bureaucratic framework, with defined duties, ranks, and supervisors.
  • Governors sent from the capital introduced a new layer of authority, and provincial headquarters brought writing, registers, and planned architecture into local landscapes.
  • The state's demands became more systematic and more intrusive: registration, allotment, standardized taxation, and conscription reached down to the household.

What Stayed the Same

  • Regional authority still rested on inherited status, control over labor, and local religious prestige.
  • The central court still needed the cooperation of powerful provincial families and rewarded it with titles and rank.
  • Local families retained substantial autonomy in day-to-day governance and used connections to the center to strengthen their positions.

Who experienced change most directly? Ordinary households encountered the sharpest change, as registration and taxation became more systematic. Local elites experienced a mixture of change and continuity: their authority was formalized but also constrained. Why the pattern matters: The pattern prepares the way for later developments in which provincial elites, military households, and estate holders became increasingly independent political actors (Chapters 20 and 21).

Section 16.5

Provincial Centers, Roads, Markets, Temples, and Regional Networks

The countryside was not a scatter of isolated villages. The Nara state built and maintained institutions that linked places together, and those institutions coexisted with older and more informal networks of kinship, exchange, and religion. The result was a landscape of nodes and routes: state-created centers connected by roads, rivers, and coasts, with exchange networks that sometimes followed and sometimes bypassed the state's infrastructure.

Headquarters, District Offices, and Storehouses

Each province had a headquarters (kokufu) and each district a district office. Excavations have shown that provincial headquarters were often laid out on planned grids with an administrative compound, storehouses, workshops, and residences, and that district offices included storehouses and offices of more modest scale. Storehouses were among the most important structures, holding grain, cloth, and other goods collected as tax or held as reserves. They were not only fiscal facilities but also symbols of state presence: a visible building in the landscape that announced the reach of the court. Written documents circulated among these sites, including tax tags, personnel records, and official orders, and wooden tablets found at provincial sites confirm that literacy and record-keeping extended well beyond the capital, though only among a small stratum of officials and elite families.

Roads and Post Stations

Roads were a defining feature of the ritsuryō state. The court organized the archipelago into seven circuits (dō) along which official roads ran, radiating from the Kinai toward the provinces. Post stations (eki) were established at intervals along major routes, equipped with horses for official messengers and travelers on state business. Excavations have uncovered straight, well-graded roads in various regions, in some places about a dozen meters wide, which suggests both the labor invested in construction and the symbolic importance of roads as expressions of state power. Roads in the west, especially the route to the Kyushu administrative center at Dazaifu, were considered especially significant. Routes heading northeast through the eastern provinces toward Mutsu and Dewa gained importance as the frontier moved. These roads served the state's messengers and troops, and also carried goods and travelers who used them for private ends when they could.

Markets, Ports, and Coastal Routes

Markets existed in the capital and in some provincial locations, but exchange in most places relied on periodic gatherings, local barter, and the movement of goods along rivers and coasts. Ports and coastal routes were essential: the Inland Sea and the Sea of Japan coast carried tax goods, people, and news. Coastal communities that specialized in fishing, salt making, and seafaring linked distant regions in ways that landlocked administrative schemes did not capture. The court's own system relied on such routes, since the transport of bulk tax goods often required water transport where possible. The islands of the strait and the southern islands also appear in the records as stopping points: Tang-bound court missions in the eighth century sometimes took a southern route via islands south of Kyushu, and the monk Ganjin's difficult voyage to Japan in 753 is recorded as having touched at islands in the Nansei chain, including Okinawa, on its way. These islands, whose peoples appear in the records as visitors, tribute bearers, and navigators, had histories of their own that will be traced in Chapters 32 and 33; for now it is enough to note that Nara-period connections reached far south of the main islands and that the Nansei Islands were not simply an empty periphery.

Temples, Shrines, and the Provincial Presence of Buddhism

Chapter 15 traced the creation of the provincial temple system, in which each province was ordered to establish a provincial temple (kokubunji) and provincial nunnery. Seen from the provinces, these temples were among the largest and most visible institutions of the state: monumental buildings, often with pagodas, staffed by monks and nuns, supported by allotted land and households, and integrated into the ritual life of the province. Local elites also founded their own temples, sometimes preceding the state's program. Shrines to local kami remained central to ritual life, and their priesthoods were often held by the same families that held district offices. Temples and shrines thus served as both religious and social institutions where elite families displayed their status, households participated in ritual, and networks of patronage were formed.

The state's institutions created nodes, but networks extended beyond them. Kinship ties linked families across districts; craft workshops distributed goods regionally; monasteries and elite households held land and labor in several places; and communities on the frontier maintained their own routes of trade and exchange. The Nara state relied on this fabric while also trying to channel it. Historians must therefore avoid reading the map of provincial headquarters and official roads as if it were a map of everything that mattered.

Section 16.6

What Was a Frontier? Mutsu, Dewa, and the Northeastern Borderlands

Before turning to campaigns and conflicts, it is necessary to define the frontier analytically. A frontier in this context was not a fixed modern border. It was a zone: a broad, shifting region in which settlements, forts, agricultural colonization, trade, tribute, diplomacy, intermarriage, migration, and conflict were all present, and in which the presence and reach of the state varied by place and time. People living in the zone could be subject to state institutions in one decade and out of reach in the next; they could be part of court-recognized districts, allied communities, or independent communities, sometimes simultaneously and in different relationships to different actors.

Mutsu and Dewa

The two principal northeastern provinces of the Nara court were Mutsu (also called Michinoku), on the Pacific side, and Dewa, on the Sea of Japan side. Mutsu had been established in the seventh century as the court extended its reach northward, and it was a huge unit, encompassing much of what would later be northeastern Honshu. Dewa was carved out of Echigo in 712 and expanded in the following years, and its center was moved further north to the Akita region in 733 with the construction of a fortified administrative site there. These names refer to court-administered categories whose effective reach changed over time. The court might claim a whole province while actually controlling a few districts near forts and roads. Areas farther north remained outside this administration or connected to it only by tribute and exchange.

The northeast was not empty. It had communities engaged in agriculture, hunting, fishing, and horse-raising, with their own leaders and networks of exchange that reached to Hokkaido and the Sea of Japan. Archaeologists have documented a region of substantial variation: in southern Tōhoku, rice farming and elite burial mounds resembling those farther south spread during the Kofun period, and the population there included communities closely connected to the societies of the Kantō and beyond; in northern Tōhoku, cultural patterns with deeper roots in earlier hunter-gatherer traditions persisted, and wet-rice agriculture had a more limited presence. In Hokkaido, the Satsumon culture of farmers and hunters and the Okhotsk culture along the northeastern coast represent other traditions, relevant to later Ainu history, which will be traced in later chapters. Court sources sometimes portray this region as unified in its "barbarism"; archaeology shows diversity and continuous contact.

Tagajō: Fort, Office, and Colonial Center

The center of court power in Mutsu was the fortified administrative site of Tagajō, located in the region north of present-day Sendai, on a low hill overlooking a coastal plain. Founded in 724, it served as the seat of the provincial government of Mutsu and as the headquarters of the military office known as the Chinjufu, established to oversee military operations in the northeast. Excavations have uncovered a large rectangular enclosure surrounded by an earthen wall, with a central administrative compound, gates, storehouses, workshops, and residences, along with a surrounding settlement. Tagajō should not be imagined as a medieval-style castle. It was a planned administrative and military center of the ritsuryō state, combining the functions of provincial headquarters, military command post, communications hub, storage depot, and center of colonization.

Tagajō's construction and rebuilding show how the court invested in the frontier. Its establishment in 724 was credited to Ōno no Azumabito, a court official; in 762 the site was rebuilt by Fujiwara no Asakari, who had been engaged in fort construction across the region. The site's material remains, including roof tiles, wooden tablets, and ceramics, document a mix of imported styles from the capital and local production, and the presence of written records at Tagajō shows that the frontier participated in the state's administrative culture. It was also a place of contact and negotiation, where court officials, settlers, local leaders, and visitors from beyond the state's reach met.

Section 16.7

Who Were the Emishi?

The term Emishi comes overwhelmingly from court-produced sources, and it needs to be handled with great care. It is written in Chinese characters that reflect continental categories for peoples outside the civilized center, and in the Nihon Shoki and Shoku Nihongi it appears as a label applied by the court to communities in the northeast. It was not a name that these communities used for themselves as far as we know, and it was not the name of a unified nation, polity, or culture.

Historical Vocabulary
Emishi

Emishi (written 蝦夷; a later reading is ebisu) was a court term for peoples living in the northeast and beyond the court's direct administration. It functioned in part as a political and ideological category: those who resisted or stood outside the state's order were "Emishi," while those who submitted might be described with other terms, such as ifu and fushū, for Emishi drawn into relationships of submission or incorporation. Their meanings shifted over time and should not be reduced to a simple "submitted" versus "captive" distinction; in later usage, fushū generally denoted more fully incorporated Emishi than ifu. The same individual or community could be described differently at different moments.

The term should not be equated with "the Ainu," "the natives of Tōhoku," or "a tribe." It was applied to communities of diverse background, and its meaning shifted with the court's policy and knowledge. Historians use it because sources do, but must remember that it is an outsider's label embedded in a colonial relationship.

Diversity of Communities

Archaeological and documentary evidence indicates that the communities called Emishi were diverse. Some lived in settlements with agricultural economies and connections to the societies of the Kantō; others relied more heavily on hunting, fishing, and gathering. Some communities had leaders recognized by the court with titles and ranks, and others are known only as collective groups appearing at moments of conflict. Material culture varied across the region, with different pottery styles, burial practices, and settlement patterns. Northern Tōhoku and southern Tōhoku differed markedly, and coastal communities differed from inland ones.

Horses were significant in the northeast, and mounted warriors were an important part of the martial reputation of the region; the court also acquired horses, hawks, and animal pelts from northeastern communities through trade and tribute. Iron tools and weapons circulated through exchange and were produced in some places. Communities on the Sea of Japan coast were connected to networks that reached across the sea; a Balhae mission that landed on the coast of Dewa in 727 met a violent reception from local people, according to the chronicle, though its context is not fully clear. Contact with peoples farther north, including those of the Okhotsk and Satsumon cultures in Hokkaido, also shaped northeastern life. The Nihon Shoki records seventh-century voyages to the far north under the official Abe no Hirafu, in which the court's fleets encountered peoples whom the chronicle names differently from the Emishi; the identification of these peoples and places is debated.

Relationships with the Court

The relationship of Emishi communities to the court was not a simple binary of enemy and subject. Some Emishi leaders traveled to the capital to present tribute and participated in court ceremonies, where their presence enacted the court's claim to rule beyond its borders. Some accepted titles, ranks, and gifts; some moved into court-recognized districts and served as officials; some traded with settlers and officials; and some allied with the court against neighbors. Others resisted, withdrew, or attacked forts and officials. Communities could shift among these positions over time, and different communities in the same region could take different stances. Submission, rank-taking, and tribute should not be taken as proof of permanent assimilation, for they were often negotiated arrangements that either party could abandon.

Historical Debate

Who Were the Emishi?

The question: Were the Emishi an ethnic group distinct from the people of the court, perhaps related to the later Ainu, or were they a heterogeneous set of communities defined largely by their relationship to the state? What can language, archaeology, and texts tell us?

Interpretation A — Emishi as ancestors or relatives of the Ainu: An influential twentieth-century tradition, associated with scholars such as the linguist Kindaichi Kyōsuke, pointed to place names in the northeast with forms resembling Ainu words, and argued that the Emishi spoke an Ainu-related language. On this view, the Emishi were the ancestors or close relatives of the people who later developed Ainu culture in Hokkaido.
Interpretation B — Emishi as a political category applied to regional Japonic-speaking populations: Other historians and archaeologists have emphasized political and cultural continuity between northeastern communities and populations farther south, arguing that "Emishi" often functioned as a political and ideological status rather than a single ethnic identity.
Interpretation C — Composite and changing populations: Many current researchers regard both positions as too simple. They emphasize that the northeast contained a range of communities with varied origins, some with deep roots in local hunter-gatherer traditions, others with connections to southern agricultural societies, and some with links to Hokkaido cultures. Language, culture, and identity may have varied from place to place and shifted over time as migration, intermarriage, and political change reshaped the region.

Evidence: The debate draws on place names, the few personal names and terms recorded in court sources, archaeological cultures and burial practices, settlement patterns, and, more recently, studies of skeletal remains and ancient DNA, which remain limited and do not by themselves resolve questions of language or self-identification. Court texts are biased by their ideological categories, and archaeological "cultures" do not map neatly onto languages or identities.

Why it matters: How historians answer the question affects how they interpret the conflicts of the eighth and ninth centuries, whether as clashes between distinct peoples, as conflicts within a shared cultural sphere, or as something more complex; and it shapes how the histories of northern Honshu, Hokkaido, and the Ainu are related to one another. It also bears on modern politics, since claims about ancient identity have been used in modern debates about indigenous status and national history.

Emishi, Ainu, and the Danger of Equation

Because later Ainu communities lived in Hokkaido and parts of northern Honshu, and because Ainu-like place names appear in the northeast, it is tempting to treat the Emishi as "the Ainu of the Nara period." This equation is not supported by the evidence as a whole. Archaeologists commonly trace the emergence of cultural patterns conventionally called "Ainu culture" through transformations of Satsumon communities and interactions with Okhotsk and other northern traditions, especially around the twelfth through fourteenth centuries, although timing and regional pathways remain debated. This archaeological periodization should not be taken to mean that Ainu history simply began at that date; the people involved also interacted continually with communities to the north and south (Chapters 32 and 48 return to these questions). Some of the people called Emishi may have been ancestors of people who later contributed to Ainu communities, while others became part of the regional societies of northern Honshu. The safest course is to treat "Emishi" as a court category applied to varied peoples, and to treat their relationship to the Ainu as a subject of continuing research and debate.

Section 16.8

Expansion by Fort, Settlement, Alliance, and Incorporation

How did the court extend its reach into the northeast? The answer involves several mechanisms working together. Expansion did not consist simply of armies marching north and defeating an enemy. It combined military installations, agricultural settlement, road building, diplomacy, and the deliberate incorporation of local leaders into the court's system.

Forts and Administrative Centers

The court established fortified administrative and military positions, often on strategic sites near rivers or plains, that served as bases for governance, supply, and communication. In Mutsu, Tagajō was the principal center, complemented in the later eighth century by forts such as Momonoo and Iji. In Dewa, a fortified site at Akita was established in 733, and another fort at Ogachi was built later, around 759. In 737, the chronicle records an expedition led by Ōno no Azumabito to open a route linking Mutsu and Dewa across the mountain ranges, a project that required negotiation and the cooperation of local communities as well as force. Forts and roads created a skeleton of state presence, but the space between them remained contested and often only loosely under court control.

Settlement and the Movement of People

The court also encouraged or compelled the transfer of households from other provinces to the northeast. The Shoku Nihongi records the movement of substantial numbers of households from eastern provinces into Mutsu and Dewa during the eighth century, with figures such as a thousand households reported for some transfers. These settlers were to farm land, provide labor and supplies for forts, and reinforce the population loyal to the court. The transfers were often burdensome for the families concerned, uprooting them from home regions and placing them in unfamiliar and sometimes dangerous places. For some, the move may have offered land and opportunity; for others, it was compulsion. Frontier settlers also came into contact with local communities, and some intermarried and traded with them, so that settlement created new mixed populations as well as tensions over land and resources.

The northeast also drew people from other backgrounds. The governor of Mutsu who presented gold to the court in 749, Kudara no Kyōfuku, belonged to a lineage descended from the royal house of Baekje, the Korean kingdom that had fallen in the seventh century and whose refugees had been settled in Japan; his career illustrates how migrants and their descendants participated in the court's frontier system. The discovery of gold in Mutsu was significant: gold from the region contributed to the gilding of the Great Buddha at Tōdaiji, connecting the frontier directly to the capital's most prestigious religious project (Chapter 15). Gold, horses, and other resources made the northeast economically valuable to the court and gave it reasons to secure and expand its presence.

Alliance, Rank, and Incorporation

Crucially, the court did not rely solely on force. It bestowed ranks, titles, and gifts on frontier leaders, appointed some as district officials or recognized their standing through kabane titles such as kimi, and integrated them into the administrative framework. The gunji of frontier districts might include people of local origin who had been brought into the court's system, and some rose to considerable standing. In this way, the court used the same mechanism it used elsewhere in the provinces, mediating rule through local elites, but with greater ideological tension because those elites were labeled as Emishi or as former Emishi.

The court also exploited divisions among frontier groups. Communities that cooperated could gain protection, trade opportunities, or advantage against rivals; communities that resisted risked attack. Some frontier leaders benefited from the alliance and pursued their own aims within it. This dynamic complicates the binary of "court versus Emishi." Expansion worked in part because some northeastern actors chose to cooperate with the state or to use it against rivals, and because the court adapted its methods to local conditions. It also meant that alliance was contingent: an incorporated leader could become a rebel if the arrangement ceased to serve his interests or if he was treated with contempt by court officials.

Frontiers Elsewhere: Hayato and the South

The northeast was not the only frontier. In southern Kyushu, the court had created provinces such as Ōsumi (713) and worked to bring Hayato communities under registration and taxation. In 720, following the killing of an official, the court sent a large force under Ōtomo no Tabito, and the campaign took months; the episode shows that frontier resistance was a wider phenomenon in the Nara state. The court's approach in the south, as in the north, combined force, settlement, and administrative extension. Attention to both frontiers shows that the ritsuryō state's territorial ambitions were pursued in multiple directions and encountered resistance in more than one place, and that the northeast should be understood as part of a broader pattern of state-building rather than as a uniquely "ethnic" conflict.

Section 16.9

Resistance and the Crisis of the Late Nara Frontier

By the middle of the eighth century, the court had invested heavily in the northeast, yet its control remained fragile. Resistance took varied forms: attacks on officials and forts, refusal to supply labor or tribute, flight, and negotiation. The Shoku Nihongi records episodes of violence from the early decades of the century, including violence against officials in the northeast around 720 and further unrest in the 720s, and the court responded with expeditions, fort building, and settlement. The late eighth century saw an escalation, as the court pushed to extend the reach of fortified administration northward and westward and as pressure on local communities grew.

Pressure and Response in the Late Eighth Century

In the 750s and 760s, under the direction of officials such as Fujiwara no Asakari, the court built or rebuilt forts, including Momonoo and Ogachi, and reorganized frontier administration. The Iji fort was established in the later 760s. These projects required labor, supplies, and settlers, and they extended the court's claims into areas where local communities had their own interests. The expansion increased friction. Settlers and officials competed with local people over land; demands for labor and provisions burdened communities inside and outside the court's framework; and frontier officials, as in other provinces, could exploit their positions.

In 774, according to the chronicle, Emishi attacked the fort at Momonoo, prompting a military response. Some modern historians take this event as the beginning of a prolonged period of conflict that lasted, with interruptions, until 811, and they call it the "Thirty-Eight Years' War." The label is a modern convenience and should be used cautiously: it implies continuous warfare, whereas the actual pattern combined episodes of intense fighting with periods of negotiation, and the court's own campaigns were sometimes limited by cost and resistance at home. The years after 774 saw further campaigns and the strain of maintaining frontier forces. Chapter 17 will take up the campaigns of the early Heian period, when the court under Emperor Kanmu launched larger expeditions.

Iji no Azamaro and the Revolt of 780

The revolt of 780 offers the most instructive single case of frontier instability, precisely because its leader had been part of the court's system. Iji no Azamaro was a magistrate of the district of Iji in Mutsu, of northeastern local origin, who had received court recognition in the form of office and rank and bore the kabane/title Kimi. In the third month of 780, according to the Shoku Nihongi, Azamaro turned against the officials with whom he had been working. He killed Ki no Hirozumi, the senior court official in Mutsu, and Michishima no Ōtate, an assistant governor, and his forces then attacked Tagajō, looting and burning it. The attack destroyed the court's principal northeastern center and shook the frontier order.

The chronicle's account of motive is instructive but must be read critically. It attributes the killing partly to personal grievance: Ōtate, who was himself reported to be of northeastern local origin but who held higher standing, is said to have treated Azamaro with contempt as an Emishi. If accurate, this suggests that the frontier system contained hierarchies within the ranks of local elites, and that the boundary between "Emishi" and "court official" was policed by officials who were themselves of frontier origin. It also suggests that ranks and offices did not guarantee respect. Yet the chronicle was compiled under a court that had every reason to present the revolt as the act of a treacherous individual rather than as a response to systemic pressures. Historians therefore consider both personal grievance and structural causes, including the burdens of forts, the demands for military participation, and the loss of autonomy, as possible contributing factors, without treating the chronicle's explanation as the whole story.

Azamaro's career is pedagogically valuable because it undermines the simple image of a "native resistance leader." He was an incorporated local elite who had served within the court's frontier order before he rebelled against it. His revolt demonstrates that incorporation and resistance could exist within the same frontier system, and that alliance was contingent on treatment and interest. The court sent forces to restore order, and the frontier remained unsettled. Shortly afterward, in 781, Kanmu became emperor, and the frontier problem passed to a new political generation whose capital and priorities would soon change. The full story of the campaigns that followed, including the leaders of the Emishi resistance and the court's commanders, belongs to Chapter 17, after the Heian political setting has been established.

Reading the Chronicle of Frontier Conflict

The Shoku Nihongi is the main narrative source for these events, and its limitations must be kept in view. It was compiled by court officials, completed in 797 under Emperor Kanmu, whose government was itself directing frontier policy. Its terminology, including labels such as "rebels" and "barbarians," expresses the court's viewpoint. Its attention is selective: it records events that mattered to the court and often omits the perspectives of frontier communities. At the same time, it preserves names, dates, and details that allow historians to reconstruct events, and archaeology at sites such as Tagajō and other forts provides an independent check. The sources describe the frontier from the standpoint of the conquering court; the language of "submission," "rebellion," and "pacification" is evidence about court ideology as well as about events.

Cause and Effect

Escalation on the Northeastern Frontier

Long-term causes

The ritsuryō state's territorial ideology, modeled on continental empires, treated peoples beyond its administration as barbarians to be incorporated; the court sought resources, including gold, horses, and land; and centuries of contact and exchange had created intertwined but unequal relationships between the northeast and the south.

Short-term causes

The building of forts and roads, transfers of settlers, demands for labor and supplies, competition over land, mistreatment by officials, tensions within frontier elites, and the strain of epidemic and fiscal pressure in the wider state.

Event or transformation

From the 720s to the 780s, the court extended fortified administration northward and westward, while episodes of resistance, including the attack on Momonoo in 774 and the revolt of Iji no Azamaro in 780, exposed the fragility of the frontier order.

Immediate effects

The destruction of Tagajō, the killing of senior officials, the mobilization of further forces, and a hardening of court attitudes that increased the scale and cost of frontier campaigns.

Long-term consequences

A prolonged period of conflict and negotiation that reshaped the northeast, the resettlement of some northeastern people elsewhere, the strengthening of military households and provincial elites, and questions about the state's capacity that fed into the political restructuring of the late eighth and early ninth centuries.

Historical complexity

The expansion was not inevitable. It depended on court decisions, local alliances and rivalries, economic incentives, and contingencies. It was not simply an ethnic war, since some people of frontier origin served the court and some of the court's own subjects bore its burdens.

Historical complexity: No single cause explains frontier escalation. Institutions, ideology, resource competition, individual decisions, and the actions of frontier communities all shaped the outcome, and none of them made the eventual course of events inevitable.

Section 16.10

How Far Did the Nara State Really Reach?

The chapter's central question can now be answered in geographical terms. Near the Kinai, court institutions were dense: officials, temples, markets, roads, and taxation were closely integrated with the capital, and the court's reach into households was strongest. Farther out, in the provinces of central and western Honshu and in Kyushu, authority depended increasingly on governors, district elites, routes, temples, and negotiation. In the eastern provinces, the state's reach was substantial and burdensome, especially in the demand for soldiers, labor, and settlers, but it still depended on local elites. On the northeastern frontier, the state's claims often exceeded its practical control, and its presence took the form of forts, roads, and settlements within a larger landscape of communities with their own relationships and interests. In southern Kyushu and the southern islands, the court's presence was similarly partial and negotiated.

The answer to "how far did the state reach?" should therefore not be "it failed" or "it unified Japan." The Nara state built a consequential but uneven territorial order. It created registers, provincial centers, roads, and taxes that shaped provincial life for centuries. It enlisted local elites into a shared political system that gave them new resources and new constraints. It expanded its institutions into the northeast, transforming the region through settlement, trade, warfare, and alliance. But it also depended on the cooperation of those whom it ruled, and it faced resistance, evasion, and local autonomy at every level. Its expansion created new relationships, identities, inequalities, and conflicts.

These developments had lasting consequences. The provincial elites who mediated between court and countryside would, in later centuries, become increasingly independent, building estates and armed followings (Chapters 20 and 21). The military burdens on eastern households and the experience of frontier conflict contributed to the rise of specialized military families. The northeastern frontier remained a zone of exchange and conflict, and the people called Emishi, some of whom were relocated or absorbed, continued to shape the history of northern Honshu. The frontier's history also intersected with the history of Hokkaido and the Ainu, whose relation to the earlier peoples of the north is a matter of continuing research. And the memory of the Nara state's ambitions, expressed in law, ideology, and monuments, would remain a resource for later rulers.

By the 780s, the state was powerful but strained. The revolt of 780, the costs of frontier maintenance, the demographic and fiscal pressures on the allotment system, and the political tensions at court together created conditions in which the emperor's generation looked for new arrangements. Chapter 17 turns from the provinces back to the center, asking why the late-Nara order was reorganized around a new capital, and how Emperor Kanmu and his advisers sought to address both the internal politics of the court and the unfinished problem of the northeast.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the Nara state from the ground and from its edges, reversing the capital-centered perspective of Chapters 14 and 15. Its central argument is that Nara rule was real but uneven. Registers, taxes, governors, district officials, roads, temples, forts, and military expeditions extended the court's authority far beyond Heijō, but the court did not govern every village directly. It depended on hereditary local elites, household production, negotiated compliance, and regional networks, and at the northeastern frontier its claims often outran its capacity.

Evidence for provincial life comes from court chronicles, law codes, household registers, wooden tablets, gazetteers, inscribed monuments, poetry, and archaeology, each with distinctive limits. Court sources describe the provinces from above and in the language of the state; the voices of farmers, women, unfree persons, and northeastern communities survive mainly as traces. Historians must combine these sources and read them critically.

Rural society was overwhelmingly agricultural but diverse. Households combined rice and dry-field farming with weaving, fishing, salt making, and other specialized production, and the household, not an abstract "peasantry," was the basic unit of labor and taxation. Labor was gendered, with men bearing the heaviest military and labor obligations and women producing textiles essential to both household and state. Unfree persons, epidemics, famines, and the uneven fertility of land shaped life chances.

Taxation and registration imposed real costs. The grain tax was nominally modest, but obligations in goods, labor, transport, conscription, and frontier service were heavy and unevenly distributed. Households responded with evasion, flight, and dependence on powerful patrons; epidemics and migration disrupted the allotment ideal; and reforms encouraging land reclamation opened a path toward later estates. Legal prescription and actual collection diverged, and coinage and cash played a limited role in rural life.

Provincial governance was co-governed and mediated. Governors came from the capital for limited terms, while district magistrates (gunji) were drawn from locally rooted families, many descended from earlier regional rulers. The court needed their local knowledge, labor connections, wealth, and prestige, and rewarded them with rank and office. Provincial elites were neither simply agents nor simply victims of the state; they were negotiators who used the system for their own advantage while also constraining it.

The provinces were connected by planned headquarters, district offices, storehouses, roads, post stations, temples, and shrines, but also by coastal routes, kinship, craft networks, and exchange that operated beyond direct state control. Provincial temples brought the state's religious institutions into the countryside, while local temples and shrines continued to express elite status and community ritual. The southern islands appear as points of navigation and contact, reminding us that the Nara world extended beyond the main islands.

The northeastern frontier was a zone, not a line. Mutsu and Dewa were court-administered categories whose effective reach shifted, and Tagajō was an administrative and military center rather than a medieval castle. The people the court called Emishi were diverse communities whose relations with the state ranged from trade and alliance to resistance; the term is a court label and not the name of a single ethnic group or polity, and it should not be equated with the later Ainu. Historians disagree about Emishi languages and identities. Many current researchers favor composite or changing models, while important uncertainties remain.

Expansion worked through forts, roads, settler transfers, alliances, rank, and gifts. It drew on eastern households for labor and settlers, on Baekje-descended and other officials for administration, and on frontier leaders willing to cooperate. It was motivated in part by ideology and in part by the desire for resources such as gold and horses. It also produced resistance, most dramatically in the revolt of Iji no Azamaro in 780, whose career shows how incorporation and resistance could coexist within the same system.

Frontier resistance was not limited to the northeast. Hayato communities in southern Kyushu also resisted the extension of provincial administration, and the court responded with military force. Attention to these parallel frontiers, and to Balhae contacts on the Sea of Japan coast, situates the northeastern story within a broader pattern of state-building and regional diversity.

By the 780s the Nara state ruled diverse regions imperfectly. It had built a consequential but uneven territorial order whose expansion created new relationships, inequalities, and conflicts, and it was strained by fiscal, demographic, and frontier pressures. The chapter therefore rejects both the idea that the state failed and the idea that it unified Japan, and it hands Chapter 17 a political order poised for reorganization.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows that the Nara state cannot be understood from the capital alone. A state is not only its laws, offices, and monuments but also the practices by which it reaches people, the intermediaries who carry its authority, and the limits at which that authority meets negotiation and resistance. Seeing the provinces reveals the households whose labor sustained the court, the local elites who made governance possible, and the frontier communities who shaped the boundaries of the state as much as the state shaped them.

The chapter also reveals larger processes that will recur throughout this textbook: the dependence of central authority on regional intermediaries, the tension between legal ideals and lived practice, and the ways frontier expansion produces both incorporation and conflict. Later chapters will trace how provincial elites and military households gained independence, how estates transformed the land system, and how the northeastern frontier remained a zone of exchange and contest. The Emishi, the Ainu, and the peoples of southern Kyushu and the southern islands must remain visible in those later stories as historical actors, not as background to a national narrative.

The chapter connects different regions of the archipelago and the wider world. Eastern households supplied soldiers and settlers for the northeast; Baekje-descended officials served in Mutsu; gold from the north gilded a Buddha in Nara; Balhae ships reached the Sea of Japan coast; and voyagers touched islands south of Kyushu. These connections show that the Nara world was a network of regions with their own histories, not a single center with an empty periphery.

Finally, the chapter invites careful thinking about the language of sources and the narratives built from them. To call the northeastern expansion the "unification of Japan" is to read the past through a later national lens; to call Iji no Azamaro simply a rebel is to adopt the court's own vocabulary. Students who learn to ask who produced a source, for what purpose, and whose voices are missing are prepared for Chapter 17, where the political center reorganizes itself around a new capital while the unresolved problems of the provinces and the northeast remain.
