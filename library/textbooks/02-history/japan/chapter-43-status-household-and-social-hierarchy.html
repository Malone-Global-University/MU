Part VII — Tokugawa Japan
43

Status, Household, and Social Hierarchy

Japanese History: From Jōmon Japan to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

In village offices, town wards, temples, and domain castles across Tokugawa Japan, households were written down: who headed the house, who lived in it, how old each member was, what relationship each bore to the head, and which institution vouched for them. These registers are plain, repetitive, and easy to overlook. Yet they reveal the unit through which early modern Japanese society actually organized itself: not the isolated individual, and not a simple pyramid of four classes, but the household embedded in a status group.

Chapter 42 described the political architecture of the Tokugawa order: the shogun and his bakufu, the domains, the daimyō, and an imperial court whose symbolic authority the warrior government carefully managed. That chapter ended by handing the story to the people who lived inside that structure. This chapter turns to them. Its question is how people were positioned within the Tokugawa order, what that position meant for their work, their obligations, their marriages, their prospects, and their dealings with authority, and how a society that by the early eighteenth century numbered roughly thirty million people was sorted, governed, and in many respects held together through hereditary status and household membership.

Many readers have met a simple answer to that question: a four-tier pyramid with warriors at the top, then farmers, artisans, and merchants, and a group of “outcastes” beneath them. This schema, often summarized as shi–nō–kō–shō, is not invented; it was a real ideological vocabulary of the period. But recent scholarship has shown that it is a poor map of how Tokugawa society worked. Large populations fit nowhere within it. The four categories were not a single nationwide legal caste system. Wealth and rank often diverged sharply. And people met government mainly through locally organized groups, such as retainer bands, villages, town neighborhoods, temples, occupational associations, and discriminated-status communities, rather than as members of four abstract classes. One of this chapter’s tasks is therefore to teach the older model and then show why it is inadequate.

The chapter’s central argument is that Tokugawa society was deeply hierarchical, but that status was produced through households, occupations, communities, privileges, obligations, and relationships with governing authorities. Samurai, villagers, artisans, merchants, religious specialists, servants, entertainers, and discriminated groups occupied unequal and sometimes overlapping positions. Gender and household membership shaped the opportunities available within every status. The resulting order was restrictive, but it was neither uniform across regions nor completely immobile.

Two organizing ideas run through the chapter. The first is status (mibun), used here as the primary analytical concept in place of modern socioeconomic “class.” The second is the household (ie), a continuing corporate unit that could include blood relatives, adopted heirs, servants, property, an occupation, a name, and obligations stretching across generations. The chapter gives extended attention to how households were formed, extended, and perpetuated through marriage and adoption, and to how gender operated within every status rather than as a separate hierarchy beside them.

Sections on the discriminated-status communities require particular care. Communities labeled with terms such as kawata, chōri, and the discriminatory label eta were a distinct category from hinin, who were typically beggars, performers, and people consigned to marginal or penal functions. Neither category may simply be identified with the modern Burakumin. Modern Buraku discrimination grew historically out of, and through transformations of, earlier discriminated statuses; it should not be read backward as an unchanged Tokugawa caste. The Ainu of Ezo and the people of the Ryukyu Kingdom, who stood outside the bakufu’s status-and-registration arrangements and in Ryukyu’s case had a status order of their own, remain visible as reminders that the “Tokugawa system” was not the whole archipelago.

Historians reconstruct this world from household and religious-inspection registers, village and ward documents, domain and bakufu legislation, petitions, litigation records, diaries, household precepts, merchant house rules, moral and instructional books, and the inconsistent records of the discriminated communities themselves. Each has limits, and each represents the viewpoint of the people who wrote it. The chapter observes careful boundaries with its neighbors: Chapter 44 explains village government, agriculture, taxation, famine, and protest; Chapter 45 covers cities, merchant houses, and finance; Chapter 46 treats pleasure districts and theater; Chapter 47 examines religious institutions; and Chapter 49 turns to crafts and everyday cultural life. Here the subject is status and household as organizing principles of society.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why historians use “status” (mibun) rather than modern economic class as the primary concept for Tokugawa society.
  2. Describe the shi–nō–kō–shō model and evaluate why historians no longer treat it as a complete description of Tokugawa society.
  3. Analyze how status operated through privileges, obligations, occupation, residence, and membership in locally organized groups.
  4. Explain the ie as a continuing corporate household and trace the roles of marriage, adoption, and inheritance in its perpetuation.
  5. Compare the social positions of samurai, hyakushō, artisans, and townspeople, including internal hierarchy within each.
  6. Distinguish status from wealth, using the contrast between impoverished samurai and prosperous commoners.
  7. Assess how gender shaped experience within each status group, distinguishing prescriptive ideals from documented practice.
  8. Interpret the evidence for servants, apprentices, and dependents and assess the extent and limits of mobility.
  9. Distinguish kawata/chōri communities, hinin, and modern Burakumin, and explain why collapsing them is historically misleading.
  10. Contextualize the later Buraku problem as historical continuity and transformation rather than simple identity.
  11. Analyze household registers and similar sources for what they reveal and conceal about status.
  12. Connect status and household organization to the village, urban, cultural, and religious topics of Chapters 44–49.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why is “status” a more useful concept than “class” for understanding Tokugawa society, and what does the contrast between rank and wealth reveal?
  2. Why did the four-status model persist as an ideology, and why do historians now regard it as an inadequate description of society?
  3. In what ways did governments deal with households and status groups rather than with individuals?
  4. How could a household continue across generations without biological descent, and what does adoption reveal about the meaning of kinship?
  5. How did the meaning of samurai status change when sustained warfare ended, and how unequal was the samurai estate internally?
  6. How did gender operate differently within samurai, rural, artisan, and merchant households, and how should historians weigh moral prescription against practice?
  7. How did hereditary status coexist with labor mobility, adoption, and wealth crossing status lines?
  8. What distinguished kawata/chōri communities from hinin, and why does the later Burakumin identity not map neatly onto either?

Historical Context

The social order described in this chapter took shape in the decades around 1600. Sengoku warfare (Chapters 35–36) had blurred lines between fighters and cultivators: village households supplied soldiers, warriors lived on the land, and armed local magnates negotiated with lords. Oda Nobunaga and, above all, Toyotomi Hideyoshi (Chapters 39–40) attacked that ambiguity through land surveys, the 1588 Sword Hunt, and an edict of 1591 aimed at freezing people’s occupational positions. The usual shorthand for the resulting policy is the separation of warriors from cultivators (heinō bunri). Historians stress that it was uneven in practice, and in some domains much slower, but its general direction was clear: those who bore arms and served lords were to be distinguished, in law and residence, from those who farmed and paid dues.

The Tokugawa settlement that followed Sekigahara (Chapter 41) and the bakuhan system of Chapter 42 inherited and extended this logic. Most domain retainers were gathered into castle towns and paid stipends rather than living directly off village land; villages were organized as units responsible to the authorities; towns were organized by neighborhoods; and the long peace removed the main justification for a society of fighting men while preserving the hereditary privileges of those descended from them. The central Tokugawa project of political stability depended on knowing who people were, where they lived, and to which group and household they belonged. That is why registration, in its many forms, mattered so much.

The century after 1600 also brought rapid economic and demographic expansion, followed by slower growth and regional divergence. Population rose from perhaps twelve to eighteen million around 1600 to roughly thirty million by the early eighteenth century, with later stagnation or regional decline in some areas. Commercial agriculture, rural manufacture, long-distance trade, and urbanization made wealth far more fluid than the formal status order suggested. This tension, between a status order built on ideals of fixed function and an economy producing new wealth and new kinds of work, is one of the chapter’s recurring themes. The detailed economic story belongs to Chapters 44 and 45; here the focus is on how people occupying different statuses lived within households and groups.

A final point of context concerns evidence. Tokugawa society produced an unusual volume of documentation: bakufu and domain legislation, collections of precedents, household and temple registers, and large village and town archives. Yet much of this record was created by or for those governing, and the voices of servants, women, and discriminated communities are comparatively indirect. The discussion of discriminated communities, in particular, relies partly on records produced by authorities and neighbors who held them in contempt. Students should read every section with this question in mind: whose perspective is represented in the evidence, and whose is missing?

Section 43.1

From Political Order to Social Order

The bakuhan system of Chapter 42 created an arena of power: a shogunate that governed directly some of the richest lands, hundreds of domains under daimyō, and an imperial court preserved as a source of legitimacy. But a political structure is not yet a society. Someone had to staff the offices, farm the land, supply the castle towns, build and repair, carry goods, keep accounts, and care for children and the aged. The question of this chapter is how those people were positioned and how the position was reproduced from one generation to the next.

The answer begins with a choice of concept. Modern readers tend to think of society in terms of economic class: the rich, the middle, the poor. Tokugawa society cannot be read that way without distortion. A samurai in a small domain might hold a stipend barely adequate to feed his family, borrow from townspeople, and send his wife and children to do piecework, yet he ranked above a wealthy merchant who owned warehouses and lent to daimyō. Rank was attached to status (mibun): a position defined by hereditary membership in a household and group, by legal privileges and duties, by occupation, and by relationship to those who governed. Wealth could improve a family’s comfort and influence, but it did not by itself alter its status.

This distinction does not mean economics was irrelevant. It means the two systems, status and wealth, ran on different principles and could pull against each other. Much of the social tension and dynamism of the period arises in the gap between them. Students should keep both in mind throughout the chapter and be alert to cases where they diverge.

Section 43.2

The “Four Statuses”: Useful Model, Misleading Pyramid

Any account of Tokugawa society must reckon with shi–nō–kō–shō: warriors, cultivators, artisans, merchants. The sequence derived from the older Chinese vocabulary of the “four occupations” and circulated in early modern Japan as a way of describing socially useful functions. Warriors unquestionably constituted the ruling status, and some moral writers disparaged commercial profit while praising agricultural production. But the sequence did not constitute a uniform nationwide legal ranking of cultivators above artisans and merchants; outside the ruling warrior status, cultivators, artisans, and merchants did not form a simple legal hierarchy among themselves. The vocabulary mattered as ideology and moral argument, and it helped justify warrior rule and the fiscal importance of rice-producing villages, but it should not be read as a ladder.

But it is a model of ideology, not a census. The four categories were never enacted as a single nationwide legal system of four castes. Large numbers of people did not fit: court nobles, Buddhist clergy and Shinto priests, physicians, scholars, performers, fishers, itinerant workers, servants, and the discriminated-status communities discussed in later sections. Even within the four, boundaries blurred. Many villagers were also craftspeople or small traders; many “merchants” were artisans who sold what they made; and warriors ranged from lords of vast domains to men nearly indistinguishable in daily circumstances from commoners. The familiar pyramid diagram owes much to later interpretation, including Meiji-era writing and twentieth-century historiography that emphasized a feudal class structure, and recent scholarship has moved away from it.

This chapter therefore treats the four statuses as something to be taught and then interrogated. Students need the vocabulary because Tokugawa people used it and because it shaped policy and ideas about worth. But they should also understand why historians now describe a more complicated landscape of locally organized status groups. The next section introduces the concept that replaces the pyramid.

Historical Vocabulary
Mibun (status)

Mibun, usually translated “status,” is the term historians use for a person’s hereditary position in the social order, comprising the privileges, duties, occupation, residence, and group membership attached to his or her household. Tokugawa documents speak in a variety of vocabularies, often naming a person by group (villager, townsman, retainer of a particular lord) rather than abstractly by rank, so mibun is best understood as a modern analytical tool for patterns Tokugawa people knew well.

Why it matters: “Class” suggests wealth and income. “Status” explains why a poor samurai could rank above a rich commoner, and why people were governed through groups and households rather than as individuals.

Section 43.3

Status as Privilege, Obligation, Occupation, and Community

If status was not a rung on a ladder, what was it? It is best understood as a bundle. A status could carry rights to certain forms of dress, weapons, or surnames; entitlement to stipends or, conversely, liability to dues and labor service; permission or prohibition to hold particular occupations; a defined place of residence within a castle town, ward, or village; membership in a group with its own leaders and internal discipline; and a particular relationship to the authorities who judged disputes and imposed obligations. Samurai, for example, had a hereditary claim to service and stipend and the privilege of wearing two swords. Villagers carried obligations to the authorities through their village. Townspeople paid for their ward’s administration and incurred obligations such as service or fees. Discriminated communities owed specific duties and suffered specific restrictions.

Crucially, people usually met government through groups and households. A samurai belonged to a lord’s retainer band and a house. A villager belonged to a village responsible collectively for its dues. A townsman belonged to a ward. Temples, occupational associations, and the organizations of discriminated groups played comparable roles. Authorities preferred to deal with the head of a group or household rather than with scattered individuals, and groups often had sufficient internal authority to enforce rules, settle disputes, and present petitions. This is why recent scholarship describes Tokugawa governance as relying on locally specific status groups, such as retainer bands, villages, neighborhood associations, monastic communities, guilds, and discriminated-status associations, whose positions depended on occupation, privilege, duty, household, and registration.

Two consequences follow. First, status was plural and local: the content of a given status varied by domain, region, and period, and many people held positions that combined features of several categories. Second, status was relational: it existed in the relationships between groups and authorities. Historians therefore examine not only the rules but how people negotiated them, through petitions, litigation, adoption, patronage, and the quiet adjustment of practice.

Historical Debate

Was Tokugawa Japan Really a Four-Class Society?

The question: Did the four-status model describe the real structure of Tokugawa society, or did it mask a more complicated order? What difference does the answer make for how students understand hierarchy, mobility, and the discriminated communities?

Interpretation A — The four-status schema as a fundamental structure: Older scholarship, including much twentieth-century writing, treated warriors, peasants, artisans, and merchants as the main classes of a feudal society, with discriminated groups beneath them. Defenders of this view point to the prominence of the schema in Confucian writing, to the real legal and fiscal separation of warriors from villagers, and to the hereditary character of most statuses.
Interpretation B — Locally organized status groups: Newer scholarship emphasizes that governance worked through many specific groups: retainer bands, villages, town wards, temples, guilds, and discriminated-status organizations. Defenders note that significant populations were outside the four categories, that occupations overlapped, that practice varied by domain, and that no single nationwide system of four legal castes existed.

Evidence: Both draw on legislation, registers, and contemporary writing. A depends heavily on ideological texts and general legal separations; B depends on local registers, petitions, and the observed diversity of people and groups. Many historians now treat the four-status vocabulary as important but partial, without denying that hierarchy was real.

Why it matters: The answer determines whether students imagine a frozen caste pyramid or a hierarchical but locally varied and negotiated order. It also affects how the discriminated communities are understood: as a bottom layer of a four-tier system or as one part of a broader field of groups with different statuses.

Student task: Choose one status group discussed in this chapter and explain whether it is better understood within the four-status model or within the “locally organized group” model. Identify one additional kind of evidence you would want.

Section 43.4

The Ie: Household Before Individual

The second foundation of Tokugawa society was the ie, usually translated “house” or “household.” English offers no exact equivalent, because the ie was neither simply a residence nor a nuclear family. It was a continuing corporate body: a unit with a name, an occupation or social function, property, a place in a status group, an ancestral tradition, and obligations extending across generations. Members could include the head and spouse, their children, the head’s retired parents, other relatives, an adopted heir, and sometimes servants, employees, and apprentices who lived within the household’s economic life. Not every member was related by blood, and not every kin member counted as part of the house in the same way.

The central idea is that the continuity of the house could be more important than biological descent. What had to be preserved was the occupation, property, name, and standing of the house: the farm and its village membership, the shop and its reputation, the samurai stipend and its attached service. A house without a suitable heir faced extinction, which meant the loss of livelihood, status, and the ritual attention that descendants owed to ancestors. A household’s head was in some respects its custodian: he was responsible for handing the house on in good order, and in many circumstances was expected to subordinate personal preference to that duty.

Empirical research on household registers cautions against overstating the ideal of the multigenerational “stem family.” Village studies by historical demographers have found that average household sizes were often modest, commonly around four to six people, with significant regional variation, and that many households were small, reflecting high mortality, late marriage in some areas, and the movement of young people into service elsewhere. The ideal of the continuing house and the demographic reality of small, fragile households coexisted, and the tension between them helps explain the importance of adoption.

Finally, the ie bound the living to the dead. Over the Tokugawa period, household ancestral rites, tied to Buddhist temple affiliation (Chapter 47), became increasingly widespread, reinforcing the sense that a house was a lineage of past, present, and future members. This sense of the house as a long-lived entity is the proper background for understanding why Tokugawa people placed such emphasis on succession, and why the same logic that mattered to the Tokugawa dynasty and to daimyō houses in Chapters 41–42 operated at every level of society.

Historical Vocabulary
Ie (household)

The ie was a continuing household that held a name, property, an occupation or office, a place in a village, ward, or retainer band, and responsibility for ancestral rites. Its head, successor, spouse, other kin, and dependents formed a social and economic unit that outlived any individual member. It could continue through an adopted heir.

Caution: The ie of the Tokugawa period should not be confused with the more formally codified “ie system” of the Meiji Civil Code of 1898, which gave legal definition to a patriarchal household headship in a modern state. Tokugawa practice was more varied by status, region, and period.

Section 43.5

Marriage, Adoption, Inheritance, and Household Continuity

Because the aim was to continue the house, marriage and adoption were management tools as much as personal relationships. Marriages commonly linked households of broadly comparable standing rather than merely uniting two individuals, and families, go-betweens, and in some cases lords or officials took part in arranging them. Among the samurai, marriage between houses of different domain or rank, and especially with commoners, could require formal approval or be handled through devices such as having the bride adopted into an appropriate samurai house first. Such formalities show how status and household reinforced each other.

Adoption (yōshi) was extremely widespread. A household lacking a son might adopt a nephew, a younger son of a related house, or an unrelated young man. A common form was the adopted son-in-law (mukoyōshi), who married the head’s daughter and carried on the house. Merchant houses frequently adopted a capable senior employee as successor, since business continuity depended on competence. The adopted heir took on the house’s name, duties, and ancestors, and was expected to treat its history as his own. This practice reveals an ethic different from modern emphasis on bloodline: a house could be “legitimately” continued by someone unrelated by blood.

The state shaped the process. Because warrior houses were units of service, the bakufu and domains regulated succession among them, requiring notification and approval of heirs. Restrictions on adoption made by a dying head on his deathbed (matsugo yōshi) could leave houses without heirs. In 1651 the shogunate relaxed these restrictions among its direct retainers through revision of the Shoshi hatto. The change influenced broader warrior practice as well, although older explanations that treat it simply as a daimyō reform, or as a direct response to the Keian Incident, have been questioned. The same logic of preserving houses applied less formally in villages and towns, where community authorities, relatives, and neighbors helped manage successions that would otherwise strand a household.

Not all children could inherit. Practices varied: many places favored the eldest son, while some regions preferred other heirs. Younger sons and daughters had to find alternatives. Some were adopted into other houses; some married out; some entered temples, apprenticeships, or service. Among samurai, a second or third son without prospects might remain as a dependent in his elder brother’s household, sometimes called a “room-dweller” (heyazumi). Retirement (inkyo) allowed a head to hand the house to a successor while remaining within it, providing for transition and care of the aged. Together these practices ensured that the house, not the individual, was the enduring social unit.

Primary Source Window

The Nishijō Village Religious-Inspection Registers (Shūmon aratamechō), Mino Province

Creator: Village officials of Nishijō, especially the village headman, with assistance from local Buddhist clergy, under the Tokugawa religious-inspection and population-registration system · Date: 1773–1869, an uninterrupted annual series used by the historical demographer Hayami Akira · Place: Nishijō, Anpachi District, Mino Province (present-day Gifu Prefecture) · Administrative context: shogunal land (tenryō) whose administration was entrusted to the Ōgaki domain (from 1748) · Type: Annual religious-inspection and population register (shūmon aratamechō)

Context: Under the religious-inspection system (discussed in Chapter 47), communities were required to report their households each year. In Nishijō the resulting registers survive as an unusually continuous series, which allowed Hayami Akira and other historical demographers to follow households from year to year. This feature paraphrases the structure of the registers and the uses scholars have made of them; it does not quote particular entries, and students should consult Hayami’s published studies for specific cases.

Paraphrase of the register’s structure (not a verbatim quotation): Each year’s register lists the village’s households, giving for each the head and the other members, their ages and relationships, and the household’s temple affiliation. Comparing one year with the next reveals births, deaths, marriages, adoptions, and members leaving to work elsewhere.

Analysis: A continuous series of this kind lets historians study household size and composition, succession, adoption, female household headship, and the movement of young people in and out of service, the very patterns discussed in this chapter. Its limits are real. It covers one village in central Honshu and cannot stand for every region. It records residents as authorities defined them, so people who had migrated away, samurai households, and groups recorded separately are missing. And it is an official document shaped by its compilers’ purposes: the experience of servants, women, and the poor appears only indirectly.

  2. Who created it, and for what possible purposes?
  3. What can historians learn from a continuous series about adoption, succession, or servants?
  4. What can historians not establish from one village’s registers, and whose perspective is missing?
Section 43.6

Samurai: A Hereditary Status of Service

Samurai (also bushi) were the status group whose members, by inheritance, owed military and administrative service to a lord and received stipends in return. Estimates generally place them at something like five to seven percent of the population, counting families, though the proportion varied: some domains, notably Satsuma, had a much higher share. The central change since the Sengoku era was the disappearance of sustained large-scale warfare after the early seventeenth century. The samurai remained a warrior estate in status, privilege, and self-image, but the substance of their daily work shifted.

In practice, Tokugawa samurai served as administrators, clerks, accountants, magistrates, guards, teachers, and household officials of the bakufu and domains. The military ideal continued, through weaponry, drill, martial training, and the carrying of two swords (daishō), a visible privilege of the status. Modern notions of a timeless “code of the samurai” should be handled cautiously: Tokugawa writers produced many, often divergent, reflections on proper warrior conduct, and the later construct of bushidō as a single ethical tradition is largely a product of subsequent eras (Chapters 49 and 58).

Most domain samurai lived in castle towns in designated quarters, drawing stipends in rice or its equivalent rather than administering land directly. This separation from the land was a main legacy of the policy of separating warriors from cultivators. There were exceptions. In some domains, such as Tosa and Satsuma, rural samurai (gōshi or similar groups) lived in the countryside, farming and holding lower samurai status; the diversity illustrates why “samurai” should be treated as a family of statuses rather than a single type.

Samurai status was increasingly a legally defined hereditary position, attached to the house. A samurai could not simply abandon the status nor easily acquire it. Education and cultivated manners were expected, and domain schools (Chapter 50) expanded the role of learning. The warrior estate’s defining features were thus service, stipend, hereditary rank, privileged dress and weapons, and residence, not constant battle. Understanding that transformation is essential to understanding the nineteenth-century crisis of the samurai order (Chapter 54).

Section 43.7

Hierarchy Within the Samurai Estate

“Samurai” covered enormous inequality. At the top stood the daimyō, lords of domains assessed at ten thousand koku or more (a koku being a unit of rice volume used to measure yields and stipends). Below them, within the bakufu, were direct shogunal retainers divided into hatamoto, who had the right of audience with the shogun, and gokenin, who did not. Each domain had its own ladder of retainers: senior councilors and household elders, middle-ranking officials, lower-ranking clerks and guards, and at the bottom, foot soldiers (ashigaru) and similar groups whose status as full samurai was often marginal. Terminology and the number of ranks differed from domain to domain.

A samurai’s standing was fixed by hereditary rank and stipend. Stipends, paid as land assignments or as rice, were attached to the house and inherited, while offices were assigned by the lord and generally open only to those of corresponding rank. This meant that talent could be locked out of high office by low birth. The bakufu in 1723 introduced a supplementary stipend system (tashidaka) that allowed able men of modest rank to hold higher posts without changing their hereditary stipend, a measure that shows both the rigidity of the system and attempts to soften it.

The gulf between top and bottom was large. Senior retainers lived in substantial compounds with servants and sizeable incomes, while lower samurai might occupy cramped quarters on small, fixed stipends, supplementing income through side work such as making small goods, a pattern documented in many domains especially in the later period. Samurai debt to townspeople grew as fixed stipends lagged behind prices and expenses, a theme developed in Chapter 45. The gap between rank and means thus was not only between samurai and merchants but within the warrior estate itself.

For social history, this internal hierarchy mattered in daily life: who one could marry, which house one could be adopted into, how one dressed, whom one could address as an equal, and how much of one’s year was spent in service. Chapter 42’s bakufu bureaucracy shows the institutional side; here the point is the household’s lived experience of rank.

Section 43.8

Hyakushō and the Problem with “Peasant”

The great majority of Tokugawa people belonged to households registered in villages as hyakushō. The word literally means “a hundred surnames” and was applied to the general population of village households liable to dues. English translations usually use “peasant” or “farmer,” and both can mislead. The term refers to a status, defined by registration in a village and liability to dues, rather than to an occupation. Historians, notably in the work of Amino Yoshihiko, have stressed that equating hyakushō with rice farmers erases the many other things these households did.

Rural households combined agriculture with fishing, forestry, salt-making, transport, textile production, crafts, small trade, and wage labor, depending on region and period. Fishing communities and mountain villages were often registered as hyakushō without growing rice. The rural economy was thus diverse, and by the eighteenth century the countryside produced goods for markets as well as subsistence. The “peasant” image of a rice-growing family should therefore be treated as one case among many.

Within the status, households differed sharply. Full landholding households (honbyakushō) held registered land and enjoyed voice in village affairs. Landless or land-poor households (sometimes mizunomi, “water-drinkers”) rented land or worked for others. Tenants, farm servants, and dependent households stood below substantial families. Wealthy rural households might lend money, employ many workers, run side enterprises, and take leading roles in the village; poorer households lived close to the margin. Early in the period, hereditary dependents (fudai genin) existed within larger households; the type declined as the period progressed.

This chapter stops here. How villages governed themselves, how taxes were assessed, how agricultural output changed, and how famine, petitions, and uprisings shaped rural life are the subjects of Chapter 44. What matters for Chapter 43 is that hyakushō was a status with its own household structures and internal inequalities, not a uniform class of rice farmers.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From Sengoku Warrior-Cultivators to Tokugawa Status Groups

What Changed

  • The ambiguous line between warriors and villagers hardened into legal and residential separation, with most domain samurai moving to castle towns.
  • Samurai service shifted from campaigning to administration and attendance.
  • Villages, wards, and temples became formal units of registration and responsibility.
  • Hereditary succession of houses came under closer political supervision.

What Stayed the Same

  • The household remained the basic unit of production and obligation.
  • Local communities retained considerable self-regulation.
  • Wealth continued to cross formal boundaries; the gap between rank and resources remained.
  • Some rural samurai and similar groups persisted in the countryside.

Who experienced change most directly? Warrior households, whose relationship to land and service was transformed. Who experienced greater continuity? Most village households, whose daily labor changed less than their legal framing. Why it matters: Tokugawa status order was not an ancient fixed hierarchy but a settlement produced by the unification era, with later adjustments.

Section 43.9

Artisans and Occupational Households

Skilled producers (shokunin) such as carpenters, smiths, textile workers, lacquer workers, potters, and builders occupied a status defined by occupation more than by a separate legal category. In towns, artisans were generally registered as townspeople in craft wards. In the countryside, many craftspeople were hyakushō households practicing a trade alongside or instead of farming. Castle towns often set aside districts for particular trades, and street names in many cities still preserve those occupational origins. Lords and the bakufu kept artisans on call for their own needs, and such retained craftsmen could enjoy privileges.

The artisan household was a workshop. A master headed a household that might include family members, journeymen, and apprentices, and craft knowledge passed through the house, often from father to son or to an adopted successor. Training was lengthy and the master’s authority over apprentices was considerable. Associations of artisans regulated entry, quality, and standards; later chapters discuss commercial associations (Chapter 45) and craft traditions (Chapter 49). The point here is organization: artisans appear in the status order as households linked to occupations, neighborhoods, and groups, not as a separate class defined by wealth.

Artisans’ obligations to the authorities frequently took the form of service or fees in lieu of land dues, and the burden varied by place. Skill could bring modest prosperity and respect, but most artisans lived by orders and wages, dependent on patrons and the economy. Their status was thus neither high nor especially low but situated in the middle of a field of occupational households.

Section 43.10

Merchants and Townspeople: Low Rank, Growing Wealth

Merchants confronted the period’s best-known paradox. Merchants were disparaged by some moral and Confucian discourse as profiting from exchange rather than direct production. Yet commerce and credit grew, and some merchant houses became wealthier than many samurai and lent to domains. This is the clearest case of status and wealth diverging: low rank, high income.

The term chōnin (townspeople) requires care. In its narrower sense it referred to those who held property in a town block and took part in its administration; in a broader sense it covered merchant and artisan households of the towns. Many town dwellers were tenants (tanako) rather than property holders, and many were employees, apprentices, or day laborers. Thus a large urban population lacked the full standing that the term suggests. A shop was a household too: its head, family, clerks, and apprentices formed a unit whose reputation and name carried on.

Merchant ideology was not uniformly negative. Writers such as Ishida Baigan (1685–1744), founder of the Shingaku movement, defended commerce as an honorable calling when conducted honestly, an early example of merchants’ own intellectual life. Authorities, for their part, depended on merchants for rice conversion, credit, and supply, even as they issued rules limiting luxury among commoners. Wealthy townsmen could obtain recognition from lords, and some used marriage, adoption, and payment to gain samurai standing for their families. The details of merchant houses, markets, finance, Edo, Osaka, and consumer society are for Chapter 45; Chapter 43 asks only where merchants stood in the status order and why that position was so ambiguous.

Geography Study

Status in Space: Castle Town, Village, and Margins

Status had a spatial form. Examine a plan or map of a Tokugawa castle town (for example, a typical domain capital or the city of Edo) and a map of a village and its surroundings, and identify the following:

  • The castle and the samurai quarters around it, ordered by rank and distance from the lord
  • Merchant and artisan wards, often organized by trade and street
  • Temple and shrine districts, frequently placed at the edge or along approach roads
  • Settlements of discriminated-status communities, which in many places stood at the margins of towns and villages or beside rivers and roads
  • Rural villages, with their fields, forests, and shared lands, and the hamlets of rural samurai or specialized communities where present

Remember that present-day city boundaries do not match Tokugawa jurisdictions, and that domain borders and bakufu lands were intermixed.

Section 43.11

Beyond the Four Categories

A large, varied population did not fit the schema, which is the clearest reason historians have set it aside. The imperial court and its nobles (kuge) were governed by a separate set of rules and standing. Buddhist clergy, Shinto priests, mountain ascetics, and other religious specialists belonged to institutions with their own hierarchies and regulations (Chapter 47). Physicians, who were often hereditary practitioners with distinctive appearance, and Confucian and other scholars occupied ambiguous positions that differed from place to place. Performers, itinerants, fishermen, boatmen, porters, post-station workers, and mountain people were registered in various ways and subject to different obligations. Masterless samurai (rōnin) lived in an uncertain space between samurai status and commoner life.

This variety is best treated as a status ecology: a field of overlapping groups with distinct privileges and duties, rather than as an incomplete pyramid awaiting additional levels. Specific topics belong to later chapters, including religion (47), entertainment and pleasure districts (46), and crafts and cultural practice (49). The purpose here is to show why a four-part scheme could never account for the whole.

Outside the system lay peoples governed by other arrangements. The Ainu of Ezo were not subject to the bakufu’s household registration or status order, though their relations with Matsumae and Wajin traders were consequential (Chapter 48). The Ryukyu Kingdom had its own state, and its own hierarchy distinguished a hereditary elite (yukatchu) from commoners. Both cases remind students that “Tokugawa society” covered only part of the archipelago’s peoples.

Section 43.12

Gender Inside the Status Order

Gender did not form a separate pyramid beside the four statuses; it operated through every status and household. A woman’s position depended heavily on the status of her household and on her role within it: daughter, wife, mother, widow, servant, head. A samurai wife, a village woman, a townsman’s wife, and a servant faced different expectations, resources, and risks.

The ideal, promoted through Confucian-influenced moral writing, assigned women obedience and household duties and men authority and public roles. Popular instructional books for women circulated widely, among them the Onna daigaku, traditionally attributed to Kaibara Ekken but of unknown authorship; it appears to have drawn substantially on the section on women’s education in Ekken’s Wazoku dōjikun (1710). Such texts became more influential later in the period. Authority within households was patriarchal in general terms: heads were typically male, public offices in samurai government and most village and ward leadership were held by men, and warrior stipends and offices passed through male lines.

But prescription is not practice. Women’s work, property, marriage, divorce, inheritance, and household authority varied widely by status, region, wealth, and period. Women sometimes headed households, notably as widows or where no male heir was available, and household registers record such cases. Recent historiography has moved from a picture of uniformly subordinated women toward one that recognizes real constraints alongside considerable variation and agency. Students should also avoid projecting backward the more formally codified Meiji ie system, which gave patriarchal headship a distinct legal definition. The Tokugawa reality was less uniform.

Section 43.13

Women, Work, Property, and Household Authority

Comparing women across statuses shows variety. In samurai households, women managed the household, supervised servants, handled etiquette and social obligations, and in many cases performed income-supplementing work as stipends lagged behind costs. Their marriages linked houses. Samurai women ordinarily did not succeed to retainer offices and stipends in their own right, although they could exercise substantial household authority, and practices concerning property and succession varied by house and domain. Property brought into a marriage could remain recognized as belonging to the wife or her natal house, although practice likewise varied. In rural households, women worked in agriculture and in the by-employments that made rural life viable, including spinning, weaving, and sericulture; their labor was indispensable to household income. In artisan and merchant households, wives and daughters took part in the family business, ran shops, managed accounts and staff, and, when widowed, sometimes carried on as head until a successor was ready.

Marriage could constrain women, and divorce customarily proceeded through a husband’s declaration in a short letter, the mikudarihan. Yet divorce and remarriage were not rare in some communities, and women had avenues for seeking an end to marriages, including mediation by kin and village or ward officials, and, famously, flight to Tōkeiji in Kamakura, a convent to which women could retreat and, after a period of residence, obtain release. These routes did not equalize the parties, but they show that women could act within the system.

Literacy and learning also varied. Some women of samurai and prosperous commoner households acquired substantial education, and women appear as writers of diaries and poetry. Historians remain cautious about generalizing, because documents were more often written by and about men. Women’s history in this period demands reading registers, wills, petitions, and household records for women’s presence, and reading moral prescriptions critically.

Everyday Life and Environment

Work Inside the Household

For most Tokugawa people, the household was where work, family life, ritual, and obligation met. A village household’s year followed the farming calendar, but also included textile work, tending of fuel and forest resources, and tasks for the village. A merchant household’s routine centered on the shop, with apprentices and clerks living and eating in the same house, and the wife often supervising meals, accounts, and staff. A samurai household’s rhythms followed service duties and ceremonial obligations, alongside household economy that could be thrifty.

The household was also the site of ritual. Domestic ancestral memorials, annual observances, and the arrangements for care of the aged were integral parts of its life. Because the line between household and workplace was thin, children learned their roles by participating, and for most, education was the gradual acquisition of the house’s occupation. Understanding this integration helps explain why the loss of a household head, or the failure to secure a successor, was both an economic and an emotional catastrophe.

Section 43.14

Servants, Apprentices, Dependents, and Social Mobility

Behind every household stood workers who were not heirs. Domestic servants, farmhands, shop clerks, apprentices (detchi), wet nurses, and other dependents (hōkōnin, persons in contracted service) lived and worked within households, often for fixed terms. Bakufu law forbade the outright sale of persons while permitting service for contracted periods, though in practice the line could blur, especially where poverty pressed families to place children. Hiring agents arranged placements in towns, and many servants in cities came from villages.

This created substantial labor mobility. Young people moved between households, between village and town, and between occupations; seasonal and temporary migration for work was common in many regions. Apprentices who completed training might become senior employees, be given a branch shop by their master, or be adopted as successors. Rural families sent children to service as a way to relieve pressure on household resources and gain training. Mobility also took more unusual forms: samurai who gave up service, commoners who obtained samurai status through adoption or payment, and families that rose or declined.

However, this mobility was not modern social equality. Servants and apprentices were subordinate to household heads, with limited protection. Moving up depended on opportunity, connections, and luck, and some boundaries, particularly those separating discriminated-status communities from others, were far harder to cross than the boundary between, say, village and town. Mobility within the order was real, but it did not dissolve it.

Multiple Perspectives

One Order, Five Viewpoints

His status gives him honor and a claim to service, yet household budgets are tight and debts to townsmen accumulate. His sons’ futures depend on adoption or office, and his wife’s labor helps sustain the house.

A merchant’s wife in a town: Belongs to a household of low formal rank but real economic weight. She supervises staff and accounts, oversees daily operations, and may stand to steer the business in a succession crisis or widowhood, within limits set by custom and her husband’s family.

A member of a discriminated-status community: Lives in a settlement set apart, performing duties and trades assigned or monopolized by the community, bearing stigma and restrictions. The community has its own leadership and economic ties to its neighbors, while social barriers, especially around marriage and residence, remain severe.
Section 43.15

Discriminated Status Communities: Kawata, Chōri, and “Eta”

Some Tokugawa communities were assigned and bore a markedly lower, stigmatized status. Terminology varied by region and changed over time. Kawata (literally related to leather) and chōri were terms used in particular places for communities associated with specific occupations; eta was a widely used label that was and is discriminatory, and appears in this chapter only as a historical term, not as neutral description. Students should note that these labels came from outside, often from those who looked down on the people described, and that the communities’ own terms and self-understanding are less well documented.

Work associated with these communities included leatherworking and the leather trade, the handling and disposal of dead livestock, certain duties connected with executions, prisons, and policing, and sanitation-related tasks. These occupations were marked by notions of ritual pollution, a concept with older roots in Japanese religious and court culture. But historians warn against two errors: assuming one occupation explains every community, and assuming that “unclean work” alone generated the status. Many communities also farmed, and the relationship between occupation, ritual notions, and political arrangements developed historically. Scholars have long debated how much the Tokugawa-period status was a product of early modern political arrangements versus medieval roots; a prominent view holds that the status was substantially consolidated in the early modern period out of earlier outcast groups.

Critically, these communities were not “outside society.” They had their own settlements, leaders, internal hierarchies, and economic functions, and they supplied goods and services the wider society needed. In the Kantō region, the hereditary head known as Danzaemon, based in Edo, exercised authority over associated communities. The discriminated-status world thus formed an organized, differentiated sector, subject to authorities’ control and neighbors’ contempt, and not a single undifferentiated mass.

Section 43.16

Hinin, Poverty, Policing, and Marginal Status

Hinin were a distinct category and should not be merged with kawata/chōri communities. The term was applied to beggars, certain performers, and people pushed into marginal life by poverty, displacement, or punishment, and in some cities hinin organizations were assigned policing, prison-related, and cleansing duties. Edo’s hinin population was organized under several hereditary leaders, the best known being Kuruma Zenshichi of Asakusa. Danzaemon’s authority over Zenshichi was contested and was formally upheld by the shogunal authorities in 1721. Hinin communities often had their own leaders, quarters, and recognized claims to certain forms of begging or work.

A key difference lay in how status was entered and left. Many kawata/chōri people held their status by hereditary membership in the community. Hinin status, while it could also pass to children, could in some circumstances be entered through poverty, destitution, or punishment (under the shogunate’s 1722 rules on lovers’ double suicide, promulgated in Edo in 1723, couples who both survived could be exposed publicly for three days and reduced to hinin status), and in some places and circumstances it could be exited through procedures involving sponsors and reinstatement. This was not freedom: the stigma was real, and exit depended on authorities and communities. Research on early modern discriminated groups warns against collapsing these populations into a single “outcaste caste.” The relationships among the categories also varied by region, with Edo and the Kansai cities, for example, organizing them differently.

Poverty linked hinin to a wider story. Famine, dislocation, and the pressure on landless households sent people into begging and itinerant life, and authorities tried to control mobility and public order. The chapter’s distinctions can be summarized: eta/kawata/chōri ≠ hinin ≠ modern Burakumin.

Historical Vocabulary
Kawata / chōri / eta, hinin, Burakumin

Kawata and chōri are regional terms for communities of discriminated status associated with leatherwork and related duties. Eta is a discriminatory label applied to such communities, historically used in official and popular language. Hinin (a term whose characters suggest “non-person”) denotes a separate discriminated status typically linked to beggary, marginal performance, and some penal and policing roles. Burakumin (“hamlet people”) is a modern term for people subject to Buraku discrimination.

Why they must be distinguished: The categories differed in occupation, organization, mode of entry and exit, and regional meaning. Modern Burakumin developed historically from earlier discriminated statuses through later changes, so the terms are connected but not interchangeable.

Section 43.17

Discrimination in Law, Space, Occupation, and Everyday Contact

Discrimination took concrete forms. Communities commonly lived in separate settlements, sometimes listed in separate registers or attached administratively to neighboring villages. Occupational arrangements both restricted and protected: a community might hold exclusive rights over dead livestock or leather within a defined territory, a source of income and an obligation. Some jurisdictions imposed rules on clothing, hairstyle, or footwear intended to mark people as belonging to these statuses. Marriage across the boundary was strongly discouraged, residence and participation in local rituals and gatherings could be restricted, and in some contexts legal treatment differed. Stigma extended into daily contact, including customs about food, shared space, and etiquette.

At the same time, the dominant society depended on these communities. Leather supplied armor components, drum heads, and footwear; carcass disposal and sanitation addressed practical needs; and prison, policing, and execution-related duties assisted governments. The communities’ economic roles gave some of them real income, and a few became prosperous. This interdependence did not remove contempt; it coexisted with it. The pattern reminds students that exclusion and economic integration could operate together.

Discriminated communities were not merely passive. Evidence survives of petitions to authorities against restrictions, disputes over territorial rights, and collective efforts to improve conditions. The record is uneven, and much comes through officials’ documents, so historians read it with care. The goal is to humanize rather than sensationalize: the people involved were households and communities with work, families, grievances, and agency, living under rules designed to mark them as lesser.

Cause and Effect

The Consolidation of Hereditary Status Order

Long-term causes

Medieval and Sengoku ambiguity between warriors and cultivators; older notions of pollution and outcast groups; Confucian ideas of functional social order; and the growth of village and household organization as units of local responsibility.

Short-term causes

Unification policies such as the 1588 Sword Hunt and the 1591 edict; the Tokugawa settlement; the end of sustained warfare; and the need for authorities to identify people and assign responsibility.

Immediate effects

Samurai concentrated in castle towns; villages, wards, and temples used as registration units; succession among warrior houses supervised; discriminated statuses made more formal and subject to organized control.

Long-term consequences

A stable but locally varied order that governed through groups and households, with hereditary advantages and disadvantages that persisted until the Meiji reforms and left inherited stigma that outlasted legal abolition.

Historical complexity: The order was not designed in one act. It emerged through policies, local adaptation, and negotiation, and no single cause, whether Confucian ideology, political calculation, or economic function, accounts for it.

Section 43.18

From Early-Modern Status Discrimination to the Later Buraku Problem

The chapter’s outline calls for the historical background of later Burakumin discrimination, and the central point must be stated carefully: modern Burakumin should not simply be projected backward as an unchanged Tokugawa caste. Modern Buraku discrimination developed historically from, and through transformations of, earlier discriminated-status communities, but the continuities were shaped by new circumstances, including legal change, urbanization, industrial labor markets, household registration, and the spread of modern ideas about lineage, hygiene, and race.

In 1871 the Meiji government issued an edict abolishing the labels eta and hinin and placing those people in the commoner category (discussed in Chapter 55). Legal abolition did not eliminate inherited stigma or community identification. Some communities lost occupational protections without gaining economic security, and discrimination persisted in marriage, employment, and residence. A later movement of organized activism emerged (Chapter 94). Modern scholarship treats the relationship between early modern statuses and modern Burakumin as historical continuity and transformation, not simple identity across centuries.

For students, the practical implication is vocabulary and caution: use the early modern terms as historical, discriminatory labels; keep kawata/chōri and hinin distinct; and avoid equating either with the modern category. The detailed Meiji story and postwar activism belong to later chapters. Chapter 43’s responsibility is to establish the background and stop.

Section 43.19

Hierarchy, Mobility, and the Limits of the Status Order

Tokugawa society was unquestionably hierarchical. Status determined dress, privilege, residence, obligation, and often occupation, and the system’s defenders and its institutions worked to preserve it. Yet people changed occupations, households adopted outsiders, wealth crossed status lines, servants and apprentices moved, households rose or declined, and local arrangements differed. Status was powerful without being perfectly static.

Several mechanisms produced flexibility: adoption could bring outsiders into houses of different origin; marriage connected households; service and apprenticeship moved young people; some wealthy commoners obtained samurai standing; and rural and urban economies created non-agricultural opportunities for villagers. Meanwhile, tensions accumulated: samurai poverty, commoner wealth, and the contradictions of a moral discourse that disparaged merchants while relying on their credit. These tensions did not destroy the order, but they shaped debates over reform (Chapter 50) and, much later, helped condition how the status system was dismantled.

At the same time, the limits were real. Some boundaries were far harder to cross than others, especially for discriminated communities. Women faced constraints on office and inheritance. The cost of mobility was often borne by those with least power. The synthesis is therefore neither a frozen caste society nor an open one, but a hierarchical order with substantial but unevenly distributed flexibility.

Section 43.20

Households and Status as the Foundations of Everyday Tokugawa Society

The chapter has argued that Tokugawa society was organized by two interlocking principles: status groups and households. Governments interacted with people through both. Households perpetuated occupation, property, name, service, and status across generations, absorbing heirs and workers through marriage, adoption, and contract. Status communities, whether retainer bands, villages, wards, temples, guilds, or discriminated-status organizations, supplied authorities with labor, revenue, service, administration, and social regulation.

Within this framework gender and servitude shaped every position, discriminated communities were organized and economically integrated while stigmatized, and mobility coexisted with inherited rank. The older four-class pyramid is useful as the ideology contemporaries invoked, but it is inadequate as a description of the order they lived in.

This leads directly to Chapter 44. If Chapter 43 explains who rural people were within the status order and how their households were organized, Chapter 44 asks how villages actually governed themselves, produced food, paid taxes, survived famine, petitioned authorities, and sometimes rebelled.

Chapter Summary

This chapter moved from the political structure of Chapter 42 to the social structure lived inside it. Its central argument is that Tokugawa society was deeply hierarchical but was not a simple four-level caste pyramid. Status (mibun) was produced through households, occupations, communities, privileges, obligations, and relationships with authorities, and it differed from wealth, so that a poor samurai could outrank a rich merchant.

The four-status model, shi–nō–kō–shō, was a real ideological vocabulary and shaped policy and moral argument, but it was not a complete or nationwide legal system. Court nobles, clergy, physicians, scholars, performers, servants, fishers, itinerants, and discriminated communities all stood outside or between its categories. Historians now describe governance through locally organized status groups, among them retainer bands, villages, town wards, temples, guilds, and discriminated-status associations.

The household (ie) was the second foundation. It was a continuing corporate unit with a name, property, an occupation or office, ancestral rites, and obligations across generations, and it could include non-kin. Continuity of the house could outweigh biological descent, which explains the prominence of adoption, adopted sons-in-law, and capable clerks chosen as successors. Marriage linked households; retirement allowed orderly succession; younger children entered other houses, service, or dependence. Registers show that actual households were often small and fragile.

Samurai were a hereditary status of service, with stipends, swords, and residence in castle towns after warriors and cultivators were separated. Their daily functions became administrative. The estate was internally unequal, from daimyō and hatamoto to low-ranking foot soldiers, with rank and stipend fixed by birth, and lower samurai often poor and indebted. Policies such as the supplementary stipend system softened but did not abolish rank rigidity.

Hyakushō were village-registered households, not only rice farmers, and included landholders, landless households, and dependents, with diverse occupations. Artisans and townspeople were organized by workshop, ward, and occupation. Merchants’ low status and growing wealth exposed the gap between status and economic power. The detailed treatment of villages, taxation, and commerce belongs to later chapters.

Gender operated through every status. Moral prescriptions emphasized male authority and female obedience, but practice varied: women worked, managed households, held property in dowry, sometimes headed houses as widows, and could seek divorce through mediation or institutional refuge. The Tokugawa household should not be confused with the later Meiji codified household system.

Servants, apprentices, and dependents moved between households, and labor mobility, adoption across status lines, and acquisition of samurai standing show that the order was flexible, though boundaries were not equally permeable. Kawata, chōri, and the community labeled eta had their own settlements, occupations, leaders, and hierarchies, were integral to the economy and governance, and suffered stigma and restriction. Hinin were a separate category with different entry and exit patterns. Neither can be equated with each other or with modern Burakumin.

Modern Buraku discrimination developed from, and through transformations of, earlier discriminated statuses; the 1871 abolition of the labels did not end inherited stigma. The chapter concludes that Tokugawa status was powerful without being static, and that households and status groups were the foundations of everyday society. It hands the story to Chapter 44’s account of how villages governed themselves and survived.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it replaces a familiar but misleading picture with a more accurate one. The four-class pyramid is easy to remember and wrong in important ways. Seeing Tokugawa society as a field of households and locally organized status groups explains how a large population could be governed without a modern bureaucracy, why adoption and succession carried such weight, and why wealth and rank could diverge.

It also reveals larger processes. The unification-era effort to define who was a warrior and who was not, and to locate people in registered groups, produced a society of remarkable stability and internal strain. The strains, including samurai indebtedness, commoner prosperity, and the poverty that pushed some into marginal life, helped shape debates over reform in the decades ahead, and the dismantling of the status order in the Meiji era (Chapters 54 and 55) cannot be understood without them.

Many continuities outlasted the period. Household-centered thinking, ideas about lineage, and inherited stigma persisted after legal categories were abolished, which is why the history of discriminated communities and of the later Buraku problem remains a living subject. The chapter also connects regions and peoples: it reminds students that the Ainu and Ryukyu stood partly outside this order, and that regional variation was substantial.

In the larger history of Japan, the chapter supplies the human foundation for what follows. Chapter 44 turns to the villages in which most people lived, Chapter 45 to roads, cities, and commerce, and later chapters to religion, culture, learning, and crisis. Each builds on the households and status groups described here.
