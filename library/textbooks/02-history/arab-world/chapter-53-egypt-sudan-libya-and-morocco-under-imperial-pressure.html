Part IX — Reform, Nahda, and European Expansion

Egypt, Sudan, Libya, and Morocco under Imperial Pressure

A History of the Arab World: From Ancient Crossroads to the Modern Arab World

Chapter Opening

Between 1875 and 1914, four societies stretching from the Atlantic coast of Africa to the upper Nile were drawn, by different routes and at different speeds, into European imperial systems. In Egypt the instrument was debt, followed by a military invasion that never produced a formal annexation. In Sudan it was a revolution that expelled Egyptian rule, built an independent state, and was then destroyed by an Anglo-Egyptian army. In Libya it was an Italian invasion that captured the coast and then discovered that a coast is not a country. In Morocco it was a slow strangulation of finances and diplomacy that ended in partition between France and Spain. The outcomes resembled one another far less than the word "colonialism" suggests.

On the morning of 13 September 1882, a British force under Sir Garnet Wolseley attacked the fortified Egyptian position at Tel el-Kebir, east of the Nile Delta, and in a short engagement destroyed the army of Colonel Ahmad Urabi. Within days British troops were in Cairo. The Egyptian government continued to exist, the khedive remained on his throne, and the Ottoman sultan remained, in law, Egypt's sovereign. Yet from that morning onward, decisions of fundamental importance in Egyptian government were taken or vetoed by British officials. The paradox of a state that survived in form while losing control in substance is one of the central problems of this chapter, and it recurs, in different forms, in every society the chapter examines.

Sixteen years later, on 2 September 1898, another army fought a decisive battle on a plain north of Omdurman, the capital of a Sudanese state that had been founded in 1881 by a religious reformer who claimed to be the Mahdi, the divinely guided figure of Islamic expectation. That state had defeated Egyptian armies, taken Khartoum, and governed much of the upper Nile for more than a decade. In 1911, Italian warships appeared before Tripoli and a war began for provinces that the Italian government had spent decades preparing to claim. In 1912, in the Moroccan imperial city of Fez, the sultan's government signed a treaty accepting a French protectorate, while Spain was assigned a zone in the north and the far south. Each date marks a different kind of imperial arrival, and each arrival was met by a different kind of local politics.

This chapter treats these histories as the histories of four societies under pressure, not merely as episodes in European foreign policy. Egyptians, Sudanese, Libyans, and Moroccans possessed political institutions, religious leaderships, regional economies, armies, and long habits of negotiation with outside powers. They disagreed among themselves about how to respond. Some sought accommodation, some reform, some armed resistance, some diplomatic maneuver; many shifted their positions as circumstances changed. European decisions were shaped by finance, strategic geography, domestic politics, and rivalry among the powers themselves, but they were also shaped, repeatedly and sometimes decisively, by what Egyptians, Sudanese, Libyans, and Moroccans did.

Three distinctions govern the chapter. First, foreign financial intervention did not automatically produce military conquest: Egypt's insolvency, the political crisis of 1879–1882, and the British decision to invade are connected, but they are not identical, and each requires its own explanation. Second, the Mahdist state must be studied as an independent political and religious experiment with its own institutions, not merely as an interval between Egyptian and British rule. Third, in Libya and Morocco, formal claims of sovereignty and effective territorial control diverged sharply; a treaty could transfer legal title on paper long before any army controlled the interior.

The evidence is uneven and must be read with care. For Egypt, historians draw on Egyptian government records, the archives of the British Foreign Office and the Caisse de la Dette, consular reports, the contemporary Arabic press, and memoirs. For Sudan, Mahdist proclamations, letters, treasury and court records survive alongside British intelligence reports and the accounts of European captives, whose hostility and ignorance shaped much of what outsiders believed about the Mahdist state for decades. For Libya, Italian parliamentary and military records are abundant, while Libyan voices survive in oral tradition, poetry, and later compilations. For Morocco, the evidence includes Moroccan chronicles and correspondence, European consular archives, and the diplomatic papers of the powers. Everywhere, historians must ask whose perspective a source represents and whose is missing.

The chapter also marks boundaries. It does not repeat Chapter 49's account of Muhammad Ali's state-building, Chapter 50's account of Ottoman provincial reform, Chapter 51's account of the Nahda, or Chapter 52's account of French rule in Algeria and Tunisia, though all four provide essential background. It describes opposition to imperial rule where that opposition explains conquest and colonial society, but it leaves the broader ideological history of Arabism, constitutionalism, and reformist Islam to Chapter 54, and the wartime transformation of the region to Chapter 55. It ends in 1914, with four distinct imperial orders in place and the Arab world on the eve of a war that would reshape it again.

Learning Objectives

  2. Analyze the Urabi movement as a revolution with military, civilian, and constitutional dimensions rather than a mere mutiny.
  3. Describe the constitutional ambiguity of British-occupied Egypt between 1882 and 1914 and why occupation did not immediately become formal annexation.

  8. Interpret Morocco's loss of autonomy as the outcome of financial vulnerability, dynastic conflict, and international diplomacy, giving Moroccan actors their own agency.

Historical Context

By 1870 the four societies examined here stood in very different relationships to the Ottoman Empire and to Europe. Egypt was an Ottoman province governed by a hereditary viceroy, since 1867 styled khedive, whose dynasty descended from Muhammad Ali (Chapter 49). It possessed its own army, bureaucracy, and fiscal system, and it was the most integrated of the four into the world cotton economy. Sudan had been conquered by Muhammad Ali's forces in 1820–1821 and was ruled as an Egyptian dependency. Tripolitania and Cyrenaica were Ottoman provinces, administered directly from Istanbul after the end of the Karamanli dynasty in 1835 and affected by the Tanzimat reforms of Chapter 50. Morocco was none of these things: an independent sultanate under the Alaouite dynasty, never part of the Ottoman Empire, which had preserved its separateness while Algeria fell to France in 1830 (Chapter 52).

Europe's relationship to the region was changing in ways that connected these histories. The industrial economies of Britain and France needed raw materials, markets, and secure routes. The opening of the Suez Canal in 1869 made Egypt a strategic corridor to India and Asia. European bankers lent heavily to Mediterranean governments whose revenues could not keep pace with their ambitions. After the French occupation of Tunisia in 1881 and the German unification of 1871, the European powers entered a period of tense competition in which control of peripheral territories became a measure of status as well as a source of profit. Diplomats spoke of compensation, balance, and spheres of influence, and in the process they traded away places whose inhabitants had not been consulted.

Students should avoid two simplifications. The first is to imagine that European strength and North African weakness made conquest inevitable. Egyptian, Sudanese, Libyan, and Moroccan societies were not static; they were reforming, arguing, and fighting, and outcomes depended on contingencies such as a ruler's death, a harvest, or a diplomatic bargain between third parties. The second is to treat the four cases as one. Egypt was ruled through an existing government, Sudan through a new hybrid arrangement, Libya through a claim not yet realized, and Morocco through a treaty. Their differences are the subject of this chapter's closing comparison.

Division I — Egypt: Debt, Occupation, and British Imperial Rule · Section 53.1

Egypt, the Suez Canal, and the Growth of European Financial Influence

Chapter 49 traced how Muhammad Ali built an army, a bureaucracy, and a cotton-exporting agrarian economy. His successors inherited that apparatus and extended it. Under Khedive Isma'il (r. 1863–1879) Egypt acquired railways, telegraphs, harbors, new canals, a modernized Cairo, a larger administrative corps, and an ambitious Sudanese and East African empire. The cost was enormous. Isma'il's government borrowed repeatedly from European banks on terms that were often ruinous: bonds were issued at large discounts, commissions were heavy, and short-term loans were refinanced at rising rates. The expectation that cotton prices would remain high, as they had during the American Civil War, proved mistaken when the boom ended in the later 1860s. By the mid-1870s Egypt's public debt had grown to a size that its revenues could not comfortably service.

The Suez Canal intensified European interest. The canal, built by the Suez Canal Company under the French promoter Ferdinand de Lesseps, opened in November 1869 after a decade of construction that had relied heavily on Egyptian labor, including forced labor until the practice was curtailed in the early 1860s following international criticism. Egypt held a substantial share of the company's stock, but the canal's profits flowed mainly to private shareholders, and the waterway belonged to a joint-stock company under a concession, not to Egypt's government. For Britain, which carried most of the canal's traffic, the new route shortened the passage to India and transformed Egypt from a distant Ottoman province into a strategic hinge. For France, the canal was a matter of national prestige and investment. Both powers wanted Egypt solvent, accessible, and not under the control of the other.

The emphasis of this section is therefore on the emergence of financial dependence and European competition, not on the state-building of earlier decades. It is worth noting what Isma'il himself achieved and attempted. He secured from the Ottoman sultan the title of khedive in 1867 and a new rule of succession from father to son, and he created consultative institutions, including an assembly of delegates (1866). He was neither a simple victim nor a simple spendthrift, but a ruler who pursued sovereignty by modernizing and expanding, and who found that the tools of modernization, foreign capital above all, carried their own constraints. European consuls and creditors, supported by their governments, increasingly treated Egyptian finances as a matter of international concern.

Section 53.2

Bankruptcy, the Caisse de la Dette, and the Loss of Financial Autonomy

In November 1875, Isma'il, in urgent need of cash, agreed to sell the Egyptian government's shares in the Suez Canal Company. The British prime minister, Benjamin Disraeli, acquired them, roughly two-fifths of the company's capital, for approximately four million pounds, borrowing the money from the banking house of Rothschild. The purchase gave Britain a stake in the canal and, more importantly, a rationale for continued attention to Egyptian affairs. It did not relieve Isma'il. In 1876 his government suspended payments on part of its debt, and European governments, acting on behalf of bondholders, pressed for supervision. In May 1876 a decree created the Caisse de la Dette Publique, a commission of foreign representatives empowered to receive designated Egyptian revenues and apply them to debt service. The same year, the Mixed Courts began operating, giving European litigants a judicial system outside both Egyptian courts and consular jurisdiction (the capitulations are discussed in Chapter 50).

Supervision deepened. Late in 1876 an Anglo-French mission recommended, and the khedive accepted, a system known as Dual Control, in which British and French officials oversaw Egyptian revenue and expenditure. In 1878 Isma'il, under pressure, accepted a ministry that included a British finance minister and a French minister of public works. When he dismissed this "European ministry" in April 1879, partly in response to an officers' demonstration against the cost-cutting that had thrown many officers onto half pay, the European powers turned to the Ottoman sultan, who in June 1879 deposed Isma'il and installed his son Tawfiq. A law of liquidation in 1880 then fixed the terms of debt service, assigning a very large share of Egyptian revenue to creditors.

It is important to distinguish three things that are easily confused: debt administration, the placement of foreign controllers, and direct governmental interference. The Caisse managed specified revenues for bondholders; the controllers audited and advised; the European ministers governed. Each step increased the degree to which Egyptian policy was shaped by outside authorities, but none by itself amounted to occupation. The burden of this system fell unevenly. Taxpayers, chiefly cultivators, bore the heavy land tax and its harsh collection; officials and officers suffered salary cuts and dismissals; landowners and village headmen resented new fiscal pressure and the surrender of control. Foreign officials, many well paid, became visible symbols of the arrangement. The army, already unsettled by defeat in Ethiopia and by pay arrears, was especially affected, which helps explain why military grievances became the spearhead of political protest.

Historical Vocabulary
Financial Control and the "Veiled Protectorate"

Financial control refers to the system, established in 1876–1880, by which European governments supervised Egyptian public revenue and debt through the Caisse de la Dette, the Dual Control, and the Law of Liquidation. It was an arrangement for the benefit of creditors that nonetheless gave foreign powers influence over Egyptian domestic policy.

The veiled protectorate is a phrase used by contemporaries and historians for the situation after 1882, when Egypt remained, in law, an Ottoman province governed by its khedive, while British officials exercised decisive control over its government. The word "veiled" captures the gap between constitutional form and political reality. It helps students avoid two errors: assuming that Egypt was a formal colony after 1882, and assuming that nothing changed because the khedive stayed in office.

The Financial-Control Decrees: The Caisse de la Dette and the Law of Liquidation, 1876–1880

Creator: The Egyptian government under pressure from European creditors and governments · Date: Decree of May 1876; Law of Liquidation, July 1880 · Type: Official decrees and legal instruments

Context: These instruments were issued in the name of the Egyptian ruler but negotiated with, and in important respects dictated by, European representatives acting for bondholders. They survive in official gazettes and in the diplomatic correspondence of the powers.

Paraphrase (not a quotation): The 1876 decree created a commission of foreign representatives to receive revenue from designated Egyptian sources and apply it to payment of the debt, placing a portion of Egypt's income outside the ordinary control of its own ministers. The 1880 law later fixed the share of revenue to be devoted to debt service and the share left for government expenses.

Analysis: The decrees show that creditors treated Egypt's finances as a matter of enforceable international obligation, and they reveal how financial instruments could limit sovereignty without a single soldier landing. They do not reveal how Egyptians experienced these measures, nor do they explain the political reactions that followed; for those, historians turn to petitions, press, and later memoirs. Students should consult the full texts in a scholarly edition rather than rely on summaries, which can omit the technical provisions that mattered most.

Section 53.3

Ahmad Urabi and the Egyptian Revolution of 1879–1882

The movement associated with Colonel Ahmad Urabi grew from several overlapping grievances. In the army, native Egyptian officers, many from peasant backgrounds, resented a hierarchy dominated by Turco-Circassian officers, whose promotion and favor outstripped theirs. Cost-cutting under financial control worsened pay and prospects. In early 1881 Urabi and two fellow colonels petitioned against the war minister, an episode that exposed the officers' willingness to confront the khedive. On 9 September 1881 Urabi led troops and a crowd to Abdin Palace in Cairo and demanded the dismissal of the ministry, the convening of a representative assembly, and an enlarged army. Khedive Tawfiq yielded in substance, and a new ministry under Sharif Pasha accepted a chamber of delegates.

The movement must not be reduced to a military mutiny. Civilians, including landowners and notables in the new Chamber, village headmen, merchants, journalists, and Muslim scholars, as well as some Christians and Jews, took part in a broad political coalition that demanded constitutional government and an end to foreign control. Orators and newspapers carried its message; scholars have emphasized its civilian participation and social depth. The coalition's aims were not identical: some members wanted parliamentary control of the budget, others the removal of European officials, others the elevation of Egyptians in government. Urabi himself became a national symbol, widely recognized as an Egyptian leading Egyptians.

A crisis of 1882 deepened the confrontation. In January, Britain and France sent a joint note supporting the khedive against the nationalists, a move that angered many Egyptians as foreign interference. The Sharif ministry fell and was replaced in February by a cabinet led by Mahmud Sami al-Barudi, in which Urabi became minister of war. The new government adopted an organic law that enlarged the Chamber's powers, though the chamber's claim to control budget items under the foreign-supervised portion of the budget provoked European objections. Tawfiq, personally distrustful of Urabi and aware that British and French attitudes could protect him, intrigued against his ministers. By spring the khedive and the nationalist leaders were in open opposition, and the European powers, uneasy at the prospect of a regime unwilling to accept foreign financial control, prepared to act.

Section 53.4

The British Invasion of 1882 and the Defeat of Egyptian Resistance

The crisis moved quickly. In May a European fleet appeared off Alexandria. On 11 June a riot in the city killed a number of Europeans and a larger, though disputed, number of Egyptians; its origins remain obscure, and historians continue to debate whether it was spontaneous, provoked, or manipulated. Britain, now governed by William Gladstone's Liberal ministry, chose to act, and when the British admiral demanded the suspension of work on shore defenses and the Egyptians refused, the fleet bombarded Alexandria on 11 July 1882. France, divided at home, withdrew its ships. Fires and looting devastated much of the city. The khedive took refuge with the British, a fact that allowed Britain to claim it was supporting legitimate authority.

The land campaign followed. Britain assembled an expeditionary force under Sir Garnet Wolseley, which was moved to the Suez Canal zone, seizing the canal and landing at Ismailia despite earlier assurances to Urabi, given in good faith by de Lesseps, that the waterway would not be disturbed. After a clash at Kassassin in late August, Wolseley's night march brought his army against Urabi's fortified line at Tel el-Kebir. The assault at dawn on 13 September 1882 overran the Egyptian trenches in a short engagement. British forces entered Cairo the next day, and Urabi, who had surrendered, was tried by an Egyptian court under British pressure and sentenced to death; the sentence was commuted to exile in Ceylon (Sri Lanka). Casualty figures from the battle come largely from British and Egyptian official reports of uncertain reliability; it is safe to say only that British losses were small and Egyptian losses were considerably greater.

Why did Britain intervene? Contemporary British explanations stressed the protection of European lives and property after Alexandria and the restoration of order. Historians generally add the strategic importance of the Suez Canal, the interests of bondholders, the fear that Egyptian nationalism would encourage similar movements elsewhere, and the determination of Britain's government not to allow France or another power to dominate the route to India. The relative weight of these motives is debated, as the Historical Debate feature at the end of this chapter explains. What is clear is that the intervention was not a simple response to disorder: it exploited a political struggle inside Egypt in which the Urabi movement, with significant popular support, challenged both the khedive and foreign control.

Section 53.5

The Veiled Protectorate: British Administration and Egyptian Sovereignty

After 1882 Britain faced a problem it had not fully anticipated: it controlled Egypt without wishing, or being able, to annex it. Annexation would have offended France and the other powers, violated Britain's repeated declarations that its stay would be temporary, and challenged the Ottoman sultan's formal sovereignty. Instead the British government sent Sir Evelyn Baring, later Lord Cromer, who served as British agent and consul-general from 1883 to 1907. The khedive continued to rule, ministers continued to be appointed, and Egyptian law, courts, and ministries continued to function. But a British policy, announced in January 1883, made clear that Egyptian ministers who rejected British advice would be expected to resign. Advisers attached to the ministries, first for finance and justice, then for other departments, became the true instruments of policy.

British promises to evacuate Egypt were made repeatedly and not fulfilled. Missions and conventions, such as the unratified agreement negotiated with the Ottoman government in 1887, explored arrangements for withdrawal, but British governments concluded that evacuation would endanger Egyptian solvency and the canal route, and that other powers might step in. The occupation army remained. The Egyptian army was reorganized under British officers, and a force of British troops stayed in Cairo and Alexandria. The result was a constitutional ambiguity that lasted until 1914: formally, Egypt was an Ottoman province; effectively, Britain governed it.

Students should understand why occupation did not immediately mean protectorate. The ambiguity suited Britain, because it left the costs of governing to the Egyptian treasury, and it allowed the powers to maintain their own rights, such as the capitulations, without formal challenge. It also left channels for Egyptian political action: a legislative council and general assembly with limited powers, a khedive who could, when circumstances permitted, resist British policy, and an Ottoman suzerain whose name still carried weight. The arrangement was not stable, and when the First World War made the Ottoman connection a matter of enemy status, Britain abandoned the veil (§53.9).

Section 53.6

Cotton, Irrigation, Landholding, and the Colonial Economy

The occupation consolidated an economy organized around the export of long-staple cotton. Cotton grew in the Nile Delta and increasingly in Upper Egypt and was shipped to British mills; by the early twentieth century it constituted the overwhelming share of Egyptian exports. British administrators prioritized the stabilization of public finance and the expansion of agricultural output. Engineering investment concentrated on irrigation: the repair of the Delta barrages, the extension of perennial canals, drainage, and the construction of the first Aswan dam, completed in 1902. The shift from basin irrigation, described in Chapter 3, to year-round perennial irrigation allowed several crops per year but also encouraged waterlogging and salinization, and bound cultivators more tightly to the cotton market.

Who benefited? Cromer's administration did balance the budget, reduced the worst abuses of tax collection, and curtailed the use of forced labor on canals, the corvée, in the late 1880s. Large landowners, including members of the Turco-Egyptian elite, village notables, and foreign companies, profited from expanding cotton acreage, and the 1890s and early 1900s saw rising land values and speculation. The small cultivator was more exposed. Small holdings were often mortgaged to moneylenders and foreign land banks at high interest; taxation, though restructured, remained heavy; and the dependence on one export crop made households vulnerable to price swings, a vulnerability exposed in the financial crisis of 1907. The Agricultural Bank, created in 1902, extended some credit but did not reach all cultivators.

Other economic changes reflected imperial priorities. Railways and telegraph lines were extended to serve the cotton trade and military needs. The Suez Canal, in which Egypt had sold its shares, produced revenues for its shareholders, not the Egyptian treasury, a source of enduring resentment. Government spending on education and industry was kept limited, partly because Cromer favored agricultural development and low expenditure, and partly because the debt service and the foreign-supervised parts of the budget constrained the government's room to maneuver. Egyptian economic growth during the occupation was real, but its distribution was unequal and its structure left the country dependent on a single crop and on foreign markets.

Section 53.7

Egyptian Society under Occupation: Cities, Villages, Education, and Social Change

The occupation altered the composition and conditions of the civil service. British officials, relatively few in number but strategically placed, filled senior advisory and technical posts, while Egyptians staffed the lower and middle ranks. Egyptian-educated clerks, teachers, lawyers, and journalists, often called the effendiyya, formed an expanding professional class whose prospects were limited by British control of senior posts and by an education policy that restricted access: government schools charged fees, and expenditure on education remained low compared with spending on irrigation. The English language gained prestige as a gateway to employment. A parallel network of private, religious, and missionary schools, including the Muslim and Coptic communal institutions, filled some of the gap.

The legal system became increasingly complex. National Courts, created in 1883 on a model drawn partly from French law, handled most Egyptian cases, while the Mixed Courts addressed cases involving foreigners and the consular courts retained jurisdiction under the capitulations. Cairo and Alexandria grew rapidly, with cosmopolitan communities of Greeks, Italians, Armenians, Levantine Christians, and Jews alongside Egyptian Muslims and Copts. The countryside changed more slowly: villages remained governed by headmen and local notables, but taxation, irrigation scheduling, conscription practice, and the cotton calendar tied village life to the state and the market in new ways. Public health efforts, including responses to cholera epidemics, expanded state involvement in everyday life, though their reach was uneven.

Women's experiences varied by class. Urban elite women gained greater access to schooling and to discussion of women's roles in print; peasant women's labor in cotton cultivation and household production was central to the rural economy but left little documentary trace. This section concentrates on the social consequences of imperial rule. The broader flowering of Arabic print culture, literature, and reform thought belongs to Chapter 51, and Chapter 54 addresses the political ideologies that developed alongside it.

Section 53.8

Opposition to British Rule: Denshawai, Political Mobilization, and Egyptian Protest

Opposition to British rule took different forms. Some Egyptians sought to work within the system and press for gradual change; others demanded immediate evacuation. In the late 1890s and 1900s a generation of Egyptian political leaders emerged who combined legal and journalistic skills with fierce criticism of the occupation. Mustafa Kamil, a young lawyer educated partly in France, mounted a campaign at home and in Europe against British rule, founding the newspaper al-Liwa' in 1900 and, in 1907, the National Party. Others, including Ahmad Lutfi al-Sayyid and the circle around the Umma Party founded in 1907, emphasized education, constitutional development, and Egyptian self-reliance as the basis for eventual independence.

The Denshawai incident of 1906 transformed the political climate. On 13 June a group of British officers on a pigeon-shooting excursion near the Delta village of Denshawai became involved in an altercation with villagers after a fire and the wounding of a woman; a British officer, fleeing in the heat, died, and a villager was killed. A special tribunal, set up under British direction, tried the villagers in a few days. At the end of June it sentenced four to death by hanging, with others flogged or imprisoned, and the sentences were carried out in the village itself. The punishment shocked many Egyptians and drew criticism in Britain. Cromer defended the verdicts as necessary to deter violence against British personnel; critics regarded them as vindictive and unjust. The episode became one of the most enduring symbols of imperial injustice in Egyptian memory.

Cromer retired in 1907. His successor, Sir Eldon Gorst, attempted conciliation, working with the khedive, Abbas Hilmi II, and relaxing some controls, though this earned him criticism from British officials. Political life continued to develop. Mustafa Kamil died in 1908; Muhammad Farid, who succeeded him, pressed the National Party's demands, and was prosecuted and exiled. Tensions also arose among Egyptians: Muslim and Coptic leaders disagreed on tactics and representation, and in 1910 the Coptic prime minister Butrus Ghali was assassinated by a nationalist. Sir Herbert Kitchener, who became consul-general in 1911, ruled more firmly, and a new legislative assembly of 1913 offered limited representation. By 1914, Egyptian opposition was organized, vocal, and divided, and the political agenda of the war years and of 1919 (Chapter 60) was beginning to take shape. This chapter does not retell the wider intellectual history of nationalism, which belongs to Chapter 54.

Section 53.9

From Occupation to Protectorate: Egypt in 1914

The outbreak of the First World War in August 1914 immediately placed Egypt in an awkward position. Its nominal sovereign, the Ottoman sultan, entered the war against Britain at the end of October. Britain declared martial law, reinforced its garrison, and had to decide what to do with a province whose legal master was now an enemy. The khedive, Abbas Hilmi II, was in Istanbul at the time and was suspected of pro-Ottoman sympathies.

On 18 December 1914 Britain declared a protectorate over Egypt, terminating Ottoman suzerainty. The next day, 19 December, the British authorities deposed Abbas Hilmi II and installed his uncle, Hussein Kamil, with the title of sultan. These two acts are distinct and should not be confused: the first changed Egypt's international status, the second changed its ruler and his title. Contemporary proclamations of both dates survive. The declaration ended the veil, formally recognizing what had been practice for thirty-two years. It also raised hopes, promptly disappointed, that Britain would respect Egyptian aspirations in return for wartime cooperation. This chapter ends the Egyptian narrative here, at the transition into wartime rule; the war and its consequences, including the revolution of 1919, belong to Chapters 55 and 60.

Division II — Sudan: The Mahdist Revolution and Anglo-Egyptian Conquest · Section 53.10

Sudan under Egyptian Rule: Conquest, Taxation, and Social Conflict

The Mahdist revolution cannot be understood without the regime it overthrew. Muhammad Ali's armies conquered the Nile Valley south of Egypt in 1820–1821, seeking gold, slaves for his army, and control over trade. Egyptian rule, remembered in Sudan as the Turkiyya, was established from a new administrative center at Khartoum, at the confluence of the Blue and White Niles. The conquest met resistance; in 1822 the ruler of the Ja'aliyyin, Mek Nimr, killed a son of Muhammad Ali, and the punitive campaign that followed left a lasting memory of violence. Over the following decades the government built an administrative system of provinces, garrisons, and tax collection, and later expanded south and west, annexing Darfur in 1874.

Taxation and military recruitment were among the sources of discontent. Taxes were assessed on agricultural implements such as water wheels, on livestock, and on trade, and were collected by troops and local intermediaries with frequent abuses. The army relied heavily on enslaved soldiers drawn from the south and the Nuba mountains. The slave trade was central to the regional economy: northern Sudanese riverine merchants, known as jallaba, and powerful traders such as Zubayr Rahma Mansur built commercial and military enterprises in the Bahr al-Ghazal that were inseparable from slave raiding. Under Isma'il, the Egyptian government, responding to British pressure, launched antislavery campaigns, and appointed European officials, notably Charles Gordon as governor-general (1877–1880). An Anglo-Egyptian convention of 1877 outlawed the slave trade. For Sudanese merchants, slave-owning elites, and others whose livelihoods depended on the trade, suppression threatened economic ruin, and since the same administration collected taxes and conscripted soldiers, antislavery action could not easily be separated from the burdens of Egyptian rule.

Regions differed. The riverine Nile Valley was most directly governed and most heavily taxed. Kordofan and Darfur in the west were linked to trans-Saharan and Chadian networks; the east, including Beja pastoralists, was partially controlled; the south was reached by trade and raiding more than by administration. Religious institutions complicated the picture: some Sufi orders, such as the Khatmiyya, cooperated with Egyptian authorities, while others kept their distance. Students should therefore avoid treating Sudan as a single society: it was a patchwork of regions with different relations to the government, which helps explain both why a movement of religious renewal could spread quickly and why it would later struggle to govern so diverse a territory.

Section 53.11

Muhammad Ahmad and the Origins of the Mahdist Movement

Muhammad Ahmad ibn Abdallah was born in the 1840s in the Dongola region of the northern Nile, into a family of boatbuilders who claimed descent from the Prophet. He received a religious education, joined the Sammaniyya Sufi order, and became a respected teacher and ascetic, settling on Aba Island in the White Nile. Disillusioned with the worldly compromises of religious leaders who accommodated the government, he began to preach moral reform and the revival of the early Muslim community's practice. In June 1881 he announced that he was the Mahdi, the guided one whom many Muslim traditions expected at the end of time to restore justice. The Egyptian authorities, who regarded the claim as a threat, sent a force to arrest him; it was defeated, and in the later part of 1881 Muhammad Ahmad moved, in imitation of the Prophet's emigration, to the Nuba Mountains of Kordofan.

Why did his message resonate? Several kinds of discontent converged. Religious reformers and ordinary believers saw in the Egyptian regime a corrupt Turkish government that ruled in the name of Islam but taxed and exploited its subjects. Merchants and slave traders harmed by antislavery measures, cultivators burdened by taxes, and pastoral groups such as the Baqqara cattle herders of Kordofan and Darfur, whose prominent member Abdallahi ibn Muhammad joined the Mahdi in this period, each had their own reasons to join. Regional alliances were crucial: the movement succeeded in part because local leaders saw advantage in supporting it. Followers, called the Ansar (helpers), took the name used for the Prophet's supporters at Medina.

The Mahdi's program was both religious and political. He proclaimed a return to the practice of the Prophet, denounced innovations, abolished the taxes imposed by the Egyptian government in favor of the Islamic alms-tax, and insisted that jihad was obligatory against those who refused his message. He relativized the authority of Sufi orders and the established scholars, and demanded absolute allegiance to himself as the divinely guided leader. His movement thus offered religious legitimacy, relief from onerous taxation, and a platform for regional ambitions at once. The combination of these elements explains the force of the movement without reducing it to any one cause.

Historical Vocabulary
Mahdi, Ansar, and Mahdiyya

The Mahdi ("the rightly guided one") is a figure expected in many Muslim traditions to appear before the end of time to restore justice and true religion. Belief in a Mahdi is not uniform across Islamic traditions, and many Muslim scholars rejected claimants. Muhammad Ahmad's proclamation of 1881 belongs to a long history of Mahdist claims in Islamic societies.

The Ansar were his followers, named for the Prophet's helpers at Medina. The Mahdiyya refers both to the movement and, more broadly, to the state it created (1881–1898). Students should avoid equating "Mahdist" with "fanatical," a word used by many European writers of the period; the movement was a religious and political formation with its own law, fiscal system, and officials.

Section 53.12

The Mahdist Revolution and the Fall of Khartoum

The Mahdist forces won a series of striking victories. In January 1883 they took El Obeid, the capital of Kordofan. Egyptian authorities, in the midst of the political crisis that culminated in the British occupation of Egypt, assembled a poorly trained force under a retired British officer, William Hicks, known as Hicks Pasha. In November 1883 at Shaykan, south of El Obeid, the Mahdists annihilated it. The victory persuaded many wavering groups of the movement's strength and had far-reaching political consequences, because it forced the British government in Egypt to decide whether to fight for Sudan or abandon it.

London chose withdrawal, and in early 1884 sent Charles Gordon to Khartoum to organize the evacuation of Egyptian garrisons and officials. Gordon reached the city in February. Instead of simply withdrawing, he remained, and by March Mahdist forces had besieged Khartoum. Gordon's reasons are debated, as is the British government's slowness in organizing a relief expedition. A column under Wolseley moved up the Nile in the autumn, and on 26 January 1885, two days before the relief force arrived, Mahdist troops stormed the city. Gordon was killed. Other Egyptian garrisons surrendered over the following months, and Egyptian and British forces withdrew northward to the vicinity of Wadi Halfa.

These events can be told from several perspectives. For the British public, Gordon's death became a national tragedy and a cause of political outrage against Gladstone's government. For Egyptian officials and soldiers, the loss of Sudan meant the collapse of a long-standing imperial enterprise and, for many, captivity or death. For Sudanese supporters of the Mahdi, the fall of Khartoum was a vindication of a religious cause that had overthrown a foreign regime. The Mahdi himself died in June 1885, probably of typhus, only a few months after his greatest triumph. His movement survived.

Section 53.13

The Mahdist State under Khalifa Abdallahi

Before his death the Mahdi had designated Abdallahi ibn Muhammad, a Baqqara from Darfur, as his khalifa, or successor. Abdallahi's authority was contested by the Mahdi's kinsmen, the ashraf, and by riverine followers who resented the rise of a westerner. He consolidated power over several years by relying on kinsmen and the Baqqara, building a loyal military force, and suppressing rivals. The state took Omdurman, on the west bank opposite the ruins of Khartoum, as its capital. The Mahdist state was not simply a continuation of conquest; it had a government, a treasury (the bayt al-mal), courts applying law derived from the Mahdi's teaching, its own coinage, and a corps of governors and military commanders in the provinces.

Communications held the state together. Letters carried orders between Omdurman and provincial amirs; riverboats and captured steamers moved men and goods; and tax and tribute flowed to the capital. The Mahdiyya also had a foreign policy. In 1887–1889 its forces clashed with Ethiopia, culminating in the battle of Gallabat in 1889 in which the Ethiopian emperor Yohannes IV was killed. In 1889 a Mahdist army marching on Egypt was defeated at Toski by an Egyptian force under British officers, ending hopes of expansion northward. Other expeditions sought control in the west and southwest, though Darfur's revolts against Omdurman and local resistance elsewhere meant that authority was never uniform.

Historians have shifted from earlier colonial portraits of Mahdist rule as arbitrary despotism to analysis of how the state actually operated, drawing on its administrative records and letters. Such studies show a regime that struggled with the tensions between religious ideals, military needs, and the economic demands of governing a vast territory. Students should therefore treat the state as an institution to be explained, rather than merely as a backdrop to reconquest.

Section 53.14

Society, Religion, Economy, and Conflict in the Mahdist State

The Mahdist economy suffered from both warfare and environment. Trade with Egypt and Europe was cut, though networks with Ethiopia, Chad, and the Red Sea persisted in altered form. The state's fiscal demands, the movement of large populations to Omdurman, and the requisition of grain during years of poor rains contributed to severe famine around 1889–1892, accompanied by disease; population loss in some regions was heavy, though the scale cannot be measured precisely, because the figures come from observers with political reasons for exaggeration or minimization. Agriculture continued along the Nile and in the central plains, and the government taxed according to Islamic categories, such as the alms-tax, while also resorting to requisitions.

Social life reflected religious reform and military priorities. The Mahdi's regulations governed prayer, dress, and public behavior, restricted marriage payments, and prohibited tobacco and other practices he regarded as sinful. Women experienced the regime in different ways: some gained from limits on marriage costs, while others, particularly captured or enslaved women, were subjected to servitude within households of the elite. Slavery did not disappear under the Mahdiyya; the regime condemned the Egyptian antislavery campaign and the practice persisted, while enslaved men served in its armies. Relations among communities varied. Some groups, such as the Baqqara, held privileged positions; others, such as the Ja'aliyyin of the river, were suspected of disloyalty and were treated harshly in the 1890s.

The regime also faced internal dissent and external threat. The Khalifa's dependence on kinsmen alienated many; provincial leaders sometimes resisted; and the famine and military conscription generated resentment. Religious scholars who did not accept the Mahdi's claims were persecuted or pressed into compliance. Scholarship on the Mahdiyya has rejected colonial descriptions of it as inherently disordered, but it also acknowledges real violence, coercion, and suffering. The task for students is to hold both in view: a state with institutions and ideals, and a state that imposed heavy costs on those it ruled.

Section 53.15

The Anglo-Egyptian Reconquest and the Battle of Omdurman

For more than a decade Britain left Sudan alone. The decision to reconquer was taken in 1896, in a changed international environment. Italy's catastrophic defeat by Ethiopia at Adwa in March 1896 raised fears that Mahdist forces might exploit the situation; more importantly, Britain feared that France, which was exploring routes toward the upper Nile from the west, might establish itself there and threaten Egypt's water supply and the British position. A British-led campaign under Horatio Herbert Kitchener, commander (sirdar) of the Egyptian army, began that year. Egyptian troops, financed by the Egyptian treasury, formed the bulk of the force, supported by British units. Kitchener advanced methodically, building a military railway across the desert to supply the army, and took Dongola in 1896.

In April 1898 the army defeated Mahdist forces at the Atbara. On 2 September 1898, at Kerreri north of Omdurman, the Anglo-Egyptian army, armed with rifles, machine guns, artillery, and gunboats, defeated the Khalifa's much larger army in an open battle. Mahdist losses were heavy; estimates, which come largely from British sources, vary widely and should be read with caution. Kitchener's army entered Omdurman, and he ordered the destruction of the Mahdi's tomb and the removal of the Mahdi's remains, actions that outraged many and were criticized in Britain. The Khalifa escaped and was killed in November 1899.

While the army was at Omdurman, a small French expedition under Jean-Baptiste Marchand had reached Fashoda on the upper Nile in July 1898. Kitchener went south and confronted Marchand in September. The Fashoda crisis brought Britain and France close to war, but France, isolated and divided at home, withdrew in November. A declaration of March 1899 delimited spheres of influence. The crisis was a diplomatic confrontation between European powers; it should be distinguished from the military destruction of the Mahdist state, which had already been achieved by Anglo-Egyptian arms.

Section 53.16

The Anglo-Egyptian Condominium of 1899

After the reconquest, Britain faced the question of what to call its authority. Annexation would have affronted Egypt's nominal sovereign, the Ottoman sultan, and ignored the fact that Egyptian soldiers and money had contributed to the campaign; leaving Sudan to Egypt alone would have left Britain without the control it sought. The solution was the Anglo-Egyptian Condominium, established by an agreement concluded on 19 January 1899 between the British and Egyptian governments, negotiated by Cromer. It applied to Sudan south of the twenty-second parallel.

The agreement provided that the British and Egyptian flags would be flown together and that supreme military and civil authority would be vested in a governor-general, appointed by the khedive on the recommendation of the British government. Kitchener became the first governor-general, followed in 1899 by Sir Reginald Wingate. The capitulations and Mixed Courts were excluded from Sudan, and the slave trade was prohibited. The condominium was legally presented as shared governance. In practice, it was operated by an overwhelmingly British administration: the governor-general was British, senior officials came from a British-recruited Sudan Political Service, and Egyptians occupied subordinate roles in the army and civil service. Egypt paid much of the costs in the early years. The term "condominium," implying joint sovereignty, thus describes the legal form more accurately than the practical distribution of power, a point students should keep in view.

Section 53.17

Sudan under the Condominium: Administration, Economy, and Provincial Society

The new administration rebuilt infrastructure and imposed order. A railway linked the Egyptian border to Khartoum by 1899 and later to the Red Sea, where Port Sudan, built after 1905, was opened around 1909. Khartoum was rebuilt as a colonial capital. Gordon Memorial College, founded in 1902, trained a small class of Sudanese for clerical and technical roles. Taxation was reintroduced, and agricultural experiments, including cotton schemes in places such as the Tokar delta, tested what would later become a larger irrigation economy; the Gezira Scheme and the independence movements of the twentieth century belong to later chapters.

Administration varied. In the north, British officers, working with tribal and religious leaders, governed through local intermediaries. British authorities also showed a wariness of the Mahdist legacy: Mahdism was suppressed publicly while the family of the Mahdi, notably his posthumous son Abd al-Rahman, was gradually allowed a place; revolts such as that of Wad Habuba in 1908 reminded officials that Mahdist feeling persisted. The Khatmiyya leadership of Sayyid Ali al-Mirghani, which had opposed the Mahdi, was favored. Darfur remained outside the condominium under Sultan Ali Dinar, who re-established his sultanate in 1898 and was overthrown only in 1916.

The south was the least governed. British patrols and officials suppressed slave raiding and established posts, but many communities, including Dinka, Nuer, Shilluk, and Azande peoples, experienced the condominium largely through punitive expeditions and occasional taxation. Effective colonial authority was uneven, and a distinct southern policy emerged only after the First World War. This section stops around 1914. Students should note that, in 1914, Sudan was a territory of very different degrees of control, with a strong British-led center and fragile peripheries.

Division III — Libya: Italian Expansion and the Struggle for Control · Section 53.18

Ottoman Libya and the Rise of Italian Colonial Ambitions

"Libya" was not a unified political unit in 1900. Italian geographers revived the ancient regional name before the invasion—Federico Minutilli used it in his Bibliografia della Libia (1903)—and the Italian government applied it to its new colonial claim after the invasion of 1911. The territory comprised three regions with distinct histories: Tripolitania in the northwest, with its port city of Tripoli and agricultural and caravan connections; Cyrenaica in the east, with Benghazi, the Green Mountain (Jabal Akhdar), and extensive pastoral and tribal communities; and Fezzan in the southwest, a desert region of oases linked to trans-Saharan trade. After the end of the Karamanli dynasty in 1835 the Ottomans had governed the coast directly, as a province, with garrisons, tax officials, and local notables. Their authority in the interior was often negotiated with tribal and religious leaders, a pattern similar to that Chapter 47 describes for other Ottoman provinces. Commerce in grain, livestock, esparto grass, and sponges, and the caravan trade to Sudan and Lake Chad, connected the regions to the Mediterranean and Africa.

The Sanusi order was the most important indigenous institution of the east. Founded by Muhammad ibn Ali al-Sanusi, an Algerian-born scholar who settled in Cyrenaica in the 1840s, the Sanusiyya was a reformist Sufi brotherhood that organized a network of lodges (zawiyas) among tribal communities, providing religious education, arbitration, trade facilities, and agricultural and caravan support. It was both a religious movement and a quasi-governmental structure, especially in Cyrenaica and the Sahara. By the late nineteenth century the order extended deep into the desert toward Chad, where it encountered French expansion. Its leaders pursued a careful relationship with the Ottomans, who valued its stabilizing influence.

Italy had not been a major colonial power. Unified only in 1861–1870, it had suffered the humiliation of France's seizure of Tunisia in 1881, a territory in which Italians were numerous and which Italian nationalists had hoped to claim. Its early African expansion included Eritrea and Somalia; an attempt to extend Italian rule into Ethiopia was defeated at Adwa in 1896. Italian opinion, shaped by nationalist writers, spoke of a Mediterranean empire and invoked the memory of Rome's North African provinces. Italian governments negotiated agreements with France (1900–1902) and others that recognized Italian interests in Tripoli and Cyrenaica in return for support for French designs in Morocco. Commercial penetration advanced through the Banco di Roma, which opened branches in Libya and invested in enterprises there, and through schools and missions. By 1911, prime minister Giovanni Giolitti's government, facing domestic pressure and fearing that a collapsing Ottoman Empire or rival powers might preempt Italy, judged the moment ripe. This section avoids repeating the general account of Ottoman provincial reform in Chapter 50 and the introduction of Tripolitania and Cyrenaica in Chapter 52.

Section 53.19

The Italian Invasion of 1911

In late September 1911, taking advantage of the diplomatic distraction of the Agadir crisis and of Ottoman weakness following the Young Turk revolution of 1908 and subsequent turmoil, Italy presented the Ottoman government with an ultimatum demanding that it accept Italian occupation of Tripolitania and Cyrenaica. The Ottoman reply, offering concessions short of occupation, was rejected, and Italy declared war on 29 September. Italian warships bombarded Tripoli in early October, and on 5 October marines occupied the city; Benghazi, Derna, Tobruk, and Homs fell over the following weeks. In November a royal decree proclaimed Italian sovereignty over the provinces, a declaration of legal title that preceded effective control.

Italian planners expected an easy victory. Their propaganda spoke of a "liberation" of the population from Ottoman rule, and some predicted that Arab inhabitants would welcome the occupiers. These expectations proved mistaken. The Ottoman garrison in Tripoli was small, but when it withdrew inland, local fighters and Ottoman officers regrouped and Libyan communities, from coastal towns to the interior, took up arms. Italy had to send reinforcements, eventually in the tens of thousands, and discovered that holding the coast required constant military effort. The belief that the war would be short was wrong because it misjudged the strength of local political and religious solidarities and the willingness of Libyans to fight in defense of land and faith.

Section 53.20

Ottoman, Sanusi, and Local Resistance to Italian Conquest

Resistance was not a single centralized movement. Ottoman officers, some sent secretly through Egypt and Tunisia because Britain and France restricted the passage of Ottoman troops and Italy controlled the sea, played an important role in organizing fighters. Among them were young officers who would later become prominent in Turkish and Arab politics, including Enver Bey and Mustafa Kemal, as well as the Egyptian-born Aziz Ali al-Misri. They worked with local leaders and tribes, often using the Ottoman administrative framework and an appeal to defend Muslim land under the sultan-caliph. In Tripolitania, regional leaders such as Sulayman al-Baruni of the western mountain, Ramadan al-Suwayhli of Misurata, and other notables from the mountain and coastal districts organized forces that engaged the Italian columns around Tripoli.

In Cyrenaica the Sanusi order was central. Its leader, Sayyid Ahmad al-Sharif, had earlier fought French forces in Chad and now led the order's followers and allied tribes against the Italians, supplying fighters and organization through its lodges and networks. The order's reach in Cyrenaica allowed a degree of coordination, but it should not be portrayed as a state or as speaking for all Libyans. Tribal alliances shifted; local leaders balanced the order, the Ottomans, and their own interests; and Tripolitanian and Cyrenaican resistance developed separately, with different leaders and different relationships to the Ottomans. Understanding the distinctions among Ottoman, Sanusi, and local forces explains both why resistance was durable and why it was difficult to bring to an end by a single treaty.

Section 53.21

Colonial Warfare, Reprisals, and the Experiences of Libyan Civilians

The fighting at Shara al-Shatt, near Tripoli, on 23 October 1911, marked a turning point. Italian troops holding the oasis perimeter were attacked by Ottoman and local forces, and some Italian soldiers were killed, and reports of mutilation circulated; Italian accounts also claimed that local residents of the oasis had joined the attack. The Italian response was a brutal reprisal in the following days, in which troops killed large numbers of civilians in the oasis and surrounding areas; precise casualty figures cannot be established, because the sources are Italian military and press reports, accounts by foreign correspondents, and later Libyan testimony, which differ widely. The episode was reported by European journalists and gave rise to condemnation in parts of the European press, while Italian authorities justified the actions as responses to treachery.

Reprisal was followed by systematic measures against suspected opponents. Thousands of Libyans, including notables and others accused of involvement in the resistance, were arrested and deported to Italian islands, among them the Tremiti, Ustica, Ponza, and others; many died of disease and hardship in detention. Bombardment of coastal and interior villages, destruction of property, and control of movement affected civilian life. These measures illustrate the relationship between military occupation and colonial punishment: the failure to win quickly produced policies aimed at intimidating the population. For Libyan families, the war meant displacement, loss of kin, and flight to Tunisia, Egypt, or the interior. Italian soldiers themselves lived in harsh conditions, and diseases such as cholera affected both sides.

Section 53.22

The Treaty of Ouchy in 1912 and the Limits of Italian Victory

The war was ended not in Libya but elsewhere. Italy extended the conflict to the Aegean, seizing the Dodecanese islands in 1912 and threatening the Dardanelles, while the Ottoman Empire faced the outbreak of the Balkan crisis. Under pressure, the Ottoman government accepted a settlement in October 1912, signed near Lausanne at Ouchy. Under its terms the Ottoman government withdrew its officers and forces, while the Sultan issued a decree granting Libya autonomy, preserving the religious authority of the caliph. Italy, for its part, treated the arrangement as recognition of its sovereignty and proclaimed it as such. Ottoman and Italian interpretations of the texts diverged; the Ottomans stressed the religious and symbolic ties, Italy the transfer of sovereignty.

The treaty ended the Italo-Ottoman war but not the fighting. Many Libyan fighters refused to regard themselves as bound by an agreement concluded by the Ottoman government, and resistance continued, now with less outside support and less coordination. Italy had occupied major coastal towns, but the interior of Tripolitania, Cyrenaica, and Fezzan remained beyond its control. The distinction between a diplomatic settlement and effective territorial conquest is central: the Italian state held legal claims recognized, in time, by the powers, but its army controlled little beyond the range of its coastal garrisons.

Section 53.23

Libya on the Eve of the First World War

By 1914 Italy held the principal ports and had advanced inland in some areas, extending its hold into parts of Fezzan, including Murzuq, and expanding in the Tripolitanian hinterland. Yet its control was thin. Armed resistance persisted in Tripolitania and Cyrenaica, where local leaders and the Sanusi continued to command loyalty, and the Italian administration's effort to govern through officials, police, and garrisons had little reach beyond the coast. The war had disrupted trade, destroyed property, and driven many people from their homes, while the settlement of Italian colonists, an ambition of nationalist writers, had barely begun.

Italy's claim to Libya therefore remained militarily and politically insecure. The outbreak of the First World War, and Italy's entry in 1915, would soon reveal how fragile the position was, as Italian forces withdrew to the coast in Tripolitania. The later campaigns of Omar Mukhtar, the Italian reconquest, and the fascist-era repression belong to subsequent chapters. For this chapter the point is that, in 1914, Libya was a colony largely on paper, an Italian claim resting on coastal enclaves and a treaty, and a society in arms.

Division IV — Morocco: European Rivalry and the Establishment of Protectorates · Section 53.24

Morocco before the Protectorates: Sultanate, Society, and Political Authority

Morocco was not an Ottoman province, and its distinctive history must be clear from the outset. Ruled since the seventeenth century by the Alaouite dynasty, a family of sharifs, descendants of the Prophet, the sultanate had survived the Ottoman expansion and the Spanish and Portuguese coastal presences. Its ruler combined several kinds of authority: he was a sultan commanding a government and army, a sharif whose lineage conferred religious prestige, and, in the language of allegiance, the Commander of the Faithful, to whom communities pledged loyalty through the oath of bay'a. The sultan's capitals were Fez, Marrakesh, Meknes, and Rabat, between which the court moved, and the diplomatic center was Tangier, where foreign consuls resided.

The government was called the makhzen. It comprised the sultan's household, ministers, secretaries, governors (qa'ids), tax collectors, and an army that included regular troops and tribal contingents. Religious scholars (ulama), especially those of the Qarawiyyin mosque-university in Fez, urban notables, merchants, and the heads of Sufi lodges (zawiyas) formed essential parts of the political order. Beyond the cities, tribes and confederations, both Arabic-speaking and Amazigh-speaking, managed their affairs through local assemblies and customary law and negotiated with the sultan through gifts, taxes, military service, and periodic expeditions (harkas) that displayed royal power. Jewish communities, living in urban quarters (mellahs) and in the countryside, played important roles in trade and under the sultan's protection.

European writers, especially French, divided Morocco into a "land of government" (bilad al-makhzen), where the sultan's rule was effective, and a "land of dissidence" (bilad al-siba), where it was not. Historians now regard this division as an oversimplification that served colonial purposes by suggesting the sultan's authority was fragile and the interior ungoverned. In practice, relations between the sultan and distant communities were negotiated and varied over time, and allegiance to the sultan as sharif often persisted even when taxes were withheld. Students should therefore read colonial descriptions of Moroccan "anarchy" critically. They should also understand the diversity of regional economies: Atlantic ports such as Essaouira and Casablanca, grain-producing plains, the Atlas and Rif mountains, and caravan trade across the Sahara.

Historical Vocabulary
Makhzen

The makhzen (from the Arabic for "storehouse," reflecting its origin as the treasury) is the Moroccan term for the sultan's government: the court, officials, army, and the networks of power around the ruler. The word is not a precise synonym for "state" or "central government," because the makhzen governed partly through negotiation with local leaders and institutions.

Its importance for this chapter is twofold. First, French protectorate policy later preserved the makhzen's forms while taking control of its functions. Second, the makhzen's continued existence explains why the protectorate was a "protectorate" and not a colony. Students should not treat the word as a pejorative label for "traditional" rule, as colonial writers sometimes did.

Section 53.25

Trade, Finance, Foreign Privileges, and the Erosion of Moroccan Autonomy

Moroccan autonomy eroded through commerce and finance well before any soldier took permanent control. Commercial treaties, notably the Anglo-Moroccan treaty of 1856, opened ports to European trade at low tariffs, and European merchants and consuls expanded their presence in coastal cities. The Spanish-Moroccan war of 1859–1860 ended in a Spanish victory and a large indemnity, which Morocco financed through a loan and the pledge of customs revenue, an early example of the pattern seen in Egypt. A growing number of Moroccans, mainly merchants and agents, gained the status of protégés, by which they came under the jurisdiction and protection of foreign consulates and escaped Moroccan taxation. The Madrid Convention of 1880, an effort by Sultan Hasan I to limit abuses of the system, instead confirmed the principle of foreign protection and extended foreign rights, including the right of foreigners to acquire property.

Hasan I (r. 1873–1894) tried to strengthen the state through military reform, arms purchases, and the recruitment of foreign instructors, and by moving his army around the country to assert authority. Costs rose, however, and revenues from customs and taxes did not keep pace with spending. After his death in 1894, the throne passed to the young Abd al-Aziz, with the grand vizier Ba Ahmad acting as regent until 1900. Foreign loans in 1902, 1903, and especially 1904, the last from French banks and secured on a large share of customs revenue, placed Moroccan finances under foreign supervision. By the early 1900s, as in Egypt, financial concessions and diplomatic privileges had weakened the government's autonomy before the protectorates were imposed.

Section 53.26

Reform, Dynastic Conflict, and Moroccan Responses to European Pressure

Abd al-Aziz's reign (1894–1908) is often remembered through European accounts that emphasized his fascination with Western gadgets and his supposed frivolity. Such portraits drew on hostile sources and obscured genuine attempts at reform. He introduced a new tax, the tertib, in 1901, replacing a system of irregular exactions with a fixed levy on agricultural land and livestock, applying it, in principle, to everyone including notables and protégés. The effort to modernize taxation and the army was constrained by lack of resources, by opposition from tribal leaders and religious scholars who regarded the tax as unlawful, and by foreign pressure. Revolts broke out: a pretender known as Bu Hmara (al-Jilali al-Zarhuni) held much of the northeast from 1902 until his capture in 1909, and local strongmen such as the brigand-chief Raisuli in the north, who kidnapped foreigners in 1904 and later, pursued their own ends.

Moroccans debated how to respond. Some urged reform along modern lines, including the creation of constitutional restraints on the sultan; others insisted on strict adherence to religious law and opposed concessions to foreign powers. Many resented the sultan's willingness to rely on foreign loans and advisers. The crisis of 1907 brought these arguments to a head. After the French landing at Casablanca (§53.28), Abd al-Aziz's government appeared to be unable to defend the country, and his brother Abd al-Hafid, a regional governor in Marrakesh, proclaimed himself sultan in August 1907. In January 1908 the ulama of Fez recognized him, conditioning their allegiance on his promise to resist foreign encroachment and reform the government. Abd al-Hafid defeated his brother in the south in August 1908 and was recognized by the European powers in January 1909, after he accepted the Algeciras Act.

Moroccan actors had agency in this story: the struggle for legitimacy was a Moroccan conflict, fought over how a Muslim sultan should respond to foreign encroachment. It also illustrates the limits of that agency. Once in power, Abd al-Hafid faced the same financial constraints, signing new loan agreements in 1910 that deepened Morocco's dependence, and found that the nationalist and religious program that had brought him to the throne was difficult to carry out. His reputation among many of his former supporters suffered accordingly.

Section 53.27

International Rivalry: The Entente Cordiale, Tangier Crisis, and Algeciras Conference

Morocco became a European diplomatic crisis because of its location, resources, and the rivalry of the powers. France, already ruling neighboring Algeria and Tunisia, sought to complete a North African empire. Spain held coastal enclaves and wanted a zone of influence in the north. Britain wished to protect the Strait of Gibraltar and had interests in trade. Germany, a newcomer to colonial competition, sought commercial opportunities and diplomatic leverage. In April 1904 Britain and France concluded the Entente Cordiale, settling colonial disputes: in return for French acceptance of the British position in Egypt (which allowed Britain to loosen the financial controls described in §53.2), Britain recognized France's special interest in Morocco. A secret Franco-Spanish agreement the same year outlined the division of Moroccan territory.

France then pressed Morocco for a program of reforms under French supervision. A French mission to Fez in early 1905 presented proposals that, if accepted, would have given France predominant influence. An assembly of Moroccan notables, convened by the sultan, rejected them. Germany, which had not been consulted, intervened: on 31 March 1905 Emperor Wilhelm II landed at Tangier and declared support for the sultan's independence and for equal commercial opportunity, a gesture aimed at testing and splitting the Entente. The result was an international conference at Algeciras, Spain, in 1906. Its Act, signed on 7 April, reaffirmed the sultan's sovereignty and the integrity of his domains and the principle of commercial equality, but it also created a State Bank of Morocco, set customs and arms-trade regulations, and authorized French and Spanish officers to organize police in eight ports. Germany's isolation at the conference strengthened the Anglo-French alignment. The agreements progressively restricted Moroccan sovereignty even as they proclaimed its preservation.

Section 53.28

French Military Expansion and the Crisis of Moroccan Sovereignty

France expanded its position by force after 1906. In March 1907, following the murder of a French doctor in Marrakesh, French troops occupied Oujda in eastern Morocco near the Algerian frontier. In July 1907 workers building a railway for the port at Casablanca were attacked and killed amid local hostility to the foreign presence there; the French navy bombarded the city in early August and landed troops. French officials justified the intervention as a defense of European lives and commercial interests under the Algeciras police arrangements. The bombardment destroyed much of the old city and killed many inhabitants; casualty figures are unreliable, since they come largely from French reports and foreign observers.

The French landing opened a campaign in the surrounding Chaouia region, where tribal communities resisted under local leaders, and French forces under successive generals gradually imposed control through military columns and negotiated submission. The episode provoked opposition across Morocco and discredited Abd al-Aziz, who was perceived as having failed to defend Moroccan territory. It also marked the beginning of a pattern in which France, claiming to protect Europeans and police the ports, extended its effective authority inland. This section distinguishes French arguments for protecting nationals and commerce from the broader expansion of colonial authority they accompanied; historians generally regard the former as a pretext for the latter, though French officials were divided about how far to go. By 1911 French power in Casablanca and the Chaouia, and in the east, was well established.

Section 53.29

The Agadir Crisis of 1911 and the Road to Protectorate Rule

In spring 1911 tribes rose around Fez, and Sultan Abd al-Hafid, besieged in his capital and financially dependent on France, requested aid. A French column advanced from the coast and relieved Fez in May, and France thereafter held the capital in effect. Spain, anxious to secure its own zone, occupied Larache and Ksar el-Kebir in June. Germany, which regarded the French advance as a violation of the Algeciras Act, responded on 1 July 1911 by sending the gunboat Panther to Agadir, on the Atlantic coast, ostensibly to protect German commercial interests. Britain, suspicious of a possible German naval base, supported France, and for some weeks war seemed possible.

The crisis was resolved by negotiation. In a Franco-German treaty of 4 November 1911, Germany recognized France's freedom to establish a protectorate in Morocco, while France ceded part of the French Congo to German Cameroon. The agreement treated Morocco as an object of exchange, which illustrates the way European power politics decided the fate of a society that had not been consulted. Yet the crisis cannot be understood without Moroccan developments: tribal uprisings, the sultan's weakness, and the financial dependence of his government created the conditions that made foreign intervention feasible and gave France its pretext.

Section 53.30

The Treaty of Fez and the Division of Morocco in 1912

On 30 March 1912, in Fez, Sultan Abd al-Hafid signed a treaty with the French representative establishing a French protectorate over the Sherifian Empire. The treaty provided that France would introduce administrative, judicial, educational, economic, financial, and military reforms, that a French resident-general would be the intermediary between the sultan and foreign governments, and that the sultan's religious prestige and customary position would be preserved. The sultan could not conclude international agreements without French consent. A few weeks later, in April, Moroccan soldiers in Fez, resentful of French officers and conditions, mutinied, and the violence spread to the city's population; French forces restored order at heavy cost to Moroccans. Abd al-Hafid abdicated in August 1912 and was succeeded by his brother Moulay Yusuf.

Spain's share was settled in a Franco-Spanish agreement of 27 November 1912. It gave Spain a zone in the north, centered on Tetuan and including the Rif and the Jibala, and a southern zone in the Tarfaya region, with Tangier excluded. Tangier's special position remained unresolved in 1912; its international administrative arrangement was agreed in 1923, not established by the Treaty of Fez. Resistance began immediately. In the south, Ahmad al-Hiba, a son of the Saharan religious leader Ma al-Aynayn, was proclaimed sultan in 1912 and seized Marrakesh, before a French column defeated his forces in September. The treaty and partition thus produced a legally recognized order and immediate armed opposition.

Section 53.31

French and Spanish Protectorate Rule, Moroccan Resistance, and Society before 1914

The first resident-general, General Hubert Lyautey, arrived in 1912 with a policy of preserving the sultan and makhzen as the visible framework of government while directing policy through French officials and military officers. Rabat, not Fez, became the administrative capital, and Casablanca grew into the commercial hub. Lyautey emphasized respect for Moroccan religious institutions, the preservation of old urban quarters beside newly planned European towns, and cooperation with local notables, an approach which also served to secure acceptance. Beneath this policy lay a more extractive reality: land registration regulations in 1913 and policies favoring European settlers and companies began to transfer land and economic opportunities to Europeans, and the protectorate's judicial and fiscal systems were remade under French direction.

In the Spanish zone, the capital was Tetuan, where a Spanish high commissioner governed alongside a Moroccan representative of the sultan, the khalifa. Spanish authority was weaker than the French, constrained by limited resources, and was resisted in the mountains of the Rif and Jibala, where leaders such as Raisuli had their own followings. Resistance elsewhere continued: in the Middle Atlas, Zayyan Amazigh forces led by Mouha ou Hammou opposed French advance until Khenifra was taken in 1914, and French columns pushed to link Fez with Algeria through the Taza corridor. The creation of the protectorates did not mean that European powers immediately controlled every part of Morocco; in 1914, large areas of mountains and desert remained outside effective control. The later Rif War led by Abd el-Krim belongs to subsequent chapters.

Everyday Life and Social History

Cultivators, Households, Civilians, and Communities under Imperial Pressure

Egyptian cultivators. For a cotton-growing family in the Delta, the occupation meant a calendar governed by irrigation schedules, the cotton season, and the arrival of tax collectors. Perennial irrigation permitted more crops but increased labor demands and, over time, waterlogging. Many families depended on credit from moneylenders or merchants, and a poor harvest or a fall in cotton prices could mean the loss of land. The reduction of forced canal labor eased one burden, but cultivators still lived with conscription, taxes, and the authority of village headmen. Women's field and household labor was essential, though seldom recorded.

Mahdist households. In the Mahdiyya, daily life was shaped by religious regulation, requisition, and war. Families who migrated to Omdurman exchanged village life for a crowded capital; grain shortages and famine around 1889–1892 brought hunger and disease. Elite households included enslaved servants and concubines, while women and children captured in campaigns could be assigned to officers. Regional differences were stark between the Baqqara, who enjoyed favored status, and the riverine communities, who were often distrusted.

Libyan civilians under invasion. After October 1911 civilians in Tripoli and its oasis experienced bombardment, house searches, arrests, and reprisals. Families lost men to death or deportation; others fled inland or abroad. Pastoral communities in Cyrenaica faced disruption of grazing and caravan routes as war continued. Oral poetry and later testimony preserved memories of loss that Italian official records seldom record.

Moroccan communities under protectorate rule. In Casablanca and the Chaouia, residents saw the bombardment and the rapid construction of a European port city; in Fez, the 1912 mutiny and its repression left lasting fear. In the countryside, land registration and settler purchases began to affect landholders, while mountain communities defended their autonomy. Urban Jewish and Muslim families alike adapted to new courts, taxes, and foreign commerce.

Historians reconstruct such lives from petitions, court records, press reports, memoirs, oral history, and archaeology, while remembering that official sources were written by those who governed.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Indigenous Political Institutions under Colonial Administration

What Changed

  • Egyptian ministries lost control of finance, army command, and key appointments to British advisers and officers
  • Sudan's Mahdist state was destroyed and replaced by a hybrid regime governed from outside
  • In Libya, Ottoman provincial administration was replaced by Italian claims and coastal garrisons
  • Morocco's sultan retained his throne while a French resident-general controlled foreign relations and policy

What Stayed the Same

  • The khedive, the Moroccan makhzen, and village and tribal leaders continued to exist and to function
  • Religious institutions, including Sufi orders such as the Khatmiyya and Sanusiyya, remained influential
  • Local landowners, notables, and tribal leaders remained intermediaries between rulers and population
  • Customary law and community structures persisted in countryside and mountains

Who experienced change most directly? Senior officials, army officers, and urban elites felt loss of authority first; who experienced greater continuity? Many villagers and pastoralists, for whom the tax collector or tribal leader remained the main point of contact, though the burdens they bore could change sharply. Why the pattern matters: Colonial rulers often governed through existing institutions, which gave them legitimacy and economy but also gave local actors room to shape outcomes.

Historical Debate

Improvised Responses to Crises or the Outcomes of Long-Term Strategic Ambitions?

The question: Were British, French, and Italian occupations in North Africa improvised responses to local crises, or the fulfillment of long-term imperial design? The question arises most sharply for the British occupation of Egypt, but applies across the chapter.

Interpretation A — Crisis and improvisation: Some historians stress that British ministers in 1882 were reluctant imperialists who intervened to protect the canal and restore order, and that many later decisions, such as the retention of the army and the invention of the Condominium, were improvised responses to unfolding circumstances, constrained by rivalry with France and Britain's own promises to withdraw.
Interpretation B — Strategic design: Others argue that the intervention reflected a long-term strategic commitment to securing the route to India and to the economic interests of bondholders and investors, and that British, French, and Italian governments pursued their aims patiently, using finance and diplomacy to prepare takeover. The 1904 Entente and the Franco-Italian agreements fit this reading.

Evidence: The interpretations rest on diplomatic archives, cabinet papers, parliamentary debates, and private correspondence, read against one another and against local developments. A third view, closer to the weight of recent scholarship, holds that the two are not exclusive: long-term strategic interests created the conditions, while the timing and form of intervention depended on contingent crises, including local ones.

Why it matters: If occupation was improvised, local actors' choices appear more decisive; if planned, structural pressures dominate. Either way, an account that omits Egyptian, Sudanese, Libyan, and Moroccan politics is incomplete.

Student task: Choose one case (Egypt 1882, Libya 1911, or Morocco 1912) and identify what evidence would best distinguish improvisation from planning.

Division V — Comparative Historical Synthesis · Section 53.32

Four Societies under Imperial Pressure: Conquest, Sovereignty, and Resistance

By 1914 four different forms of European domination existed. In Egypt, institutions survived under decisive British control, and in December 1914 the occupation became a formal protectorate. In Sudan, the Condominium gave joint sovereignty in form and British predominance in administration. In Libya, Italy's formal claims exceeded its effective control. In Morocco, the sultanate continued, but foreign powers controlled major governmental functions.

How did each system change sovereignty, landholding, trade, political institutions, education, and ordinary life? Sovereignty was altered by different legal devices: occupation and later protectorate, condominium, treaty claims, and protectorate treaty. Landholding changed most visibly in Egypt, where cotton and credit concentrated land and debt, and, beginning in 1913, in Morocco, where land registration facilitated European acquisition; in Sudan and Libya, pre-1914 changes were more limited. Trade was reoriented toward European markets in all four, with Egypt's economy most deeply tied to a single crop. Political institutions were hollowed out or replaced: Egyptian ministries advised by British officials, Mahdist institutions destroyed, Ottoman administration in Libya removed, and the Moroccan makhzen supervised. Education expanded only modestly and selectively, and served colonial needs. For ordinary people, the common experiences were new taxes, new officials, and new risks, but their forms differed: cotton debt in Egypt, famine and conquest in Sudan, bombardment and deportation in Libya, and land and labor pressures in Morocco.

Local responses were equally varied. Egyptians organized constitutional and political activity, through the Urabi movement and later the National and Umma parties. Sudanese created an independent Mahdist state before its destruction. Libyans relied on armed and religious networks, notably the Sanusiyya, together with Ottoman officers and tribal leaders. Moroccans responded through dynastic, regional, diplomatic, and military means, including the sultan's maneuvers, the ulama's conditional allegiance, and armed uprisings. None of these responses was foolish or futile; they faced powers with superior finance, firepower, and diplomatic leverage, and in some cases, as in Libya, they forced an invader to accept a much less complete victory than expected.

On the eve of the First World War, the imperial political geography of the Arab world in North Africa and the Nile Valley was largely set: Britain in Egypt and Sudan, Italy on the Libyan coast, France and Spain in Morocco, and France in Algeria and Tunisia (Chapter 52). Only the Ottoman-ruled Arab East (Chapter 54) and parts of Arabia remained under other arrangements. The war would soon test every one of these structures, a story begun in Chapter 55.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how European powers used financial leverage, military intervention, diplomacy, and colonial administration to establish control over Egypt, Sudan, Libya, and Morocco between about 1875 and 1914, and how the peoples of these societies resisted, negotiated, and responded. It has argued that these were four distinct histories, not variations of one colonial script, and that each requires its own chronology, institutions, local actors, and interpretation.

In Egypt, the borrowing and spending of Khedive Isma'il led to bankruptcy and to a system of European financial supervision: the sale of Egypt's canal shares in 1875, the Caisse de la Dette in 1876, Dual Control, and the Law of Liquidation of 1880. Isma'il's deposition in 1879 showed the power of creditors and the Ottoman sultan. These developments created grievances among taxpayers, officials, and especially army officers, and they set the stage for the Urabi movement, a broad coalition of officers and civilians that demanded constitutional government and an end to foreign control.

British intervention in 1882, culminating in the bombardment of Alexandria and the defeat of Urabi at Tel el-Kebir, did not produce annexation. Instead, Britain governed Egypt through the veiled protectorate for thirty-two years, using advisers and the army while the khedive and Ottoman suzerainty remained in law. The occupation stabilized finances and expanded irrigation and cotton exports, but benefits were unevenly distributed and the economy grew dependent on a single crop. Opposition, expressed in the Denshawai affair of 1906 and the National and Umma parties, grew despite British efforts. In December 1914 wartime necessity ended the veil and produced a formal protectorate.

Sudan's story was different. Egyptian rule since 1820–1821 generated resentment through taxation, conscription, and the suppression of the slave trade. Muhammad Ahmad's proclamation as the Mahdi in 1881 combined religious reform with political grievance, and his Ansar destroyed Hicks's army, took Khartoum in 1885, and founded an independent state that survived his death under Khalifa Abdallahi. The Mahdiyya had a treasury, courts, an army, and a foreign policy, and it also bore heavy human costs through war and famine. It must be studied as an institution, not merely as a prelude to reconquest.

The Anglo-Egyptian reconquest of 1896–1898, driven by strategic concern about the Nile and French ambitions, destroyed Mahdist military power at Omdurman, and the Fashoda crisis was a distinct diplomatic confrontation. The Condominium of 1899 gave Sudan a legal form of joint governance but placed power in British hands. Administration was uneven: strongest in the north and center, weak in the south and in Darfur, which remained under Ali Dinar.

In Libya, Italy, a latecomer to empire, invaded the Ottoman provinces in 1911, expecting an easy victory. Instead, Ottoman officers, the Sanusi order, and local leaders resisted, and Italy responded with reprisals, deportations, and bombardment. The Treaty of Ouchy of October 1912 ended the Italo-Ottoman war and allowed Italy to claim sovereignty, but fighting continued, and by 1914 Italy's control was largely confined to the coast and a few interior positions.

Morocco, an independent sultanate with its own makhzen, lost autonomy first through commercial treaties, the protégé system, and loans, then through the diplomacy of the Entente Cordiale, the Tangier visit of 1905, and the Algeciras Act. French military expansion at Oujda and Casablanca in 1907, the struggle between Abd al-Aziz and Abd al-Hafid over legitimacy, the Fez relief expedition and the Agadir crisis in 1911, led to the Treaty of Fez of 30 March 1912 and the Franco-Spanish agreement of 27 November 1912. Tangier's special status was settled only in 1923. Resistance in the Atlas, the south, and the northern mountains meant that control remained incomplete.

Across the four cases, European rivalry mattered enormously: the Egyptian occupation, the race to Fashoda, the Franco-Italian bargain over Libya and Morocco, and the Agadir crisis show how decisions about North African societies were made by third parties. But local politics always shaped outcomes, and the survival of local institutions under imperial control, the khedive, the makhzen, Sufi orders, tribal leaders, and village headmen, meant that colonial rule depended on collaboration, negotiation, and sometimes failure.

By 1914 four imperial orders existed: a British occupation about to become a protectorate in Egypt, a condominium in Sudan, an incompletely realized Italian claim in Libya, and French and Spanish protectorates in Morocco. These arrangements shaped the political geography of the Arab world on the eve of the First World War. The following chapter turns to the ideas and movements, including Arabism, constitutionalism, and Zionism, that were already contesting the late Ottoman order, and Chapter 55 to the war that would transform the entire region.

Key Terms

Capitulations
Treaty privileges that exempted foreigners from local jurisdiction and taxation, central to European influence in Egypt and Morocco.
Khedive
The title of Egypt's hereditary viceroy from 1867, recognized by the Ottoman sultan.

A commission of foreign representatives, created in 1876, that received designated Egyptian revenues for debt service.

The Anglo-French supervision of Egyptian finances from the late 1870s until 1882.

The 1880 settlement fixing the share of Egyptian revenue devoted to debt service.

British control of Egypt from 1882 to 1914 that left the khedive and Ottoman suzerainty intact in form.

Egyptian courts, begun in 1876, with mixed foreign and Egyptian judges for cases involving foreigners.

Obligatory labor service, used on canals and public works and curtailed under the occupation.

The 1879–1882 coalition of army officers and civilians demanding constitutional government and an end to foreign control.

The 1906 incident and trial in which British authorities hanged and flogged Egyptian villagers, a symbol of imperial injustice.
Mahdi
A figure of Islamic expectation expected to restore justice; Muhammad Ahmad claimed the role in 1881.

The followers of the Sudanese Mahdi, named for the Prophet's helpers at Medina.

The Mahdist movement and the state it established in Sudan, 1881–1898.

The Sudanese name for the period of Turco-Egyptian rule, 1820s–1885.

The 1899 Anglo-Egyptian arrangement for Sudan: joint in form, British-dominated in practice.

A reformist Sufi order, strong in Cyrenaica and the Sahara, that led resistance to Italy and France.

A Sufi lodge serving religious, educational, and economic functions.

The October 1912 treaty ending the Italo-Ottoman war, interpreted differently by the two states.

The Moroccan sultan's government and the networks around it.

An arrangement in which a state retains formal existence while a foreign power controls foreign relations and, usually, much of government.

The French official who directed the Moroccan protectorate.

Overlordship, as the Ottoman sultan's formal authority over Egypt until 1914.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows that European domination of North Africa and the Nile Valley was neither a single process nor a simple one. Finance, invasion, conquest, treaty, and protectorate each gave a different relationship between imperial power and local society, and each left a different legacy. Understanding those differences is essential to the later history of the region, from the Egyptian revolution of 1919 to the Libyan wars of the interwar years, the southern question in Sudan, and the nationalist movements of Morocco.

It also reveals how deeply these societies were connected to wider systems: the Suez route, the cotton economy, Mediterranean rivalries, Ottoman politics, and European alliance diplomacy. The fate of Morocco was decided partly in London, Paris, and Berlin, and that of Egypt partly in the bondholders' counting houses. At the same time, the chapter shows that Egyptians, Sudanese, Libyans, and Moroccans were historical actors whose choices mattered even when they could not prevail.

The institutions examined here, the khedivate, the makhzen, Sufi orders, tribal confederations, and the Mahdist legacy, persisted beyond 1914 and shaped the political forms of the twentieth century. Chapter 54 turns to the ideas that were circulating through these societies, and Chapter 55 to the war that would dissolve the old order of the Ottoman world and reshape the imperial arrangements described here.
