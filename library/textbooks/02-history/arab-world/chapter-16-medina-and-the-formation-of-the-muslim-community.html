Part III — Late Antiquity and the Rise of Islam
16

Medina and the Formation of the Muslim Community

A History of the Arab World: From Ancient Crossroads to the Modern Arab World

Chapter Opening

In the autumn of 622 CE, a small number of Meccans, and then Muhammad himself, arrived at an oasis some three hundred kilometers to the north. What they found was not an empty refuge but a crowded agricultural settlement of date groves, fortified houses, wells, and rival clans, whose leading Arab groups had recently exhausted themselves in war with one another. Five years later, in 627, the same oasis withstood a large confederate army behind a defensive trench. Between those two moments a scattered body of believers had become something historians struggle to name: a community, a confederation, an emergent polity, and a religious fellowship all at once.

This chapter studies that transformation. Chapter 15 followed Muhammad through his early life at Mecca, the beginning of the Qur'anic revelation, the opposition his message provoked, and the gathering of the first believers. It ended on the threshold of migration. Chapter 16 begins with the Hijra and traces, through roughly 622 to 627, how the migrants and their Medinan supporters built institutions of prayer, leadership, alliance, redistribution, dispute resolution, and war, and how the concept of the umma took shape through negotiation and conflict rather than through a single founding act.

The chapter's central argument is that the Hijra did more than relocate the early believers. In Medina, religious allegiance became intertwined with political authority, military obligation, law, alliance, and communal institutions. This was not a case of religion suddenly replacing older Arabian structures. Kinship, oaths, protection, compensation, raiding, and negotiated leadership all continued to matter. What changed was that a new form of allegiance, grounded in acceptance of Muhammad's prophetic authority, began to cut across and sometimes override older boundaries, and that a single leader acquired unusual authority to arbitrate among groups who had long lacked a common arbiter.

A second argument concerns plurality. Medina was not a purely Muslim settlement in 622, and it did not become one by 627. Its population included Arab clans of the Aws and Khazraj, several Jewish groups, and Arabs who accepted Muhammad's leadership with varying degrees of conviction and others who did not. This chapter therefore treats the Jewish communities of Medina as part of the oasis's social landscape rather than as an appendix or as inevitable antagonists, and it treats the conflicts that developed with three of them as historical problems to be reconstructed carefully, not as episodes in an inevitable religious hostility.

The chapter also confronts the evidence directly. The Qur'an is the earliest and most contemporary source for the Medinan period, but it is not a narrative; it alludes to events without describing them. The detailed story of these years survives principally in Arabic biographical and historical works compiled from the eighth century onward, including the biography associated with Ibn Ishaq and the campaign histories of al-Waqidi. These are indispensable, and modern source-critical scholarship has argued that some early transmission clusters, including those surrounding Badr, Uhud, and the Trench, can be traced back with reasonable confidence. Yet the details of speeches, numbers, and motives remain provisional. The Medina Document, preserved in later collections but widely regarded as early, offers a rare glimpse of the political vocabulary of the first years.

The chapter deliberately stops after the Trench and the Banu Qurayza crisis of 627. The Hudaybiyya agreement of 628, the wider consolidation of Arabian alliances, the conquest of Mecca, and the unification of Arabia belong to Chapter 17. Readers should therefore treat Medina in 627 as a polity that has survived and can act collectively, not as a state that has already conquered the peninsula. The chapter's purpose, in the language of the Authoritative Chapter Outline, is to trace the Hijra, political organization at Medina, warfare, alliances, law, and the development of the umma, and every major section that follows is written to that end.

Finally, students should read the chapter against two anachronisms. One is retrospective inevitability: the assumption that because the Medinan community later became the core of a vast empire, its survival was assured. The battles of these years could have gone very differently. The other is categorical anachronism: the use of modern labels such as "state," "constitution," "secular," or "theocracy," or of later legal concepts such as fully elaborated fiqh, as if they described a seventh-century oasis. Where such terms appear, they are qualified, and the chapter tries to describe what people at Medina actually did.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why the Hijra of 622 was a political as well as a religious and geographic transformation, and why its status as a calendar epoch was assigned retrospectively.
  2. Describe the social and political structure of Yathrib/Medina on the eve of Muhammad's arrival, including the Aws, Khazraj, and Jewish groups.
  3. Analyze the relationship between the Muhajirun and the Ansar, and the ways migration reshaped property, patronage, and obligation.
  4. Interpret the Medina Document as historical evidence, distinguishing what it appears to regulate from what later scholars have read into it.
  5. Trace the development of the concept of the umma and distinguish political solidarity, religious allegiance, and later communal definition.
  6. Evaluate the sources for the Jewish communities of Medina and explain why their perspectives are recoverable only indirectly.
  7. Distinguish Medinan Qur'anic regulation from the later, fully elaborated system of Islamic jurisprudence.
  8. Explain the causes, course, and political consequences of the battles of Badr, Uhud, and the Trench.
  9. Assess the conflicts with Banu Qaynuqa', Banu al-Nadir, and Banu Qurayza as distinct episodes with unequal evidence.
  10. Evaluate in what sense Medina in 627 can be called a polity, and in what sense it cannot.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did the Hijra transform the movement's political character, and what did it not change?
  2. How did a fragmented oasis with a history of bitter internal conflict come to accept the authority of an outsider?
  3. What can the Medina Document tell historians about the earliest political vocabulary of the community, and what are the limits of that evidence?
  4. In what senses did the umma replace older forms of belonging, and in what senses did it coexist with them?
  5. How should historians reconstruct relations with Medina's Jewish groups when the surviving narratives were shaped by later Muslim tradition?
  6. How did warfare, and the economic and political needs behind it, shape the community's institutions?
  7. Why is it misleading to treat Medinan Qur'anic regulation as identical with later Islamic law?
  8. What made the difference between survival and collapse for the Medinan community between 624 and 627?
  9. In what sense was Medina in 627 a polity, and why might that description require qualification?

Historical Context

Chapter 14 described a western Arabia of oasis towns, sanctuaries, and kinship-based political authority, without a state capable of speaking for the peninsula. It portrayed Yathrib as a fragmented oasis in which several Arab groups and established Jewish communities shared date groves and wells without any single authority governing the whole. Chapter 15 then narrated Muhammad's life at Mecca, the opposition of many Quraysh leaders, the departure of some believers, and the negotiations with visitors from Yathrib that opened the possibility of migration. This chapter takes those foundations for granted and adds only what is needed to understand Medina itself.

The essential background is Yathrib's political exhaustion. Later tradition records a long series of clashes between the Aws and the Khazraj, the two principal Arab groups, in which Jewish groups were said to be entangled through alliances, and it identifies a battle at Bu'ath, traditionally placed a few years before the Hijra, as a particularly damaging episode. The precise sequence and scale of this fighting cannot be verified, and later narrators had reasons to dramatize pre-Islamic strife as a foil for the unity Islam brought. Still, the recurring claim that Yathrib lacked an accepted arbiter fits the wider pattern of oasis politics described in Chapters 3 and 14, and it helps explain why some Yathribis were willing to consider inviting an outside figure with religious prestige and no local kin entanglements.

The chapter also depends on the evidentiary caution established in Chapter 14. For Medina the Qur'an provides contemporary but allusive testimony; the Medina Document offers an apparently early legal text; and the later biographical and campaign literature supplies narrative at the cost of hindsight. Non-Muslim sources for these specific years are sparse and largely later. The result is that the outline of events is more secure than their details, and this chapter marks that difference throughout.

Section 16.1

The Hijra: Migration as Political Transformation

The Arabic word hijra denotes departure, and it carries connotations of severing ties, since to migrate was to leave the protection of one's kin. That is why the movement of the Meccan believers to Yathrib was more than a change of address. In a society where security depended on kinship and protective alliance, emigrants without a local kin-group exposed themselves to vulnerability, and an arrangement that could replace what they had left was a precondition of the move. The pledges exchanged between Muhammad and representatives of Yathrib before the migration, discussed in Chapter 15, supplied that arrangement: the Yathribis undertook to protect him as they would protect their own.

The migration was staggered. Tradition reports that most believers left Mecca in small groups over the months before Muhammad himself departed, and that his own journey, in the company of Abu Bakr, involved a period of concealment and an indirect route. These accounts are rich in detail, including stories of pursuit and of divine protection, and much of that detail reflects the devotional and literary purposes of later narrators. The historian can be confident of the core: in 622 CE Muhammad and a substantial part of the Meccan community settled at Yathrib, and from then on the center of gravity of the movement lay there. Beyond that core, particular episodes should be treated as traditions rather than documents.

The year 622 became an epoch only afterward. The early community did not count years from the Hijra. The Islamic calendar, discussed in this chapter's Vocabulary box, was adopted some years after Muhammad's death, when the Muslim leadership needed a system for dating administrative records, and its choice of the Hijra rather than the birth or the first revelation as its starting point reflects a later judgment about what had been decisive. That judgment is itself historical evidence, indicating that within a generation the Medinan foundation was recognized as the beginning of the community as a public, governing entity. But students should not assume that participants in 622 understood their move as the start of a new era.

What the Hijra changed was the setting. At Mecca, Muhammad and his followers were a minority within a society whose leadership rejected them, dependent for protection on personal arrangements. At Yathrib they were a growing body with allies, a place of worship, and a need to be organized. The question was no longer only how to preach, but how to live together, resolve disputes, feed migrants, and defend a settlement, which are the questions the rest of the chapter examines.

Section 16.2

Yathrib Becomes Medina

Yathrib was not a town in the sense of a single walled center. It was an oasis of scattered settlements, each often organized around a fortified compound or tower-house, surrounded by date palms, cultivated fields, and lava fields. Different clans held different quarters and orchards, and the oasis supported a mixed economy of agriculture, craft production, and local commerce. This physical dispersal mirrored its political dispersal: groups lived in close proximity but under separate leaderships, and disputes over land, water, and honor could escalate because there was no shared institution to settle them.

Its population was plural. The Aws and Khazraj were Arab groups of long standing in the oasis, and later tradition traces both to a common southern Arabian ancestry, a genealogy that historians treat with the caution described in Chapter 14. Jewish groups, whose presence at Yathrib is well attested in later tradition and consistent with the wider pattern of Jewish settlement in the Hijaz, are depicted in later Muslim sources as landholders, craftspeople, and traders; because these socioeconomic details come chiefly through that later literature, they are reconstruction rather than contemporary documentation. The later sources name the Banu Qaynuqa', Banu al-Nadir, and Banu Qurayza as leading groups, though other Jewish clans are also mentioned. Alliances cut across religious lines, so that particular Arab and Jewish groups were often partners against other pairings, and religion did not map neatly onto political camps.

The place-name itself has a history. Yathrib is the older name, attested in pre-Islamic contexts and used in one Qur'anic verse in the mouth of hostile speakers. Al-Madina, literally "the city" or "the town," became the dominant designation in Muslim usage. Later usage expanded this into expressions such as Madinat al-Nabi ("the City of the Prophet"), but historians should not assume that al-Madina itself originated as an abbreviation of that later title. The change should not be read as an instantaneous act of renaming in 622; the sources use both names, and the shift reflects the settlement's evolving identity as the seat of the community. Modern historians commonly use "Medina" for the period of Muhammad's residence while retaining "Yathrib" when describing the pre-migration oasis.

The oasis's relation to Mecca and the wider Hijaz also mattered. Medina lay along the north-south routes described in Chapter 14, and while it was not itself a great commercial center, it sat astride the paths by which Meccan caravans traveled to Syria. That geography would give Muhammad's community both a strategic pressure point against Quraysh and, in turn, vulnerability to their retaliation. The interplay of religious community, oasis politics, and control of routes runs through the whole chapter.

Section 16.3

Muhajirun and Ansar

The later tradition speaks of two overlapping categories of believers. The Muhajirun, "the emigrants," were those who had left Mecca and other places to join Muhammad; the Ansar, "the helpers," were Yathribis of the Aws and Khazraj who accepted his leadership and sheltered the newcomers. Both terms appear in the Qur'an, which praises the migrants for sacrificing their homes and the helpers for their generosity, and the terms became durable markers of status. In the political culture of the next generations, descent from early Muhajirun or Ansar carried prestige, and disputes about their relative weight would echo in the succession crisis of Chapter 18.

The social consequences were large. The migrants arrived with little property; many had left houses and goods at Mecca. They needed housing, food, land or work, and above all a place within a network of obligation. The Ansar provided this through hospitality, shared orchards, and in some accounts the lending of land, and the arrangement placed novel strains on Medinan households. It also reshaped patronage: an Ansari who sheltered a migrant assumed a duty that would ordinarily fall to kin, and a migrant who relied on him became partly incorporated into his household's circle. Such relationships help explain how allegiance to Muhammad could create binding ties across clans that had not previously acted together.

Not all migrants were Meccan aristocrats, and not all Ansar were equally prosperous. Some Muhajirun came from lower-status backgrounds, including freed and formerly enslaved individuals, and their experience of migration differed from that of established Quraysh merchants who had commercial skills and family capital. Some Ansar households gained influence through their role as hosts, while others bore heavy costs. The tradition's tendency to describe both groups in idealized terms should be balanced by the recognition that tension over resources and status was a normal feature of any large migration, and that later accounts occasionally hint at friction before smoothing it over.

Section 16.4

Building a New Community

Tradition places the construction of a mosque at the center of the new community's first months. The mosque, a courtyard enclosure of mud brick with palm-trunk supports and palm-leaf roofing in the standard accounts, functioned as a place of prayer but also as a gathering space where news was shared, delegations were received, disputes were heard, and the poor could find shelter. Its multiple uses illustrate a general feature of the Medinan community: institutions that later Muslim societies would differentiate, such as worship, assembly, and adjudication, were initially fused in the person of the Prophet and the space where he could be found.

The tradition also describes a ritual of mu'akhat, "brothering," in which each migrant was paired with a Medinan helper as a sworn brother, sharing obligations of mutual support and even, in some accounts, inheritance. The idea fits the context: Arabian society knew formal pacts that created fictive kinship, and pairing migrants with hosts could have been a practical method of integration. However, the detailed lists of pairs and the vivid anecdotes attached to them come from the later biographical tradition, and some scholars suspect that the institution was systematized or idealized retrospectively. The safest formulation is that the community used pacts of brotherhood and mutual support to incorporate newcomers, while the specific pairings should not be treated as transparent documentary fact.

Leadership was still personal and negotiated. Muhammad's authority rested on his acknowledged status as messenger of God, which gave his decisions weight beyond that of a clan chief, but it operated alongside older mechanisms. He consulted companions, delegated missions, and settled disputes as an arbiter. Resources moved through practices of voluntary charity, shared hospitality, and later the redistribution of booty and the collection of contributions. Nothing in the earliest years resembles a bureaucracy, and the sources do not show a standing treasury or salaried officials. The community's institutions in these years were therefore thin but efficient: a leader with unusual authority, a shared place of worship, networks of mutual obligation, and a growing body of rules.

Internal politics were not uniformly harmonious. The Qur'an and later tradition refer to a group called the munafiqun, often translated "hypocrites," who professed allegiance while being reluctant or hostile. Historians read this category with care. It may cover a range of people from those with real doubts to political rivals unwilling to subordinate their standing, and it is embedded in a text that evaluates them morally. Tradition associates the Khazraj leader Abdallah ibn Ubayy with such reservations, and his case suggests that acceptance of Muhammad's leadership at Medina was gradual and contested, not immediate.

Section 16.5

The Document of Medina

One of the most important sources for these years is a text preserved in later collections and commonly called the "Constitution of Medina." It survives as part of Ibn Ishaq's biography, in the recension of Ibn Hisham, and in a separate version transmitted by Abu Ubayd al-Qasim ibn Sallam in a work on fiscal and legal matters. It is not preserved as an independent original document, and its clauses are arranged in a sequence that suggests either a single agreement or a compilation of several. Nonetheless, many modern scholars, while differing on details, regard it as substantially early and probably dating to Muhammad's Medinan years, in part because its vocabulary and concerns fit a community not yet fully defined by later institutions.

Its contents are practical. The text identifies constituent groups, including believers from Quraysh and Yathrib, various named clans of the Aws and Khazraj, and participating Jewish groups attached to particular Medinan clans, and it lays out mutual obligations. Believers are to help one another, especially in the payment of blood-money and in the ransoming of captives, following established custom; the parties are to support one another against those who wrong them; and disputes are to be referred to God and to Muhammad. The text contains provisions on defense, on not making separate peace in war against the community's enemies, on not aiding Quraysh, and on the inviolability of Yathrib as a sacred zone for the parties. It treats different groups differently, so that some clauses concern believers specifically while others address the wider set of parties.

This heterogeneity is why the label matters. "Constitution" suggests a written frame of government for a state, with defined institutions and a sovereign people. The Medina Document is better read as a covenant or set of agreements that adapted Arabian conventions of alliance, protection, and compensation to a new situation in which a religious leader served as arbiter and a community defined by belief participated alongside existing clans. Some scholars prefer the neutral Arabic label kitab, "writing," and this chapter adopts "Medina Document" to avoid importing modern assumptions. The document does not describe a government with ministers or taxes. It describes obligations among groups.

The text also shows how much continued from older practice. Clans remain the units of responsibility; blood-money is handled through customary payment structures; protection is extended and reciprocated. What is new is the insertion of Muhammad as the ultimate point of reference for disputes and the definition of a category of believers whose duties extend across clan lines. The document therefore captures, in miniature, the chapter's central argument: religious allegiance and older kinship structures were interwoven in the first Medinan order, and the balance between them shifted over time.

Section 16.6

What Did Umma Mean?

The term umma appears in the Qur'an and in the Medina Document, and it has become central to later Muslim self-understanding as the community of believers across time and place. Students should be careful not to project that developed meaning back onto 622. In the Qur'an the word is used flexibly for a group or people, sometimes for the followers of a prophet, sometimes for a moral or religious community, and sometimes for a group with a shared way of life. In the Medina Document the word appears in a clause that presents the believers, and in some readings the wider set of groups covered by the agreement, as a single community distinct from outsiders. Scholars disagree about who exactly is included in that community at each point in the text.

The concept therefore fused several layers. There was political solidarity, meaning the practical obligation to defend one another and to act collectively in war. There was religious allegiance, meaning shared acceptance of Muhammad as messenger and adherence to the revealed rules. And there was a still-developing sense of a bounded community with its own boundaries of membership. Which layer dominated depended on context. In the document's provisions on blood-money and defense the emphasis is on solidarity among groups. In Qur'anic passages addressing the believers the emphasis is on religious duty. Later Islamic thought would combine these into a fully articulated conception of the community, but that synthesis was the product of centuries of reflection.

It is equally important to avoid the opposite error of saying the umma instantly replaced the tribe. Kin-groups continued to organize daily life, to carry obligations, and to shape political loyalties. Rather than displacing them, the umma gradually acquired a claim on loyalty that could, in specific circumstances such as war against Quraysh, take precedence. The tension between these two forms of belonging, kin and community, would recur in the early caliphate and remain a feature of Arab politics for centuries.

Section 16.7

Jews of Medina and the Problem of Later Sources

Jewish groups were integral to Medina's social landscape. Later Islamic sources depict particular Jewish groups as landholders, occupants of fortified compounds, craftspeople, traders, and participants in local credit and alliance networks. Because these socioeconomic details come chiefly through later Muslim literature, they should be treated as historical reconstruction rather than contemporary documentation. The Medina Document treats participating Jewish groups attached to specific clans as parties within its framework, and some specialists argue that the Jewish participants it names were not necessarily the three major groups, which may have had separate agreements with Muhammad; other scholars disagree. Either way, the document recognizes their communal identity while including them in mutual defense arrangements. That inclusion suggests that, at least initially, Muhammad and his supporters conceived of a political order in which non-believing groups could participate under agreed terms, a possibility that later history complicated.

The difficulty lies in the sources. There is no surviving Jewish account from Medina in the period. What we know of these communities comes from the Qur'an, which contains polemical engagement with Jewish positions, and from Muslim biographical and historical literature composed generations later. Scholarship on Jewish-Muslim polemic notes that detailed accounts of disputation survive almost entirely through Islamic sources. This does not mean the material is fabricated, but it means that the perspectives, motives, and internal politics of the Jewish groups are known chiefly through the narrators of the winning side. Later Jewish tradition, in turn, preserves little about Medina and was itself shaped by subsequent centuries of interaction.

Students should therefore avoid two errors. The first is to collapse the Jewish groups into a single bloc with a common policy. The sources depict distinct clans with different histories, alliances, and fates. The second is to read the later conflicts as the inevitable outcome of religious difference. The tradition itself shows relationships of neighborliness and alliance, followed by breakdowns that historians can connect to specific political and military circumstances: security, property, treaty obligations, and the balance of power after Badr, Uhud, and the Trench. Religious polemic accompanied these conflicts, but it does not by itself explain them.

Section 16.8

Qur'anic Change in the Medinan Setting

The Qur'an itself registers the change of setting. Traditional scholarship classifies chapters and verses as Meccan or Medinan, and although modern scholars debate the criteria and the precision of such classification, there is broad agreement that the material associated with Medina differs in tone and content. Where the Meccan passages emphasize monotheism, moral warning, resurrection, and the stories of earlier prophets, the Medinan passages more often address a defined community and its practical concerns: relations with Jews and Christians, obligations of charity, regulation of marriage and family, inheritance, warfare, the treatment of captives and booty, and the maintenance of internal discipline.

This shift is instructive for the historian because the revelation appears to respond to circumstances. Certain chapters refer to Badr, Uhud, and the Trench and comment on their meaning, exhorting the believers, rebuking hesitation, and explaining setbacks. Others deal with disputes over spoils or with the standing of those who avoided fighting. Historians can use these passages as contemporary evidence that such events occurred and mattered, while recognizing that the text does not narrate them and that identifying which verse refers to which incident sometimes relies on later commentary.

A caution follows. Although the Medinan Qur'an contains rules, students should not infer that every rule can be dated to a specific month or that the corpus provides a complete legal code. Some passages address particular episodes; others state general principles. Later scholars developed sophisticated methods for ordering revelations chronologically and for reconciling apparent differences, but the sequence of individual verses often cannot be established with certainty. This chapter therefore speaks of the Medinan setting in general and dates specific rulings only where the tradition offers reasonably early support.

Section 16.9

Qibla, Fasting, and Emerging Ritual Institutions

The Medinan years saw the crystallization of practices that gave the community a distinct ritual profile. Tradition holds that early Muslim prayer was oriented toward Jerusalem and that, some months after the Hijra, the direction (qibla) was changed to the Ka'ba at Mecca. The Qur'an alludes to a change of direction and to the reaction it provoked, and the episode is often read as a marker of both an emerging distinct identity and a reorientation toward the Meccan sanctuary discussed in Chapter 14. The exact chronology and the interpretive links between the change and relations with Medina's Jewish groups are debated; the historically defensible statement is that the direction of prayer was formalized during the Medinan period and that the change carried symbolic weight.

Fasting during the month of Ramadan is also associated with the second year after the Hijra. The Qur'an prescribes fasting and addresses its conditions, and later tradition links its adoption to the period following the qibla change. Almsgiving developed in parallel. Earlier voluntary charity (sadaqa) was accompanied by more structured obligations, later described as zakat, whose early administrative forms are difficult to reconstruct. The community's collective life thus acquired a calendar of shared observances, a fiscal dimension in the form of obligatory giving, and an increasing set of markers distinguishing its members from others.

Historians should keep theology and reconstruction separate here. Muslim tradition understands these practices as divinely instituted, and this textbook does not adjudicate that belief. What historical analysis can say is that the observable process of ritual formation, whatever its origin, had social effects: it reinforced solidarity, gave believers common duties that cut across clan lines, and made membership in the community publicly visible. The pilgrimage rites, which would play a large part in the next chapter's story, remained tied to Mecca and were not yet practiced by the Medinan community in the form they later took.

Section 16.10

Raiding, Caravans, and the Road to Badr

Armed conflict in western Arabia had long-standing conventions. Raiding (ghazw) was an accepted way of acquiring livestock and goods and of asserting honor, and it was governed by norms that limited bloodshed: raiders sought property more than lives, and killing invited retaliation through the compensation and revenge structures described in Chapter 14. Sacred months, in which fighting was customarily suspended, provided periods of relative security for travel and trade. The Medinan community operated within this environment, and the earliest expeditions attributed to Muhammad should be understood in that light, not as the campaigns of a fully formed military state.

The motives were mixed. The migrants had left property behind at Mecca, and Quraysh had in some cases seized it, so the emigrants had grievances and economic needs. Medina's location on the Syrian route made Meccan caravans natural targets. The Qur'an contains passages granting permission to fight those who had wronged the believers and later passages regulating warfare more generally, and the tradition describes a series of small expeditions in the first year or two, many of which found no enemy. Historians debate whether these were primarily attempts to intercept caravans, to demonstrate strength to nearby tribes, to establish alliances with groups along the routes, or all three. The sources probably compress and systematize what was in practice a set of improvised initiatives.

A revealing episode is the raid at Nakhla, traditionally dated to early 624. A party sent by Muhammad attacked a Meccan caravan, killing one man and capturing others, and it seems to have done so during a sacred month. The tradition reports that the act provoked controversy even among the believers, and the Qur'an addresses the question, acknowledging the gravity of fighting in a sacred month while emphasizing the wrongs committed by opponents. The incident illustrates that the community's leadership had to justify its actions within an existing moral framework and that the boundaries of permissible violence were themselves being negotiated.

It would be a mistake to describe these years either as unprovoked aggression or as a coherent military strategy. The sources show a small community with limited resources, in conflict with a powerful neighbor, exploiting opportunities and reacting to pressures. Only in retrospect do these initiatives form a story of escalation toward Badr.

Section 16.11

The Battle of Badr, 624

Badr, a watering place southwest of Medina, was the site of the first major engagement between the Medinan community and Quraysh, traditionally dated to the month of Ramadan in the second year after the Hijra, corresponding to March 624. The traditional narrative says that Muhammad led a force to intercept a Meccan caravan returning from Syria under Abu Sufyan, that Abu Sufyan diverted the caravan and sent word to Mecca, and that a larger Meccan force, once it learned the caravan was safe, chose to proceed to Badr anyway, leading to a battle neither side had initially planned. The Medinan force is traditionally given as a little over three hundred men and the Meccan force as several times that number. These figures are round and symbolic in character, and historians treat them as indicating a Meccan numerical advantage without accepting them as precise.

The engagement ended in a decisive Medinan victory. Several prominent Quraysh leaders were killed and a number of prisoners were taken. The Qur'an refers to Badr in terms of divine assistance and explains the victory as a sign; a whole chapter, associated with the distribution of spoils, is closely tied to its aftermath. Because Badr appears in the Qur'an itself, its historicity is not in doubt. Modern source-critical work has also identified the Badr traditions as one of the major early complexes of transmission, tracing elements of the narrative to authorities of the late seventh and early eighth centuries. Yet the battle scenes, the individual duels, and the reports of angelic participation belong largely to later elaboration, and students should read them as literary and devotional memory.

Badr's importance lies in what it did politically. Victory over a substantial Quraysh force enhanced Muhammad's prestige among wavering Medinans and among Arabian tribes watching the conflict. It demonstrated that the community could fight effectively, and it converted an experimental alliance into a credible military and political entity. At the same time, it hardened the enmity of Quraysh, whose leaders had lost kinsmen and now had a duty of vengeance under the honor code described in Chapter 14. Success therefore created its own danger. Badr made further major confrontation much more likely.

Section 16.12

Badr's Political Consequences

The first consequences were material and administrative. Booty had to be distributed, and the tradition indicates that disagreement arose about how. The Qur'anic chapter connected with Badr assigns the spoils to God and the Messenger and later sets out a principle of allocating a fifth for the Prophet and specified recipients. The rule, whatever its precise chronology, placed the leader at the center of redistribution, giving him a fiscal role that strengthened his capacity to reward supporters and support the needy. It also displaced the older custom by which fighters kept what they seized, and the tension between the two practices is visible in the tradition's accounts of disputes.

Captives raised another question. Prisoners were held for ransom, in line with Arabian practice, and the wealthy among them were released for payment, while some who could not pay were reportedly allowed to earn release by teaching. Some accounts say that a few prisoners were executed and that the Qur'an later commented on the handling of captives. The details vary, and they show that the community was improvising rules in real time. For the Meccan families, the ransoms and the losses at Badr became part of the ongoing exchange of grievance and obligation that shaped the war.

Politically, Badr strengthened Muhammad's authority within Medina. Groups that had hesitated to align with him now had reasons to reconsider, and conversion to Islam among Medinan clans expanded. But the victory also intensified the wariness of some. Leaders who had been ambivalent, such as Abdallah ibn Ubayy in the tradition, now faced a Prophet whose position was too strong to challenge openly, and they adapted. The result was that the Medina of late 624 was more cohesive than the Medina of 622 but not uniform, and the shift in power set the background for the confrontations with Jewish groups discussed next.

Section 16.13

Conflict with Banu Qaynuqa' and Banu al-Nadir

The later tradition reports that after Badr a dispute involving the Banu Qaynuqa', a Jewish group that later tradition associates with the market and with crafts, escalated into a siege of their quarter and their expulsion from Medina. The tradition typically traces the escalation to an incident of harassment in the marketplace that led to killing and retaliation, framing the affair as a breach of the agreement. Historians observe that the Qaynuqa' had been allied with the Khazraj, that Abdallah ibn Ubayy is said to have intervened on their behalf, and that the group's expulsion removed a significant economic and military actor from the oasis. The specific pretext should be treated with caution, since the sources explain political outcomes through incidents that may have served later narrators' purposes.

The expulsion of the Banu al-Nadir followed at a later date. The order of events is disputed: some early traditions place it in the months after Badr, while the biographical account associated with Ibn Ishaq places it after Uhud, in 625. The tradition connects it to an alleged plot against Muhammad, to a request for assistance in paying blood-money, and to a period of siege after which the group was permitted to leave with portions of their goods, many settling at Khaybar. A chapter of the Qur'an addresses the episode and the treatment of the abandoned property, and the fact that such a text exists supports the claim that a confrontation took place. It does not supply the causal narrative. The property left behind, according to the tradition, was treated differently from ordinary booty and became a resource under the Prophet's control, again strengthening the leadership's capacity to support the migrants.

These episodes should not be reduced to a story of Jewish treachery, nor assimilated to a sweeping account of religious conflict. The evidence points to a set of political and security problems: the obligations of alliance under the Medina Document, suspicion of collaboration with Quraysh, tensions over property and authority, and the changing balance of power. Religious polemic in the Qur'an and in later tradition accompanied these conflicts, but the sources attribute concrete actions to specific groups and do not treat the Jewish population as a whole as an enemy. The three groups' fates were also different, as the account of Qurayza will show, and treating them as equivalent obscures the differences in evidence and outcome.

Section 16.14

Uhud, 625

The Meccan response to Badr took time to organize. In March 625 a Quraysh-led force, traditionally reckoned at about three thousand, advanced to the vicinity of Medina, camping near the hill of Uhud. The Medinan leadership debated whether to defend from within the oasis, where its compounds and orchards offered protection, or to meet the enemy outside. Tradition says that Muhammad favored remaining within but yielded to those who wished to fight outside, and that a substantial contingent led by Abdallah ibn Ubayy withdrew before the battle. Whether this detail is reliable or reflects later polemic about the "hypocrites" is uncertain, but it captures the real division within the community.

The battle initially favored the Muslims, but the tradition records that archers stationed to guard a flank left their positions in pursuit of booty, allowing Meccan cavalry to attack from the rear. The Muslims suffered heavy casualties, including Hamza, Muhammad's uncle, and Muhammad himself was wounded. The Meccans, however, did not press their advantage to the destruction of Medina, and withdrew. The result was a setback for the Muslims and not a catastrophe; Quraysh had avenged Badr in part but had failed to eliminate the community.

The Qur'an devotes extended reflection to Uhud, addressing the believers' loss and explaining defeat as a test, a consequence of disobedience, and a reminder that Muhammad was a messenger who, like earlier messengers, could die. This is precious evidence, in that it shows an early community confronting the challenge of reconciling divine promise with military failure. Modern scholarship regards the Uhud narrative as another early transmission complex with recoverable early elements, but the tactical details, the roles of individuals, and the numbers should be held provisionally. The battle's lasting significance lies in what it required of the community: renewed discipline, attention to obedience, and reinforcement of solidarity after loss.

Section 16.15

Internal Discipline, Law, and Community Authority

After Uhud the community faced a period of insecurity. Tribes in the surrounding region, aware of Medina's vulnerability, tested its influence; the tradition records ambushes and reversals, and the community responded through expeditions and alliance-building with groups along the routes. Within Medina, the pressures of war intensified questions of obedience, loyalty, and responsibility. Qur'anic passages address those who avoided fighting, urge steadfastness, and regulate matters that war made urgent: the care of the families of the dead, the position of widows and orphans, and the distribution of inheritance.

Family and property law occupy a considerable place in the Medinan Qur'an. Passages set out rules of inheritance that gave women and other relatives defined shares, regulated marriage and divorce, restricted certain practices, and set out obligations toward orphans and the poor. These rules addressed a society whose wars had left many dependents and whose transfers of property needed regulation. In doing so they modified existing custom in some respects while retaining it in others. Historians debate how far such provisions changed the actual position of women relative to pre-Islamic practice, given the uneven evidence noted in Chapter 14, and they caution against blanket claims either that the rules were a sudden liberation or that they merely continued old norms.

The central distinction for this chapter is between Medinan regulation and later jurisprudence. What existed in Muhammad's lifetime was a combination of Qur'anic injunctions, decisions made by the Prophet as arbiter, customary practice, and agreements like the Medina Document. The comprehensive system of Islamic law, with its schools, methods of deriving rulings, and technical vocabulary, developed over the following two centuries and is examined in Chapter 28. It drew on the Medinan material, but it also elaborated and systematized it. To describe the Medinan community as governed by "Islamic law" in the later sense would project a mature institution onto an early stage.

Section 16.16

The Battle of the Trench, 627

In 627 Quraysh and their allies organized a larger campaign. The Qur'an refers to the coalition as al-Ahzab, "the confederates," and the tradition identifies Quraysh, allied Arabian groups including Ghatafan, and elements sympathetic to them, including Jewish exiles from the Banu al-Nadir who had settled at Khaybar and who are said to have encouraged the campaign. The size given in the sources, around ten thousand, is a round figure and should be taken as indicating an unusually large force by Hijazi standards, not as a precise count. The scale suggests that the coalition was a serious attempt to eliminate the Medinan community.

The defensive response was innovative. The tradition credits the idea of digging a trench across the exposed northern approach to the oasis to Salman al-Farisi, a Persian convert, and reports that the believers dug it in a matter of days, working under Muhammad's direction. The idea is often linked to Sasanian practice and, if the attribution is reliable, illustrates the diversity of the early community and its access to wider knowledge. Whether the story of Salman is authentic or a later embellishment, the trench itself is widely accepted, and the Qur'an describes the siege in vivid terms, referring to the fear of the believers, the wavering of some, and the eventual repulsion of the attackers.

The siege lasted for a few weeks. The attackers, unable to cross the trench in force, skirmished and probed, and their coalition suffered from internal divisions, weather, and shortages. The tradition also reports diplomatic maneuvering in which Muhammad's side sought to weaken the alliance and to encourage mistrust among its members. Eventually the confederates withdrew. The failure was decisive: the coalition's failure to take Medina despite its apparent numerical superiority damaged its credibility, and Quraysh's strategic initiative diminished. From this point the initiative shifted toward Medina, a change the next chapter traces.

Section 16.17

Banu Qurayza and the Problem of Historical Reconstruction

The Banu Qurayza were the Jewish group that remained in Medina after the expulsions of the Qaynuqa' and the Nadir. The later tradition says that during the siege they were approached by the confederates and that some form of negotiation took place regarding their stance, and that, after the siege lifted, Muhammad marched against them on the grounds that they had broken their agreement. After a siege of some weeks they surrendered. In the early Muslim tradition, Sa'd ibn Mu'adh, an Aws leader associated with the Qurayza alliance, is named as the arbitrator; he is reported to have ordered the killing of the fighting men and the enslavement of women and children, with the sentence then carried out. The Qur'an contains passages, in the chapter that also describes the Trench, that allude to the fate of a group of the People of the Book and to the seizure of their lands.

This is among the most difficult episodes of the Prophet's career for the historian, and it requires precision. Several points can be said with reasonable confidence. The episode is part of an early and well-attested transmission complex linked with the Trench; the Qur'anic allusion supports the historicity of some punitive action; and the sources agree that a substantial number of men were killed. Beyond this, uncertainty grows. The numbers in the tradition vary and are often given in the hundreds; they cannot be treated as verified statistics. The narrative of the negotiations with the confederates, the details of the arbitration, and the speeches attributed to participants are the kind of material most susceptible to later shaping. Some modern scholars have questioned the scale of the reported executions or the extent of the group's collective responsibility, while others regard the core account as reliable; the debate concerns the amount of detail that can be established, and the underlying event is generally accepted.

Two further points require emphasis. First, there is no surviving Qurayza account, so the group's motives and internal disagreements are inaccessible except through hostile or partial transmission. Second, the episode should be read with attention to how the tradition frames it: later Muslim narratives present the alleged breach as a wartime betrayal and present the selection of an Aws ally as arbitrator as legitimating the judgment within the alliance conventions of the story. That framing is part of the evidence, not an independently documented legal standard. Neither point excuses or condemns; the second clarifies the terms in which the sources justify the event. Later Muslim, Jewish, and modern writers have interpreted it in very different ways, and a responsible treatment presents it without sensationalism, without presenting disputed numbers as exact fact, and without using it as a shorthand for all relations between Muslims and Jews.

Section 16.18

Medina by 627: A Community Becoming a Polity

By the end of 627 the community at Medina possessed most of the ingredients of a polity, though in embryonic form. It had a leader whose authority combined prophetic, arbitral, and military dimensions; a shared center of worship and assembly; a body of rules drawn from revelation, precedent, and agreement; mechanisms for redistribution through the fifth of booty, charity, and confiscated property; a capacity to mobilize fighters across clan lines; a set of alliances with neighboring groups; and procedures for resolving disputes within and among groups. It had also survived two serious military tests and had removed or subdued potential rivals within the oasis.

At the same time, "polity" must be used with restraint. There was no territorial administration, no fixed tax system, no professional army, no permanent officials, and no clear distinction between public and private roles. The community remained dependent on the personal authority of Muhammad and on the voluntary commitment of its members, and many of its links to surrounding tribes were fragile. Arabia was not unified, and Quraysh remained powerful. To call Medina a state in 627 would overstate the case; to deny that it had become something more than a clan or a religious circle would understate it.

The chapter's argument can now be restated. The Hijra placed a community with a religious message in a setting that needed arbitration and protection, and in that setting religious allegiance, political authority, law, war, and alliance became intertwined. The umma that emerged was neither an abstraction nor a replacement of kinship but a practical form of solidarity that developed through negotiation and conflict. Its survival was contingent: Badr could have been lost, Uhud could have been worse, and the Trench could have been breached.

The next phase belongs to Chapter 17. Having withstood the Confederates, the Medinan community was positioned to negotiate with Quraysh and to extend its influence across Arabia, beginning with the agreement at Hudaybiyya in 628. The transformation from a community that survived to a polity that expanded is the subject of that chapter, and it rests on the foundations established here.

Historical Vocabulary
Umma

The Arabic word umma denotes a community, people, or group united by something shared, whether a prophet, a way of life, or a religious commitment. In the Qur'an it is used flexibly, and in the Medina Document it is used to describe a community of believers, and in some readings a wider set of groups covered by the agreement, distinct from outsiders. Later Islamic thought used it for the worldwide community of Muslims.

It matters because it names the new form of belonging that developed at Medina without fully replacing kinship. Modern equivalents such as "nation," "church," or "state" mislead. It was not a territorial nation, it was not an institution separate from society, and it did not yet have the fixed boundaries later theology gave it. Students should treat it as a concept in formation across 622–627, not as a settled category.

Historical Vocabulary
Hijra and the Hijri Calendar

Hijra means migration or departure, with connotations of breaking ties. It refers to the movement of Muhammad and his followers from Mecca to Yathrib in 622 CE. The Hijri calendar is a purely lunar calendar of twelve months that counts years from this event; years are marked "AH" (anno Hegirae). It was adopted some years after Muhammad's death, when the leadership needed a system for dating administrative documents, and it therefore reflects a retrospective judgment about which event was foundational.

The lunar year is about eleven days shorter than the solar year, so Hijri and Common Era dates drift apart, and conversions are approximate for events within a year. Students should also remember that people in 622 did not use the system, and that the term "Hijra" later acquired wider meaning in Islamic thought as a religiously valued act of leaving one's home for the sake of faith.

Historical Vocabulary
Kitab, Sahifa, and "Constitution"

Kitab ("writing," "document") and sahifa ("sheet," "document") are terms used in the Arabic sources for written agreements. Modern scholars have called the Medina text a "constitution," a label that suggests a written framework for a sovereign state. Because the text regulates obligations among groups and refers disputes to God and Muhammad without describing offices, taxes, or institutions of a state, the label can mislead. This textbook uses "Medina Document" and notes the alternatives. The choice of terminology is itself a historical judgment about what kind of order existed at Medina.

Primary Source Window

The Medina Document (Kitab / the so-called "Constitution of Medina")

Creator: Traditionally an agreement associated with Muhammad and the groups of Medina · Date: probably the early Medinan years, though scholars debate whether it is a single agreement or a compilation · Type: Legal or covenantal text, preserved in later works and not as an independent original

Context: The text is preserved in the biography associated with Ibn Ishaq, as transmitted by Ibn Hisham, and in a version cited by Abu Ubayd al-Qasim ibn Sallam. Both were compiled generations after Muhammad. Many modern scholars regard the text as early and substantially authentic, and its vocabulary and preoccupations, focused on alliance, compensation, and defense, fit an early setting.

[Paraphrase, not a verbatim quotation.] The text lists constituent groups, including believers of Quraysh and Yathrib and named Medinan clans, and describes them as a community distinct from others. It requires members to bear blood-money and ransom obligations according to established custom, to support one another against wrongdoing, and to refrain from separate peace in war. It attaches participating Jewish groups to particular clans, allows them to keep their religion and to share in defense obligations, and treats Yathrib as an inviolable zone. It provides that serious disputes are to be referred to God and to Muhammad.

Analysis: The document shows an early attempt to define obligations among groups in a shared space, using familiar categories such as blood-money and protection. It shows that Muhammad was already accepted as an ultimate arbiter and that the concept of a community of believers existed. Its limitations are considerable: it survives only in later transmission, its order suggests layers, it does not tell us how far its provisions were enforced, and it reflects the perspective of its framers. Participating Jewish groups are represented in the text, but their own perspectives and reactions are unrecorded. It cannot alone establish how the political order actually functioned.

Questions for students:

  1. What does this source say about mutual obligations among groups?
  2. Who created it and for what possible purposes, and how do we know of it today?
  3. What can historians learn from it about the earliest Medinan community?
  4. What can they not establish from it alone, such as its enforcement or the views of the parties?
Historical Debate

What Kind of Community Did the Medina Document Create?

The question: Did the Medina Document establish a broad political confederation in which believers and non-believing groups were partners in a single community, or was its core an emerging religious community of believers to which other groups were attached on different terms?

Interpretation A — A broad confederative community: Some scholars emphasize clauses that include Jewish groups in mutual defense and describe a single community of parties to the agreement, and they read the text as a pragmatic pact modeled on Arabian alliances, creating a pluralistic political community under Muhammad's arbitration.
Interpretation B — A believers' core with layered attachments: Other scholars stress the text's distinct provisions for believers, the possibility that it combines earlier and later stages, and the differences in how it treats groups, and they argue that the term umma refers primarily to the believers, with others tied to them as allies or dependents.

Evidence: Both readings depend on the text's ambiguous wording, on its arrangement into clauses of different kinds, and on comparison with Qur'anic usage and Arabian alliance practice. They differ in how they weigh apparent tensions between clauses and in whether they think the document is a single act or a compilation.

Why it matters: The answer shapes how historians understand the earliest relationship between religious identity and political membership, and the status of Jewish groups. Debates over whether Medina was "secular" or "theocratic" are anachronistic, and the more productive question is how the document actually distributed obligations.

Student task: Choose two clauses described in the source paraphrase and explain how each supports or complicates one of the interpretations. Identify what additional evidence would help.

Multiple Perspectives

Five Lives in Medina, 622–627

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns historians have identified across many sources. They are not quotations from any named historical actor, and these groups did not hold equal power or bear equal responsibility.

From the perspective of a Muhajir who had left property at Mecca: Migration meant separation from kin and dependence on a host, and the community's mutual support and later redistribution offered a livelihood and a place, while the conflict with Quraysh carried a personal edge of loss and grievance.
From the perspective of an Ansar household: Sheltering migrants brought prestige and religious purpose but also economic strain, and acceptance of Muhammad's arbitration offered relief from years of clan warfare while creating new obligations beyond one's own kin.
From the perspective of a member of a Medinan Jewish group: A place within the oasis's alliances and agreements had long depended on relationships with particular Arab clans, and the arrival of a new authority, and later the shifting balance of power, created uncertainty about security, property, and status; the surviving sources do not let us hear this view directly.
From the perspective of a Quraysh opponent: The emigrants were kin who had abandoned the community's ways and now threatened its trade routes, and losses at Badr created a duty of vengeance and a concern for Mecca's standing.
From the perspective of a believer without strong kin backing: Membership in the community offered protection and status that a weak lineage could not provide, making the new form of allegiance especially valuable, though it also depended on the leadership's continued success.
Continuity and Change Over Time

Community and Authority at Medina, 622–627

What Changed

  • Religious allegiance increasingly structured political identity
  • Qur'anic rules began to regulate community life, including family, charity, and war
  • Leadership became more centralized around Muhammad as arbiter, commander, and recipient of a share of spoils
  • Obligations extended across older clan boundaries

What Stayed the Same

  • Kinship, blood-money, oath, and protection remained operative
  • Raiding and alliance-making continued as ordinary tools
  • Leadership still relied on persuasion and negotiation as well as authority
  • Medina remained socially heterogeneous, and the surviving evidence does not justify treating every resident as part of a single uniformly Muslim community

Who experienced change most directly: Migrants and Medinan clans that accepted Muhammad's authority experienced the greatest shift in obligation, while pastoral neighbors and non-aligned groups experienced more continuity. Why the pattern matters: It shows that transformation occurred through adaptation of existing structures rather than sudden replacement.

Cause and Effect

Why the Medinan Community Became More Centralized

Long-term causes

Yathrib's fragmented politics and lack of an accepted arbiter, together with Arabian traditions of alliance, protection, and negotiated leadership that could accommodate an outside authority.

Short-term causes

The need to integrate migrants, the conflict with Mecca, competition for resources and routes, and the requirement to coordinate defense and dispute resolution.

Event / Transformation

Military success at Badr and survival at Uhud and the Trench, together with rules on booty, law, and obligation, consolidated Muhammad's authority within Medina.

Immediate effects

Expansion of adherents, removal or subordination of internal rivals, redistribution capacity, and a community able to mobilize collectively.

Long-term consequences

A community able to act beyond the oasis and to negotiate with Quraysh, leading to the developments of Chapter 17.

Historical complexity

Political authority did not grow from religion alone. War, alliance, kinship, property, law, and ritual interacted, and outcomes such as Badr were contingent, so the story cannot be reduced to a single cause or to inevitability.
Everyday Life and Social History

Living in an Oasis Community at War

For most residents of Medina the rhythms of date cultivation, irrigation, market exchange, and household labor continued through the political changes. The harvest calendar, the demands of wells and palm groves, and the needs of livestock structured daily life as described in Chapter 3. What changed for many was the addition of new obligations: daily and communal prayer, fasting in Ramadan, contributions to charity, and the possibility of military service. Migrants, who often lacked land, faced the work of establishing livelihoods, relying on hospitality, labor, and trade.

War brought particular burdens. Families lost members at Badr and Uhud, and the care of widows and orphans became a communal concern reflected in Qur'anic rules. Women's roles are only partly visible in the sources, which emphasize male figures, but women managed households, worked in agriculture and craft, cared for the wounded, and in some accounts took part in defense; the extent varied and is hard to quantify. Enslaved people, including captives from wars, formed part of the labor force, and manumission received religious encouragement. Because the surviving narratives were composed by later scholars, the experiences of ordinary residents, non-Muslim households, and the poor must be inferred from incidental references.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced how the migration of Muhammad and the Meccan believers to Yathrib in 622 CE initiated the formation of a durable political-religious order. Its central argument is that the Hijra did more than relocate a persecuted group. In Medina, religious allegiance became intertwined with political authority, military obligation, law, alliance, and communal institutions, and the umma developed through negotiation, conflict, ritual change, and the practical problems of governing a plural oasis.

The chapter began with the setting. Yathrib was a dispersed oasis of date groves and fortified compounds inhabited by the Aws and Khazraj and by Jewish groups, all bound by cross-cutting alliances and lacking a shared arbiter after prolonged internal conflict. This fragmentation, described in Chapter 14, made it possible for an outsider with religious prestige to be accepted as a mediator, and the pledges made before the migration gave the migrants a form of protection in place of the kin they left.

The Muhajirun and Ansar became the core of the new community, and their relationship reshaped property, patronage, and obligation. The mosque, communal prayer, and the traditional practice of brothering provided institutional frameworks, though the details of the latter come from later tradition. Internal politics remained contested, as the case of the munafiqun shows, and acceptance of Muhammad's authority was gradual.

The Medina Document was examined as an early and important text, best understood as a covenant that adapted Arabian conventions of alliance, protection, and blood-money to a new situation, and not as a modern constitution. It shows continuity with older structures alongside novelty in the position of Muhammad as arbiter and in the definition of a community of believers. The concept of the umma combined political solidarity and religious allegiance without yet having the boundaries later assigned to it, and it coexisted with, rather than instantly replaced, kinship.

The chapter emphasized the plurality of Medina and the evidentiary problem of the Jewish communities, whose perspectives survive only through later Muslim sources and the polemical Qur'an. The conflicts with Banu Qaynuqa', Banu al-Nadir, and Banu Qurayza were treated as separate episodes with different evidence and outcomes, connected to concrete political and security circumstances and not explained by religious hostility alone.

The Qur'anic material of the Medinan setting was shown to reflect the needs of a community: regulation of family, inheritance, charity, and war, as well as ritual institutions such as the change of prayer direction and the fast of Ramadan. The chapter distinguished this Medinan regulation from the later, elaborate system of Islamic jurisprudence examined in Chapter 28.

Warfare formed the chapter's main narrative arc. Raiding within Arabian conventions led to the confrontation at Badr in 624, a victory that enhanced Muhammad's authority and hardened Meccan enmity. Uhud in 625 was a setback that tested discipline and led to Qur'anic reflection on defeat. The Trench in 627 defeated a large confederate coalition and shifted the strategic initiative, and the Qurayza episode that followed remains among the hardest to reconstruct: Qur'anic allusion and comparatively early transmission support a core confrontation and punitive action, while the scale, numbers, negotiations, and speeches remain uncertain.

Throughout, the chapter emphasized the sources: the Qur'an as contemporary but allusive, the Medina Document as early but transmitted, and the biographical and campaign literature as indispensable but shaped by later concerns. Modern source-critical work suggests that some transmission clusters preserve early material, while individual scenes, numbers, and speeches require caution.

By 627 the community had a leader, a center of worship, rules, mechanisms of redistribution, the ability to mobilize across clan lines, alliances, and dispute-resolution procedures. It lacked territorial administration, fixed taxation, professional officials, and a clear public-private distinction, and Arabia remained disunited. Medina was therefore a polity in formation, not a completed state.

The chapter's significance lies in the foundations it laid. The institutions, concepts, and precedents of these years shaped the succession crisis of Chapter 18, the conquests of Part IV, and the long development of Islamic law and political thought. Chapter 17 now turns to how the Medinan community moved outward, beginning with Hudaybiyya in 628.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Hijra
The migration of Muhammad and the Meccan believers to Yathrib in 622 CE; later used as the starting point of the Islamic calendar.
Hijri calendar
A lunar calendar counting years from the Hijra, adopted some years after Muhammad's death.
Umma
A community united by a shared prophet, religion, or way of life; at Medina, a developing form of solidarity among believers that coexisted with kinship.
Muhajirun
The emigrants who left Mecca and elsewhere to join Muhammad at Medina.
Ansar
The Aws and Khazraj supporters who accepted Muhammad and sheltered the migrants.
Aws and Khazraj
The two principal Arab groups of Yathrib, long in rivalry before the Hijra.
Yathrib / al-Madina
The oasis's older name and the Muslim designation "the city," which became dominant.
Medina Document (Kitab)
An early text regulating obligations among Medina's groups, often called a "constitution," a label that can mislead.
Mu'akhat
The traditional pairing of migrants with helpers as sworn brothers; specific pairings rest on later tradition.
Munafiqun
Those described in the Qur'an and tradition as professing allegiance without sincere commitment; a moral category covering varied motives.
Diya (blood-money)
Compensation paid to resolve a killing or injury without retaliation; a continuing obligation under the Medina Document.
Ghazw
Raiding, an accepted Arabian practice for acquiring goods and asserting honor, governed by norms limiting bloodshed.
Haram
A sacred zone within which violence was prohibited; Yathrib is declared one by the Medina Document.
Qibla
The direction of prayer, changed during the Medinan period from Jerusalem to the Ka'ba.
Sadaqa and zakat
Voluntary charity and the more structured obligatory giving that developed in the Medinan period.
Ghanima
Booty; its distribution, with a fifth assigned to the Prophet and specified recipients, centralized redistribution.
Badr
The 624 battle in which the Medinan community defeated a Meccan force.
Uhud
The 625 battle in which the Muslims suffered a setback but Medina survived.
Al-Ahzab
The Confederates, the coalition that besieged Medina in 627 and was repulsed at the Trench.
Maghazi and sira
Later Arabic literary genres recounting Muhammad's campaigns and life, the main narrative sources for these years.
Isnad
The chain of transmitters supporting a report, used by early scholars and by modern historians assessing reliability.
Source criticism
The method of assessing the origin, transmission, and reliability of a source; central to reconstructing the Medinan period.
Arbitration
Settlement of disputes by a respected third party; the function that gave Muhammad authority at Yathrib.
Fiqh
The later, systematic science of Islamic jurisprudence, distinct from Medinan Qur'anic regulation.
Polity
An organized political community; a cautious term for Medina in 627, which lacked many features of a state.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a community becomes a political order without a blueprint. The Medinan years were improvised: rules were made in response to conflicts, institutions grew from practical needs, and allegiance was built through pacts, rituals, and shared danger. Understanding this process guards against reading the early community either as a finished state or as a purely spiritual gathering.

The chapter also reveals the tension between kinship and community that would shape Arab political life for centuries. The umma did not abolish the tribe, and the negotiation between the two recurs in the succession crisis of Chapter 18, in the garrison societies of Chapter 20, and in later dynastic politics. Likewise, the fusion of arbitration, worship, and command in a single leader raised questions about authority that later generations would address through law, theology, and institution-building.

Finally, the chapter models a method. Reconstructing Medina requires reading a contemporary but allusive scripture, an early but transmitted document, and a rich but retrospective narrative tradition, each with its own strengths and limits. Students who have learned to distinguish these layers, and to treat difficult episodes with precision and without sensationalism, are prepared for Chapter 17, where the Medinan community moves outward, and for the wider story of how a polity in a single oasis became the core of an empire.
