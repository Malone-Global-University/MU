Part VIII — The Ottoman Arab World
47

Provincial Power in the Seventeenth and Eighteenth Centuries

A History of the Arab World: From Ancient Crossroads to the Modern Arab World

Chapter Opening

In the 1740s and 1750s, each year the governor of Damascus, As'ad Pasha al-Azm, was responsible for assembling the great pilgrimage caravan to Mecca: provisioning it, paying the Bedouin groups along its route, and providing troops. The sultan had appointed him, yet the household, revenue, and much of the authority he used were his own. That combination, imperial office joined to local power, defines this chapter.

Between roughly 1600 and 1800 the Ottoman Arab provinces were transformed in how they were governed. Powerful households, military elites, local dynasties, and tribal confederations acquired control over soldiers, taxes, and offices that Istanbul had once held more directly. In Egypt, mamluk households dominated the government of Cairo. In Damascus, the al-Azm family held the governorship. In Palestine and Mount Lebanon, tax farmers and emirs built regional regimes, and in Iraq, mamluk pashas and local dynasties governed Baghdad, Mosul, and Basra. In Algiers, Tunis, and Tripoli, military oligarchies and hereditary dynasties emerged.

Older histories explained this as Ottoman decline: a weakened center losing its provinces. This textbook adopts the more careful view of recent scholarship, which sees provincial power as developing through the empire's own mechanisms: appointments, tax-farming contracts, military recruitment, and negotiated recognition. The sultan remained the source of legitimacy, and provincial rulers sought his confirmation, paid tribute, and fought in his wars. Autonomy was not independence.

The chapter studies how this worked. It begins with the structures that made provincial power possible, namely households, patronage, tax farming, garrisons, notables, and tribal alliances. It then moves through regional case studies, from Egypt to Syria, Palestine, and Lebanon, then Iraq, then the North African regencies, before assessing the consequences for ordinary people and the larger interpretive question.

Historians reconstruct this period from Ottoman appointment orders and tax-farming registers, provincial court records, local chronicles such as al-Jabarti's account of Egypt, endowment deeds, architecture, and European consular and merchant reports. Each source type has its own perspective, and the chapter shows how they can be read against one another.

This chapter follows Chapters 43 to 46, which described the Ottoman conquests, provincial government, cities and commerce, and religious and communal life, and it precedes Chapter 48 on Arabia and the first Saudi state and Chapters 49 and 50 on the nineteenth century. Those chapters reserve the conquest, the Saudi–Wahhabi alliance, the French invasion, Muhammad Ali, and the Tanzimat reforms.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how provincial leaders acquired control over troops, revenue, and offices while remaining within the Ottoman system.
  2. Distinguish rebellion, negotiated autonomy, and delegated imperial authority.
  3. Describe the political household and explain why it was not simply a family or a dynasty.
  4. Analyze the iltizam and malikâne systems and their political consequences.
  5. Trace the political development of Egypt from the Janissary and bey establishments through Ali Bey and the late-eighteenth-century beys.
  6. Compare provincial regimes in Damascus, Aleppo, Mount Lebanon, and Palestine.
  7. Compare mamluk government in Baghdad with the Egyptian households, and with dynasties in Mosul and Basra.
  8. Contrast the political systems of Algiers, Tunis, and Tripoli.
  9. Assess how different groups experienced provincial power.
  10. Evaluate the decline and transformation interpretations of Ottoman history.
  11. Interpret primary and secondary sources for provincial politics, noting perspective and limits.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How did revenue control translate into political power in the provinces?
  2. Why did Istanbul so often confirm provincial leaders it could not control?
  3. In what sense was a household different from a dynasty?
  4. How did Egyptian, Iraqi, and North African regimes differ in origin and form?
  5. Why should "autonomy" not be equated with "independence" or with nationalism?
  6. What roles did tribal confederations and urban notables play in provincial government?
  7. How did different social groups experience the same regime?
  8. What evidence supports the argument for transformation rather than decline, and what are its limits?

Historical Context

By the early seventeenth century the Ottoman Empire governed a vast territory through governors, garrisons, and provincial administrations established in the sixteenth century, as Chapters 43 and 44 explained. The provinces differed: Egypt and Algiers had distinctive institutions, Baghdad and Basra were frontier posts toward Safavid Iran, and Syria combined cities, countryside, and the pilgrimage route. Chapter 45 described the commerce and pilgrimage that tied them together, and Chapter 46 the religious and communal institutions that shaped them.

Pressures on the empire changed the context for provincial governance. Prices rose, the cost of warfare increased, firearms-equipped infantry became central, and long wars required more cash than the older land-grant system could raise. Governors changed frequently, and the garrison and tax-collection systems came to depend on locally rooted people. These shifts, which this chapter examines for their political consequences, created openings for households and local rulers.

Two cautions apply from the start. First, provincial elites came from varied ethnic, linguistic, and social backgrounds and pursued different goals; they were not a unified Arab or nationalist movement. Second, the regional cases differed substantially and cannot be reduced to one pattern. Morocco, ruled by its own dynasty, was outside the Ottoman Empire, and the Arabian regions are reserved for Chapter 48.

Division I — The Transformation of Provincial Authority

Section 47.1

The Ottoman Empire and the Rise of Provincial Power

By 1600 the Ottoman sultans claimed sovereignty over Arab lands stretching from Algiers to Basra, a conquest and administrative system described in Chapters 43 and 44. The following two centuries did not overturn that sovereignty, but they changed how it worked. Several pressures acted together. Inflation and currency problems strained the imperial treasury from the later sixteenth century. Firearms and the growth of salaried infantry reduced the importance of the provincial cavalry paid through land assignments (timars). Long wars against the Habsburgs and the Safavids, culminating in the Great Turkish War (1683–1699) and the Treaty of Karlowitz in 1699, and later the wars with Russia from 1768, demanded cash and soldiers on a scale the old system could not easily supply. Whoever could raise money and men locally became useful to Istanbul, and usefulness became leverage.

Governors compounded the problem by serving short terms. A governor sent from the capital typically held a province for a year or two, brought a modest retinue, and left before he could build durable local ties. The people who remained were the garrison officers, the tax farmers, the great families of the towns, and the tribal leaders of the countryside. When a governor needed to collect taxes, protect a pilgrimage caravan, or raise troops for a campaign, he needed their cooperation, and they understood that he would soon be gone. This structural asymmetry between a rotating official and a permanent local elite is central to everything that follows.

Students should distinguish three situations that are often blurred together. Rebellion meant open repudiation of the sultan's authority, and was rare. Negotiated autonomy meant that a governor, household, or dynasty exercised substantial control over troops, taxes, and appointments while continuing to seek imperial confirmation, to pay tribute, to have the sultan's name pronounced in the Friday sermon, and to supply forces in wartime. Delegated authority meant the ordinary powers any Ottoman governor held. Most of the political history in this chapter belongs to the second category, which is why the word "autonomy" is used here and the word "independence" only with care. Even the most defiant figures, such as Ali Bey in Egypt or Zahir al-Umar in Galilee, operated within the vocabulary of Ottoman offices and titles.

The chapter's central question is how provincial leaders acquired greater control over military forces, revenue, and local government while remaining connected to the imperial system. Older scholarship answered that the empire was in decline and the provinces were slipping away. The Historical Debate later in this chapter examines that argument and the revisionist scholarship that has largely replaced it, while also noting that some scholars, such as Shimon Shamir in his study of As'ad Pasha al-Azm, have drawn a more pessimistic picture of provincial governance. The chronological focus is the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, ending around 1800; the French invasion of Egypt, Muhammad Ali, and the nineteenth-century reassertion of central power belong to Chapters 49 and 50.

Section 47.2

Households, Patronage, and the Foundations of Political Power

The basic unit of provincial politics was the household. An Ottoman political household (in Arabic bayt, in Ottoman Turkish often kapı, "gate") was not simply a family. It was a network of patrons and dependents: relatives by blood and marriage, secretaries and stewards, soldiers, purchased or captured slaves who were educated and later freed, clients who gained protection and employment, and allied merchants and scholars. The head of a household supported this network from salaries, tax revenues, trade, and the income of pious endowments (waqf), and in return commanded its loyalty. Istanbul's grand viziers and palace officials ran households of the same kind, and provincial leaders imitated the pattern. The household was at once a military unit, a school of administration, a financial enterprise, and a political party.

Households recruited and trained followers. In Egypt, Iraq, and some other regions, military slaves, commonly called mamluks, were bought in the Caucasus and the Black Sea region, brought up in the household, trained in arms and administration, and manumitted into its service. Such followers owed their careers to the patron rather than to birth, and a patron's most trusted successor was often a favored freedman or a son-in-law rather than a son. This is why historians caution against equating a household with a hereditary dynasty. Some households did become dynasties, as the Jalilis of Mosul and the Husaynids of Tunis did, but many perpetuated themselves through patronage, marriage alliances, and the adoption of talent. Marriage was a political instrument: the great figures of Egypt and Baghdad often married their daughters, widows, or the widows of predecessors to ambitious followers, so that the household's property and loyalties passed with them.

The household explains why provincial power was both resilient and fragile. It could survive the death of its head if a capable successor held the network together, and it could finance armies without drawing on the sultan's treasury. But it depended on a constant flow of revenue and on the loyalty of followers who could defect to a rival household, as the story of Ali Bey and Abu al-Dhahab shows. Jane Hathaway's studies of Ottoman Egypt, especially The Politics of Households in Ottoman Egypt: The Rise of the Qazdağlıs (1997), are the standard demonstration that household politics, rather than formal institutions alone, organized provincial life, and her argument that such households emerged from within Ottoman institutions rather than against them underlies much of this chapter.

Section 47.3

Tax Farming, Land Revenues, and the Financial Foundations of Autonomy

Political autonomy rested on revenue. Chapter 44 described Ottoman provincial finance in general terms; this section is concerned with its political effects. Under iltizam, the government auctioned the right to collect the taxes of a given source of revenue (a mukataa: a village, a group of villages, a customs post, a mint, a bundle of dues) to a tax farmer (multezim), who paid the treasury a fixed sum and kept the difference. Early iltizam contracts were short, often a year or a few years, which let the state reassign them but also discouraged tax farmers from investing in the land or treating the peasants with restraint.

In 1695 the government introduced the malikâne, a lifetime tax farm. The buyer paid a large advance sum and then fixed annual installments, and held the revenue source for life, with arrangements for transfer on death that evolved over time. The state gained immediate cash during an expensive war and a more predictable income. Wealthy buyers, among them provincial officials, military men, and urban notables, gained long-term access to revenue and a stake in the imperial system. Historians such as Ariel Salzmann have argued that this arrangement tied provincial elites to the empire financially even as it enriched them. Holding a malikâne was a right to collect revenue, not ownership of the underlying land, and the malikâne was applied unevenly across the provinces. Egypt, for instance, had its own long-standing iltizam practice that became increasingly heritable in practice, and different provinces had different mixes of timars, short-term iltizam, and lifetime grants.

The political consequences were large. A household that held many tax farms could pay its soldiers, buy offices, and extend credit to peasants and townspeople, and it could outlast a governor who held office for only a year or two. At the same time, the central government retained claims on every contract: it received the advance and installments, could confiscate tax farms from disgraced holders, and used the sale and reassignment of fiscal rights to reward or discipline local figures. A dramatic example is the grant of iltizams in the Palestinian districts of Gaza, Ramla, Jaffa, and Nablus to Egypt's Abu al-Dhahab in 1771, which he later invoked against Zahir al-Umar. Revenue control gave provincial leaders power; imperial ownership of the system gave Istanbul continuing influence. Historians read the evidence for these arrangements in tax-farm registers, appointment orders, and the court registers that record sales, loans, and disputes.

Section 47.4

Provincial Governors, Garrisons, and Military Households

The Ottoman governor was appointed by the sultan, usually served for a short term, and was normally sent from Istanbul or from the central elite. He commanded a limited personal retinue and had to govern through local forces. These included the Janissary garrisons (kapıkulu) posted in the main cities and fortresses, whose members over the seventeenth century intermarried with townspeople, entered trade, and became a local force with local interests, often called yerliyya ("local" Janissaries) in the Syrian cities. They also included locally recruited soldiers, mercenaries paid from provincial revenues, and in several provinces the mamluk households described in Sections 47.6 to 47.9 and 47.15. Friction between the imperial and the local Janissaries was a recurrent source of urban violence, as in Damascus in 1757.

This produced a recurring problem. A governor who needed troops to collect taxes, protect the pilgrimage caravan, or fight a war found that the strongest forces in his province answered to someone else. Governors made alliances with the dominant household, married into it, or tried to play factions against each other. Alternatively, powerful local figures obtained the governorship for themselves: the al-Azm family in Damascus, the Mamluk pashas in Baghdad, and Ahmad Pasha al-Jazzar in the province of Sidon each did so, and Istanbul's decision to confirm them was usually a decision to accept an existing balance of power while keeping the right to appoint, to rotate, and eventually to remove.

The distinction between an appointed outsider and an entrenched insider is important. The former depended on imperial favor and on whatever local support he could buy in a short term. The latter could outwait a series of hostile appointees. Istanbul had a range of responses: confirmation, transfer to a distant province, the dispatch of an expedition, secret orders to rivals, or, as in the case of As'ad Pasha al-Azm, arrest and execution on a pretext. The cases that follow show it using each of these, usually in combination.

Section 47.5

Local Notables, Tribal Confederations, and Political Alliances

Provincial households did not govern alone. Their authority depended on relationships with local notables, known in Ottoman sources as ayan: landholders, merchants, ulama (religious scholars), heads of Sufi orders, descendants of the Prophet (ashraf), and the leaders of guilds and neighborhoods. Notables supplied credit, advice, legal and religious legitimacy, and the intermediaries who collected taxes and kept order. In return they obtained protection, offices, tax farms, and influence over appointments. Chapter 46 treated the religious and communal roles of these figures; here they appear as political participants.

Rural authority was often negotiated with tribal leaders. Bedouin and pastoral confederations, discussed in Chapter 3 as an integral part of the region's economy, controlled the routes over which grain, pilgrims, and troops moved. Provincial rulers paid them subsidies, appointed their shaykhs as tax collectors or escorts, married into their families, and sometimes fought them. Tribal confederations were not states, and they were not uniformly rebellious: a shaykh could serve a governor for years and then break with him over a payment, a jurisdictional dispute, or a change of governor. Examples include the Hawwara of Upper Egypt under Shaykh Humam, the Bani Sakhr and other groups along the Syrian pilgrimage route, and the Muntafiq of southern Iraq. The Syrian pilgrimage shows the logic clearly: the payments made to Bedouin along the route were a form of insurance, and when they failed or were withheld the consequences could be catastrophic.

These alliances were unstable because the bargains were renegotiated whenever the balance of force, harvests, or the identity of the governor changed. For students, the useful concept is not "center versus periphery" but a series of overlapping bargains among actors who each needed something from the others: the sultan needed revenue and loyalty, the household needed legitimacy and troops, the notable needed protection, and the tribe needed access to markets, pasture, and subsidies. Our knowledge of these bargains comes mostly from the writings of urban scholars, officials, and travelers, so the tribal side of the story must be reconstructed with care.

Historical Vocabulary
Household (bayt / kapı) and Patronage

A household was a political and economic network headed by a patron, comprising kin, servants, clients, soldiers, and often purchased and later freed slaves. Patronage was the exchange of protection, office, and income for loyalty and service. In this chapter the household is the basic unit of provincial power.

The term matters because it explains how leaders recruited armies, financed them, and transmitted authority without necessarily relying on hereditary succession. Students should not translate it as "family" or "dynasty": the successor of a household head was often a freedman, and some households never became dynasties.

Historical Vocabulary
Mamluk

A mamluk (Arabic, "owned") was a slave recruited as a soldier, trained, and normally freed into the service of his master. The Mamluk Sultanate of Chapters 36 to 38 was a sovereign state governed by such soldiers. In the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries the term described the members of mamluk households inside the Ottoman system, in Egypt and in Baghdad.

The same word therefore covers different institutions. Egypt's beys and Baghdad's pashas were not restored medieval sultans, and their households formed in different circumstances. Mamluk status was also a legal and social condition, not an ethnicity, though recruits were typically brought from the Caucasus.

Historical Vocabulary
Iltizam, Malikâne, and Ayan

Iltizam was the auctioned right to collect the taxes of a revenue source (mukataa). The malikâne (from 1695) made this right a lifetime grant in return for an advance and annual payments. Neither conferred ownership of the land itself. The ayan were local notables who acted as intermediaries between the state and local society, and who by the eighteenth century were sometimes recognized in official documents.

These terms matter because they connect local wealth with imperial finance. A tax farm made its holder dependent on the state's legal order as well as enriched by it.

Historical Evidence

Historians reconstruct provincial politics from several kinds of source. Ottoman central records (appointment orders, tax-farm registers, financial accounts, and the sultan's replies to petitions, found in the Ottoman archives in Istanbul) show the imperial view. Provincial court registers (sijills) record property, credit, marriages, and disputes, and show how ordinary people and notables used the courts. Chronicles by local authors, such as al-Jabarti in Cairo, offer detailed narratives but reflect the writer's class and views. Household papers, endowment deeds, architecture, and the reports of European consuls and merchants supplement these.

Each has limits. Ottoman records emphasize the state's priorities; chronicles are written by scholars who were often hostile to military rulers; consular reports reflect European commercial concerns. Peasants and tribal groups appear mostly through the eyes of others, which is why historians read these sources against each other.

Division II — Egypt: Military Households and Provincial Autonomy

Section 47.6

Egypt after the Ottoman Conquest: Janissaries, Mamluks, and Political Competition

After the conquest of 1517 and the settlement associated with the law code of the 1520s, Egypt was governed by an Ottoman governor, the pasha, assisted by a council (diwan) and supported by several regiments (ocaks), the best known being the Janissaries (Mustahfizan) and the Azeban. Alongside the pasha stood the beys: military governors who held the title sancak bey and who were from the beginning drawn from households of mamluk background. These Ottoman-era beys must be distinguished from the sovereign Mamluk sultans overthrown in 1517 (Chapters 36 to 38). They were officers inside the Ottoman system, not a surviving independent regime, though they drew on mamluk recruiting practices and on the prestige of the earlier tradition. Egypt remained among the empire's richest provinces, and its tribute and its annual shipments of grain and funds for the holy cities made it important to Istanbul.

Over the seventeenth century, the regiments became enmeshed in Egyptian urban life. Soldiers engaged in crafts, trade, and the tax-farming economy, and the officers of the regiments, especially the Janissary kahya (deputy commander), accumulated tax farms, commercial interests, and followings. The pasha, who changed every year or two, had little ability to control them, and military rebellions against governors were frequent in the seventeenth century. Hathaway has shown how Egyptian politics were organized by two rival factions, conventionally called Faqari and Qasimi, which cut across regiments and households and competed for the offices of the beys, for tax farms, and for control of the revenue passing through Cairo. The chronicler al-Jabarti, writing a century later, treated the origins of the factions and the "civil war" of 1711 as foundational episodes, and modern historians regard 1711 as a turning point that weakened the older bey factions and opened space for new households grounded in the regiments.

Local competition thus gradually changed who effectively governed Egypt. The pasha represented the sultan, received the annual tribute remittance, and presided over the diwan, and Ottoman recognition remained essential. But access to money, soldiers, and appointments passed to the household leaders. The result was a system in which the Ottoman governor was a figurehead of imperial legitimacy and a negotiator among local power-holders, rather than their commander.

Section 47.7

The Qazdağlı Household and the Consolidation of Power

The Qazdağlı (Kazdağlı, in Arabic sources al-Qazdaghli) household emerged from the Janissary establishment in the late seventeenth century. Its early leader, Mustafa Kahya, rose through the regimental hierarchy, accumulated tax farms and commercial interests, and built a following that included relatives, retainers, and mamluks bought and trained in his house. Hathaway argues that this household was a product of Ottoman institutions: its leaders were Ottoman military officers who adopted the mamluk model of household recruitment, rather than heirs restoring pre-1517 Mamluk sovereignty. The distinction matters because it explains why the household's legitimacy depended on service to the empire and on regimental office.

By the middle of the eighteenth century the household had become a large network whose members held the offices of bey and kahya, controlled many tax farms, and had interests in the trade carried through Cairo, including the coffee trade from the Red Sea, which al-Jabarti's biographies of merchants illustrate. Its leaders in the 1740s and early 1750s, notably Ibrahim Kahya and Ridwan Kahya, shared power in a partnership that later writers remembered as a period of stability, and a third figure, Abd al-Rahman Katkhuda (whom al-Jabarti lists among the notable lives and who died in 1776), remained influential for decades. The household's power rested on its ability to protect its dependents, pay its soldiers, and place its followers in office, and it did not require abandoning the formal Ottoman framework. The pasha remained, the tribute continued to be sent, and Egypt's leading figure commanded the pilgrimage caravan as amir al-hajj, an office through which Cairo's military leaders showed their service to the sultan and their standing in the Muslim world.

The stability proved temporary. When Ibrahim Kahya and Ridwan Kahya died in the 1750s, competition among the mamluks they had trained, who were not bound by kinship, opened the way to a new generation of leaders. One of them was Ali Bey, a former mamluk of Ibrahim Kahya, whose career is the subject of the next section. The Qazdağlı household thus illustrates both the strength of the household as a form of political organization and its weakness: the very mamluks whose loyalty sustained it were also potential rivals.

Section 47.8

Ali Bey al-Kabir: Ambition, Military Power, and Challenges to Istanbul

Ali Bey (1728–1773), later called al-Kabir ("the Great"), was born in the Caucasus (sources point to Abkhazia), was brought to Egypt as a slave, and became a mamluk in Ibrahim Kahya's household. He served as amir al-hajj in the early 1750s and became shaykh al-balad, the leading bey of Cairo, in 1760 after a struggle among the household's mamluks. His rise was interrupted: rivals drove him out in 1766, and he returned in 1767 with a following of mamluks and eliminated his enemies. He also pursued the Red Sea trade and sought to strengthen his own household by recruiting and promoting his own mamluks, among them Abu al-Dhahab and Ismail Bey.

In 1768 the Ottoman Empire went to war with Russia, a conflict that absorbed imperial attention and resources, and Ali Bey used the opportunity. He deposed the Ottoman governor, Rakım Mehmed Pasha, and in 1769 withheld the tribute and issued coinage. The coins deserve careful reading: they retained the tughra of Sultan Mustafa III while including Ali Bey's name in a stylized form, a statement of autonomy rather than a straightforward rejection of Ottoman sovereignty. In the same period he suppressed a revolt in Upper Egypt associated with the Hawwara, whose chief Shaykh Humam, once a powerful partner of the Cairo beys, died in 1769, an episode al-Jabarti treated at length. In 1770 a force under Abu al-Dhahab went to the Hijaz and entered Mecca, and in 1770–1771 Egyptian forces advanced into Syria in alliance with Zahir al-Umar.

The Syrian campaign ended in a reversal. Abu al-Dhahab's forces took Damascus in June 1771, but within ten days he withdrew to Egypt, apparently persuaded by Ottoman agents and by his own interests. He moved against Ali Bey, who was forced out of Egypt in April 1772 and reached Palestine in May, where he joined Zahir al-Umar. The Russian fleet in the Mediterranean bombarded and briefly occupied Beirut in June 1772, in the same context of cooperation. Ali Bey returned to Egypt in 1773 with a small force, was defeated near Salihiyya, and died of his wounds in Cairo on 8 May 1773.

Historians have interpreted Ali Bey in different ways, and students should avoid the temptation to see him as an early Egyptian nationalist. Daniel Crecelius's study of the regimes of Ali Bey and Abu al-Dhahab shows a mamluk ruler concerned with control of Egypt's revenues, the Red Sea and Syrian trade, and the security of his household. His coalition was composed of mamluks of Caucasian origin and mixed followers, and his objectives appear to have been the control of Egypt and the surrounding trade, not an Egyptian state. His downfall shows the limits of his project: his authority depended on the loyalty of mamluks who could, as Abu al-Dhahab did, defect, and Ottoman legitimacy, including the Ottoman grant of iltizams to Abu al-Dhahab in 1771, proved a powerful instrument against him.

Section 47.9

Egypt's Late-Eighteenth-Century Mamluk Leadership

Muhammad Bey Abu al-Dhahab became the leading bey in 1772–1773 with Ottoman acceptance and governed until his death in 1775 during a campaign in Palestine that the sultan had sanctioned against Zahir al-Umar. He took Jaffa in 1775 and died soon afterward, near Acre. His mamluks, Ibrahim Bey and Murad Bey, emerged after a brief period of competition with other members of the household, among them Ismail Bey, who was shaykh al-balad in 1777–1778, as joint rulers from 1778, an arrangement that lasted until 1798 with one major interruption. Ibrahim Bey handled much of the civil administration and Murad Bey the military command, a division of labor that recurs in Egyptian household politics.

The regime faced continuing difficulties. Competition among households and factions required constant payments to soldiers, and the beys raised additional levies from villages, merchants, and townspeople. Al-Jabarti attributed high prices and shortages in 1783–1784 to measures of Ibrahim and Murad (see the Primary Source Window in this chapter) and recorded a popular protest in 1786 triggered by a bey's attack on a Sufi shaykh. Relations with Istanbul were strained. In 1786 the Ottoman government sent an expedition under the admiral Cezayirli Gazi Hasan Pasha, which forced Ibrahim and Murad to withdraw to Upper Egypt. The expedition was not sustained: Hasan Pasha withdrew, Ottoman authority in Cairo weakened again, and in 1791 the beys returned, again acknowledging the sultan's sovereignty while controlling Egyptian government.

By the late 1790s Egypt was governed by an Ottoman pasha with little actual power and by a mamluk household regime that collected revenue, commanded the troops, and sent tribute to Istanbul unevenly. Murad Bey served as amir al-hajj in the period after the return. The events of 1798, the French invasion and what followed, belong to Chapter 49. For this chapter the point is the political order that existed immediately before them: a provincial government dominated by military households whose legitimacy still depended on Ottoman recognition, and whose members were Caucasian-born mamluks governing an Arabic-speaking population from Cairo.

Fiscal and Military Change and Provincial Consolidation

Long-term causes

Inflation and fiscal strain from the later sixteenth century; the shift from cavalry to infantry and firearms; the growth of cash taxation; Janissary integration into local society.

Short-term causes

Costly wars, including the Great Turkish War and the Russo-Ottoman wars, that required cash and manpower; the 1695 malikâne; frequent rotation of governors.

Event or transformation

Households and local rulers took control of troops, tax farms, and appointments in many provinces.

Immediate effects

Rivalry among households; recurrent factional violence; negotiated confirmation of local rulers by Istanbul.

Long-term consequences

Durable provincial establishments that nineteenth-century reformers would confront (Chapters 49 and 50).

The pattern was not uniform and was not caused by fiscal factors alone: frontier position, local resources, and personal leadership also mattered.

Division III — Syria, Palestine, and Mount Lebanon

Section 47.10

The al-Azm Family and Provincial Authority in Damascus

The al-Azm family, which came from the Syrian town of Ma'arrat al-Nu'man, provided governors of Damascus and neighboring provinces for much of the period from the 1720s to the 1750s. Isma'il Pasha al-Azm, who had been the local military commander at Ma'arrat al-Nu'man, was appointed governor of Damascus in 1725 and also served as commander of the pilgrimage caravan until his dismissal in 1730. Historians such as Karl Barbir, in Ottoman Rule in Damascus, 1708–1758, regard the charge of embezzling pilgrimage funds that was brought against him as dubious, and the timing suggests he fell with the officials appointed under Sultan Ahmed III after the coup of 1730. His brother Sulayman Pasha governed Damascus in the 1730s, and his son As'ad Pasha, after governing Hama, became governor of Damascus in 1743 and held the office until 1757. Abdul-Karim Rafeq's studies of the province of Damascus place the family within a wider history of notables, Janissaries, and Ottoman officials.

The governorship of Damascus carried a special responsibility: leading the annual pilgrimage caravan to Mecca, the Syrian hajj. The governor served as the amir al-hajj, organized supplies and escorts, paid the Bedouin groups along the route, and in the spring toured the province's districts to collect the taxes earmarked for the caravan. This duty gave the office prestige throughout the Muslim world and required the governor to command substantial funds and troops. As'ad Pasha was widely regarded as an effective governor; the caravan reached its destination throughout his tenure, and he spent his revenues on construction, including the Azm Palace in Damascus (built around 1749–1752) and the caravanserai known as Khan As'ad Pasha (1752), buildings that displayed wealth and legitimacy and were also productive investments through endowments.

His end is a lesson in the limits of provincial power. Istanbul removed him from Damascus in early 1757, under a new grand vizier, Ragip Pasha, who was reported to distrust the family's power, and appointed Husayn Pasha ibn Makki, a member of a Damascene household with Gaza roots. The pilgrimage caravan of that year, commanded by Husayn Pasha, was attacked in the autumn by Bedouin under Qa'dan al-Fayez of the Bani Sakhr, with heavy losses, and the failure caused a crisis in the empire. Husayn Pasha was dismissed. The government suspected that As'ad, who had been shifted to other posts after his removal, had incited the attack, in part because no such disaster had occurred during his governorship, and he was arrested and executed in 1758 while being taken toward exile; accounts differ over whether this occurred on a ship or at Ankara. Thus the order of events matters: As'ad's success in protecting the caravan helped build his standing, his dismissal came before the disaster, and the suspicions arising from the disaster contributed to his execution. The family still provided governors afterwards, but the era of unusual family dominance ended.

Section 47.11

Aleppo, Northern Syria, and the Politics of Urban Notables

Aleppo developed a different political pattern from Damascus. As Abraham Marcus has shown in his study of eighteenth-century Aleppo, The Middle East on the Eve of Modernity, effective local power rested in competing groups of urban elites more than in a single governing family. Two groupings were especially important: the local Janissaries (yerliyya), who had become an urban force with roots in neighborhoods, crafts, and tax farming, and the sayyids or ashraf, descendants of the Prophet, whose leader, the naqib al-ashraf, commanded prestige and a following. Wealthy merchants, religious scholars, and the administrators of endowments influenced both. The Ottoman governors sent to Aleppo were frequently rotated and had to work with these groups, and the provincial administration also relied on the revenue of the city's commerce and of its hinterland.

Rivalry between Janissaries and notables produced periodic violence in the city and unsettled the surrounding countryside, but it also produced bargains. Notables who held tax farms or endowments had an interest in order, and the governor needed their cooperation to collect revenue and provision the army and the pilgrimage. Imperial connections mattered: a local figure who could secure an appointment or a favorable ruling from Istanbul gained advantage over rivals, and the sultan's government could favor one faction to restrain another. Aleppo's court records show how disputes over property, credit, and status were carried on in legal as well as military terms, and its foreign merchants, including European consuls, observed the factional contests and recorded them in their reports.

A documented example comes from Yahya Araz's study of Aleppo's Janissaries between the early eighteenth century and the 1760s, published in Cihannüma (2022) and based on preliminary findings from the Aleppo court records. Araz finds that most of the city's Janissaries and their families had come from the surrounding countryside and lived as ordinary townsmen, concentrated in particular quarters and increasingly involved in credit and other economic activities, a process that signaled their integration into the urban fabric. Their expansion brought their interests into conflict with those of the ashraf, a group that, in his account, included members of established merchant families, religious dignitaries, and others claiming descent from the Prophet. Araz argues that the competition, especially after the 1760s, reshaped the Janissaries' role and strengthened a solidarity among them that had earlier been divided along ethnic, tribal, and regional lines. The example shows how political rivalry in Aleppo grew out of economic and social change, not only out of office-holding.

Students should not assume that Aleppo's politics resembled those of Damascus, Baghdad, or Cairo. There was no single dominant household of the al-Azm or Qazdağlı type; the political order consisted of shifting alliances among groups none of which could monopolize power for long. This is an important reminder that provincial strengthening could take the form of a plural urban elite rather than a single ruler. Aleppo's commercial history appears in Chapter 45 and is not repeated here, and specific episodes of factional conflict should be studied through Marcus and the other scholarship cited at the end of the chapter.

Section 47.12

Mount Lebanon: The Ma'n and Shihab Houses

Mount Lebanon was governed through local emirs who held tax farms from the Ottoman provinces of Sidon and Tripoli and ruled Druze and Christian communities on a mountain suited to defense and to silk production. The best-known early figure is Fakhr al-Din II of the Ma'n family (ruled c. 1590–1635), who expanded his authority over coastal and interior districts, spent years in exile in Italy, and was eventually defeated and executed in Istanbul in 1635. His career is background: it shows both the potential and the limits of mountain-based power, since Ottoman forces could overcome it when the government chose to do so. After a period of upheaval, Ahmad Ma'n, a grandnephew of Fakhr al-Din, was granted the tax farms of the Shuf, Gharb, Jurd, and Matn districts in 1667 and governed with the support of the Qaysi faction until his death in 1697.

Ahmad Ma'n died without a male heir. The Qaysi shaykhs, including the Jumblatt family, assembled at Semqaniyeh and chose Bashir Shihab, a Sunni Muslim emir of the Wadi al-Taym whose mother was Ahmad's sister, to succeed him. The Ottoman government's response and the role of Ahmad's grandson Haydar Shihab are reported differently in the sources: the tax farms went to Haydar, who was young, and Bashir was prominent as the leading figure during Haydar's minority. The Shihabs were thus related to the Ma'ns through the female line and succeeded as multazims rather than as hereditary sovereigns. Haydar Shihab, who took power afterward, led the Qaysi alliance to a decisive victory over the Yamani faction at Ayn Dara on 20 March 1711, after which many Yamani Druze left for the Hawran, and he ruled until 1732.

Over the eighteenth century the Shihab emirs relied on tax farming, on alliances with powerful local lords (the muqata'ji families such as the Jumblatts and Abu'l-Lama), on the Maronite Church and the Maronite notables who gained influence in the mountain, and on the geography of the mountain itself. Some Shihabs became Maronite Christians. The emirate's relations with the governors of Sidon and Damascus were tense. Yusuf Shihab (ruled 1770–1789), whose reign coincided with the Ali Bey and Zahir al-Umar episodes and the rise of al-Jazzar, was drawn into the contests of the Palestinian coast and died in Acre in 1790 after falling into al-Jazzar's hands. His successor Bashir II (from 1788) belongs mostly to the nineteenth century, and this chapter stops before the history of Lebanon in Chapters 50 and 54.

Section 47.13

Zahir al-Umar and the Rise of Provincial Power in Palestine

Zahir al-Umar al-Zaydani came from a family of tax farmers in the Galilee, the Banu Zaydan. He acquired the tax farm of Tiberias around 1730, fortified the town, and expanded his authority over Galilean villages by combining fiscal rights, a military following, and alliances with rural communities. Around 1750 he took Acre, which became his capital and a port for the export of cotton and grain to European merchants, above all the French. Thomas Philipp's study Acre: The Rise and Fall of a Palestinian City, 1730–1831 describes how Zahir used a mixed army of locally recruited and mercenary troops, supported cotton cultivation and trade, rebuilt Acre's fortifications, and cooperated with merchants who needed secure markets. His authority was recognized, grudgingly or otherwise, by governors of Sidon and Damascus, and his sons and relatives administered the districts under his control.

His expanding power brought conflict with Ottoman officials. After the Syrian hajj disaster of 1757, the Ottoman authorities regarded him with suspicion, although the sources do not show that he was responsible. In the period of the Russo-Ottoman war he cooperated with Ali Bey of Egypt, from 1769, with Egyptian forces fighting alongside his own against the governor of Damascus in 1771, and in 1772 with Russian naval forces operating in the eastern Mediterranean. These decisions gave Istanbul strong grounds to treat him as a rebel. After Abu al-Dhahab turned on Ali Bey and then received the sultan's sanction to move against Zahir in 1774–1775, the Egyptian army advanced on Palestine, but Abu al-Dhahab's death in 1775 left the field to the Ottoman admiral Cezayirli Gazi Hasan Pasha, whose fleet and forces attacked Acre. Zahir was killed in the fighting in August 1775, when he was an elderly man.

His career illustrates how local authority could grow from fiscal and political arrangements inside the empire, and also how those arrangements could end. He was an Ottoman tax farmer who became a regional ruler, and his later defiance proved fatal. Like Ali Bey, he should not be described as a nationalist: his ambitions were regional and dynastic, his army and followers were mixed, and he worked within a framework of Ottoman titles and obligations that he never entirely repudiated. Palestinian historiography has reassessed him as an early example of local initiative in the region's economic development, and the debate over how far to read his career in that light is part of the interpretation of this period.

Section 47.14

Ahmad Pasha al-Jazzar and the Political Power of Acre

Ahmad Pasha al-Jazzar, of Bosnian origin, began his career as a mamluk in the household of Ali Bey in Egypt, left after the fall of that regime, and entered Ottoman service. After Zahir al-Umar's death in 1775 he became governor of Sidon with his seat at Acre and ruled the Palestinian coast and the Galilee, with interruptions, until his death in 1804 (some sources give 1805). He also held the governorship of Damascus at times, and he came to dominate the region by means of an Ottoman office, an armed household of mamluks and mercenaries, and control of the revenues and trade of Acre. Al-Jabarti included his life among the notable biographies of the period, and the French merchants and consuls on the coast recorded his regime from the standpoint of foreigners.

Al-Jazzar's style of rule differed from Zahir's. He relied more on formal Ottoman appointments and the machinery of the province, ruled with notorious severity (his nickname "the Butcher" reflects this), and built a monopolistic system in which the governor controlled much of the trade in cotton and grain. He invested in the city's fortifications and public buildings, including the mosque that bears his name, begun in the early 1780s. His relations with the Druze emirs of Mount Lebanon and with the governors of Damascus shifted with circumstances; the death of Yusuf Shihab in Acre in 1790, and the alternating choices of Shihab emirs, show how he used and constrained the mountain's rulers. Ottoman recognition remained valuable to him as protection against enemies at court and as legitimation of his rule over a large area, and the imperial government in turn found him useful as a loyal commander and tax collector, though it also dismissed and reinstated him.

By the end of the eighteenth century, he governed one of the strongest provincial regimes in the Arab East. His resistance to Napoleon Bonaparte at Acre in 1799 belongs to Chapter 49, but it indicates what an Ottoman provincial ruler could mobilize: a fortified port, a loyal household, and the support of the imperial navy. The contrast with Zahir is instructive: Zahir built a regional state beside Ottoman government and was destroyed for it, while al-Jazzar built one inside it and survived.

Division IV — Iraq: Military Households, Local Dynasties, and Frontier Politics

Section 47.15

Baghdad and the Rise of Mamluk Provincial Government

In 1704 Hasan Pasha, an Ottoman governor of Georgian origin sent from Istanbul, took charge of Baghdad after a period of political instability and frequent change of governors, and he and his son Ahmad Pasha, governor from 1723 (some sources date it 1724) to 1747, reorganized provincial power. Hasan Pasha built a household that included mamluks bought in the Caucasus, especially Georgia, and Ahmad Pasha expanded it. These mamluks were trained and placed in key positions in the army and administration, and by the time of Ahmad's death the household was a self-perpetuating corps numbering in the thousands. Ahmad Pasha's successful defense of Baghdad against the army of Nadir Shah of Iran in the siege of 1733 strengthened his standing, and the household's prestige as the defender of Baghdad and the frontier against Iran remained a source of legitimacy.

After Ahmad Pasha's death in 1747 the Ottoman government tried a series of appointees, but in 1749 Sulayman Abu Layla, Ahmad's son-in-law and a mamluk of the household who had been governor of Basra, marched on Baghdad with the Georgian guard, ousted the Ottoman administrator, and was recognized by the Porte as governor, inaugurating the Mamluk period in Iraq. He governed from 1749 to 1762, and the governorship thereafter passed among members of Ahmad's family (his son Omar Pasha, 1762–1776) and the household's mamluks, notably Sulayman the Great (1780–1802), until the Ottoman government ended the arrangement in 1831, an event that belongs to the nineteenth century. The Mamluk pashas thus formed a durable establishment based on a mixture of recruitment, marriage, and descent, not on a single hereditary line. The pashas also governed Basra and Shahrizor and tried to reconcile the demands of Istanbul with those of Iraq's tribal and religious communities, including the Shi'i shrine cities and the Kurdish districts.

Comparison with Egypt is instructive. In both provinces mamluk households became central, but their origins and arrangements differed. The Egyptian beys emerged from regiments and factions within a province that already had a mamluk past, and the system produced competition among several households. In Baghdad, a single governor's household, created by an Ottoman official, monopolized the governorship and transmitted it to its own members with the sultan's consent. Both illustrate the point that mamluk leadership was not identical across regions. The Baghdad pashas relied on local tax revenues, the customs of the Gulf trade, and subsidies and alliances with tribes, and they also faced revolts by their own mamluks and by rival tribal leaders, so that their stability was real but not complete.

Section 47.16

Mosul and the Jalili Family

Mosul lay on the Tigris between Anatolia, Syria, Iran, and Baghdad, and its governors had to manage trade routes, the surrounding agricultural districts, and Kurdish and Arab tribal groups. The Jalili family, local notables who built influence through administrative service, landholding, and local alliances, rose to dominate the governorship of Mosul in the first half of the eighteenth century. Isma'il Pasha al-Jalili became governor in 1726, and Husayn Pasha al-Jalili succeeded him in 1730 and led the successful defense of the city when Nadir Shah besieged it in 1743, a victory that confirmed the family's prestige. The defense mattered politically as well as militarily: Mosul was a frontier city, and a governor who could hold it against an Iranian army showed Istanbul that he could perform the service the empire most needed there, which made it far harder for the government to replace him and gave him a claim on imperial gratitude and on the loyalty of the city's notables. Dina Rizk Khoury's State and Provincial Society in the Ottoman Empire: Mosul, 1540–1834 traces how the Jalilis combined Ottoman appointments with local power, and how the province's administrative arrangements, which retained timars longer than Baghdad and Basra did, shaped the options available to them.

The Jalilis relied on tax farms, on a network of clients among Mosul's notables, on military followers, and on negotiations with rural and tribal communities. They held the governorship, with interruptions, until the 1830s, and Ottoman officials accepted their dominance because the family provided order and revenue and could defend the frontier. Mosul's position between powers gave the Jalilis options, such as managing relations with Iran and Baghdad, but also made them dependent on imperial support in wartime. Khoury's analysis emphasizes that the Jalilis did not oppose the state: they became its local form in Mosul, and their power was built on legal and fiscal instruments the state had created. The Jalilis are an example of a locally rooted dynasty, in contrast to Baghdad's household of mamluks, and the contrast illustrates how different households within the same broad region could take different shapes.

Section 47.17

Basra, Tribal Confederations, and the Politics of the Gulf Frontier

Basra, at the head of the Gulf, was an exposed provincial center, close to Safavid Iran and surrounded by marshlands, rivers, and tribal populations. In the seventeenth century it was governed by the Afrasiyab family, whose members held the governorship for several decades from around 1612 and gradually reduced Istanbul's practical control, while still acknowledging Ottoman sovereignty and negotiating with the Safavids. Rudi Matthee's research on Basra between 1600 and 1700, and recent archival studies, describe a contest among the Ottomans, the Safavids, local rulers, and tribal confederations in which sovereignty was negotiated and contested. The Ottoman government restored direct rule in 1667, a date that more recent scholarship has clarified, ending the family's autonomy.

The tribal confederation of the Muntafiq, whose leading shaykhly house was the Sa'dun family, was already a major power in southern Iraq in the seventeenth century, and other groups such as the Khaza'il and Banu Lam also appear in the sources. The clearest example of its political weight is that the Muntafiq controlled Basra itself during 1694–1697, a reminder that a tribal confederation could hold an Ottoman provincial capital, if only for a few years, and that Istanbul and the governors who followed had to bargain with it afterward. Students should distinguish this from the Afrasiyab era, when the city was held by a local ruling family, and from the mid-eighteenth century, when the Baghdad governors, one of whom, Sulayman Abu Layla, had himself governed Basra, were the dominant force. Basra's role in Gulf trade made it attractive to Persian, European, and Arab merchants, and the Zand occupation of the 1770s shows its exposure to external intervention. Karim Khan Zand's forces began a siege of the city in 1775, Basra surrendered in 1776, and Iranian occupation continued until 1779, when Ottoman control returned after Karim Khan's death. Iraq's provincial arrangements were therefore shaped not only by bargaining between Istanbul and local rulers but also by the military reach of a neighboring state.

Basra's politics were shaped by geography: its access to Gulf shipping and trade, the river systems that tied it to tribes upstream, and the marshes that made central control difficult. Provincial rulers in Basra did not simply defy the empire. They worked within it, sought confirmation, and used their position on a frontier to bargain. For students comparing Iraq's three cities, the pattern is clear: Baghdad was governed by a household of mamluks, Mosul by a local dynasty, and Basra by a changing combination of Ottoman governors, tribal chiefs, and frontier diplomacy.

Division V — The North African Regencies

Section 47.18

Algiers: Janissaries, Deys, and Military Government

The regency of Algiers, established in the sixteenth century, was at first governed by beylerbeys and then by Ottoman-appointed pashas whose authority diminished during the seventeenth century as the Janissary corps (ocak) and the corsair captains (ta'ifat al-ru'asa') grew in power. In 1659 the Janissaries took control of the government, and from 1671 the regency was headed by a dey elected by the military establishment and the council (diwan). The system was refined further in 1710, when the dey Ali Shawsh refused entry to an incoming Ottoman governor and then obtained recognition from the sultan of the combined dey-pasha arrangement, after which the dey governed as the sultan's representative. This produced a largely non-hereditary system of military government in which the head of state was chosen by a military electorate and often removed violently. Stephen Cory's overview of the Ottoman Maghrib, published in 2020 in the Oxford Research Encyclopedia of African History, stresses how far the three regencies diverged in these political forms.

The regency's finances depended on taxation of the countryside, collected by the beys of the three provinces (Constantine, Titteri, and Oran) and by tribal intermediaries; on corsair activity, which brought prizes and tribute from European states; and on trade. The beys led periodic tax-collecting expeditions and bargained with rural tribal leaders, using alliances with some and force against others, and religious brotherhoods and local notables mediated between the regime and the rural population. The recapture of Oran from Spain in 1708 by the Algerian bey and again in 1792 shows the beylical forces' military capabilities and the regency's role in the western Mediterranean.

Algiers maintained a relationship with the Ottoman sultan that was mainly symbolic and military: the sultan was named in the sermon, Ottoman titles were conferred, and the regency sometimes sent naval or military assistance. In contrast to Tunis and Tripoli, Algiers never developed a hereditary dynasty in this period, and the regime's chief weakness was the instability of its leadership, a pattern of violent turnover that its local rulers accepted as part of its political culture.

Section 47.19

Tunis: The Muradid and Husaynid Dynasties

In Tunis, power passed in the seventeenth century from the Ottoman governors to the beys, who commanded the tax-collecting expeditions into the countryside. The Muradid dynasty, founded by Murad Bey (ruled 1612–1631), kept the beylical office within a single family for several generations, was recognized by Istanbul, and combined the offices of bey and pasha in its later phase. Its later decades were disturbed by family quarrels and by Algerian military intervention, and in 1705, after Algerian forces defeated the last Muradid ruler, Husayn ibn Ali, an officer of Cretan origin, took power and founded the Husaynid dynasty.

The Husaynids built a hereditary line that ruled Tunisia into the twentieth century. Husayn I's rule (1705–1735) was followed by a civil war with his nephew Ali Pasha, who took power in 1735 and ruled until 1756, when Algerian forces intervened again and Husayn's son Muhammad was installed. The dynasty then regained stability under later beys, notably Ali II (1759–1782) and Hammuda Pasha (1782–1814). The beys maintained a state based on military forces drawn from the Turkish-speaking elite and from local recruits, tax collection from the countryside by seasonal expeditions (mahalla), trade, the support of urban notables including the scholars of the Zaytuna Mosque, and the Mediterranean connections of Tunisian merchants and corsairs.

The Husaynids maintained a formal relationship with Ottoman sovereignty, receiving the sultan's investiture and acknowledging his name in the Friday sermon. In practice, they were autonomous, and the major threat to Tunis in the eighteenth century came from Algiers, not Istanbul. Their hereditary rule distinguishes Tunis from Algiers and shows that similar pressures could produce different political results, a comparison that Cory's article develops. The Tunisian state also relied on a degree of integration between the capital and the Sahel towns that gave its beys a more settled social base than most of their Algerian counterparts.

Section 47.20

Tripoli: The Qaramanli Dynasty and Regional Authority

In Tripoli, Ahmad Qaramanli, a cavalry officer whose family's precise ancestry is uncertain (it has been linked to Ottoman soldiers and to the local-born kuloglu population), seized power in 1711 by eliminating the Ottoman governor's party in the city. Ottoman recognition came afterward and in stages, as the sultan conferred the office of pasha upon him, so that the seizure of power and the later confirmation of the dynasty should be distinguished. He established the Qaramanli dynasty, which governed Tripolitania until 1835. Ahmad's rule, which lasted until 1745, was followed by that of his successors, among them Ali I (1754–1793), under whom the dynasty consolidated.

The Qaramanlis governed a province where the coastal cities and the Janissary garrison were separated from large inland territories inhabited by tribal groups and from the oasis region of Fezzan to the south. Authority over the interior was negotiated, exercised through tribute, tax-collecting expeditions, alliances with tribal leaders, and the support of local rulers. Fezzan remained a tributary region whose own ruling house had local autonomy, and its role in the trans-Saharan trade, including the movement of slaves, made the relationship worth maintaining. The Qaramanlis also faced the difficulty of governing distant inland territories with limited forces and had to accept that their authority diminished with distance from the coast. The coast's Mediterranean connections, including corsair activity and diplomacy with European states, supplied revenue.

Tripoli's evolution resembles that of Tunis in that it produced a hereditary dynasty, and differs in that the Qaramanlis had a weaker agricultural base and relied more on external revenue and on the trans-Saharan trade. It differs from Algiers in the same way as Tunis does. Morocco, it should be noted, was outside Ottoman rule, with its own dynasty, and is not treated as a regency. The three regencies together show that the same Ottoman framework could host military oligarchy and dynastic rule. They can be compared on three axes. In succession, Algiers chose its dey by military election and removed him by violence, while Tunis and Tripoli passed power within ruling families, though not without civil war, as the Tunisian conflict of 1735–1756 shows. In fiscal resources, Algiers relied on the tax-collecting provincial beys and on corsair prizes and tribute from European states, Tunis on the seasonal tax expeditions into a more settled countryside and on its merchants, and Tripoli on a thinner agricultural base supplemented by the coast's maritime income and the trans-Saharan trade through Fezzan. In relations with tribal authorities, all three governed the interior through alliances, tribute, and expeditions rather than through uniform administration, but the distances in Tripolitania were the greatest, and the authority of its rulers there was the most negotiated. In all three, the sultan's name and investiture supplied legitimacy that the regencies' rulers had no reason to give up.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Seventeenth- and Eighteenth-Century Provincial Politics

What changed?

  • Short-term tax farms gave way to lifetime tenure in many places after 1695
  • Households and regional dynasties took over offices once filled by outsiders

  • Ottoman courts, the pilgrimage, and imperial wartime mobilization

Division VI — Consequences and Historical Interpretation

Section 47.21

Provincial Power and the Lives of Ordinary People

For farmers, townspeople, merchants, soldiers, and religious communities, the rise of powerful households and regional rulers meant different things in different places. Peasants might face competing demands from tax farmers, soldiers, tribal chiefs, and governors, and when rival households fought one another the villages that supplied revenue and provisions bore the cost. Al-Jabarti's chronicle of Egypt in the late eighteenth century records price rises, levies, and the disruption of normal trade and farming during mamluk rivalries. The demands of regimes such as al-Jazzar's in Acre, or the tax-collecting expeditions of the beys of Tunis and Algiers, were heavy. In Damascus the annual tax collection tour for the pilgrimage caravan was a direct burden on villages, and the violence between local and imperial Janissaries in 1757 affected the city's residents as well as its elites.

Provincial rulers could also provide security, justice, and economic opportunity. Zahir al-Umar's Galilee saw expanded cotton and grain production for export, and French merchants dealing with the governors of Sidon and Acre found that regimes that could guarantee payment and protection were commercially attractive. The Shihab emirs and Baghdad's pashas offered a stable framework for trade and landholding, and the Damascene governors' patronage produced buildings and endowments that served the city. Merchants and notables who allied with a household could gain protection and access to credit. Soldiers found employment, though they were also subject to violence in factional conflicts, and religious scholars gained patronage through endowments and appointments. Court registers show that ordinary people continued to use Ottoman courts to claim rights and settle disputes, an indication that imperial legal institutions remained meaningful at local level.

The consequences could not be summarized as beneficial or harmful. Some rulers brought order and then extorted heavily; others lost control, and their regions suffered from conflict. The experience also varied by group within a single region: a merchant allied to a household and a villager with no protector experienced the same regime very differently, and the Bedouin who raided the Syrian hajj in 1757 were acting within a bargaining system, not simply defying order. The Multiple Perspectives and Everyday Life features in this chapter develop these differences, using documented cases.

Everyday Life and Social History

Factionalism, Taxation, and Security in Ordinary Life

Taxpayers. For many villagers, provincial politics was experienced as taxation. In Syria, the governor's annual tour (dawra) to collect funds for the pilgrimage caravan, such as that of April 1757, fell directly on the districts he visited. In Egypt, al-Jabarti's account of the high prices and shortages of 1783–1784 shows how the demands of the ruling households could reach the city's markets and consumers.

Soldiers and townspeople. In Damascus in 1757 the dispute between the local Janissaries (yerliyya) and the imperial garrison (kapıkulu) occurred in the city's quarters, among families who were also artisans and shopkeepers. In Cairo the members of the regiments were involved in crafts and tax farming, so that a soldier might also be a shopkeeper, and an urban population lived with the possibility that factional fighting would close markets.

Merchants. French merchants at Sidon and Acre were creditors and trading partners of the governors; the debts left by Ibrahim Pasha al-Azm at Sidon in 1741, for example, were settled by his brother As'ad. Local merchants, such as the coffee merchants whose lives al-Jabarti recorded, depended on households for protection and credit.

Tribal leaders and notables. Shaykh Humam of the Hawwara in Upper Egypt, whose life al-Jabarti recounts, was a powerful rural intermediary who was first a partner and then a casualty of the Cairo beys. Local ulama and notables used the courts and endowments to protect property. Women appear in court registers as property owners and litigants, and women in elite households could influence politics through marriage alliances, though sources are thin.

Most surviving narratives were written by urban, educated, usually male authors, so the experiences of villagers, women, and pastoral groups must be reconstructed from court registers, petitions, travelers' accounts, and archaeological and architectural evidence.

Section 47.22

An Empire Transformed: Provincial Autonomy without Imperial Collapse

Egypt, Damascus, Mount Lebanon, Palestine, Baghdad, Mosul, Basra, Algiers, Tunis, and Tripoli shared several features. In each, powerful local actors controlled military forces and revenues to a degree that earlier Ottoman governance had not allowed. In each, those actors relied on households, patronage, tax farming, and negotiated relationships with notables and tribal groups. In each, they continued to acknowledge the sultan, seek recognition, and perform imperial functions. But their arrangements differed: Egypt and Baghdad were governed by mamluk households of different origin, Damascus by a family of governors, Aleppo by a plural urban elite, Mosul by a local dynasty, Mount Lebanon by emirs holding tax farms, Palestine by two successive regional rulers, and the regencies by a military oligarchy in Algiers and hereditary dynasties in Tunis and Tripoli. The differences reflect varied geography, frontiers, resources, histories, and relations with Istanbul. Frontier provinces such as Basra and Mosul faced Iran; port-centered ones such as Acre and Tunis depended on Mediterranean commerce; and Egypt's wealth made it a prize that both its own beys and the sultan wanted to control.

The central argument is that increased provincial autonomy did not mean that the Ottoman Empire ceased to be a meaningful political, fiscal, and institutional system. Istanbul retained the power to appoint, confirm, and remove; it received revenue through tax-farming contracts and tribute; it mobilized provincial forces in wartime; and its legitimacy was invoked by the very provincial leaders who held power. Its limits were real. It could not always impose its candidates, and when it tried, as in Egypt in 1786 or Acre in 1775, results depended on circumstances: it eliminated Zahir al-Umar and executed As'ad Pasha but could not hold Egypt in 1786. The pattern of selective, often successful intervention shows a state that governed through leverage rather than direct administration. The empire survived through bargaining and adaptation, and the same households that restricted imperial authority also helped sustain imperial structures.

For students, the lesson is also methodological. The sources, from Ottoman appointment orders to local chronicles and consular reports, each describe the same developments from a different angle, and the interpretation of provincial autonomy changes as one shifts among them. The Historical Debate that follows examines the main interpretive question. Chapter 48 turns to Arabia, where the Hijaz, Yemen, Oman, the Gulf, and the Saudi-Wahhabi movement created distinctive political developments. Chapters 49 and 50 then take up the nineteenth century, when the empire and Muhammad Ali would reshape the provincial order. The provincial households treated in this chapter formed the political context in which those later changes occurred.

Historical Debate

Ottoman Decline or Imperial Transformation?

The question: Does the rise of powerful provincial households show that the Ottoman Empire was in decline, or that it was transforming?

Interpretation A — Decline: Influential mid-twentieth-century works, such as Gibb and Bowen's Islamic Society and the West and Bernard Lewis's 1958 article on decline, saw the empire after its sixteenth-century peak as weakened by corruption, military indiscipline, and loss of central control, with provincial notables as symptoms and beneficiaries.
Interpretation B — Transformation and negotiation: More recent scholarship (including Hathaway, Rafeq, Khoury, Salzmann, Yaycioglu, and others) argues that the empire adapted to changing fiscal and military conditions, that provincial elites were integrated into imperial institutions through tax farming and appointments, and that autonomy was negotiated rather than a sign of collapse. Albert Hourani's "politics of notables" concept, developed for the Arab provinces, was an influential early step in this reassessment.
A third strand — limits and breakdown: Not all work that departs from the older decline narrative is optimistic. Shimon Shamir's 1963 study of As'ad Pasha al-Azm and Ottoman rule in Damascus, for example, has been read as presenting a more pessimistic picture of provincial governance, with stress on the strain of the governor's dependence on local forces and the empire's limited ability to maintain order. Evidence of provincial breakdown, such as the loss of the 1757 caravan, is therefore not obsolete simply because the decline paradigm has been criticized.

Evidence: Both views draw on the same records: appointment orders, tax-farm registers, chronicles, and court registers. Decline arguments tend to emphasize military defeats and territorial loss; transformation arguments emphasize the persistence of imperial institutions, revenue flows, and recognition.

Why it matters: If one assumes decline, provincial leaders appear as symptoms of weakness or as precursors of nationalism. If one assumes transformation, they become participants in imperial politics. Scholars also note that the two views are not complete opposites, since fiscal and military pressures were real.

Chapter Summary

Between 1600 and 1800, provincial leaders across the Ottoman Arab provinces gained substantial control over soldiers, revenue, and offices. The causes included fiscal strain, military change, costly wars, and the frequent rotation of governors, which made local actors indispensable to imperial government.

The chapter's key concept is the household: a network of kin, clients, slaves, soldiers, and allies bound to a patron. Households could outlast individual leaders and recruit by merit rather than descent, so they should not be equated with dynasties. Their finances rested on tax farming, especially after the lifetime malikâne of 1695, which joined local wealth to the imperial fiscal system without conferring land ownership.

Egypt illustrates the military household at work. After 1517 the Janissary regiments and the beys became embedded in Egyptian society and competed through factions. The Qazdağlı household, a product of Ottoman institutions, dominated mid-century Cairo. Its heir Ali Bey challenged Istanbul in 1768–1773 but fell when his mamluks defected, and Ibrahim Bey and Murad Bey ruled until 1798 with a mixture of autonomy and acknowledged sovereignty.

In Syria, Palestine, and Lebanon, regimes differed: the al-Azm family held Damascus and the pilgrimage command; Aleppo's politics rested on competing urban groups; the Shihabs governed Mount Lebanon through tax farms and alliances; Zahir al-Umar built a Galilean regime that Istanbul eventually destroyed; and al-Jazzar combined imperial office with a mamluk household at Acre.

In Iraq, Baghdad's Mamluk pashas formed a durable household government; the Jalilis of Mosul were a locally rooted dynasty; and Basra's politics were shaped by tribal confederations and the Gulf frontier. The Egypt–Baghdad comparison shows that mamluk leadership differed by region.

In North Africa, Algiers developed an elected military regime of deys, while Tunis and Tripoli developed hereditary dynasties, the Husaynids and Qaramanlis. All acknowledged the sultan but ruled in practice with autonomy, and Morocco remained outside Ottoman rule.

For ordinary people, provincial power meant competing tax claims, military levies, and sometimes violence, but also protection, trade opportunity, and access to legal institutions. Experience depended on place, status, and connections.

The chapter's argument is that these developments represent imperial transformation rather than collapse. Istanbul retained appointment, fiscal, and military powers and its legitimacy was invoked by provincial rulers. Its limits were also real. Chapter 48 turns to Arabia, and Chapters 49 and 50 to the nineteenth century.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it changes how the Ottoman Arab world is seen. The provincial households, governors, and dynasties of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries were not signs of an empire dissolving. They were the way the empire operated across a vast and diverse territory, through bargains between center and provinces.

It also reveals processes that shaped later developments: tax farming and household networks structured local elites, and the military regimes of Egypt, Syria, Iraq, and North Africa formed the political settings that later rulers would transform or confront. Those developments appear in Chapter 48 and in Chapters 49 and 50.

Finally, the chapter shows regional variety within a common imperial frame. Egypt, the Levant, Iraq, and North Africa developed different arrangements for similar pressures. Understanding this variety guards against treating the Arab provinces as uniform and against assuming that later nation-states were foreshadowed in earlier regimes.
