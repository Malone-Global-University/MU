Part V — The Abbasid World
25

The Abbasid Revolution and the Founding of Baghdad

A History of the Arab World: From Ancient Crossroads to the Modern Arab World

Chapter Opening

Abu al-Abbas ibn Muhammad was proclaimed caliph in the mosque of Kufa on 28 November 749, taking the honorific title al-Saffah — months before the Umayyad caliph Marwan II met final defeat at the Greater Zab in January 750, and before Marwan's death in Egypt that August. Chapter 24 carried the revolutionary campaign through the Zab and Marwan's death, leaving a coalition drawn from Khurasan, Kufa, and the wider Hashimite movement suddenly in possession of an empire. But a proclamation in a mosque, months before the old regime had even been fully destroyed, is not yet a government. Within a single generation the family that had ridden a revolutionary coalition to power had to decide what kind of state it would actually build — who would command its armies, who would staff its offices, where its capital would stand, and what language of legitimacy would justify a dynasty that had won its throne, in part, by outbidding its own rivals with promises of restored prophetic justice.

This chapter opens Part V of the textbook, which traces the Abbasid period as a transformation from an early Arab conquest empire into a broader Islamic civilization — one in which Arabic remained the language of government, scholarship, and high culture, but in which political and intellectual life grew steadily more cosmopolitan. Chapter 25's task is to explain the new dynasty: its eastern support base, the founding of Baghdad, the beginnings of a distinctive court culture, the growth of a functioning bureaucracy, and the changing concepts of imperial rule that accompanied all of these developments. The chapter's chronological frame runs from the proclamation of al-Saffah in November 749 to the death of al-Mansur in 775, a quarter-century in which a revolutionary movement became an imperial monarchy.

It is worth pausing on how easily this story could be told the wrong way. One temptation is to treat 750 as though the real work were already finished — as though defeating the Umayyads settled the question of who would rule and how. It did not. A second temptation is to leap forward into the cultural and intellectual flowering conventionally associated with the "Abbasid Golden Age," folding the administrative and political history of the 750s and 760s into a hurried preamble to Harun al-Rashid's court, the translation movement, or the great compilations of Islamic law. This chapter resists both temptations. Its subject is the hard, often violent, frequently improvisational work of turning a victorious coalition into a durable state — work accomplished chiefly under two caliphs, al-Saffah and, at far greater length, al-Mansur.

The chapter's central argument can be stated directly. The Abbasid Revolution did not simply replace one Arab dynasty with another while leaving the machinery of empire otherwise unchanged. Between 750 and al-Mansur's death in 775, the Abbasid family converted a revolutionary movement rooted heavily in the eastern Iranian province of Khurasan into a centralized imperial monarchy based in Iraq. In doing so they preserved a great deal from the Umayyad state — Arabic administration, provincial government, the underlying fiscal structures examined further in Chapter 26 — while substantially altering the regime's military constituency, its administrative personnel, its court culture, its geography of power, and its claims to legitimate authority. Baghdad, founded by al-Mansur in 762, embodied this transformation in physical form: a new Iraqi capital, deliberately positioned on the empire's east-west axis, built around the person of the caliph and the apparatus of his government rather than inherited from any earlier regime.

Readers should also understand this chapter as, in significant part, a study of political danger. The same coalition that carried the Abbasids to power — Khurasanian soldiers, revolutionary commanders, provincial governors distantly related to the ruling family, and supporters who had rallied to the vague and expansive slogan of restoring justice to "the family of the Prophet" — immediately became a set of potential threats to the new dynasty's survival. Much of this chapter's narrative concerns how al-Saffah and, more consequentially, al-Mansur managed, rewarded, and where necessary eliminated the very people who had made their rule possible. The killing of the revolutionary general Abu Muslim in 755 stands as this chapter's central turning point precisely because it makes that dynamic unmistakable: revolutionary legitimacy and independent military popularity, the new regime concluded, could not be allowed to coexist with the authority of the caliph.

Finally, this chapter introduces a methodological caution that will recur throughout Part V. Much of what later Arabic historical writing reports about the early Abbasid decades — court anecdotes, precise dialogue attributed to caliphs and commanders, detailed narratives of conspiracy and betrayal — was compiled by historians writing generations afterward, often within a tradition invested in explaining and justifying the dynasty's rise. This material is frequently our best and only detailed source for events like the fall of Abu Muslim, and it cannot simply be discarded; but it should be read as a literary and political tradition as well as a historical record, a caution this chapter will apply consistently.

By the chapter's end, students should understand how a coalition became a dynasty, why Baghdad was built where and how it was, what a developing early Abbasid bureaucracy was beginning to look like by 775, and why the ideological language of Abbasid rule differed in important respects from that of the Umayyads before them — a state that Chapters 26 through 30 will go on to examine in economic, intellectual, religious, and social depth.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain the distinction between the dynastic overthrow of 750 and the longer institutional consolidation that followed it.
  2. Describe the reign of Abu al-Abbas al-Saffah and its limited but necessary role in establishing the new dynasty.
  3. Analyze the significance of the Khurasaniyya as the new regime's central military constituency.
  4. Explain why victorious revolutionary commanders posed an inherent political danger to the new dynasty.
  5. Trace the succession crisis of 754 and Abu Muslim's role in defeating Abd Allah ibn Ali's challenge to al-Mansur.
  6. Evaluate the causes and consequences of the killing of Abu Muslim in 755.
  7. Describe the Alid revolts of 762–763 and their significance for Abbasid claims to legitimacy.
  8. Explain why al-Mansur chose to found a new capital rather than govern from Damascus, Kufa, or another existing city.
  9. Describe the founding, planning, and symbolism of Baghdad as Madinat al-Salam.
  10. Trace the early development of Abbasid administration, including the diwans, the barid, and the beginnings of the vizierate, without projecting the mature Barmakid administration backward onto this period.
  11. Analyze the changing ceremonial and ideological language of Abbasid caliphal rule.
  12. Assess the scholarly debate over how far the early Abbasid state should be described as "Persianized," and articulate a historically careful alternative to that framing.

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  2. Why did the Abbasids' own revolutionary supporters become a source of danger to the new regime?
  3. What does the killing of Abu Muslim reveal about the relationship between revolutionary legitimacy and dynastic authority?
  4. Why did the Abbasids' rise to power on a broadly Hashimite, pro-"Family of the Prophet" platform create lasting difficulties with Alid claimants?

Historical Context

Chapter 24 traced the coalition of grievances, propaganda, and military organization that produced the Abbasid Revolution: the erosion of Umayyad authority, the missionary organization known as the dawa operating from Khurasan, the ambiguous appeal to the rights of "the Family of the Prophet" that allowed the movement to attract both Abbasid and Alid sympathizers, the generalship of Abu Muslim in Khurasan, and the final Umayyad defeat at the Battle of the Zab in 750, followed by the flight and death of the last Umayyad caliph, Marwan II. This chapter does not renarrate that campaign. It begins where Chapter 24 left off: with the Abbasid family in possession of the caliphate and facing the considerably more difficult task of governing.

Students should carry forward several points established in Chapter 24 without relearning them here. First, the revolutionary coalition was never a purely Abbasid movement; it drew on a broader Hashimite appeal that many participants understood, at least at the outset, as a vehicle for restoring rule to the Prophet's family in some more general sense, leaving room for later disappointment among those who had expected an Alid rather than an Abbasid outcome. Second, the coalition's military core lay overwhelmingly in Khurasan, the northeastern Iranian province where the dawa had organized for decades before the open revolt of 747. Third, Abu Muslim, the movement's chief general in the east, had accumulated enormous personal authority and a large, personally loyal following well before Abu al-Abbas was proclaimed caliph in Kufa.

This chapter's task is to explain what the Abbasid family did with the victory Chapter 24 described — how a coalition assembled to overthrow a dynasty was reorganized, narrowed, and in places forcibly reshaped into the governing apparatus of a new one.

Section 25.1

From Revolution to Dynasty, 750

When Abu al-Abbas received the oath of allegiance in the Great Mosque of Kufa in the autumn of 749, he inherited an empire but not yet a government of his own making. The provincial administration, the tax registers, the garrison cities, and the Arabic-language bureaucracy Chapter 22 and Chapter 23 described as Umayyad achievements continued to function largely as they had before, now nominally under new management. What the new caliph did not yet possess was a settled answer to several urgent questions: which of his revolutionary supporters would be rewarded with power, which would be sidelined, where the dynasty's capital would eventually stand, and what story it would tell about why God and history had favored the family of al-Abbas rather than any other claimant to the Prophet's legacy.

This chapter's task is to trace how those questions were answered — not all at once, and not without violence, but steadily, across the twenty-five years separating al-Saffah's proclamation from al-Mansur's death in 775. The central problem the new dynasty faced can be stated simply: how does a coalition become a government? A coalition wins battles through the loyalty of commanders who expect to be rewarded, through the mobilization of provincial grievances that expect to be redressed, and through slogans broad enough to attract support from groups whose ultimate goals differ considerably. A government, by contrast, requires a narrower, more durable structure: a recognized line of succession, a loyal and controllable military establishment, a functioning administration, and a persuasive account of why this particular family, rather than any other, should hold supreme authority. Converting the first into the second was neither automatic nor peaceful, and it occupies the whole of this chapter.

Section 25.2

What Had Actually Changed in 750?

Before tracing the new dynasty's consolidation, it is worth pausing to ask precisely what the Abbasid Revolution had, and had not, changed. Students should resist the assumption that a dynastic overthrow of this scale automatically produced an equally sweeping institutional transformation. In several important respects, continuity outweighed change in the years immediately following 750. Arabic remained the language of administration, as it had become under Abd al-Malik and his successors, examined in Chapter 23. The caliphate continued to govern through provinces, governors, and the diwans, the administrative registers and bureaus inherited from Umayyad, and ultimately from earlier Byzantine and Sasanian, practice. Land taxation continued along broadly familiar lines, a subject Chapter 26 will examine in detail. Many local elites — landowners, urban notables, and provincial administrators who had served Umayyad governors — survived the transition and continued to perform essentially the same functions for their new masters, provided they had not been closely identified with the losing side.

What did change, and change significantly, were three things this chapter will trace in turn. First, the geography of political privilege shifted decisively. Syria, the heartland of Umayyad power and the base from which Damascus had governed the empire, lost its privileged position almost overnight; Syrian troops who had formed the backbone of Umayyad military power were viewed with suspicion by the new regime, and Syria itself would not regain anything resembling its former political centrality. Second, a new military and political constituency — the Khurasaniyya, examined in Section 25.4 — moved from the empire's eastern periphery to the very center of power, bringing with it commanders, soldiers, and administrative personnel whose relationship to the ruling family differed fundamentally from that of the old Syrian establishment. Third, the ideological basis of caliphal authority began to shift, a development this chapter traces to its fullest expression in Section 25.16. The Abbasids could not simply claim the same kind of legitimacy the Umayyads had claimed; they had won power partly by attacking Umayyad rule as illegitimate, and they needed a persuasive account of their own right to succeed where the Umayyads, in their telling, had failed.

Section 25.3

Abu al-Abbas al-Saffah and the First Abbasid Regime

Abu al-Abbas, who took the caliphal title al-Saffah, reigned only briefly, from his proclamation in 749/750 until his death in 754. His short reign should not be read as the period in which the mature Abbasid state took shape; that achievement belongs chiefly to his successor, al-Mansur, examined throughout the remainder of this chapter. Al-Saffah's reign was instead a period of establishment and consolidation of the most basic and urgent kind: securing Abbasid authority across Iraq, the new regime's effective center of gravity, and eliminating or neutralizing the most immediate sources of Umayyad resistance.

Al-Saffah governed heavily through revolutionary commanders and members of his own extended family, appointing relatives and trusted associates to key provincial governorships rather than relying, as the Umayyads had, on an established administrative aristocracy loyal to the previous regime. This approach had an obvious short-term logic: family members and revolutionary veterans had proven loyalty to the new dynasty in a period when that loyalty could not be taken for granted. It also carried an obvious long-term risk, one that would become fully apparent only after al-Saffah's death: a regime staffed heavily by ambitious relatives and independently powerful commanders was a regime in which succession disputes and challenges to central authority were almost inevitable, a dynamic examined directly in Section 25.6.

Al-Saffah's reign also saw the new dynasty begin, almost immediately, to move away from Kufa as a long-term seat of government. Kufa had served admirably as the site of proclamation, but its factional politics and its deep historical association with Alid sympathy, examined further in Section 25.10, made it an uncomfortable long-term base for a caliph trying to govern rather than merely announce a revolution. Al-Saffah accordingly established his court at other Iraqi sites, including Anbar on the Euphrates, and Abbasid sources associate the family with an early foundation at al-Hashimiyya, near Anbar, built specifically as a new Abbasid seat distinct from any inherited Umayyad or garrison-city settlement. These early experiments in relocation should be understood as the first, tentative steps toward the fuller solution al-Mansur would reach at Baghdad in 762 — not as a settled arrangement in their own right, but as evidence that the search for a capital genuinely suited to Abbasid rule began under al-Saffah rather than springing fully formed from al-Mansur alone.

Al-Saffah's reign also saw the continued, though incomplete, suppression of scattered Umayyad resistance and the beginnings of the new dynasty's efforts to define itself publicly as a restoration of righteous rule rather than merely another dynastic seizure of power — an ideological project that Section 25.16 will examine in its fuller, more developed form under al-Mansur. By the time of al-Saffah's death in 754, the Abbasid family held the caliphate securely enough that succession passed, not without challenge, to his brother rather than collapsing into open civil war — itself a modest but real achievement given how recently the dynasty had been founded.

Section 25.4

The Khurasaniyya: Victors Who Had to Be Governed

No single development mattered more for the shape of the early Abbasid state than the rise of the Khurasaniyya, the body of Khurasan-based soldiers, both Arab settlers long resident in the province and non-Arab converts and allies, who had formed the revolutionary movement's military core under Abu Muslim's leadership and who now occupied the dominant military position within the new regime. Understanding this shift in concrete terms matters more than treating it as an abstract label. The Khurasaniyya were not simply "Persian troops" replacing "Arab troops," a simplification this chapter's Historical Debate feature examines and rejects; they were a genuinely mixed force, many of whose members were Arabic-speaking descendants of earlier conquest-era settlers in Khurasan, fighting alongside non-Arab converts and local allies recruited through the dawa's decades of organizing in the province.

What made the Khurasaniyya politically significant was not their ethnic composition alone but their structural position: they owed their prominence directly to the revolution rather than to inherited status within the old Umayyad military establishment, and their loyalty ran, in the first instance, through the chain of revolutionary command that led back to Abu Muslim rather than directly to the caliph in Iraq. This created an immediate and enduring problem for the new dynasty. The soldiers who had won the Abbasids their throne were not, by that fact alone, soldiers whose ultimate loyalty the Abbasid family could take for granted; their loyalty had to be actively secured, and where necessary redirected, away from Abu Muslim and toward the caliphate itself. The importance the new regime attached to its Khurasanian base is visible in the very design of Baghdad, examined in Section 25.12, whose gates included one named specifically for the road leading toward Khurasan — a permanent architectural acknowledgment of where Abbasid power had originated.

Over the following decades, Khurasanian commanders and their descendants would occupy central positions in the Abbasid military and administrative establishment, forming something like a new imperial aristocracy whose prestige rested on revolutionary service rather than on tribal genealogy or association with the old Umayyad order. This transformation of the empire's military and social elite is one of the clearest ways in which the Abbasid Revolution changed the substance, and not merely the personnel, of imperial rule.

Section 25.5

Revolutionary Commanders and the Problem of Competing Power

The same revolutionary victory that brought the Abbasid family to power also created, almost automatically, a set of rival power centers the family had to manage. Commanders who had led troops to victory, organizers who had built the dawa's clandestine networks across Khurasan for decades before 747, and relatives who had commanded armies in the family's name all emerged from the revolution with independent reputations, personal followings, and, in some cases, resources and territorial bases that rivaled those of the caliph himself. This is a recurring pattern in the history of successful revolutionary movements, and the early Abbasid state offers one of the clearest examples available to students of this textbook.

Abu Muslim stood at the extreme end of this problem. By 750 he governed Khurasan in practice with an authority that in some respects exceeded that of any provincial governor appointed by an established, secure dynasty; his rapidly accumulated prestige among the Khurasaniyya, built through intensive organizing and campaigning from the mid-740s onward, when Ibrahim al-Imam dispatched him to take charge of the Khurasanian dawa, gave him a base of loyalty that ran to him personally rather than to the Abbasid family as an abstract institution. For a new and still-insecure dynasty, this arrangement was tolerable only so long as Abu Muslim's power served, rather than threatened, the caliph's own authority. The chapters that follow in this section — the succession crisis of 754 and the killing of Abu Muslim in 755 — should be understood against this structural background: not as a story of purely personal rivalry or jealousy, though later tradition frequently frames it in exactly those terms, but as the working-out of a genuine and serious problem of political power that any dynasty emerging from a revolutionary coalition was likely to face.

Section 25.6

The Succession of al-Mansur, 754

Al-Saffah's death in 754 immediately exposed how fragile the new dynasty remained. Succession passed to his brother, who took the caliphal title al-Mansur, but the transition was contested almost at once by Abd Allah ibn Ali, an uncle of the two brothers who had himself commanded Abbasid forces during the revolutionary period and who possessed both the military standing and the family connections to mount a serious challenge for the caliphate. Abd Allah ibn Ali's revolt should be read as direct confirmation of the danger Section 25.5 described in general terms: a family whose own members had commanded revolutionary armies could not assume that those same relatives would accept a settled line of succession without contest.

The new caliph, only recently installed and without an independent military base of his own comparable to Abd Allah's, turned to the one figure capable of defeating the challenge decisively: Abu Muslim. Abu Muslim's Khurasanian forces defeated Abd Allah ibn Ali's rebellion on al-Mansur's behalf, an outcome that secured al-Mansur's hold on the caliphate but that simultaneously demonstrated, in the starkest possible terms, just how dependent the new caliph remained on Abu Muslim's military power and personal authority. Al-Mansur owed his throne, quite literally, to the one man in the empire whose independent power most threatened it. This paradox — indispensable in 754, unacceptable within a year — sets up directly the events examined in the following section.

Section 25.7

The Killing of Abu Muslim, 755

Having just defeated al-Mansur's rival on the caliph's behalf, Abu Muslim remained, in the months that followed, extraordinarily powerful in Khurasan and enjoyed a level of prestige among the Khurasaniyya that no other figure in the empire, including the caliph himself, could match. Later Arabic historical tradition preserves an extended and often dramatic account of the growing tension between the two men: al-Mansur's mounting suspicion of Abu Muslim's ambitions, exchanges of increasingly guarded correspondence, and finally a summons to the caliphal court under circumstances that historians understand as a deliberate trap. In February 755, on al-Mansur's orders, Abu Muslim was killed at court.

Students should treat the vivid narrative detail surrounding this episode — precise dialogue, described gestures, specific accusations exchanged in the moments before the killing — with the same evidentiary caution this textbook applies throughout to later-compiled historical narrative, discussed already in this chapter's opening. What can be stated with confidence is the outcome and its structural logic. Al-Mansur judged, correctly from the standpoint of dynastic security, that a revolutionary hero commanding the personal loyalty of the empire's most powerful military constituency could not be permitted to remain an independent actor indefinitely, however loyal his service had been to that point. The killing of Abu Muslim was, in this sense, less a personal betrayal than a structural resolution: the caliph was asserting, through the most forceful means available, that revolutionary legitimacy and independent military popularity could not coexist alongside the authority of the Abbasid caliphate. This chapter's Cause and Effect feature examines the episode's causes and consequences in fuller detail.

Section 25.8

The Cost of Eliminating the Revolutionary Hero

Abu Muslim's death did not simply remove a political problem; it generated a new one. Abu Muslim had been, for many among the Khurasaniyya and for broader populations touched by the revolutionary movement, a figure of genuine popular loyalty and, in some circles, even quasi-religious veneration, reflecting the millenarian and messianic currents that had run through parts of the Hashimite dawa's original appeal. His killing accordingly provoked unrest rather than simply consolidating al-Mansur's authority. The revolt of Sunbadh, drawing on former adherents of Abu Muslim and seizing the towns of Ray and Qumis in the region northeast of Iraq in 755–756, illustrates this cost directly, even though the movement's geography extended beyond Khurasan proper into the wider northeastern Iranian world.

This textbook treats Sunbadh's revolt briefly and does not attempt a full religious history of the movements associated with Abu Muslim's memory in Khurasan and Transoxiana in the years that followed; such movements belong more properly to the intellectual and religious history examined in later chapters of Part V. The point relevant to this chapter is narrower but important: consolidating Abbasid authority by eliminating a popular revolutionary figure carried real costs, generating new pockets of resistance even among populations connected to the regime's own founding constituency. Al-Mansur's caliphate would spend real military and political resources suppressing these aftershocks in the years immediately following 755, a reminder that dynastic consolidation in this period was rarely a single decisive act but rather an ongoing, often costly process.

Section 25.9

The Abbasids and the Alids: Who Was the Prophet's Family?

Chapter 24 established that the Hashimite revolutionary movement's appeal was deliberately broad and, in important respects, deliberately ambiguous: propaganda calling for the restoration of rule to "the Family of the Prophet" could be, and was, understood by different supporters as pointing toward different specific claimants, including descendants of Ali and Fatima — the Alids — rather than the Abbasid line descended from the Prophet's uncle al-Abbas. This chapter now traces the direct consequence of that ambiguity. Once an Abbasid, rather than an Alid, had actually taken the caliphate in 750, supporters who had expected or hoped for Alid leadership could reasonably regard the revolution as having been diverted from its original promise, whatever the precise expectations of any individual participant had been.

This tension erupted into open revolt in 762, when Muhammad ibn Abd Allah, known to history as al-Nafs al-Zakiyya ("the Pure Soul"), an Alid claimant with a serious body of support, rose in revolt at Medina, asserting a claim to leadership grounded in descent from the Prophet's family that implicitly challenged the Abbasids' own version of that same claim. His revolt was suppressed, but the challenge did not end there: his brother Ibrahim ibn Abd Allah raised a parallel revolt in Iraq in 762–763, carrying the Alid challenge directly into the Abbasid dynasty's own heartland at the very moment al-Mansur was engaged in founding his new capital, examined beginning in the next section. Both revolts were ultimately defeated, but their significance extends well beyond their military outcome. They forced al-Mansur and his supporters to articulate, more precisely than the broad revolutionary slogans of the 740s had required, a specifically Abbasid rather than a generally Hashimite justification for caliphal authority — an ideological project this chapter returns to in Section 25.16, and one whose religious and doctrinal dimensions Chapter 28 will examine at greater length in connection with the broader development of Sunni and Shia traditions.

Section 25.10

Why Build a New Capital?

By the early 760s, al-Mansur faced a set of practical and political problems that pointed toward the construction of an entirely new capital. Al-Saffah's earlier experiments with Anbar and al-Hashimiyya, examined in Section 25.3, had already signaled that Kufa was politically unworkable as a long-term seat, but neither of those sites had yet given the dynasty a capital built fully to its own specifications. Each of the empire's other major existing centers carried further liabilities. Damascus, the former Umayyad capital, was too closely associated with the defeated dynasty and with Syrian political and military networks the new regime distrusted. Kufa itself carried the opposite problem: it possessed strong and long-standing Alid associations, having served historically as a center of support for Ali and his descendants, an association that recent tension over Hashimite legitimacy, examined in Section 25.9, made freshly uncomfortable. Governing indefinitely from a city so closely tied to the Abbasids' most dangerous rivals for legitimacy carried obvious risks, quite apart from any specific revolt.

Beyond these specific liabilities, al-Mansur had a broader strategic interest in a capital more directly under his own control — a city built from the ground around the caliph's palace and administration rather than inherited, with its existing populations, loyalties, and local elites, from an earlier regime or adapted, as al-Hashimiyya had been, from a still-provisional Abbasid encampment. A new capital could be positioned deliberately at the empire's effective center of gravity, well placed to communicate with both Syria and the Mediterranean world to the west and with Khurasan and the Iranian east, whose soldiers and administrators now formed the regime's core constituency. Iraq itself, the historic heartland of Mesopotamian civilization examined in Chapter 7 and the site of the earliest Arab garrison cities discussed in Chapter 20, offered fertile agricultural land, established trade networks, and easy river transport along the Tigris — advantages a new foundation could exploit without inheriting any single existing city's particular political baggage. The foundation of Baghdad thus reflected longer-standing concerns about Kufa's associations, the dynasty's recent succession crises, and the desire for a capital under direct caliphal control, concerns already at work well before 762.

Section 25.11

Baghdad, 762: Madinat al-Salam

Al-Mansur founded his new capital in 145 AH, corresponding to 762 CE, on the western bank of the Tigris in central Iraq, not far from the site of the ancient Sasanian capital region and within easy reach of older Mesopotamian settlement examined in Part II of this textbook. The city's official name was Madinat al-Salam, "the City of Peace," a name carrying obvious ideological resonance for a dynasty eager to present its rule as the restoration of righteous and divinely favored order after decades of Umayyad misrule, in the new regime's own telling. In everyday usage the settlement quickly became known by the older place-name Baghdad, the name by which it is known to history and by which this chapter refers to it throughout.

Students should understand the founding of Baghdad as a deliberate and considered political act rather than as incidental urban history, undertaken for the longer-standing reasons traced in Section 25.10. The traditional foundation date for the city, chosen by court astrologers as auspicious, fell in late July 762 — months before the major Alid revolts of that autumn broke into open rebellion at Medina and in Iraq, examined in Section 25.9. The Alid revolts cannot, therefore, have caused a decision already underway; rather, they arrived as a dramatic confirmation of exactly the political dangers — Kufa's Alid associations chief among them — that al-Mansur was already working to escape by building a capital of his own. Choosing to build an entirely new capital, rather than adapting an existing city, allowed al-Mansur to design a seat of government suited specifically to the needs of the new dynasty: a city organized physically around the caliph's own residence and administration, populated initially by the regime's own soldiers, officials, and supporters, and free from the inherited political associations that made Damascus and Kufa each unsuitable in their own ways.

The founding of Baghdad in 762 should accordingly be read as the culmination of a search for a workable Abbasid capital that had begun already under al-Saffah at Anbar and al-Hashimiyya, examined in Section 25.3, rather than as a sudden leap directly from Kufa.

Section 25.12

The Round City and the Geography of Power

Baghdad's original core, planned under al-Mansur's direct supervision, took the form of a great circular walled city, a design that has led historians to refer to it as the Round City. At its physical and symbolic center stood the caliphal palace and the congregational mosque, joined together in a single central compound from which the rest of the city's administrative and residential life radiated outward. Concentric walls and controlled gates regulated access to this central zone, a physical arrangement that expressed, in architectural form, the increasingly centralized and ceremonially guarded character of caliphal authority examined further in Section 25.15.

Scholars have long noted that circular capital planning of this kind had precedents in the broader late-antique and ancient Iranian world, and Baghdad's design should be understood as drawing selectively on a range of available administrative and architectural traditions rather than representing a wholly unprecedented invention. At the same time, students should avoid the oversimplified claim that Baghdad was merely a copied "Persian city." Its planning combined elements familiar from the wider Fertile Crescent's long urban history, examined in Part II of this textbook, with a functional logic suited specifically to the needs of a new Islamic imperial capital: a central mosque and palace complex, radial approach roads, and gates oriented toward the major routes connecting the city to the rest of the empire.

Those gates carried real symbolic and strategic weight. Among them, the gate oriented toward the northeast — conventionally associated with the road toward Khurasan — stood as a permanent architectural acknowledgment of the province whose soldiers and administrators had done so much to bring the Abbasid dynasty to power, discussed already in Section 25.4. The city's overall orientation, situated on the Tigris and well connected both westward toward Syria and the Mediterranean and eastward toward Iran and Central Asia, physically expressed the new empire's shifted center of gravity: an Iraq-centered state governing an empire whose most important military and administrative constituencies now extended deep into the Iranian east, rather than a Syria-centered state oriented primarily toward the Mediterranean world, as the Umayyad caliphate had been.

Section 25.13

From Revolutionary Network to Bureaucracy

Governing an empire required more than a new capital and a loyal army; it required a functioning administration capable of collecting revenue, issuing correspondence, recording appointments, and communicating decisions across a territory stretching from the Maghreb to Central Asia. In the revolution's immediate aftermath, much of the new regime's actual governance had depended on personal networks: trusted commanders, family members, and revolutionary agents dispatched to secure specific provinces on the strength of personal loyalty rather than formal institutional office. Over the course of al-Mansur's reign, this personal, improvisational style of governance gradually gave way to a more regular, institutionalized central administration.

The Abbasids did not invent this administrative apparatus from nothing. They inherited the diwans — the administrative bureaus and registers responsible for taxation, correspondence, military pay rolls, and land records — that Chapter 23 traced to Abd al-Malik's reforms and to still older Byzantine and Sasanian administrative practice absorbed into the early conquest-era state. What changed under al-Mansur was less the basic structure of these offices than their scale, their prestige, and the growing sophistication with which they were staffed and supervised. Secretaries, record keepers, and specialized officials assumed an increasingly important role in the day-to-day operation of government, and correspondence and recordkeeping expanded considerably as the caliph sought reliable means of monitoring a vast and diverse empire from a single new capital.

Central to this effort was the barid, the empire's postal and intelligence system, which under al-Mansur became particularly important as a mechanism for monitoring provincial governors and transmitting information rapidly between the capital and the provinces. A caliph governing from a brand-new capital, surrounded by a still-untested administrative establishment and acutely aware of how quickly provincial commanders could turn into rivals, had strong practical reasons to invest in reliable channels of communication and surveillance, and the barid's growing importance under al-Mansur reflects exactly that concern.

Section 25.14

Secretaries, Mawali, and the Emerging Vizierate

Historical Vocabulary
Diwan and Barid

A diwan is an administrative bureau or register, a term inherited from earlier conquest-era Arab-Islamic administration and, before that, from Byzantine and Sasanian bureaucratic practice. Different diwans handled different functions — taxation, military pay, correspondence, and land records among them — and their number and specialization grew considerably under the early Abbasids as the demands of governing a vast empire from a single new capital increased.

The barid was the empire's postal and intelligence system, responsible for the rapid transmission of official correspondence and for supplying the caliph with information about conditions and personnel in distant provinces. Under al-Mansur the barid took on particular importance as a tool for monitoring provincial governors, whose loyalty, as this chapter has repeatedly shown, could not always be assumed.

The growing administrative apparatus examined in the preceding section increasingly relied on a professional secretarial class, conventionally referred to using the Arabic term kuttab, whose members possessed the literacy, numeracy, and specialized training necessary to manage correspondence, taxation records, and provincial reporting. Many of these secretaries came from families classified as mawali — clients or freed-person lineages attached through the bond of wala, a clientage category that, as Chapter 24 established, included freed persons, their descendants, and many but not all non-Arab converts to Islam and their descendants, rather than serving as a simple synonym for "non-Arab Muslim." A significant number of these secretarial families had Iranian, and specifically Khurasanian, backgrounds, reflecting the same eastward shift in the regime's personnel already traced in Section 25.4.

The word and function of a chief administrator directing the government in the caliph's name were not entirely new by al-Mansur's reign. Abu Salama al-Khallal, who had headed the Kufan dawa before 750 and briefly directed the provisional Abbasid government at its very founding, bore the title wazir Al Muhammad — "vizier of the family of Muhammad" — before his assassination in 750, and some historians regard him as the first figure to exercise something resembling a vizier's general direction of government. His brief and violently curtailed tenure illustrates a useful nuance: the term and the underlying function existed from the movement's earliest days, but the durable, institutionally secure office familiar from later Abbasid history had not yet taken shape.

Among the administrators who followed, Khalid ibn Barmak achieved particular prominence under al-Mansur, serving in senior administrative roles and beginning the association between his family and the highest levels of Abbasid government that would become far more famous under his descendants in later decades. Students should exercise real historical care here. It is tempting, in hindsight, to read the Barmakid family's later fame — the powerful vizierate exercised by Khalid's son Yahya and grandsons under Harun al-Rashid — backward onto this earlier period, treating Khalid's career under al-Mansur as though it already constituted the mature office of vizier familiar from later Abbasid history. Modern scholarship instead treats the specific office of wazir as a development that took clearer institutional shape somewhat later in the Abbasid period; under al-Saffah and al-Mansur, the language and formal structure of a single chief minister exercising something like a vizier's later comprehensive authority had not yet fully crystallized, even as individual secretaries and administrators, Khalid ibn Barmak prominent among them, already exercised very substantial influence in practice.

Historical Vocabulary
The Emerging Vizierate

The term wazir, conventionally translated "vizier," came to describe a chief minister exercising broad delegated authority over the caliph's administration. The title itself appeared as early as 750, when Abu Salama al-Khallal briefly bore it while directing the provisional Abbasid government, but the office reached its fuller, more clearly institutionalized and durable form later in the Abbasid period, most famously under the Barmakid family during the reign of Harun al-Rashid. Students should understand the early Abbasid administrators discussed in this chapter, Khalid ibn Barmak among them, as important precursors to that later office rather than as viziers in the later, fully developed institutional sense. Projecting the mature vizierate backward onto the 750s and 760s risks obscuring exactly the kind of gradual institutional development — from personal, improvised revolutionary administration toward a more regular bureaucratic state — that this chapter has traced throughout.

Section 25.15

The Abbasid Court

Alongside administrative centralization, the early Abbasid period saw the development of a more elaborate and ceremonially guarded court culture, one that increasingly separated the person of the caliph from direct, casual contact with ordinary subjects and even with many officials. Later tradition reports that al-Saffah himself made use of a curtain separating the ruler from those granted audience, an early and symbolically significant instance of a broader pattern that would become more pronounced under al-Mansur and his successors: the caliph as a figure surrounded by chamberlains, controlled points of access, and layered ceremonial protocol, rather than as a tribal leader accessible on the older, more informal terms Chapter 14 described for pre-Islamic Arabian authority and Chapter 22 described, with important qualifications, for the early Umayyad court.

This increasingly formal court culture drew on multiple available traditions of late-antique and Middle Eastern kingship, including practices associated with the Sasanian imperial court that Iraq's new administrative and secretarial class, examined in the preceding section, would have known well, alongside older patterns of Arab and Islamic leadership. Students should again resist an oversimplified reading in which the Abbasids simply "became Persian kings," a framing this chapter's Historical Debate feature addresses directly. What the sources instead suggest is a genuinely mixed and evolving court culture, drawing selectively on multiple precedents to construct a distinctively Abbasid style of caliphal ceremony — one suited to a dynasty governing from a brand-new capital and eager to project an image of majesty, order, and divinely sanctioned authority appropriate to the "City of Peace" it had just built.

Section 25.16

A New Language of Caliphal Rule

The developments traced throughout this chapter — military consolidation, administrative centralization, the construction of a new capital, and an increasingly formal court — were accompanied by an equally important, though less physically visible, transformation: the construction of a new ideological language justifying Abbasid rule. This language drew on several overlapping claims. The Abbasid family asserted a specific genealogical connection to the Prophet Muhammad through his uncle al-Abbas, a claim distinct from, and in competition with, the Alid claim through Ali and Fatima examined in Section 25.9. The dynasty also claimed to represent the fulfillment of the Hashimite movement's revolutionary promise: the restoration of righteous, divinely favored governance after what its propaganda characterized as decades of Umayyad injustice, a theme reflected directly in Baghdad's own official name, "the City of Peace," discussed in Section 25.11.

This ideological project was expressed through multiple concrete mechanisms of communication reaching subjects across the empire. The khutba, the sermon delivered at Friday congregational prayer, regularly invoked the caliph's name and could be used to communicate political messages and claims to legitimate authority to congregations throughout the empire's cities. Coinage, following the pattern Chapter 23 traced to Abd al-Malik's reforms, continued to serve as a vehicle for state messaging, circulating the caliph's authority in the form of everyday currency. Ceremonial textiles, prophetic relics associated with the caliphal office, and elaborate regnal titles all contributed further to this project of public self-presentation.

The regnal titles themselves deserve particular attention. Rather than reigning simply under personal names, Abbasid caliphs from al-Saffah onward adopted honorific titles carrying strong religious and ideological content: al-Saffah, al-Mansur ("the Victorious," or "the one made victorious by God"), and, in the generations that followed, titles such as al-Mahdi, al-Hadi, and al-Rashid. These titles asserted, in compact and repeatable form, claims about divine favor, guidance, and historical mission. Students should not conclude that religiously charged claims to authority were themselves an Abbasid invention: Umayyad caliphs, examined in Chapters 22 and 23, had already articulated strongly religious conceptions of caliphal authority, including language presenting the caliph as God's deputy on earth. The sharper and more historically accurate distinction lies elsewhere — in the Abbasids' increasingly systematic use of distinctive dynastic laqabs, or honorific throne names, repeated and varied across successive reigns, and in the specifically Hashimite genealogical ideology binding that titulature to a claim of kinship with the Prophet through al-Abbas. This combination of systematic titulature and genealogical claim functioned as a continuously repeated public statement of the dynasty's claim to represent not merely political power but a specifically righteous and divinely sanctioned form of Islamic rule. This language of legitimacy, developed substantially during the period this chapter examines, would continue to shape how Abbasid caliphs presented themselves for generations afterward, even as the practical realities of imperial governance, examined in Chapter 30, eventually diverged considerably from this exalted ideological self-image.

Section 25.17

Al-Mansur's Legacy, 775

When al-Mansur died in 775, he left behind a dynasty in a fundamentally different condition than the one he had inherited from his brother two decades earlier. The Abbasid family had survived the immediate succession crisis of 754. It had subordinated, at real political and human cost, the independent power of the revolutionary general most responsible for its military triumph. It had defeated serious Alid challenges to its legitimacy at Medina and in Iraq itself. It had built an entirely new imperial capital, Baghdad, deliberately positioned to reflect and reinforce the empire's shifted center of gravity toward Iraq and the Iranian east. It had begun converting a personal, improvisational revolutionary administration into a more regular bureaucratic state, staffed increasingly by professional secretaries drawn substantially, though not exclusively, from Khurasanian and Iranian backgrounds. And it had articulated a new, more explicitly monarchical and religiously charged conception of caliphal authority, expressed through regnal titles, court ceremonial, and the physical symbolism of Baghdad's Round City.

This chapter has deliberately stopped at al-Mansur's death rather than proceeding directly into the reigns of his successors and the fuller cultural flowering conventionally associated with Harun al-Rashid's court. The state al-Mansur left behind in 775 is the essential foundation for everything Chapters 26 through 30 will examine: the fiscal and commercial system that sustained this new imperial structure, the intellectual and scientific translation movement that flourished within it, the religious and legal institutions that developed alongside it, the rich urban society that grew within Baghdad and other Abbasid cities, and the eventual fragmentation of centralized caliphal power that later chapters will trace. None of those developments are intelligible without first understanding how a revolutionary coalition, in the space of a single generation, became the durable imperial state this chapter has described.

Historical Debate

Did the Abbasid Revolution "Persianize" the Caliphate?

The question: How should historians characterize the shift in personnel, culture, and administrative practice that accompanied the Abbasid Revolution? Older historical narratives often framed the transition from Umayyad to Abbasid rule as a shift from an "Arab" state to a "Persian" one. Is this framing historically accurate?

Interpretation A — The Abbasid state as substantially Persianized: This view emphasizes the Khurasanian military base, the growing prominence of Iranian secretaries and administrative families such as the Barmakids, the adoption of court ceremonial practices with recognizable roots in Sasanian tradition, and Baghdad's location and circular planning as evidence that the Abbasid Revolution shifted the caliphate's center of political and cultural gravity decisively toward Iran and its administrative traditions.
Interpretation B — A more mixed, still fundamentally Arab-Islamic state: This view emphasizes that Arabic remained the central language of imperial government and increasingly the dominant language of Islamic scholarship and public religious discourse, even as Syriac, Greek, Middle Persian, Hebrew, and other languages remained active within the empire's diverse intellectual and religious communities; that the ruling Abbasid family was itself Arab and grounded its legitimacy in Arab genealogical descent from the Prophet's family; that Arab elites, administrators, and troops continued to play major roles throughout the period this chapter examines; and that the Khurasaniyya were themselves a genuinely mixed force including many Arabic-speaking soldiers rather than a simply "Persian" army.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on overlapping evidence — the ethnic and regional composition of the Khurasaniyya, the backgrounds of prominent administrators, the architectural and ceremonial precedents visible in Baghdad's planning and the Abbasid court, and the language of Abbasid administration and scholarship — but differ in how much explanatory weight to place on Iranian and Khurasanian influence relative to continuing Arab-Islamic institutional and linguistic dominance.

Why it matters: This textbook treats the early Abbasid state as neither a simple continuation of Umayyad Arab rule nor a wholesale substitution of Persian for Arab government, but as a genuinely mixed and evolving order: an Arabic-speaking, Arab-led caliphate whose military base, administrative personnel, and court culture had shifted substantially toward Khurasan and drew selectively on multiple available late-antique traditions, including Iranian ones. Reducing this transformation to a simple binary of "Arab" versus "Persian" rule obscures the more historically accurate picture of gradual institutional blending this chapter has traced.

Student task: Using evidence from this chapter, evaluate which interpretation better accounts for the specific developments examined in Sections 25.4, 25.14, and 25.15, and explain what kind of additional evidence would help refine the debate further.

Continuity and Change Over Time

The Caliphate, 740 to 775

What Changed

  • The ruling dynasty shifted from Umayyad to Abbasid, and Syria lost its former political centrality
  • The empire's core military constituency shifted from Syrian troops to the Khurasaniyya
  • The capital moved from Damascus to a newly built city, Baghdad, positioned on the Iraq–Khurasan axis
  • Court ceremonial grew more elaborate and more physically separated from ordinary subjects
  • Regnal titles and public ideology placed greater emphasis on divine favor, restored justice, and prophetic kinship

What Stayed the Same

  • Arabic remained the language of formal administration and scholarship
  • The basic administrative structure of provinces, governors, and diwans continued from Umayyad practice
  • Land taxation continued along broadly familiar lines, examined further in Chapter 26
  • The caliphate remained, in principle, a single office claiming leadership over the entire Muslim community
  • Revolutionary commanders and relatives continued to pose recurring threats to dynastic stability

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both change and continuity prevents students from treating the Abbasid Revolution as either a purely cosmetic change of dynasty or a complete institutional rupture; the actual transformation was selective, gradual, and shaped as much by inherited administrative practice as by genuinely new political arrangements.

Cause and Effect

Why al-Mansur Killed Abu Muslim

Long-term causes

The structural danger, examined in Section 25.5, inherent in any revolutionary movement whose successful commanders accumulate personal followings and reputations independent of the ruling family they helped install.

Short-term causes

Abu Muslim's decisive victory over Abd Allah ibn Ali in 754 on al-Mansur's behalf, which simultaneously confirmed his military indispensability and demonstrated how completely the new caliph depended on his goodwill.

Event

Al-Mansur's summons of Abu Muslim to court in February 755, followed by his killing there on the caliph's order.

Immediate effects

The removal of the single most powerful independent military figure in the empire, and the provocation of unrest among his Khurasanian following, including the revolt of Sunbadh examined in Section 25.8.

Long-term consequences

A clearer establishment of the principle that military command in Abbasid service, however distinguished, remained subordinate to caliphal authority, alongside a lasting popular memory of Abu Muslim that would recur in later political and religious movements in the Iranian east.

Historical complexity

Much of the vivid narrative detail surrounding the killing survives only through later historical compilation, and historians read the specific dialogue and motives attributed to al-Mansur and Abu Muslim with the same evidentiary caution this textbook applies throughout to later-transmitted political narrative.
Everyday Life and Social History

Building a New Imperial Capital

Behind the political history this chapter has traced lay the very concrete, physical labor of rapidly constructing a planned imperial center amid existing settlements. A small settlement called Baghdad and neighboring communities already occupied parts of the site before al-Mansur's foundation, but the scale of construction he ordered transformed the area into something categorically new. Baghdad's founding required the mobilization of laborers, craftsmen, and materials on an enormous scale: brickmakers and builders to raise the Round City's concentric walls, canal diggers to extend irrigation and water supply from the Tigris into the new urban site, and the transport of timber, stone, and other building materials from considerable distances. Skilled and unskilled workers alike, drawn from across Iraq and beyond, found employment in this sudden burst of urban construction, and the new city's rapid growth drew merchants, artisans, and settlers eager to participate in the opportunities a fresh imperial capital promised.

For the ordinary households who came to populate the new city in its first years, daily life centered on the practical business of establishing a livelihood in a rapidly expanding urban environment rather than on the high political drama of succession crises and court intrigue examined elsewhere in this chapter. Markets had to be organized, residential quarters built and settled, and much of the everyday infrastructure of urban life — wells, canals, bakeries, and workshops — established or greatly expanded within a few short years, even as the city's population grew with striking speed following 762. This experience of rapid, state-driven urban growth, and the social world it created, will receive fuller treatment in Chapter 29's examination of Abbasid urban society.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how a revolutionary coalition became a durable imperial dynasty in the quarter-century following the Abbasid victory of 750. Its central argument has been that the Abbasid Revolution did not merely replace one Arab dynasty with another while leaving the substance of imperial rule unchanged. Between the proclamation of al-Saffah and the death of al-Mansur in 775, the Abbasid family converted a coalition assembled to overthrow the Umayyads into a centralized monarchy governed from a newly built capital, with a substantially altered military base, an increasingly professional administration, a more elaborate court culture, and a new ideological language of caliphal legitimacy.

Students examined al-Saffah's short, foundational reign and the structural problem his successor al-Mansur inherited: a dynasty owing its throne to revolutionary commanders and provincial constituencies whose loyalty could not simply be assumed. The chapter traced this problem through the succession crisis of 754, in which Abu Muslim's military intervention secured al-Mansur's throne even as it demonstrated the caliph's dangerous dependence on Abu Muslim's own power — a tension resolved, at significant cost, through Abu Muslim's killing in 755 and the unrest that followed, including the revolt of Sunbadh.

The chapter also examined the Abbasids' fraught relationship with Alid claimants to the Prophet's family inheritance, tracing how the broad, deliberately ambiguous appeal of the original Hashimite movement produced serious challenges to specifically Abbasid legitimacy, culminating in the revolts of Muhammad al-Nafs al-Zakiyya and his brother Ibrahim in 762–763.

A substantial portion of the chapter addressed the founding of Baghdad in 762, examining why al-Mansur rejected both Damascus and Kufa as long-term capitals, how the Round City's design expressed the new regime's centralized and increasingly ceremonial conception of caliphal authority, and how the city's location and gates, including the symbolically significant Khurasan Gate, reflected the empire's shifted center of gravity toward Iraq and the Iranian east.

The chapter traced the gradual development of Abbasid administration from personal, improvisational revolutionary networks toward a more regular bureaucracy built around the diwans and the barid, and it examined the rising importance of a professional secretarial class, including early Barmakid administrators such as Khalid ibn Barmak, while cautioning against projecting the later, fully developed vizierate backward onto this earlier period. It examined the growing formality of Abbasid court ceremonial and the new, more explicitly religious and monarchical language of caliphal legitimacy expressed through regnal titles, the khutba, and coinage.

Finally, the chapter's Historical Debate feature addressed directly the question of how far the early Abbasid state should be understood as "Persianized," concluding that the evidence supports a genuinely mixed and evolving Arabic-speaking, Arab-led state rather than either simple continuity with Umayyad rule or a wholesale substitution of Persian for Arab government.

By 775, the state al-Mansur left behind possessed a secure dynasty, a new and defensible capital, a developing bureaucracy, and an articulated language of legitimate rule. This is the foundation Chapters 26 through 30 will build upon, examining in turn the Abbasid economy and government, the translation movement and the sciences, Islamic law and religious authority, the social life of Abbasid cities, and the eventual fragmentation of centralized caliphal power.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Caliphate
The office and institution of supreme leadership over the Muslim community, claimed successively by the Rashidun, Umayyad, and Abbasid dynasties.
Khurasaniyya
The Khurasan-based, ethnically mixed soldiery that formed the military core of the Abbasid Revolution and the new regime, discussed in Section 25.4.
Dawa
The clandestine missionary and organizational movement that built support for the Hashimite cause in Khurasan before the open revolt of 747, examined in Chapter 24.
Diwan
An administrative bureau or register responsible for functions such as taxation, correspondence, or military pay, inherited from earlier Umayyad and pre-Islamic practice.
Barid
The empire's postal and intelligence system, used to transmit correspondence and monitor provincial governors, especially important under al-Mansur.
Wazir (vizier)
A chief minister exercising broad delegated administrative authority; the office reached its fuller institutional form later in the Abbasid period than the period this chapter examines.
Mawali
Non-Arab converts to Islam and their descendants, many of whom rose to administrative prominence under the early Abbasids.
Alid
A descendant, or supporter of the claims of descendants, of Ali ibn Abi Talib and Fatima, rival claimants to leadership of the Muslim community discussed in Section 25.9.
Hashimite
Relating to the broader clan of the Prophet Muhammad, including both the Abbasid and Alid lines; the term used for the revolutionary movement's broad pre-750 appeal.
Madinat al-Salam
"The City of Peace," the official name of Baghdad as founded by al-Mansur in 762.
Round City
The circular walled core of al-Mansur's original Baghdad, centered on the caliphal palace and congregational mosque.
Khutba
The sermon delivered at Friday congregational prayer, used to communicate political as well as religious messages, including recognition of the ruling caliph.
Regnal title
An honorific throne name, such as al-Saffah or al-Mansur, carrying religious and ideological claims about the caliph's legitimacy and mission.
Chamberlain (hajib)
A court official responsible for regulating access to the caliph, reflecting the increasingly formal and ceremonial character of the Abbasid court.
Kuttab (secretarial class)
The professional class of administrative secretaries who staffed the diwans and conducted the day-to-day business of government.
Garrison city
An early Arab-Islamic settlement originally established for military purposes, such as Kufa, examined further in Chapter 20.
Al-Saffah
The regnal title, meaning roughly "the one who pours forth" or is understood as associated with generosity or the shedding of blood in righteous cause, taken by the first Abbasid caliph.
Al-Mansur
"The Victorious," the regnal title of the second Abbasid caliph and founder of Baghdad.
Revolutionary constituency
This chapter's term for the coalition of commanders, soldiers, and supporters whose loyalty had won the Abbasids their throne and who had to be managed, rewarded, or subordinated afterward.
Centralization
The process by which political and administrative authority is concentrated around a single ruling institution, examined throughout this chapter's account of the shift from personal networks to regular bureaucracy.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has told a story that recurs across the history of revolutionary movements far beyond the Arab-Islamic world: the moment of victory is not the moment of security, and the coalition that wins power must often be reshaped, narrowed, and in places forcibly disciplined before it can govern durably. The Abbasid family's achievement between 750 and 775 lay precisely in accomplishing this difficult transformation — surviving a contested succession, subordinating the independent power of its own most successful general, defeating rival claimants to the Prophet's inheritance, and building, from nothing, an imperial capital suited to the state it wished to become.

The chapter has also modeled an important historical corrective, one that will recur throughout Part V: resisting the temptation to describe the Abbasid transformation through a simple binary of Arab versus Persian, or through an equally simple narrative of straightforward institutional continuity from Umayyad to Abbasid rule. The actual pattern this chapter has traced is more complicated and, in the end, more historically interesting — a genuinely mixed and gradually developing state, Arabic in language and Arab in ruling family, yet substantially reshaped by its Khurasanian military base, its Iranian and mawali administrators, and its selective borrowing from multiple available traditions of late-antique kingship.

Students who understand how al-Saffah's improvised early regime became al-Mansur's more centralized state, why Abu Muslim's death mattered structurally and not merely personally, why Baghdad was built where and how it was, and how a professional bureaucracy and a new ideological language of caliphal rule developed alongside these political changes are well equipped to read Chapter 26's account of how this same state organized its economy, and the chapters that follow, which trace the flowering of the civilization this state made possible.

The Abbasid caliphate that entered its second generation in 775 was not the caliphate that had proclaimed itself in Kufa a quarter-century earlier. It was a state that had learned, often at considerable cost, what it took to convert revolutionary victory into lasting imperial rule — a lesson embodied permanently in the walls, gates, and central palace of the City of Peace on the Tigris.
