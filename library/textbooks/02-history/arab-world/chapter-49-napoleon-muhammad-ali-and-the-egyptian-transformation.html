Part IX — Reform, Nahda, and European Expansion

Napoleon, Muhammad Ali, and the Egyptian Transformation

A History of the Arab World: From Ancient Crossroads to the Modern Arab World

Chapter Opening

In July 1798 a French army of roughly thirty-five thousand men marched up the Nile toward Cairo, and within weeks the most important province of the Ottoman Empire's Arab lands had been shaken in a way no earlier invasion had managed. Fifty years later, in 1848, Egypt was governed by a dynasty that had its own army, its own fleet, its own bureaucracy, and its own cotton-based economy, and that had marched armies into Arabia, Sudan, Greece, and Syria. This chapter explains how one of those facts led to the other.

The story begins with a shock and ends with a settlement. The shock was Napoleon Bonaparte's invasion, which destroyed the Mamluk military households that had dominated Egyptian politics for generations and exposed how weak the Ottoman state's hold on its richest province had become. The settlement was the Ottoman imperial decree of 1841 that recognized Muhammad Ali Pasha and his male heirs as hereditary governors of Egypt, within limits that European powers and the Ottoman court had imposed after a war that brought the empire close to collapse. Between those two points lie the French occupation of 1798–1801, three years of confused struggle among Ottoman officers, Albanian soldiers, and surviving Mamluk factions, and the long reign of an officer from the Macedonian port of Kavala who took power in Cairo in 1805 and held it until the end of the 1840s.

Historians have described this period in sharply different ways. Egyptian nationalist writers of the twentieth century often presented Muhammad Ali as the founder of modern Egypt. European observers of his own day sometimes praised him as an enlightened despot and sometimes condemned him as a threat to the balance of power. More recent scholars, working from Egyptian archives, military records, and court registers, have stressed the human cost of his policies and the extent to which he built on Ottoman and Mamluk institutions rather than replacing them. This chapter takes that scholarly shift seriously. It treats the Egyptian transformation as a process driven by military necessity and fiscal extraction, carried out through a mixture of old and new institutions, and experienced very differently by peasants, soldiers, scholars, merchants, and the people of the territories Egypt conquered.

The chapter is organized in five divisions. The first examines the French invasion, the occupation, and the production of European knowledge about Egypt. The second follows Muhammad Ali's rise and the destruction of the old Mamluk order. The third, and largest, examines the machinery of the new state: the conscript army, land and taxation, irrigation and cotton, monopolies and industry, administration, education, and the social groups who gained or lost. The fourth traces the use of that machinery abroad, in Arabia, Sudan, the Greek war, and above all Syria. The fifth steps back to ask what had actually changed by 1848, and what had not.

The evidence for this period is unusually rich, and unusually partisan. The Cairo chronicler Abd al-Rahman al-Jabarti recorded the French occupation and the first decades of Muhammad Ali's rise with a mixture of precision and open hostility toward both. French savants produced a vast published record, the Description de l'Égypte. Ottoman, Egyptian, and European archives preserve military registers, tax records, court cases, and diplomatic correspondence. European consuls, travelers, and merchants wrote voluminously, often with a commercial or political purpose. Students should read each kind of source for what it can show and what its author wanted.

Two boundaries need to be stated. First, this chapter does not retell the history of the First Saudi State, which Chapter 48 examined; Egyptian campaigns in Arabia appear here only to show how the Egyptian army was built and used. Second, the chapter ends around 1848, with the close of Muhammad Ali's active reign. The broader Ottoman reform program of the Tanzimat belongs to Chapter 50, the cultural movements that came to be called the Nahda belong to Chapter 51, and the later nineteenth-century story of the Suez Canal, Egyptian debt, and British occupation belongs to Chapter 53.

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to explain why an Ottoman provincial governor became powerful enough to threaten the empire that appointed him, why that power was nonetheless limited, and why a transformation often praised as modernization looked very different from the village, the barracks, and the conquered province.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain the strategic motives and military course of the French invasion of Egypt and why the Mamluk defeat created a political vacuum.
  2. Analyze how French rule interacted with Egyptian institutions, especially the ulama, the Diwan, and urban and rural resistance.
  3. Evaluate the French scientific expedition as an encounter between European knowledge production and Egyptian society rather than as a simple story of modernization.
  4. Trace Muhammad Ali's rise between 1801 and 1805 and distinguish popular, scholarly, and Ottoman sources of his legitimacy.
  5. Describe how he neutralized rival military and fiscal elites, including the events of 1811, without assuming an overnight transformation.
  6. Explain how conscription, land and tax reorganization, irrigation, cotton, and state monopolies fitted together as a system serving military power.
  7. Assess scholarly debate over whether Muhammad Ali's economic program amounted to industrialization or to a more limited state-directed experiment.
  8. Compare the experiences of ulama, Copts, merchants, village shaykhs, peasants, workers, Bedouin, and enslaved people under the new order.
  9. Trace Egyptian expansion into Arabia, Sudan, Crete, the Morea, and Syria, and its consequences for the societies affected.
  10. Explain the Ottoman–Egyptian crisis of 1831–1841 and the terms and limits of the settlement of 1841.
  11. Distinguish continuity from change in Egyptian state and society between 1798 and 1848.
  12. Evaluate competing historical interpretations of Muhammad Ali as modernizer, authoritarian state-builder, and Ottoman provincial magnate.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did a French invasion succeed so quickly in 1798, and why did French rule fail to last?
  2. What did French and Egyptian sources respectively show about the occupation, and how do their purposes shape what they report?
  3. On what combination of force, popular support, and Ottoman consent did Muhammad Ali's authority rest in 1805, and how did that mixture change?
  4. How did military reform drive changes in taxation, agriculture, industry, education, and administration?
  5. Who benefited and who bore the costs of the new order, and what kinds of evidence reveal their experiences?
  6. Why was Egypt able to project power abroad so rapidly, and why did that expansion eventually meet resistance?
  7. Why did European powers intervene to limit Muhammad Ali, and what did they hope to preserve?
  8. How much of the Egyptian "transformation" was new, and how much was a reworking of Ottoman and Mamluk practice?

Historical Context

Egypt had been an Ottoman province since the conquest of 1517 (Chapter 43). Chapters 44 and 47 showed that Ottoman rule there was negotiated rather than simply imposed: governors sent from Istanbul stayed briefly, while the real power in Cairo rested with garrison regiments, with Mamluk households whose leaders held the rank of bey, and with the ulama and merchants who mediated between rulers and population. By the late eighteenth century the Mamluk households had grown strong enough that the Ottoman government once sent an expedition, in 1786, to restore its authority, and then had to accept their return in 1791. Egypt remained one of the empire's most valuable provinces, supplying grain to Istanbul and the holy cities, and hosting the coffee, textile, and Red Sea trade that passed through Cairo.

Beyond Egypt, three larger developments formed the background. The Ottoman Empire was under strain from wars with Russia and Austria, from provincial notables who had built regional power bases, and from the fiscal problems that Chapter 47 described. European states, especially Britain and France, were in a global contest in which control of eastern Mediterranean routes had become a strategic concern, particularly after the French Revolution opened a long war in Europe. And the Arabian interior had seen the rise of the Saudi–Wahhabi movement, whose capture of Mecca and Medina in the early 1800s embarrassed the Sultan as protector of the holy cities and set the stage for Egypt's first overseas campaign (Chapter 48).

Two historical mistakes are worth avoiding from the outset. The first is to treat 1798 as the moment when a stagnant Egypt was suddenly awakened by Europe. Eighteenth-century Egypt had a commercial economy, a vigorous scholarly culture, and its own political dynamics. The second is to treat Muhammad Ali's reign as a national awakening in the modern sense. He was an Ottoman officer who spoke Turkish, ruled through a Turco-Circassian elite, and for most of his career did not claim to represent an Egyptian nation. This chapter treats the French invasion as a real shock and the Egyptian transformation as a real change, while insisting that both worked through preexisting institutions and for purposes that Egyptians themselves did not choose.

Section 49.1 · Division I: The French Occupation, 1798–1801

Egypt on the Eve of the French Invasion

In 1798 Egypt had a population usually estimated at between two and three million, concentrated along the Nile and in the Delta, with Cairo, a city of perhaps a quarter of a million, as its political, commercial, and scholarly center. Agriculture rested on the annual flood and on basin irrigation of the kind Chapter 3 described, producing wheat, barley, beans, rice, flax, and sugarcane, with grain also supplied to Istanbul and the holy cities of Arabia. Cairo's merchants handled the Red Sea coffee trade, Indian cloth, and Mediterranean commerce, in which French merchants based in Alexandria and Cairo played a significant part. The countryside was divided into tax districts, and the Ottoman government's share of revenue had shrunk as local holders kept more of it.

Politically, the province was governed by a four-sided balance. An Ottoman governor, appointed from Istanbul and usually serving briefly, held formal authority. Garrison regiments, many of them long since merged into the local population, held weapons and revenue claims. Mamluk households, built on the purchase and military training of young men from the Caucasus and elsewhere, supplied the beys and military governors who actually controlled much of the countryside. Alongside them the ulama and the heads of merchant and craft groups acted as intermediaries and as a check on arbitrary rule. In the 1790s two Mamluk leaders, Ibrahim Bey and Murad Bey, shared effective power in Cairo, while the governor's authority was largely ceremonial.

Chapter 47 treats this system in detail. For the purposes of this chapter, what matters is its character: a decentralized order in which revenue and force were held by competing households and regiments, mediated by religious and commercial notables, under the loose sovereignty of a distant sultan. It was this order that the French army broke in 1798 and that Muhammad Ali later replaced with a centralized one. The principal sources for the eve of the invasion are al-Jabarti's chronicle, Ottoman and Egyptian court and tax records, the writings of European consuls and merchants, and, after 1798, the French survey of the country.

Section 49.2

Napoleon's Invasion and the Battle of the Pyramids

The French Directory, at war with Britain and eager to give its most successful general a command away from Paris, authorized an expedition to Egypt in 1798. Its aims were mixed. Planners hoped to compensate for French colonial losses, to take control of a rich and strategically placed province, and to threaten British routes to India. Napoleon also saw the venture as an opportunity to cast France as the bearer of European science and enlightened government to the East, a framing that his later propaganda emphasized. The expedition sailed from Toulon in May 1798 with about thirty-five thousand soldiers, hundreds of transport ships, and a contingent of scholars. It took Malta in June and eluded British pursuit.

The French landed near Alexandria on 1 July and took the city the next day. Napoleon issued a proclamation in Arabic claiming that France had come to punish the Mamluks, respected Islam, and remained a friend of the Ottoman Sultan (see the Primary Source Window below). The army then marched across the desert to the Nile, suffering badly from heat and thirst, and fought a river and cavalry action at Shubra Khit on 13 July. On 21 July, near the village of Imbaba across the river from Cairo, French infantry formed in hollow squares and defeated the charges of Murad Bey's Mamluk cavalry. The battle has long been called the Battle of the Pyramids, after the monuments visible in the distance, although the fighting took place some miles from them. Ibrahim Bey withdrew eastward, and the French entered Cairo within days.

The speed of the victory shows how little the Mamluk system had adapted to European tactics and artillery. The cavalry that had dominated Egyptian politics could not break disciplined infantry with modern firepower. But the French position was less secure than the victory suggested. On 1–2 August the British fleet under Horatio Nelson destroyed the French fleet at Abu Qir Bay, in the Battle of the Nile, leaving the army stranded in Egypt. The Ottoman government, which regarded the invasion as an attack on its sovereign territory, declared war on France in September. What had begun as a French conquest became a theater of the wider European war, and Egypt's fate would now be decided partly by forces beyond its borders.

Section 49.3

French Rule, the Diwan, and Egyptian Resistance

The French tried to govern Egypt through familiar institutions. In the summer of 1798 they created a Diwan, a council in Cairo drawn from leading ulama, merchants, and notables, with provincial councils to follow. The senior religious scholars who sat on it, among them members of the Azhar establishment, were placed in an awkward position: they could use the Diwan to protect the population and negotiate over French demands, but their cooperation also lent French rule a measure of legitimacy. Napoleon's courtship of the ulama, his participation in Muslim festivals, and his claims to Muslim sympathy were largely tactical, and most Egyptians who left written accounts regarded them with skepticism.

The deeper problem was fiscal. A large army cut off from France had to be paid for by Egypt itself, and French officials imposed new taxes, forced loans, and requisitions, and introduced registration measures that cut across the customary distribution of authority and income. In October 1798 these pressures, together with rumors and resentment of foreign occupation, produced a revolt in Cairo. Crowds gathered around the Azhar quarter, killed French officers, and barricaded streets. The French answered with artillery fire from the Citadel and the Muqattam hills and entered the Azhar mosque itself with horses, an act al-Jabarti recorded as a profound desecration. Several leaders of the revolt were executed. A second and larger revolt broke out in March and April 1800 during the fighting between the French and an Ottoman army, and was suppressed with heavy destruction in Cairo and Bulaq.

Resistance was not confined to the capital. In Upper Egypt French forces under General Desaix pursued Murad Bey's Mamluks and fought local forces in a prolonged campaign that left heavy losses among the population. Some Egyptians, notably certain Coptic administrators and a minority of Cairo's non-Muslim communities, found it advantageous to work with the French, and a few soldiers and officials converted to Islam; General Menou, who took command in 1800, adopted the name Abdallah and married a woman from Rosetta. Plague, famine, and the confiscation of resources shaped daily life throughout. French rule revealed, above all, that legitimacy in Egypt depended on the ulama and the urban population, and that a regime that could not secure their consent could rule only by force.

Primary Source Window

Napoleon's Proclamation to the Egyptians (July 1798) and al-Jabarti's Response

Creator: Napoleon Bonaparte (proclamation, drafted in French and translated into Arabic by French interpreters); Abd al-Rahman al-Jabarti (Arabic chronicler who reproduced and commented on it) · Date: July 1798, with al-Jabarti's chronicle composed in the following years · Type: Official propaganda and a contemporary scholarly chronicle

Context: Shortly after landing, the French circulated an Arabic proclamation addressed to the people of Egypt. Al-Jabarti, a Cairo scholar with close ties to the religious establishment, included its text in his chronicle and added a critical commentary. This chapter paraphrases both rather than quoting them.

In paraphrase: the proclamation presents the French as friends of the Ottoman Sultan who have come only to remove the Mamluks, whom it describes as oppressors and usurpers. It stresses French respect for God, the Prophet, and the Qur'an, assures the population that their religion, property, and persons will be respected, and calls on Egyptians to cooperate and to treat the French as liberators. Al-Jabarti, for his part, ridicules the document's awkward Arabic, rejects its claim to represent a friend of the Sultan, and treats its religious language as a pretense.

Analysis: The proclamation shows how Napoleon expected to justify the invasion to a Muslim population: by appealing to Ottoman sovereignty, Islamic legitimacy, and resentment of Mamluk rule. Its claims were contradicted by the fact that France and the Ottoman state were about to be at war. Al-Jabarti's commentary shows that educated Egyptians read the text critically and quickly saw through its claims, and illustrates how a chronicler's perspective, that of an urban scholar, shapes what he records. Neither source shows how peasants or the rural population understood the invasion, and al-Jabarti's account was revised in later versions.

Questions for students:

  1. What claims does the proclamation make to win Egyptian support, and which were contradicted by events?
  2. Who created each source, and what was each author's purpose?
  3. What can historians learn from al-Jabarti's reaction about how educated Egyptians regarded the invaders?
  4. What can historians not establish from these two sources alone?
Section 49.4

The French Scientific Expedition and the Production of Knowledge

Napoleon's army was accompanied by roughly one hundred and fifty to one hundred and seventy scholars, engineers, artists, and technicians, known as savants. In August 1798 they founded the Institut d'Égypte in Cairo, modeled on the French national institute, which met to discuss research and to propose practical projects. Its members, among them the mathematician Gaspard Monge, the chemist Claude-Louis Berthollet, the naturalist Étienne Geoffroy Saint-Hilaire, the artist Dominique Vivant Denon, and the mechanic Nicolas-Jacques Conté, surveyed the country's geography and antiquities, studied its agriculture, population, medicine, and natural history, and produced maps, drawings, and measurements on a scale never before attempted.

The results were published as the Description de l'Égypte, a monumental multi-volume work issued from 1809 onward in the following two decades. It remains a basic source for the topography, monuments, and economy of late eighteenth-century Egypt, and its plates helped create a European public appetite for Egyptian antiquity. In July 1799 French soldiers near Rashid (Rosetta) found the stone later called the Rosetta Stone, carrying the same decree in three scripts; it passed to British hands under the capitulation of 1801 and, in the 1820s, became the key to deciphering hieroglyphs. These achievements belong to the history of European scholarship, but the knowledge they produced was gathered by an occupying army and used in part for administration and control.

Egyptian responses were varied. Some scholars, including the Azhar shaykh Hasan al-Attar, visited the Institut and were intrigued by its instruments and libraries. Al-Jabarti too visited and recorded his impressions, admiring the scientific tools while condemning the occupation and what he saw as the savants' disregard for religion. The expedition did not create a lasting Egyptian scientific institution, and its direct effects on Egyptian education were small. Its importance for this chapter is different: it taught European states how much could be known about Egypt, and it showed Egyptian observers how much European powers valued technical and geographic knowledge as an instrument of power. Chapter 51 considers the later intellectual encounter; here it is enough to see the savants as part of the invasion itself rather than as an innocent introduction of modernity.

Section 49.5

The British–Ottoman War and the End of French Egypt

With the Ottoman Empire at war, Napoleon moved against Syria in early 1799 to forestall an Ottoman advance. His army took Gaza and Jaffa, where it put a large number of prisoners to death after the city's capture, and then besieged Acre, held by the Ottoman governor Ahmad al-Jazzar with support from a British naval squadron under Sidney Smith. After two months, with plague spreading and heavy losses, the French lifted the siege in May and retreated to Egypt. In July Napoleon defeated an Ottoman landing at Abu Qir, and in August he left Egypt secretly for France, leaving General Kléber in command.

Kléber, deeply pessimistic about the army's prospects, negotiated an agreement at al-Arish in January 1800 for evacuation, which the British refused to honor. He then won a victory over an Ottoman force at Heliopolis in March and put down the second Cairo revolt. In June 1800 he was assassinated in Cairo by Sulayman al-Halabi, a young Syrian student, who was tried and executed. Menou succeeded him. In March 1801 a British expeditionary force landed at Abu Qir, fought the French at Alexandria, and, together with an Ottoman army, advanced on Cairo. The French in Cairo capitulated in June, and Menou's force in Alexandria followed in September. Britain withdrew its troops in 1803.

The French occupation had lasted three years. It left behind a broken Mamluk elite, an economy damaged by requisition and plague, and a population that had seen how weak the old order was. It also left a power vacuum. The Ottoman state, the British, the surviving Mamluk beys, and the regiments that had come to Egypt with the Ottoman army all had a stake in what came next. Egypt's reconstruction would be decided not by any of the great powers but by a contest among local and Ottoman forces on the ground, which is where Muhammad Ali enters.

Section 49.6 · Division II: Muhammad Ali's Rise and the Destruction of the Old Order

Egypt after the French: The Struggle for Power, 1801–1805

The Ottoman force that retook Egypt included a contingent of irregular troops recruited in the Macedonian port of Kavala, and Muhammad Ali, born around 1769 in a family of local standing and experienced in commerce and tax collection, served in it as a junior officer. He had not come to Egypt as a ruler. But the Ottoman army was a coalition of competing leaders, regiments, and factions, many of them Albanian-speaking, and the years after 1801 were chaotic. Governors were appointed and removed in rapid succession. Troops demanding pay plundered the countryside and the cities. The Mamluk beys who had survived the French returned to dispute control of the revenue, with one faction supported by Britain for a time.

Within this struggle, Muhammad Ali showed a talent for coalition-building. He cultivated Albanian soldiers, negotiated with Mamluk leaders, and learned the politics of Cairo. In 1803 the Albanian commander Tahir Pasha, who had briefly held power, was killed in a mutiny, and his death left Albanian troops leaderless. Muhammad Ali emerged as one of their principal commanders. When the Ottoman government appointed a new governor, Khurshid Pasha, in 1804, with a mandate to restore order, he found that he could not pay the troops or control the Mamluks, and that the Cairo population was at the end of its patience.

Our knowledge of these years comes mainly from al-Jabarti, from European consular reports, and from later dynastic histories written under Muhammad Ali's descendants, which present his rise as a natural and even inevitable emergence of the strongest man. Students should be cautious about such accounts. In 1803 Muhammad Ali was one of several contenders, not the obvious successor, and the outcome depended on contingencies as much as on his abilities.

Section 49.7

The 1805 Crisis and Muhammad Ali's Appointment

The immediate crisis of 1805 grew out of the governor's unpaid troops, new taxes, and the violence of the soldiery in Cairo. In spring 1805 leading ulama, together with Umar Makram, head of the descendants of the Prophet, and other notables, mobilized the city. Shops closed, crowds gathered, and the religious leaders declared that Khurshid Pasha should be replaced. In May they announced the choice of Muhammad Ali as governor, with the apparent consent of the Albanian soldiers who followed him, and Khurshid took refuge in the Citadel. The Ottoman court did not at first accept the change, but faced with a fait accompli and unwilling to risk further disorder, it confirmed Muhammad Ali in July 1805.

The episode has been read in several ways. Nationalist historians of the twentieth century saw it as an expression of the Egyptian people's will, in which the people chose their ruler. Other historians stress that the leaders involved were Cairene notables with interests of their own, that the decisive force was Albanian soldiery, and that the Sultan's confirmation was indispensable. Al-Jabarti's account makes clear that the ulama saw themselves as exercising a traditional right to demand justice from rulers, and that they hoped Muhammad Ali would be a restrained and just governor. They were mistaken. He later exiled Umar Makram, in 1809, and steadily reduced the political weight of the Cairo notables.

The legitimacy that Muhammad Ali held after 1805 was therefore compound: acclaim from the Cairo religious establishment, the loyalty of part of the army, and Ottoman appointment. The Sultan also tried, in 1806, to transfer him to a post in Salonica, an attempt that was reversed after pressure from the Cairo notables and the army and after payments to Istanbul. The episode showed that his hold was still precarious and that his most reliable strategy was to make himself useful to the Ottoman government and indispensable in Egypt.

Section 49.8

Consolidating Power: Mamluks, Albanian Troops, and Provincial Authority

Muhammad Ali's first years in power were not a smooth ascent. The Mamluk beys remained a serious military force, particularly in Upper Egypt, and held revenue and local followings that he needed. In 1807 a British force landed at Alexandria, hoping to secure the port and to support the Mamluks; it was defeated near Rosetta and withdrew in September. The episode showed that Britain, then at war with the Ottoman Empire, was still willing to intervene in Egypt, and that Muhammad Ali could mobilize enough force and local support to repel it.

He proceeded through a combination of alliance, pressure, and division. He negotiated with some Mamluk leaders and played them against each other, taxed heavily to pay his troops, and gradually gained control of key tax districts. He also faced repeated mutinies from the Albanian and Turkish soldiers whose pay was always in arrears and whose loyalty could not be taken for granted. The Ottoman government's own demands added to the pressure. In 1810 the Sultan ordered him to send an army against the Wahhabi state in Arabia, a task that would both serve Ottoman interests and send his turbulent troops away from Egypt (see Chapter 48).

It is important not to project the later state backward onto these years. Between 1805 and 1811 Muhammad Ali governed a province in which revenue was still largely held by tax farmers, where authority in the countryside remained dispersed, and where his own power depended on keeping an army paid and the notables placated. The institutions of the new order, examined in Division III, emerged from the need to solve these problems.

Section 49.9

The Citadel Massacre and the New Political Order

On 1 March 1811 Muhammad Ali invited the Mamluk beys, who had been drawn into an uneasy accommodation, to a ceremony at the Citadel marking the departure of his son Tusun for the Arabian campaign. As the procession passed through a narrow gateway, the gates were closed and soldiers opened fire. Most accounts put the number of Mamluks killed at several hundred, commonly around four hundred and seventy, though the figure varies. A popular tradition holds that a single bey escaped by leaping his horse from the walls; historians treat the story as legend. Over the following days Muhammad Ali's troops attacked Mamluk households in Cairo and the provinces, and others fled south into Upper Egypt and Nubia.

The massacre was an act of calculated political violence. It broke the Mamluk military elite as an organized force and freed their large landed and fiscal holdings for confiscation. Contemporary observers, including European consuls, recorded varying reactions. Al-Jabarti's account notes the horror of the killings and the widespread looting that accompanied them. Muhammad Ali's defenders later presented the event as a necessary step in ending an anarchic order, while critics saw it as a betrayal. Neither description captures the long-term effect: it removed the one internal group capable of challenging him militarily and opened the way for the fiscal and military restructuring that followed.

Yet the end of the Mamluks as a military elite did not mean the end of Mamluk practice. Muhammad Ali's own governing structure relied on household ties and on a Turco-Circassian elite recruited from outside Egypt, many of whom were purchased or brought up in his household. Cambridge scholarship on the period emphasizes this continuity: the new state was built partly out of old materials. The surviving Mamluks who fled to Nubia remained a concern, and their presence in Dongola was one of the pretexts for the conquest of Sudan a decade later (Section 49.17).

Section 49.10 · Division III: Building the Egyptian State

The New Army: Conscription, Training, and Military Institutions

Everything else in Muhammad Ali's state was built around the army, and the army itself was built slowly, through experiments that overlapped rather than succeeded one another. When he took power, his forces consisted of the Albanian and Turkish irregulars who had brought him to office. They were expensive, they mutinied when their pay fell behind, and they were politically dangerous, since the same soldiers who made a governor could unmake him. The Hijaz campaigns (1811–1818) were in part a way of sending such men away from Cairo, but they also taught him how difficult it was to supply, pay, and control an army of this kind. In 1815 an attempt to introduce European-style drill among the existing troops provoked a mutiny in Cairo that forced him to retreat. The lesson he drew was that a reliable army had to be made of men who owed their position to him alone and had no independent power base in Egypt.

The Sudanese slave army, 1819–1821 and after

His first answer was to create a force of enslaved soldiers drawn from the Sudan. Planning for this began about 1819, and it was a leading motive for the conquest described in Section 49.17: the captives taken in Sennar, Kordofan, and the Nile Valley were meant to supply the manpower of a disciplined infantry. Recruits were marched north to a training camp at Aswan, where a former French officer, Joseph Sève, who had converted to Islam and taken the name Sulayman, drilled them in European tactics, and later to a camp at Farshut in Upper Egypt. Some of the new troops were organized into regiments around 1820–1821, and the establishment of schools to train officers, clerks, and instructors for them accompanied the camps. Mortality among the recruits was severe. Many died on the long march, from disease, from the effects of an unfamiliar climate, and from the conditions of confinement and training, and Muhammad Ali and his officers were aware of the problem.

It would nevertheless be wrong to say that this experiment was abandoned. Sudanese slave troops remained an important part of Muhammad Ali's forces, served in later campaigns, and continued to be recruited through the reign, although they never grew to the size he had hoped. The enterprise also had lasting effects on the Sudan itself, because the demand for captives fed slave raiding on a large scale (Section 49.17). What changed was not the existence of the slave army but its place within a larger system, as it became clear that it alone could not supply the numbers the state required.

Peasant recruitment, 1822 onward

In 1822 Muhammad Ali issued orders to recruit Egyptian peasants, an extraordinary step in a province whose peasants had not been armed or drafted for generations. The first drafts, taken from Upper Egypt, were limited in number, and the results were poor: villagers fled, hid, or resisted, and officials had little experience of how to organize recruitment. In 1823–1824 a large rebellion broke out in Upper Egypt, partly in response to conscription and heavy taxation, and was put down with considerable bloodshed. From 1824 onward, however, the state developed a systematic method. Each district was assigned a quota, village shaykhs and officials compiled lists of eligible men, recruits were examined, registered, marched under guard to training camps, and bound to service for long terms, often many years. By the 1830s Egyptian peasants made up the principal part of the infantry, alongside the Sudanese regiments and the Turco-Circassian officer corps. The two recruitment systems therefore overlapped for a decade or more, and the peasant-conscript army grew into the dominant one only gradually.

Officers, training, and the supporting institutions

The officer corps was recruited differently from the rank and file. Senior officers were drawn largely from Muhammad Ali's household and from a Turkish- and Circassian-speaking elite, many of whom had come to Egypt as young men and been raised in service. European instructors, mostly French but also Italian and others, taught drill, artillery, and engineering, and some converted to Islam and took Ottoman names. Egyptian-born men entered the ranks in large numbers but were rarely promoted beyond the lower officer grades in the early decades, a barrier that fostered resentment and shaped the later politics of the army. Schools for infantry, cavalry, artillery, and staff training were created, and a naval academy and fleet were built in Alexandria around the arsenal there, with French naval engineers directing construction.

Around the army grew a network of industrial and logistical institutions. Cannon foundries and small-arms factories at Bulaq and the Citadel supplied weapons, cloth mills provided uniforms, and the Alexandria arsenal built ships. Grain, fodder, and transport animals had to be requisitioned from the countryside, and campaigns far from the Nile required systems of supply and recordkeeping that Egypt had never needed. The medical service was equally important. A military hospital and a medical school were established at Abu Za'bal in 1827 under Clot Bey, and physicians examined recruits, treated wounds and epidemic diseases, and, by the 1830s, carried out vaccination. These developments created state capacity, but they also made the army a heavy burden on the people who paid for it.

Discipline, desertion, and self-mutilation

Discipline was severe. Conscripts were often marched to camp in groups, guarded or bound to prevent flight, paid little, and subject to a military code that prescribed harsh punishments for desertion and disobedience. Villagers fled to towns, to other districts, or into the desert, and families sometimes hid sons from the recruiting parties or paid officials to overlook them. Some young men mutilated themselves to become unfit, cutting off a finger of the hand that pulled the trigger, blinding an eye, or pulling out teeth needed to tear cartridges. The state responded by treating these acts as offenses, examining recruits for signs of intentional injury, and punishing the men and sometimes their families and village officials. Khaled Fahmy has argued that these conflicts show how conscription brought the state into an unprecedented relationship with ordinary bodies, through medical examination, registration, and military law. Whatever one makes of that interpretation, the evidence of flight and self-injury is among the clearest records of how peasants experienced the new order.

The army was the central engine of state-building. It required revenue, and so drove the fiscal reforms of Section 49.11. It required food and animals, and so shaped agriculture. It required weapons, cloth, and ships, and so inspired the industrial projects of Section 49.13. It required trained officers, surgeons, and engineers, and so led to the schools of Section 49.15. And it required an administrative apparatus capable of counting, assigning, and moving large numbers of people, which fed the bureaucratic growth of Section 49.14. Muhammad Ali's regional expansion, treated in Division IV, was in turn the use to which this machine was put.

Historical Vocabulary
Nizam Jadid (New Order)

Nizam Jadid (Ottoman Turkish nizam-i cedid) means "new order" or "new regulation." It was first used for the European-style infantry that Sultan Selim III tried to create in the 1790s, and Muhammad Ali adopted the term for his own disciplined, drilled, uniformed troops. It refers to a model of army, not to a particular kind of recruit.

This distinction matters. Muhammad Ali's earliest "new order" regiments, from about 1820 onward, were composed largely of enslaved Sudanese recruits trained at Aswan and Farshut, and only from 1824 onward did Egyptian peasants become the principal source of manpower. The term therefore marks the contrast between the household and irregular forces of the old order and an army commanded and paid by a state, rather than describing a peasant-conscript force from the outset. Translating it simply as "modernized army" can hide both its Ottoman precedent and the coercion involved in creating it.

Section 49.11

Land, Taxation, and the Centralization of Rural Revenue

An army of this size needed a steady flow of revenue, and in the Egyptian countryside the revenue was held by others. Before Muhammad Ali, much of the agricultural surplus passed through the hands of tax farmers under the iltizam system. A multazim paid the state an agreed sum, kept whatever he could collect above it, and often passed the right to heirs or sold it. Mamluk beys, regimental officers, members of the ulama, and some merchants and women of the elite held such rights, and religious endowments and other assigned lands further reduced what reached the treasury. Muhammad Ali's reforms were a sustained attack on this arrangement, and their chronology matters because the changes came in stages rather than in a single decree.

Surveys, confiscation, and the abolition of iltizam

Land surveys were not new to Egypt; Ottoman administrators had conducted them in earlier centuries, and the French had gathered extensive information during the occupation. After the Citadel massacre of 1811, Muhammad Ali confiscated the holdings of the Mamluks who had been killed or driven out, and by doing so acquired control of large areas of land and revenue, especially in Upper Egypt. The decisive step came in 1813–1814. In 1813 the iltizam system was abolished in Upper Egypt, where the confiscated Mamluk estates were concentrated, and direct taxation was established. In 1814 a cadastral survey of Lower Egypt was carried out, and village registers recorded the cultivable land, its category, and the cultivators responsible for it. The former tax farmers were compensated, at least in part, by pensions rather than being left with nothing, a measure that reduced the risk of organized resistance from a large and influential group.

Direct taxation, villages, and endowments

The new system assigned taxes to villages and, within them, to individual cultivators on the basis of the survey. Taxes were paid in cash and in kind, with grain and other crops delivered to government stores. State officials supervised collection, but they depended on the village shaykhs to identify cultivators, apportion burdens, and enforce delivery. The shaykhs gained authority, and many accumulated land of their own, but their position was also precarious, since officials held them responsible for shortfalls and for the flight of peasants. Endowment lands were affected as well. Muhammad Ali took over or reduced many tax-exempt grants and the lands that supported mosques, schools, and charitable foundations, weakening institutions such as al-Azhar that had relied on them. This helped to explain why the ulama, who had been so important in 1805, lost much of their economic independence.

Grants, estates, and peasant responses

At the same time the regime created a new landed elite. Muhammad Ali and his successors granted estates to members of the ruling household and to senior officials and officers, often on reclaimed or abandoned land, and these grants grew over time into large holdings. In the following decades the rights of holders became more secure and more heritable, a process that contributed to the emergence of private landed property in the countryside and that continued under his successors (Chapter 53). Historians such as Kenneth Cuno have shown that rural society was changing before 1805 and that the new regime built on and altered existing arrangements rather than inventing everything anew.

Peasants responded in many ways. They fled to towns or other districts to escape taxes and corvée labor, concealed land and crops, bribed or negotiated with officials, petitioned, and at times rose in revolt. The state's reach into the countryside was real but uneven: officials were few relative to the population, orders were often evaded, and the system depended on local intermediaries who had interests of their own. The evidence for these developments comes from state registers, village records, court cases, and the accounts of foreign observers, and each type of source reflects the viewpoint of those who produced it.

Historical Vocabulary
Iltizam (Tax Farming)

Iltizam was the Ottoman system under which the right to collect taxes from a district or village was granted to a multazim in return for a payment to the state. In Egypt, multazims were often Mamluk beys, regimental officers, or members of the ulama and merchant classes, and they sometimes passed rights to heirs or sold them.

The system mattered because it kept a large share of rural revenue in private and household hands, which limited what the central state could collect and gave Mamluk households their economic base. Muhammad Ali's transfer of this revenue to state officials was a central act of centralization. Modern "tax collection" understates the way iltizam combined fiscal, social, and sometimes military power.

Section 49.12

Agriculture, Irrigation, and the Cotton Economy

Revenue depended on water, and the relationship between water and crops determined what the state could extract. As Chapter 3 explained, traditional Egyptian agriculture rested on basin irrigation: the Nile's annual flood, arriving in late summer and autumn, was trapped in earthen basins, and once the water drained the fields were planted with winter crops such as wheat, barley, beans, and clover. This produced one main harvest a year and left the land largely unused during the hot summer months, when the river ran low. A system of perennial irrigation, by contrast, used canals dug deep enough to carry water to the fields throughout the year, and in some places lifted by water wheels and other devices, so that summer crops could be grown. Summer crops, among them cotton, sugarcane, rice, and indigo, were also those most valuable on world markets.

Muhammad Ali therefore invested heavily in canals. Existing channels were deepened and extended, and new ones were dug, using corvée labor drawn from villages. The most famous project was the Mahmudiyya Canal, built between 1817 and 1820 to connect Alexandria with the Nile. It gave the port a reliable supply of fresh water and a navigable link to the interior, and was named in honor of Sultan Mahmud II. The labor was enormous, and estimates of the number of workers and of the deaths among them vary widely but agree that the toll was severe. Later projects included the Delta Barrage, begun late in the reign and completed only in the 1860s. Construction of this kind raised the demand for labor in the countryside and added to the burdens already imposed by taxes and conscription.

The crop that came to dominate this system was long-staple cotton. Cotton was not new to Egypt: earlier varieties had been grown for local use, and perennial cotton plants were found in gardens and fields. What was new was the discovery of a variety suited to Europe's growing mechanized textile industry. Around 1819–1821 a French engineer, Louis Alexis Jumel, who was working on Muhammad Ali's textile projects, identified a superior long-staple cotton growing in a garden in Cairo, promoted its cultivation, and demonstrated its commercial potential. Exports to Europe began in the early 1820s and grew quickly. Jumel's role was therefore that of identifying, propagating, and publicizing a valuable variety, not of introducing an entirely new plant.

State direction determined how cotton was grown and sold. Officials assigned land to the crop, supplied seed, and required peasants to deliver the harvest to government stores at set prices. The state then resold it to European merchants, chiefly through Alexandria, at prices closer to those of the international market, and kept the difference. European demand, driven above all by British mills, made the crop highly profitable for the treasury. Over time cotton became Egypt's dominant export and progressively reshaped the agricultural economy, altering land use, the calendar of labor, and the ties between villages and the port.

The consequences for peasants were mixed and mostly harsh. Cotton required a great deal of labor at specific times of the year, and compulsory cultivation, fixed prices, and the obligation to deliver left cultivators with little control over their crops or income. Many tried to avoid the obligation by flight or concealment. Yet the system also integrated Egypt more deeply into world trade, raised the value of land, and strengthened the group of officials, notables, and landholders who benefited from it. Cotton should be seen as one element of a larger system, in which irrigation, land tenure, the army, and the state's role as monopoly buyer and seller reinforced each other.

Section 49.13

Monopolies, Industry, and State-Directed Commerce

The monopoly system, called inhisar, was not a single decision but a process that deepened over about a decade, and its chronology helps explain its purpose. Around 1809–1811, during the Napoleonic Wars, the government began to control exports of grain, which European buyers, especially in the Mediterranean, were eager to obtain, and to buy from producers at prices it set. Rice was brought under similar control around 1812, sugar around 1815, and in 1816 the principle was extended to a wider group of cash crops. By about 1819 the state had acquired a monopoly over most of the country's major export products, with cotton added as its cultivation expanded in the 1820s. Producers were required to deliver their crops to government stores, were paid the official price, and often received less than the market would have paid. The state then sold the produce to merchants, mostly European and in Alexandria, at much higher prices. The profits financed the army.

The monopoly displaced many Egyptian merchants who had handled these goods and left others as agents of the state. It also created friction with Britain and other European states, whose merchants and consuls wanted direct access to producers and objected that the system violated the principle of free trade. This conflict was one of the reasons the British government opposed Muhammad Ali's power, and it shaped the diplomacy of the 1830s.

State industry and military requirements

The state also founded industries, and their purposes were connected closely to military needs. Textile mills, producing cotton and woolen cloth and, to a lesser extent, silk and linen, were established beginning in the late 1810s and expanded in the 1820s and 1830s, in part to clothe the army. Sugar refineries, indigo works, and other processing plants handled export products. Arms factories and foundries produced cannon, muskets, and ammunition, and the Alexandria arsenal built warships. These enterprises were located in Cairo, Alexandria, and provincial towns, often employed European technicians, and relied on imported machinery in some cases. They drew tens of thousands of workers, many of them recruited or held under compulsion and paid low wages. Power came largely from animals and human labor, with steam used only to a limited degree. The state supplied the raw materials, set prices, and was the main customer.

The debate over industrialization

Historians disagree about how to interpret this program. Some have seen it as a genuine, if short-lived, attempt at industrialization that failed because of European pressure and Ottoman constraints. Others emphasize that the factories were sustained by coerced labor, imported expertise, and artificially protected demand, and did not generate a self-sustaining industrial economy; on this view they are better described as a state-directed and highly coercive system of protected production serving military ends. Economic historians such as Roger Owen have stressed how the state tied Egypt to the world market, while more recent quantitative studies have revisited the question with new data. A fair assessment recognizes that the enterprises were real and substantial, that they were organized primarily to supply the state, and that the question of "success" depends on what standard is used.

Balta Liman and the end of the system

The system came under direct challenge in 1838, when Britain and the Ottoman Empire signed the Convention of Balta Liman, which lowered tariffs on British goods and abolished monopolies and other restrictions throughout the empire. Muhammad Ali, who ruled Egypt as a province under the Sultan but resisted Istanbul's authority, refused to accept that the convention applied to him, and for the time being his monopolies remained in force. Its practical effect in Egypt came only with the settlement of 1841, which extended Ottoman laws and treaties to Egypt and thereby opened it to European commerce on the empire's terms. Together with the limits placed on the army, this removed the principal reason for the monopolies, and many of the factories declined in the following years.

Historical Vocabulary
Inhisar (State Monopoly)

Inhisar refers to the state's exclusive control over the purchase and sale of particular goods. In Muhammad Ali's Egypt, producers were required to sell designated crops and goods to the government at prices the government set, and the government resold them. The system covered many agricultural products and, increasingly, manufactured items.

It matters because it was the central tool by which the state captured the profits of the export economy. It also explains European hostility: foreign merchants wanted direct access to producers and objected to the state's pricing. The term should not be confused with modern "nationalization," since the state's aim was fiscal and military rather than ideological.

Section 49.14

Bureaucracy, Administration, and the Reach of the State

To manage an army, an irrigation system, a monopoly economy, and a direct revenue system, Muhammad Ali built a larger administration than Egypt had previously had, and it grew in stages. In the early years he relied on a small group of household officials and on traditional Ottoman diwans, or administrative offices, for finance, the treasury, and other matters. As the state's functions multiplied, the offices multiplied with them. A High Council was established in 1824 to coordinate decisions among senior officials. In 1829 a Consultation Council (majlis al-mashura) was convened that brought together officials, notables, and village leaders to discuss policy and administration, though its role was advisory. These bodies gave the government a measure of institutional memory and a forum in which different branches could report, but none could overrule the pasha.

The most significant reorganization came in 1837, with the regulations known as the Siyasatname. They reorganized the central administration into seven major departments or ministry-like divisions, covering matters such as the interior, finance, war, the navy, education, foreign affairs and commerce, and industry and public works, with each placed under a senior official and expected to follow written procedures and to report. The regulations defined ranks, duties, and lines of reporting, set penalties for neglect and corruption, and created a high council and court of judgment to review administrative matters and hear certain appeals. They also divided the provinces into districts under governors and subordinate officials. The reforms strengthened coordination between departments and gave the state a clearer chain of command than it had had before.

Yet the Siyasatname illustrates the limits of the system as well as its expansion. Ultimate authority remained with Muhammad Ali, who appointed and dismissed officials, confirmed important decisions, and used the new structure as an instrument of his will rather than as a framework that restrained him. Officials were drawn largely from the Turkish-speaking elite and wrote in Turkish, while Arabic was used by the clerks and village intermediaries who staffed the lower levels. Many orders went unenforced, officials took bribes or favored their own households, and the state relied on village shaykhs and local notables to carry out its decisions. New institutions of public order grew up alongside the departments: a more regular police and guard system, and, after the plague of 1834–1835, a quarantine service that tried to control the movement of people and goods. Courts under Islamic law continued to operate alongside the new councils, and registers and surveys, including population counts late in the reign, made society more visible to the state than before. The bureaucracy was therefore an expansion of state capacity without a corresponding expansion of legal limits on state power, and later chapters show how his successors reshaped it.

Section 49.15

Education, Translation, Medicine, and Technical Knowledge

The new army and administration needed trained personnel, and Muhammad Ali created schools to produce them. Early missions sent students to Italy and later, from 1826, a larger group of students to France, accompanied by a religious scholar, Rifa'a al-Tahtawi, as imam. Military and technical schools were founded at Aswan, Qasr al-Ayni, Bulaq, and Abu Za'bal for engineering, artillery, medicine, veterinary science, and other subjects. A medical school opened in 1827 under the French physician Antoine Clot, known as Clot Bey, together with a hospital and, later, a school for midwives. A School of Languages opened in 1835, with Tahtawi as head.

Translation and printing served the same state needs. A state press at Bulaq, founded in the early 1820s, printed military manuals, administrative regulations, and translations of European works on science, medicine, and military affairs. These projects trained a first generation of Egyptians in modern technical professions, and some of its graduates later became important figures in Egyptian intellectual life. Foreign advisers, including engineers and followers of the French Saint-Simonian movement who arrived in the 1830s, also contributed to canal, building, and public works projects.

Yet this education was designed to supply the state, not to educate a population. Students were chosen and supported by the state, instructed in languages unfamiliar to most Egyptians, and expected to serve it. Many schools shrank or closed in the 1840s when budgets contracted. The wider cultural and intellectual changes that later writers called the Nahda, including journalism, literary revival, and new public debate, belong to Chapter 51. Here the point is that the state's need for technical knowledge opened channels through which European ideas and techniques entered Egyptian life.

Section 49.16

Society under Muhammad Ali: Ulama, Copts, Merchants, Peasants, Workers, and Enslaved People

The new order changed the position of every major social group. The ulama of al-Azhar lost income when endowment lands were confiscated and lost political influence after Umar Makram's exile, though they remained important as judges, teachers, and legitimizers, and some, like Hasan al-Attar, supported educational reform. Coptic scribes continued to dominate financial administration, and officials such as Mu'allim Ghali held high posts; Armenian and Levantine Christians, notably Boghos Yusufian, handled commerce and diplomacy. Egyptian merchants, by contrast, were squeezed by monopolies and often reduced to acting as agents of the state or of European firms.

The village shaykhs gained new duties and some new advantages, since they were the state's links to the countryside, and many accumulated land. The peasants bore the heaviest burdens: taxes, compulsory cultivation, corvée labor, and conscription. Workers in state factories and arsenals worked under supervision and discipline, often without freedom to leave. Bedouin communities lost autonomy as the state disarmed and sedentarized them, imposed taxes, and used force against those who resisted. Women's lives were affected indirectly but deeply: households lost men to the army and the canals, and women took on additional agricultural and domestic labor, though evidence about their own experience is sparse because most sources were written by men.

Slavery remained part of the social order. Egyptian households continued to buy and use enslaved people, mostly from Sudan and the Caucasus, and the state itself drew heavily on slave raiding in Sudan for its early armies and workers. Evidence about enslaved people's experience is limited and mostly comes from the records of others. Recent scholarship stresses that any account of the period must include these costs rather than treat Muhammad Ali simply as a modernizer. The reign produced winners, but it did so by extracting labor and resources from many who had little say.

Everyday Life and Social History

A Village under the New Order

For a Delta village in the 1820s or 1830s, the state was no longer a distant presence. Officials decided which crops were planted, how much was due, and when men would be called to dig canals or join the army. The calendar of work was set by the flood and by the cotton harvest, but also by the demands of corvée and by the arrival of recruiting parties. A family's survival could depend on whether a son was taken for the army, whether a shaykh was sympathetic, and whether a harvest was good enough to meet the state's price.

Women worked in fields, tended animals, processed food, and spun thread, and in the absence of men they bore much of the burden of keeping households functioning. Some villagers fled to towns or neighboring districts to avoid taxes or service, a pattern that officials tried to stop with registration and penalties. Epidemics, including the plague of 1834–1835, struck cities and villages alike. Because most surviving sources reflect the perspective of officials and foreign observers, historians must work carefully to recover what such households themselves thought and did.

Section 49.17 · Division IV: Egypt Becomes a Regional Power

Arabia, the Red Sea, and Sudan: From Ottoman Service to Egyptian Expansion

Muhammad Ali's first overseas war began as an Ottoman commission. The Sultan, humiliated by the Saudi capture of Mecca and Medina, ordered him in 1810 to recover the holy cities. Egyptian forces under Tusun, and then Muhammad Ali himself and later Ibrahim Pasha, fought in the Hijaz and Najd between 1811 and 1818 (Chapter 48 narrates the Saudi side). For this chapter, the significance is what the campaigns did for Egypt: they gave Muhammad Ali prestige as defender of the holy cities, exposed his army to hard campaigning and logistical challenges, and gave him control of Hijaz ports and, for a time, a share in the Red Sea trade and the pilgrimage economy. Egyptian garrisons remained in the Hijaz until 1840.

In 1820 he turned to Sudan. His motives were several: to eliminate the Mamluk refuge at Dongola, to find gold and other resources, to control the Nile's upper reaches and trade routes, and above all to obtain enslaved men for his army. An Egyptian force under his son Ismail and a second force under the defterdar Muhammad Bey Khusraw advanced up the Nile and into Kordofan in 1820–1821. The Funj sultanate of Sennar capitulated, and Kordofan was taken after a battle. In late 1822 Ismail was killed at Shendi when the local ruler, Mek Nimr, burned the house in which he was staying, and the defterdar's retaliation devastated the region. Khartoum was established around this time as a military camp and later became the administrative center.

Sudanese societies bore heavy costs. The conquerors imposed taxes on land, water wheels, and livestock, and raided for captives, many of whom died on the march to Egypt or in training camps. The raids and taxes disrupted farming and pastoral economies, and many people fled. Sudan's conquest did bring new routes of trade and, later, a lasting Egyptian administrative presence, but it also created grievances that shaped later history (Chapter 53). Egypt's Red Sea ambitions continued: its forces and agents extended control over ports such as Suakin and Massawa in the following decades, with Ottoman consent.

Section 49.18

Crete, the Morea, and the Egyptian Intervention in the Greek War

When the Greek revolt broke out in 1821, the Ottoman government found its own forces overstretched and its fleet weak. Sultan Mahmud II turned to Muhammad Ali, whose navy and army had been built up since the Arabian campaigns. In return, the Sultan offered rewards, including control of Crete and the Morea (the Peloponnese) for Muhammad Ali's family. Egyptian forces landed in Crete in 1822 and gradually suppressed the revolt there. In February 1825 Ibrahim Pasha landed in the Morea with an Egyptian army and fleet, and over the next two years captured Navarino and other positions and joined Ottoman forces in the siege of Missolonghi, which fell in April 1826.

Egyptian military success in Greece demonstrated that the new army and fleet could operate far from the Nile and defeat irregular opposition. It also alarmed European governments and publics. The Egyptian occupation of the Morea was accompanied by destruction of villages and crops, and European accounts reported plans to remove Greek populations and resettle Egyptians; historians disagree about the extent to which such plans existed and were carried out. Britain, France, and Russia, drawn together by Greek sympathies and strategic interests, concluded a treaty in 1827 calling for Greek autonomy. On 20 October 1827 their combined fleet destroyed the Ottoman–Egyptian fleet at Navarino. Egyptian forces were withdrawn from the Morea in 1828 under European pressure.

The war's consequences were mixed. Egypt lost much of its fleet and many soldiers, and Muhammad Ali discovered that European naval power could stop him. But he had also shown his value to the Sultan and expected his reward. After Greek independence was recognized, he received Crete in 1830, which he considered inadequate. The Sultan's refusal to grant him Syria, which he had also sought, fed a resentment that led to the next war.

Section 49.19 · Division IV

Syria, the Ottoman Empire, and the Crisis of 1831–1841

The conflict between Muhammad Ali and the Ottoman government was the central political crisis of the Egyptian transformation, because it raised a question no earlier Ottoman governor had posed so sharply: how could an imperial province acquire an army and a treasury large enough to defeat the empire itself? The answer lay in the machinery described in the preceding sections, and the crisis tested its limits. This section follows the war and diplomacy in sequence and then examines their regional consequences.

Why Syria?

Muhammad Ali wanted Syria for several reasons. It had timber, iron, and other raw materials that Egypt lacked and that his arsenals and shipyards needed. It was a market and a source of revenue, and its control would give Egypt a strategic buffer and a land route to Anatolia. He also believed he had earned it through service. He had defeated the Saudi state, fought in Crete and the Morea, and lost much of his fleet at Navarino, and the Sultan had given him Crete rather than the Syrian governorship he sought. His resentment was compounded by a specific quarrel. Abdullah Pasha, the governor of Acre, sheltered Egyptian peasants who had fled conscription and taxation, and refused to return them. Muhammad Ali used this as a pretext, and in late 1831 Ibrahim Pasha led an Egyptian army into Syria.

The first war, 1831–1833

Ibrahim besieged Acre, whose fortifications held for about six months before the city fell in May 1832. He then advanced into the interior, entered Damascus in June, defeated Ottoman forces near Homs in July, and took Aleppo, passing the mountain pass of Belen north of Antioch. In December 1832 he won a decisive victory at Konya in central Anatolia, where the Ottoman grand vizier was captured, and his army stood within reach of Istanbul. The Sultan, Mahmud II, appealed for help to the European powers, and when Britain and France were slow to respond, he accepted Russian assistance. A Russian force landed on the Bosphorus in 1833, which alarmed Britain and France and persuaded them to press both sides for a settlement.

The Convention of Kütahya (May 1833) gave Muhammad Ali the governorship of the provinces of Syria, together with the district of Adana, in addition to Egypt, Crete, and the Hijaz, and left Ibrahim in charge as governor. The Sultan remained his nominal sovereign. In July 1833 Russia and the Ottoman Empire signed the Treaty of Hünkâr İskelesi, a defensive alliance that expanded Russian influence at Istanbul and deepened British and French concern about the Ottoman Empire's dependence on a single great power. The agreement ended the first war but settled nothing: the Sultan regarded the loss of Syria as intolerable, and Muhammad Ali regarded the settlement as insecure.

Egyptian rule in Syria

Ibrahim Pasha's government, which lasted from 1832 to 1840, introduced administrative councils in Damascus and other cities, extended equal treatment before the law to Christians and Jews, encouraged trade, and sought to establish order and security. It also imposed heavy taxes, including a head tax, state monopolies on certain products, forced labor, and, above all, conscription and disarmament of the population. Syria's communities responded with resistance. In 1834 peasants in Palestine, led by local notables in the Nablus region, rose in a large revolt that spread to Jerusalem and Hebron and was suppressed with force. Druze communities in the Hawran revolted in 1837–1838 against conscription and disarmament. In 1840 communities in Mount Lebanon, including Maronites and Druze, rose against the Egyptians and their ally, Emir Bashir II Shihab, who had supported Ibrahim and whose rule had become increasingly burdensome. Recent scholarship stresses that Egyptian rule provoked substantial resistance rather than imposing an efficient new order, and that responses varied by region, community, and social position: some merchants and members of religious minorities benefited, while peasants, notables who lost power, and religious leaders often did not.

The second war and the European intervention

The Ottoman government, under Mahmud II and then his successor Abdulmecid, made no secret of its intention to recover Syria. In June 1839 an Ottoman army crossed the Euphrates and was destroyed by Ibrahim at Nizip on 24 June. Mahmud II died within days, and the Ottoman fleet's commander took it to Alexandria and surrendered it to Muhammad Ali. The empire appeared to be on the verge of dissolution, with a teenage new sultan, a shattered army, and no fleet. In July 1839 the five great powers sent a joint note to the Porte urging it not to settle with Muhammad Ali without them, and in November the new government issued the Gülhane edict, a statement of reform principles examined in Chapter 50.

The European powers were divided about what to do. France, whose commercial and cultural influence in Egypt was strong and whose government under Adolphe Thiers in 1840 favored Muhammad Ali, backed his retention of Syria. Britain, under Lord Palmerston, wanted to preserve the Ottoman Empire as a barrier to Russia and to protect routes to India, and regarded Egyptian power with suspicion, partly because of the monopoly system. Russia and Austria feared instability, and Prussia followed. In July 1840 Britain, Russia, Austria, and Prussia concluded the London Convention with the Ottoman government, without France. It offered Muhammad Ali hereditary rule of Egypt and life tenure of southern Syria, including the district of Acre, if he accepted within a fixed period of days, and threatened to restrict him to Egypt if he did not. He refused, counting on French support.

The French support did not materialize. France was unwilling to risk a general war, and Thiers's government fell in October. Meanwhile an Anglo-Austrian fleet, together with Ottoman troops, appeared off the Syrian coast. In September 1840 it landed forces near Beirut, bombarded the city, and aided the Lebanese rebels, and in November it bombarded the fortress of Acre, which fell on 3 November. Ibrahim's army, harried by rebels and cut off from supply by sea, withdrew from Syria through the Sinai in difficult conditions. In late November 1840 the British commodore Charles Napier concluded a convention at Alexandria with Muhammad Ali, under which he agreed to evacuate Syria and return the Ottoman fleet, in exchange for a British assurance that he would retain hereditary rule in Egypt.

The diplomatic settlement of 1840 and the firman of June 1841

It is important to distinguish the successive stages of the settlement. The London Convention and the Alexandria agreement of 1840 were diplomatic arrangements among the powers and with Muhammad Ali, and they promised him hereditary rule. In February 1841 the Sultan issued a first firman restoring him to the governorship of Egypt, and negotiations over its terms, which Muhammad Ali found too restrictive, continued through the spring. The final settlement was the Ottoman firman of 1 June 1841. It confirmed hereditary rule in his male line, according to the Ottoman principle of succession to the eldest male member of the family, and imposed conditions. The Egyptian army was limited to eighteen thousand men in peacetime, the Egyptian governor could not build warships without Ottoman permission, high-ranking officers required imperial approval, a tribute was to be paid, and Ottoman laws and commercial treaties were to apply in Egypt. Syria, Crete, Adana, and the Hijaz returned to Ottoman control, although a separate arrangement left the administration of Sudan in Egyptian hands.

Consequences

The regional consequences were considerable. Ottoman authority returned to Syria in a form complicated by what the Egyptians had done: communities had been armed and disarmed, the balance among Maronites, Druze, and other groups in Mount Lebanon had shifted, and the experience of Egyptian administration, with its councils and its legal equality for non-Muslims, left a mark on later Ottoman reform. The diplomatic consequences were equally important. The crisis established a precedent for collective European action in the Eastern Question, confirmed Britain's role as the guardian of Ottoman integrity, and left France resentful. For Egypt, it ended the possibility of an independent empire while securing a hereditary dynasty. It also showed how quickly the new Egyptian power could be constrained once European navies and diplomacy were brought to bear.

Cause and Effect

The Egyptian–Ottoman Crisis, 1831–1841

Long-term causes

Ottoman weakness and dependence on provincial governors; the growth of a conscript army and fiscal system in Egypt; Muhammad Ali's expectation of reward for service in Arabia and Greece; and European rivalries over the eastern Mediterranean.

Short-term causes

Refusal of the Sultan to grant Syria after the Greek war; the dispute over Egyptian peasants sheltered at Acre; and Egypt's need for timber, revenue, and markets.

Event

Ibrahim Pasha's conquest of Syria (1831–1833), Egyptian rule there, the Ottoman defeat at Nizip (1839), and the European intervention of 1840.

Immediate effects

Withdrawal from Syria, reduction of the army, recognition of hereditary rule, and the extension of Ottoman and European commercial rules to Egypt.

Long-term consequences

A constrained Egyptian dynasty; heightened European involvement in Ottoman and Syrian affairs; and a precedent for collective European action in the Eastern Question.

Historical complexity

The crisis cannot be reduced to Muhammad Ali's ambition: it also reflected Ottoman weakness, Syrian resistance, and the decisions of European governments, each pursuing its own interests.

Section 49.20 · Division V: Synthesis

The Egyptian Transformation: State, Society, Economy, and Regional Power

What actually changed between 1798 and 1848? Egypt acquired a conscript army, a fleet, and a state that could collect revenue directly from villages and direct the production of key crops. It became more closely tied to the world market through cotton, and more tightly governed than before. The old Mamluk military elite was destroyed, and a ruling household of foreign origin, with a hereditary claim recognized by the Sultan, took its place. Egypt's armies operated in Arabia, Sudan, Greece, and Syria, and for a decade Egypt was a rival to the Ottoman state.

What remained? Egypt stayed an Ottoman province, with a governor who paid tribute and held office by imperial decree. Its ruling elite was still largely Turkish-speaking and recruited from outside the country. Existing institutions, including Islamic courts, village shaykhs, religious scholars, and Coptic and other scribes, continued to function, and the new state used them. Peasants still lived by the Nile and by agriculture. The economy, though more commercial, remained overwhelmingly agrarian.

How should Muhammad Ali be understood? He was a modernizer in the sense that he adopted European military and technical methods and created institutions to support them. He was an authoritarian state-builder, whose power rested on compulsion and extraction. He was also an Ottoman provincial magnate who built a household state in the tradition of earlier provincial rulers, and an imperial ruler who conquered and taxed neighboring peoples. These descriptions are not mutually exclusive, and emphasizing one at the expense of the others misleads. Military reform drove economic and administrative reform, since the army required revenue, supplies, and trained men. The beneficiaries included the ruling family, the officer elite, some landholders, and some officials and technicians. The costs fell on peasants, conscripts, workers, Bedouin, enslaved people, and the peoples of conquered lands.

Egyptian power expanded rapidly because Muhammad Ali had a motivated and well-supplied army, access to Egypt's agricultural wealth, and rivals who were divided or weak. It was constrained because the Ottoman state, though weak, still commanded legitimacy, and because European governments would not allow the empire's collapse or the rise of a single power in the eastern Mediterranean. The settlement of 1841 recognized hereditary rule and Egyptian autonomy, but it did not make Egypt independent, did not allow a large army, and opened the economy to European trade. Muhammad Ali's active reign ended around 1848, when illness removed him from government; his son Ibrahim governed briefly and died, and Muhammad Ali died in 1849. The story of his successors and of European involvement belongs to Chapter 53.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Egypt, 1798–1848

What Changed

  • The Mamluk households were destroyed as a military and fiscal power
  • A conscript army, fleet, and arsenals replaced irregular troops and household forces
  • Rural revenue was shifted from tax farmers to state officials
  • Perennial irrigation and long-staple cotton tied Egypt to the world market
  • New schools, translation, and technical professions appeared
  • Egypt held a new regional position, then lost much of it in 1840–1841

What Stayed the Same

  • Ottoman sovereignty and the governor's formal dependence on the Sultan
  • A foreign-origin, Turkish-speaking ruling elite and household-based governance
  • Reliance on village shaykhs, ulama, Coptic scribes, and other intermediaries
  • The Nile as the basis of agriculture and revenue
  • The peasantry's position as the main source of labor and taxes

Who experienced change most directly? Peasants, conscripts, and Mamluk households experienced the sharpest change, the first through new burdens and the last through destruction. Who experienced greater continuity? Many Cairo scholars, scribes, and merchants kept their roles, though on altered terms. Why the pattern matters: It shows that rapid transformation can sit on top of long-standing structures, and that the labels "old" and "new" obscure how the two were combined.

Historical Debate

Muhammad Ali: Founder of Modern Egypt, or Ottoman State-Builder?

The question: Did Muhammad Ali create modern Egypt, or did he build a powerful provincial state out of Ottoman and Mamluk materials for the benefit of his household?

Interpretation A — Founder of modern Egypt: Many Egyptian nationalist writers and some European and American historians, among them Afaf Lutfi al-Sayyid Marsot, have emphasized that he created a centralized state, an army, an educational system, and an export economy, laying foundations for the modern Egyptian state. On this view, his reign marks a decisive break with the past.
Interpretation B — Ottoman provincial ruler: Historians such as Khaled Fahmy and Kenneth Cuno emphasize continuity with Ottoman and Mamluk practice, the dependence of his regime on household ties and local intermediaries, and the human costs of conscription and extraction. They question whether Egyptians in the period understood themselves as members of a nation, and they note that his achievements were built on coercion.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on state registers, court records, chronicles, and foreign accounts. They differ in what they stress: Interpretation A looks at institutions that persisted and at the long-term effects, while Interpretation B looks at how the system operated at the level of villages, barracks, and courts, and at what it cost.

Why it matters: The question affects how modern Egyptian history is told, whether as a story of national awakening or as one of continuity, coercion, and dependency. The two views are better seen as overlapping than as opposed: many historians now accept both that his reign changed Egypt significantly and that it did so through methods and with costs that older accounts tended to minimize.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced the transformation of Egypt from the French invasion of 1798 to the end of Muhammad Ali's active reign around 1848. It argued that the invasion was a shock that destroyed the Mamluk military order and revealed the limits of Ottoman control, and that out of the resulting vacuum emerged a ruler who combined local acclaim, military force, and Ottoman appointment.

The French occupation of 1798–1801 was short but consequential. The French army defeated the Mamluks quickly, but it could not secure legitimacy among the ulama and the population of Cairo, and it faced revolts, financial strain, and the loss of its fleet at the Battle of the Nile. The savants produced a major body of knowledge about Egypt, but they did so as part of an occupying force. British and Ottoman forces ended the occupation in 1801.

Muhammad Ali rose in the confusion that followed. He was one of several contenders and succeeded through alliance-building and the support of Cairene notables and Albanian soldiers. His appointment in 1805 rested on a combination of acclaim and imperial consent, and he moved steadily to reduce the influence of those who had helped him. The massacre of the Mamluk beys in 1811 ended the old military elite and opened their holdings to confiscation.

The core of the chapter examined the construction of the new state. The army, built first from Sudanese slave recruits and then increasingly from conscripted Egyptian peasants, in turn required revenue, supplies, and trained men. The state replaced tax farming with direct collection, dug canals, promoted long-staple cotton, and operated monopolies and factories. It built a larger administration and sent students abroad and founded schools. These changes were interconnected, and all were driven by the needs of the military.

The costs were substantial. Peasants bore conscription, taxation, and corvée labor, and resisted through flight, concealment, and revolt. Workers in state enterprises labored under compulsion. Bedouin communities lost autonomy, and enslaved people were captured and used by the state and by households. The ulama lost resources and political influence, and Egyptian merchants were squeezed. Some groups, including officials, officers, landholders, and technicians, benefited. Any account of the period must include both sides.

The new state was turned outward. Egyptian armies fought in Arabia, conquered Sudan with devastating effects on its peoples, intervened in Crete and the Morea, and, under Ibrahim Pasha, conquered Syria. Egyptian rule in Syria brought administrative change and extensive resistance. The Ottoman defeat at Nizip in 1839 seemed to threaten the empire's survival.

European powers intervened in 1840, largely to preserve the Ottoman Empire and prevent a single power from dominating the eastern Mediterranean. The settlement of 1841 recognized Muhammad Ali's hereditary rule in Egypt but limited his army, ended his Syrian conquests, and opened Egypt to Ottoman and European commercial rules. It confirmed Egypt's autonomy without making it independent.

Historians continue to debate how to characterize the period. Older accounts stressed modernization and national foundation, while more recent work emphasizes continuity with Ottoman and Mamluk practice, the role of coercion, and the social costs. This chapter has treated these views as complementary rather than opposed.

The chapter also showed the regional variety of the experience. Cairo and the Delta, Upper Egypt, Sudan, the Hijaz, Syria, Crete, and the Morea all encountered Egyptian power differently, and their responses shaped its limits. Egypt itself was a society with many layers, and its transformation did not affect them equally.

The period set up the later nineteenth century. It left Egypt with a dynasty, an export economy, a bureaucratic and military apparatus, and a position in European diplomacy. These legacies, and the debt, reform, and imperial pressure that followed, are examined in Chapters 50, 51, and 53.

Chapter Reflection

The period examined in this chapter is often called the beginning of modern Egyptian history, and in some respects it was. A new kind of state appeared, one able to reach into villages, raise a large army, and shape the economy. But the chapter has also shown that this state grew out of crisis, was built through coercion, and depended heavily on existing Ottoman and Mamluk practices. Understanding it requires holding both facts together.

The chapter also illuminates how Egypt connected different regions. Egyptian armies and officials linked the Nile Valley to Arabia, Sudan, the Aegean, and Syria, and Egypt's cotton tied it to Britain and France. These connections were not one-way: Egyptian power shaped the societies it reached, and European powers shaped the limits of Egypt's reach. The history of Egypt in these decades is inseparable from that of the Ottoman Empire and of European diplomacy.

Later developments built directly on this foundation. The Ottoman reforms of Chapter 50 responded to some of the same pressures, and the cultural changes of Chapter 51 drew on the schools, translation work, and press that Muhammad Ali established. The European expansion and imperial rivalry of Chapters 52 and 53 unfolded in a world where Egypt's economy, dynasty, and debts were already tied to Europe. And the experiences of conscription, taxation, and resistance shaped Egyptian social memory.

For students, the chapter's larger lesson is methodological. The same reign can be told as a story of national founding, imperial ambition, or social extraction, depending on whose evidence is used. A careful historian looks at all of these and asks how each fits with the others.
