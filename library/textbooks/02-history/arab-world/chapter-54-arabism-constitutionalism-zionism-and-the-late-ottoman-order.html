Part IX — Reform, Nahda, and European Expansion
54

Arabism, Constitutionalism, Zionism, and the Late Ottoman Order

A History of the Arab World: From Ancient Crossroads to the Modern Arab World

Chapter Opening

In July 1908 a sultan who had governed for more than thirty years without a functioning parliament announced that the constitution he had suspended in 1878 was in force once more. Within weeks, Ottoman subjects from the Balkans to Baghdad were speaking the vocabulary of liberty, equality, and fraternity, and asking what those words would mean in their own provinces. This chapter studies the question that revolution opened: what political future did the empire's Arabic-speaking subjects want, and who else was competing to define it?

Five years later, in June 1913, a group of Arab journalists, students, reformers, and politicians gathered in a hall of the Société de Géographie in Paris. Their resolutions did not demand an independent Arab state. They asked for decentralized provincial government, a stronger Arab voice in the capital, recognition of Arabic as an official language in Arab provinces, and the posting of Arab soldiers near their home districts. Among the observers in the hall were representatives of the Zionist movement, a reminder that Arab reformers, Ottoman politicians, European governments, and a new Jewish national movement were all reading one another's moves.

Between those two moments the Ottoman political world changed profoundly. The Young Turk revolution restored the constitution, opened a new era of elections and party politics, and then drifted, through a counterrevolution in 1909, a lost war in the Balkans, and a coup in 1913, toward the dominance of the Committee of Union and Progress (CUP). Newspapers multiplied, societies formed and were suppressed, and deputies from Damascus, Jerusalem, Baghdad, and Mecca argued in Istanbul about language, taxation, conscription, and the meaning of equal citizenship.

At the same time, other political projects took shape alongside and against Ottoman reform. Arabism moved from a largely cultural movement into associations, petitions, and parliamentary pressure. Islamic reformers argued about the caliphate, despotism, and the sources of legitimate authority. In Palestine, a European-born Zionist movement founded in 1897 began building institutions, buying land, and settling immigrants, while Palestinian Arabs, Ottoman officials, and the long-established Jewish communities responded in different and sometimes divergent ways.

A central argument runs through the chapter. Arab cultural consciousness did not automatically mean Arab separatism. Before 1914, most politically active Arabs who sought change sought it within an Ottoman framework, and different groups meant different things by reform. The Young Turks, too, were not a single bloc, and Islamic reformers disagreed with one another as sharply as they disagreed with secular critics. The chapter therefore avoids reading the post-1918 map backward into the pre-1914 record.

Historians reconstruct this world from varied evidence: the constitution and parliamentary proceedings; Ottoman provincial records and petitions; consular and diplomatic reports; the Arabic, Turkish, French, Hebrew, and other press; the resolutions and correspondence of political societies; memoirs; and the writings of reformers and thinkers. Each source speaks for particular interests, and many were written or recollected after the war, when the outcome seemed more settled than it was. Students will be asked repeatedly to ask who produced a source, for whom, and what it leaves out.

The chapter's boundaries are deliberate. It builds on the administrative reforms of Chapter 50 and the cultural and intellectual ferment of Chapter 51 but treats them as background, and it takes European imperial pressure (Chapters 52 and 53) into account only where it shaped Ottoman loyalties and reform proposals. It stops on the eve of the First World War. The wartime repression of Arab activists, the Arab Revolt, wartime diplomacy, and the collapse of Ottoman authority belong to Chapter 55, and the mandates and later struggles over Palestine to subsequent chapters. As the final chapter of Part IX, it shows the Arab world entering 1914 not with one political program but with several unsettled ones.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain Ottomanism as a political project of common imperial citizenship and trace its relationship to the Tanzimat and the Young Ottomans.
  2. Trace the constitutional experiments of 1876–1878 and 1908–1913 and the changing balance among sultan, parliament, cabinet, and army.
  3. Distinguish cultural Arabism, political Arabism, Ottoman patriotism, regional loyalty, and independence nationalism.
  4. Analyze why the CUP's policies, and perceptions of centralization and Turkification, moved many Arab reformers from support toward opposition.
  5. Compare the aims, membership, and influence of the Arab political societies and the Decentralization Party.
  6. Assess the Arab Congress of 1913 as evidence of what Arab reformers demanded, and evaluate the debate over whether pre-1914 Arabism sought reform or independence.
  7. Contrast the intellectual projects of al-Afghani, Abduh, Rida, and al-Kawakibi, and explain how Islamic reform related to Ottomanism and Arabism.
  8. Describe the administrative, social, and demographic setting of Ottoman Palestine before and during the early Zionist period.
  9. Distinguish long-established Jewish communities from late-nineteenth-century migrants, and differentiate the varieties of Zionism.
  10. Evaluate evidence on land purchase, labor, and local encounters, and on Palestinian Arab and Ottoman responses, without assuming either harmony or inevitable conflict.
  11. Evaluate regional variation among Syria, Palestine, Iraq, the Hijaz, and Yemen in Arab political experience.
  12. Contextualize the competing political futures of 1914 and explain why none was inevitable.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How could an Ottoman subject be Arab, Muslim or Christian, provincial, and loyal to the empire at once, and why did these identities not necessarily conflict?
  2. Why did constitutional government in 1876 and again in 1908 raise hopes so widely, and why did those hopes meet different outcomes?
  3. What changed between the optimism of 1908 and the Arab Congress of 1913?
  4. What did Arab reformers actually demand before 1914, and what does that evidence suggest about the claim that Arabism was already a separatist movement?
  5. How did conditions in Syria, Iraq, the Hijaz, and Yemen make Arab political claims differ from region to region?
  6. How did Islamic reformers connect religious renewal to politics, and why did they disagree about the caliphate?
  7. How should historians evaluate the early interactions between Zionist settlement and the Arab inhabitants of Palestine when sources are uneven and later memory is politically charged?
  8. Why might Ottoman policy toward Jewish immigration have been restrictive on paper but uneven in practice?
  9. In what sense were the political futures imagined before 1914 open, and in what sense were they constrained?

Historical Context

By the 1870s, the Ottoman state had already been transformed by the reforms examined in Chapter 50. The Tanzimat had expanded central bureaucratic authority, reorganized provincial government, promised legal equality to non-Muslim subjects, and created new schools, courts, and communications. The empire's Arab provinces (the Syrian lands, Iraq, the Hijaz and Yemen, and, in a different legal status, Egypt and the North African regencies) had been drawn more tightly into this order, although they differed enormously in how fully Istanbul's authority reached them.

The same decades brought pressure from outside. Territorial losses in the Balkans, the Ottoman Public Debt Administration established in 1881, the British occupation of Egypt in 1882, and French and Italian ambitions in North Africa (Chapters 52 and 53) persuaded many Ottomans that the state's survival required stronger institutions and clearer loyalties. Great powers claimed to protect particular religious communities, and national movements among Balkan Christians showed how imperial loyalty could erode. For Ottoman statesmen the challenge was to bind a multilingual, multireligious population to the state without alienating any large constituency.

Chapter 51 described the Nahda: printing presses, journalism, Arabic school systems, translation, and the revival of classical Arabic. That chapter's cultural developments are assumed here. This chapter asks how some of those ideas became political demands, associations, and claims about sovereignty, and how they competed with other political ideas that were equally modern: imperial citizenship, parliamentary government, Islamic renewal, and Zionism.

A caution about vocabulary is needed from the outset. "Arab" in this period could describe a language community, a cultural heritage, a tribal lineage, a regional population, or a political identity, and a person might invoke several in different settings. The categories "Ottoman," "Muslim," "Arab," and "Syrian" overlapped. The modern states that later claimed these histories had not yet been drawn, and the term "Palestine," though a long-standing geographical and religious designation, did not correspond to a single Ottoman province.

Division I · Section 54.1

The Late Ottoman Empire and the Question of Political Identity

The late Ottoman Empire governed a population divided by language, religion, region, and way of life. Muslims formed the majority overall, but Sunni and Shi'i, Arabic-, Turkish-, Kurdish-, Persian-, Albanian-, Greek-, Armenian-, and Hebrew-speaking communities coexisted with Eastern and Catholic Christians and Jews, and in Arab provinces with Druze, Alawis, Ismailis, Yazidis, and many others. Each was also divided by region, class, and rural or urban setting. The empire's rulers had to secure loyalty across this mosaic while territory was being lost on the Balkan and North African frontiers and European governments claimed to speak for particular communities.

For individuals, identity was layered rather than exclusive. A Damascene Muslim notable might think of himself as Ottoman in his allegiance to the state, Arab in his language and descent, Syrian in his regional attachments, Sunni in his religious community, and a member of a particular quarter and family. A Maronite journalist in Beirut, an Aleppo merchant, a Jewish teacher in Baghdad, and a Bedouin shaykh in the Jazira each combined such affiliations differently. Few felt compelled to choose one to the exclusion of the others before the pressures of the early twentieth century made certain choices more insistent. The chapter's central task is to explain how those pressures arose and what choices people made.

The sources for this complexity are uneven. Official Ottoman documents record how the state classified and addressed its subjects; the press shows what literate urban readers argued about; memoirs reveal how individuals later recalled their choices, often shaped by what happened after 1914. Students should treat late recollection of "Arab nationalist" convictions before the war with particular care, since the postwar political order gave later writers reasons to emphasize some loyalties and forget others.

Division I · Section 54.2

Ottomanism: Equal Citizenship and Imperial Unity

Ottomanism (Osmanlılık) was the proposal that all subjects, regardless of religion or ethnicity, should be bound to the imperial state through shared citizenship. It grew out of the Tanzimat promises of legal equality, discussed in Chapter 50, and was given legal form in the 1869 Ottoman Nationality Law, which defined Ottoman nationality as a status held by all who qualified, rather than by religious community. It was a political idea and not merely an administrative arrangement: its advocates hoped that common rights and obligations could preserve the empire against both European intervention and the national separatism of the Balkans.

The Young Ottomans, a circle of writers and officials active in the 1860s and 1870s that included Namık Kemal, argued that Ottoman reform should be anchored in constitutional government and in Islamic and Ottoman legitimacy rather than copied from Europe, and that representation was the necessary foundation of equal citizenship. Their writings are important for the history of political ideas because they supplied a language, in which parliament, liberty, and the homeland (vatan) were joined to Ottoman and Islamic tradition, that later reformers in Istanbul and the Arab provinces reused.

Ottomanism was never uncontested. Conservative Muslims, particularly in some Arab and Anatolian towns, were uneasy about equality between Muslims and non-Muslims. Some Christian and Jewish leaders valued the promise of equality but were wary of losing communal privileges; Balkan nationalists considered it too late. Ottoman officials themselves disagreed about whether a shared identity should rest on Islam, on the Ottoman dynasty, on the Turkish language of government, or on a deliberately plural civic idea. Because of these tensions, Ottomanism is best understood as a flexible and contested program rather than a single doctrine.

Division I · Section 54.3

The Constitution of 1876 and the First Ottoman Parliament

The constitutional movement reached its first climax in 1876. After a political crisis in which Sultan Abdülaziz was deposed and his successor Murad V quickly proved unable to rule, Abdülhamid II acceded. Midhat Pasha, who had served as governor of Baghdad and was a leading advocate of constitutional government, had pressed for a charter at a time when the empire faced Balkan rebellions, war with Serbia and Montenegro, and a European conference in Istanbul. The Kanun-i Esasi, promulgated on 23 December 1876, declared the empire indivisible, affirmed that all subjects were Ottomans, and established a bicameral parliament: an appointed Senate and an elected Chamber of Deputies.

The charter also preserved wide sultanic authority. It recognized the sultan as caliph and sovereign, entrusted him with appointing and dismissing ministers, and contained a provision that allowed him to banish persons judged dangerous to the state. Elections were indirect and limited, and provincial representation reflected the administrative geography of the empire rather than any principle of national representation. The first parliament met in March 1877, shortly before war with Russia began in April, and deputies from Arab provinces took part. Yusuf Diya al-Khalidi of Jerusalem, a figure we will meet again in §54.28, was among them. The Chamber debated provincial administration and official corruption with surprising frankness, and the sultan, uneasy at its criticism, prorogued it in February 1878 and did not reconvene it.

The episode is a useful corrective to two simple stories. It was not merely a sham, for deputies did speak and the constitution remained, in form, in force. Nor was it a genuine parliamentary regime, because the executive remained in the sultan's hands. Historians generally treat 1876–1878 as a formative precedent: it gave Ottoman and Arab reformers a text, a vocabulary, and a memory to invoke in 1908.

Division I · Section 54.4

Abdülhamid II: Autocracy, Surveillance, and Imperial Legitimacy

After 1878 Abdülhamid governed from Yıldız Palace. Censorship regulated what could be printed, a network of informants reported on officials, officers, students, and notables, and political criticism was driven into exile or underground. The sultan relied on a circle of palace advisers, among them Arab notables and religious figures, and was suspicious of the autonomous power of ministers and of the army's officer corps.

Yet the Hamidian decades were also a period of institution-building. The state expanded secular and religious education from primary schools to professional colleges; telegraph lines, roads, ports, and railways extended administrative reach; the bureaucracy grew and professionalized; and provincial administration was reorganized (Chapter 50). Specific schools such as the tribal school (Aşiret Mektebi) in Istanbul educated the sons of Arab and other tribal leaders in the imperial system, while provincial schools trained a new generation of clerks, teachers, and officers. Greater literacy and better communications, ironically, helped create the educated readers and networks on which later opposition would build.

Students should therefore avoid describing the era as simple stagnation or simple progress. Political repression coexisted with administrative modernization, and what the state built often served purposes its builders did not intend. Many provincial subjects experienced the regime less as an abstract tyranny than as a mixture of taxation, conscription, new schools, and a distant sultan whose religious prestige the palace worked to enhance (§54.19).

Division I · Section 54.5

Arab Provinces, Local Elites, and the Limits of Imperial Integration

Between the sultan in Istanbul and the villagers of Syria, Iraq, or Yemen stood layers of intermediaries. Governors and provincial officials, many of them sent from the capital, depended on local notables (a'yan): landowning families, religious scholars, tax-farmers and merchants, tribal leaders, and the members of municipal and administrative councils created by Tanzimat reform. Those notables mediated between state and society, collecting information, tax, and support in exchange for status, offices, and protection of their interests. In cities such as Damascus, Aleppo, Beirut, Jerusalem, Baghdad, and Basra, family networks held religious, judicial, and municipal positions across generations.

It is important to distinguish among kinds of loyalty. Loyalty to the dynasty and sultan-caliph was often warm, in particular among Sunni religious elites. Support for the Ottoman state as an institution could coexist with strong criticism of particular officials. Attachment to local or provincial interests, such as tax assessments, appointments, trade, or control of a district, often mattered more to notables than any ideological program, and demands for greater political participation arose when these interests seemed threatened by centralization or favoritism. These distinctions explain why constitutional ideas could find a sympathetic audience in the Arab provinces without any intention of leaving the empire.

Integration was uneven. Parts of Syria and Iraq were closely tied to provincial administration, while mountainous, desert, and Red Sea regions (Yemen, Asir, much of Arabia) were governed through negotiation with local rulers and tribes, often backed by uncertain military force. Beyond Ottoman Arab provinces proper, Egypt, under British occupation, and the North African territories were drifting away through European control (Chapters 52 and 53). Awareness of those losses made many Arab Ottomans both more anxious about the empire's fate and more attentive to reform.

Historical Vocabulary
Ottomanism and Constitutionalism

Ottomanism was the political idea that all Ottoman subjects, whatever their religion or ethnicity, should share equal citizenship in a single imperial polity. Constitutionalism was the demand that the powers of the sultan and government be defined and limited by a written charter and an elected representative body.

The two ideas supported each other: representation was the proposed means of making equality real, and common citizenship was the proposed basis of a legitimate parliament. But they are not identical. One could support Ottomanism without parliament, as Hamidian officials sometimes did, or constitutionalism while favoring a particular religious or linguistic group, as some critics feared the CUP did.

Modern English words such as "nationalism" and "citizenship" can mislead here. Ottoman citizenship was a legal status within an empire, not membership in a nation-state, and was compatible with strong communal and regional attachments.

Primary Source Window

The Ottoman Constitution (Kanun-i Esasi), December 1876

Creator: Drafted by a commission under Midhat Pasha and promulgated by Sultan Abdülhamid II · Date: 23 December 1876 · Type: Fundamental law

Context: The constitution was proclaimed at a moment of acute external and internal crisis, with European powers meeting in Istanbul to press for reforms. It was amended in 1909 (§54.9). Several translations exist, and students should consult a reliable edition rather than rely on summaries.

Paraphrase (not a verbatim quotation): The charter declares the empire indivisible and its capital Istanbul; names the sultan as caliph and sovereign; declares Islam the state religion while guaranteeing the free exercise of recognized religions; calls all subjects Ottomans regardless of religion; provides for a Senate and a Chamber of Deputies; makes knowledge of Turkish a requirement for state service; and gives the sultan authority to appoint ministers and, in an article later much criticized, to expel those he judges dangerous to the state.

Analysis: The text shows how a constitution could simultaneously promise equality and preserve autocratic powers. Its provisions on citizenship express the Ottomanist program, while its provisions on the sultan, the language of state service, and exile reveal where authority actually lay. Because the document was issued by the sultan himself, it tells us what the state was prepared to concede, not what all reformers wanted. It also does not tell us how the charter was experienced by ordinary subjects or how its articles were applied.

Questions for students:

  1. What does this source say about the status of non-Muslims and of Islam in the empire?
  2. Who created it, and what pressures might have shaped its drafting?
  3. What can historians learn from the balance between its citizenship articles and its provisions on sultanic power?
  4. What can historians not establish from the text alone about how the constitution worked in practice?
Division II · Section 54.6

Young Turk Opposition and the Committee of Union and Progress

Opposition to Hamidian rule did not begin as an Arab, Turkish, or any other single-community movement. Its earliest nucleus formed around 1889 among students at the Imperial Military Medical School in Istanbul, a cohort drawn from many parts of the empire. Several of the founders came from Kurdish, Albanian, Circassian, and Azerbaijani backgrounds, and the society, later called the Committee of Union and Progress (CUP), recruited among students at other professional colleges. The state's own modern schools, built to produce loyal officers, physicians, and officials, thereby produced a generation that judged the regime by the standards it had taught. Persecution scattered many members to Paris, Geneva, and Cairo, where exile journals, among them organs of Ahmed Rıza and of other emigrés, circulated the case for restoring the constitution and smuggled copies back into the empire.

The exile movement was divided from the beginning. At the Congress of Ottoman Opposition held in Paris in 1902, delegates including Turks, Armenians, Arabs, Albanians, and others disagreed over whether to welcome European pressure on the sultan and over how far the future constitutional state should decentralize. Prince Sabahaddin, a grandson of Sultan Abdülmecid through his mother Seniha Sultan, argued that provincial initiative, private enterprise, and local autonomy were the remedies for imperial weakness, and he was willing to contemplate foreign involvement to force reform. Ahmed Rıza, shaped by positivist ideas, insisted on a strong, centralized, and independent state and rejected foreign intervention. This quarrel between decentralizers and centralizers was not a minor tactical dispute. It anticipated the argument that Arab reformers would pursue with the CUP after 1908, and it shows that decentralization had Turkish as well as Arab advocates.

The movement that actually overthrew the regime had a different base: the officer corps and officials of the Balkan provinces. Macedonia was subject to nationalist insurgency by Bulgarian, Greek, and Serbian bands, to foreign supervision under the reform schemes of 1903, and to the fear that European powers would partition the region. A secret Ottoman Freedom Society formed in Salonika in 1906, and in 1907 it merged with the Paris exiles under the name of the Committee of Union and Progress. Its members included young officers, junior officials, and some civilians, and its structure (cells, oaths, and secrecy) reflected both conspiratorial necessity and the influence of Freemasonry, in which several members participated. It was not a mass party, and it did not claim to speak for the population at large.

Arabs took part in the opposition as exiles, officers, officials, and students, though their numbers within the core Salonika organization were modest and their influence is difficult to measure. Some Arab officers served in Macedonia and moved in Unionist circles. Several Arab intellectuals in Cairo and Paris wrote for opposition journals. Evidence for the movement's inner life comes from the later memoirs of its leaders, from police and diplomatic reports, and from the exile press. All of these are partial: memoirists tended to claim credit or to settle scores after 1908, and the secret police and consular reports often misunderstood the movement. The term "Young Turks" is therefore a European label for a shifting coalition that included Muslims and non-Muslims, centralizers and decentralizers, liberals and authoritarians.

Division II · Section 54.7

The Revolution of July 1908 and the Restoration of the Constitution

The revolution began in Macedonia in July 1908, when officers and their followers, among them Niyazi in Resne and Enver in the hills, took to the mountains or issued ultimatums demanding the restoration of the constitution. The assassination of a Third Army officer sent to investigate the disturbances, and the refusal of troops dispatched from Anatolia to act against their fellow soldiers, convinced the palace that it could no longer count on the army. On 23 July the constitution was proclaimed in Salonika and other Balkan towns, and on 24 July the sultan confirmed its restoration and ordered elections. The speed of the capitulation surprised contemporaries, and many participants themselves later described it as less a conquest than a collapse of the old regime's will to resist.

In the weeks that followed, public celebrations took place in cities from the Balkans to Mesopotamia. In Salonika, Istanbul, Beirut, Damascus, Jerusalem, Baghdad, and other cities, newspapers describe demonstrations at which Muslims, Christians, and Jews, sometimes accompanied by clergy of several faiths, spoke of liberty, equality, and fraternity. Political prisoners were released, spies were denounced, and exiles returned. Censorship was relaxed so abruptly that the number of newspapers and periodicals multiplied within months. These sources should be read as evidence of the hopes the revolution raised, not as a measure of how deeply constitutional principles were understood outside the literate urban population.

Arab reactions varied by region, class, and religion. Reformist journalists and teachers hailed the end of arbitrary rule and often saw in the constitution the realization of ideas long associated with Midhat Pasha. Arab officers and officials hoped that careers would be opened to talent. In Cairo, reformers such as Rashid Rida followed events closely and welcomed the news while remaining watchful. Palace-connected notables and religious figures who had benefited from Hamidian patronage lost influence, and some conservative Muslims were suspicious of a movement they associated with Freemasonry and secularism; in the Hijaz and Yemen the change in regime altered the balance between Ottoman officials and local leaders. In the Hijaz, Sharif Hussein was appointed Emir of Mecca in the aftermath of the revolution, a decision with long-term consequences that belong to §54.18.

The euphoria of 1908 was short-lived. Within weeks of the revolution, the empire suffered the loss of Bulgaria's formal independence and the annexation of Bosnia-Herzegovina by Austria-Hungary, and the union of Crete with Greece was declared. These blows to the new regime strengthened the argument of those who believed the empire needed a strong center. Understanding 1908 requires holding two things together: the real breadth of initial enthusiasm across communities, and the pressures that rapidly narrowed what the new government considered possible.

Division II · Section 54.8

Elections, Parliament, and Arab Political Representation, 1908–1909

The elections held in the autumn of 1908 used the indirect, two-stage system established in 1877, in which male Ottoman subjects who met tax and age qualifications chose secondary electors who in turn chose deputies. Suffrage was restricted, officials and local notables had considerable influence over the process, and the CUP, though not formally a governing party, used its organization to promote favored candidates. The Chamber that assembled in December 1908 had just under three hundred members. According to commonly cited estimates, roughly one-half were Turks, with Arabs forming the second largest group at about sixty, followed by Albanians and Greeks in the twenties, Armenians, Bulgarians, Serbs, Jews, and others in smaller numbers. These counts are approximate, because classification by ethnic group was not an official Ottoman category and deputies' identities were often layered.

The Arab deputies were not a bloc. They included landowning notables, religious scholars, lawyers, journalists, and former officials from Damascus, Aleppo, Hama, Jerusalem, Baghdad, Basra, the Hijaz, Yemen, and other districts, and they were Muslim and Christian, Sunni and Shi'i. Figures such as Ruhi al-Khalidi and Sa'id al-Husayni of Jerusalem and Abd al-Hamid al-Zahrawi of Hama illustrate how different the background of Arab deputies could be. Some aligned with the CUP, some with its opponents, and many shifted positions as issues changed. The existence of an Arab contingent, however, gave the Chamber a recognizable body of members who could be addressed on matters affecting the Arab provinces, and gave the provinces a new sense that Istanbul was accessible by political means.

The proceedings of the Chamber and the press that reported them are among the best evidence for what Arab politicians wanted when they could speak publicly. The recorded themes include the allocation of public works and schools, the conduct of provincial officials, taxation and its collection, security, and the use of Arabic in local administration and instruction. Deputies also pressed on constitutional questions: the powers of governors, the role of provincial councils, and the equal treatment of communities. The records are valuable but not complete. Speeches were reported selectively, deputies often spoke in the cautious language that parliamentary politics rewarded, and most villagers and tribesmen had no direct voice in the process. Nevertheless, the emphasis on concrete administrative grievances rather than separation shows what kind of politics seemed possible in 1908–1909.

Division II · Section 54.9

The Counterrevolution of 1909 and the Transformation of Constitutional Government

The counterrevolution of April 1909 was an uprising of soldiers in Istanbul in the second week of that month, often called the 31 March Incident after the date in the Ottoman fiscal calendar. It followed months of tension between the CUP and critics who accused it of secular tendencies and of encroaching on the sultan, the army, and religious institutions. The assassination of a journalist critical of the Committee heightened tensions, and a mutiny by troops of the First Army, backed by religious students and a group calling itself the Muhammadan Union, led to the forced resignation of the cabinet and demands for the application of Islamic law. Historians still debate how far the sultan encouraged or merely tolerated the rising, and the evidence is contested: the CUP and its allies later presented the episode as proof of the palace's guilt, while Abdülhamid and his defenders denied any role.

Units from Salonika and the Third Army, organized as the Action Army and commanded by Mahmud Şevket Pasha, entered Istanbul on 24 April, and within days the rebellion was suppressed, with executions and martial law following. Parliament, meeting with the support of a fatwa, voted on 27 April to depose Abdülhamid II, and his brother was enthroned as Mehmed V Reşad. During the same weeks, violence in the Adana region against Armenians resulted in massacres in which thousands were killed, reminding all communities of how fragile constitutional equality was. These events are essential background for later Armenian, Arab, and other minority perceptions of the Committee's rule.

The constitutional amendments adopted in August 1909 strengthened parliamentary government on paper: the sultan's power to dissolve the Chamber was restricted, ministers became answerable to the Chamber, and the sultan's right to exile persons without trial was curtailed. At the same time, new laws narrowed political freedoms. The Law of Associations of 1909 prohibited the formation of political associations on the basis of ethnic or national designations, which affected the Arab, Armenian, Greek, Albanian, and other societies founded in 1908–1909 and encouraged secret or differently named organizations (§54.16). Further legislation restricted strikes and the press, and martial law continued in the capital for some time. The result was an unusual combination: a more representative constitutional framework alongside a stronger security apparatus and a political army that had shown it could decide the fate of cabinets and sultans. Arab opinion was divided. Some deputies and journalists saw the suppression of the rising as the defeat of reaction; others warned that the new restrictions were unconstitutional and anticipated greater centralization.

Division II · Section 54.10

Parties, Political Journalism, Opposition, and the Struggle over Centralization

After 1908, politics became organized around parties, societies, and newspapers. The CUP, though initially reluctant to become a formal party, increasingly acted as one. Opposition took shape in groups such as the Liberal Union (Ahrar) formed in 1908, in the Moderate Liberal and other parties, and finally in the Freedom and Accord Party (Hürriyet ve İtilaf) founded in November 1911, which gathered liberals, decentralizers, conservatives, and some national minority politicians whose only strong common ground was hostility to Unionist methods. In the Chamber and the provinces, Arab deputies and journalists were found on both sides, and they changed alliances as events unfolded. It is therefore misleading to speak of an Arab party before 1913.

Newspapers were the principal arena. In Damascus, Muhammad Kurd Ali's al-Muqtabas became an influential voice for reform and later for Arab rights; in Beirut, journals such as al-Mufid and Lisan al-Hal debated administrative reform; Cairo's al-Muayyad, al-Manar, and al-Ahram reached readers across the Ottoman Arab provinces; and papers in Baghdad, Basra, Jerusalem, Jaffa, and Haifa joined in. These Arabic papers carried on a war of words with Turkish-language Unionist organs such as Tanin. Armenian, Greek, Ladino, Hebrew, and other presses likewise pursued their own communities' concerns. Press laws, suspensions, and prosecutions followed, particularly after 1909.

The issues that recurred were concrete: whether Turkish alone would serve as the language of administration and higher education; how far provincial offices would be open to local people; how conscription and military postings would be organized; and whether Unionist appointments favored Turkish speakers and loyalty to the Committee over experience. Perceived "Turkification" (tatrik) became a charge in Arab journalism, though historians debate how consistently the CUP pursued such a policy before 1914, since Unionists also used Islam and Ottomanism as unifying languages and cooperated with Arab notables. The press shows what the literate, urban, politically engaged minority argued about; it cannot speak for peasants, nomads, or women who did not read, and circulations were small even if papers were passed from hand to hand and read aloud.

Division II · Section 54.11

The Crises of 1912–1913 and the Consolidation of CUP Power

The years 1911–1913 delivered a succession of blows to the Ottoman state and to the credibility of Ottomanism. Italy's invasion of Tripolitania in 1911 (Chapter 53) stirred sympathy among many Arabs for fellow Muslims under attack, and Arab volunteers and donations were part of the response. Albanian revolts in 1910–1912 and then the Balkan League's attack in October 1912 produced military defeat in Europe, the loss of nearly all remaining Ottoman territory in Rumelia, and a flood of Muslim refugees into Anatolia and Istanbul. The Balkan War ended the fiction that the empire could be held together by civic Ottomanism among Muslims and Christians in Europe, and it increased the strategic and symbolic importance of the Anatolian and Arab provinces.

Domestic politics became more bitter. The elections of spring 1912, widely called the "big stick" elections because of accusations of Unionist pressure and fraud, secured the CUP a large majority, but military unrest and opposition mobilization led to the dissolution of parliament and the appointment of an opposition-backed cabinet in the summer. On 23 January 1913, during the Balkan crisis, a group of Unionist officers led by Enver stormed the Sublime Porte, where the cabinet was meeting; the grand vizier was forced to resign and the Minister of War was killed. Mahmud Şevket Pasha became grand vizier, and after his assassination in June 1913 the government passed to Said Halim Pasha as grand vizier while real authority shifted to a circle of Unionist leaders, including Talat, Enver, and Cemal. The constitution formally remained in force, but the political space available to opposition parties and critical newspapers narrowed.

For the Arab provinces, the CUP's thinking combined centralization with pragmatism. Some Unionists believed that a more uniform administration, standardized schooling, and a larger role for Turkish were necessary to resist foreign domination and revive the army. Others recognized that Arab notables and officials were indispensable partners. A new provincial administration law in 1913 expanded provincial councils on paper, and in the summer of 1913 the CUP negotiated with Arab reformers (§54.17). Historians therefore distinguish the Committee's centralizing aims from the often limited policy that could be implemented across distant provinces with limited resources. The relationship between the CUP and Arab reformers remained negotiable, strained, and unresolved when war came, and it would be a mistake to describe it as a story of inevitable rupture.

Division III · Section 54.12

From Arabic Cultural Revival to Arab Political Consciousness

The Nahda (Chapter 51) had revived classical Arabic, expanded Arabic printing and schooling, and encouraged interest in Arab history and literature. Institutions such as the Syrian Protestant College (founded in 1866 and later the American University of Beirut) and the Jesuit Université Saint-Joseph (1875), together with Ottoman, communal, and private schools, trained a literate class that read and wrote Arabic in new ways. Writers such as Butrus al-Bustani, who used the concept of a Syrian homeland (watan) in the 1860s, and Jurji Zaydan, whose popular historical novels and journal al-Hilal made Arab and Islamic history accessible to a wide public, helped create a shared cultural vocabulary. None of this by itself constituted a political program. Historians therefore distinguish several things that are easily blurred: cultural Arabism, pride in the Arabic language and heritage; political Arabism, the demand that Arabs as a group receive recognition, rights, or representation; Ottoman patriotism, loyalty to the imperial state and dynasty; regional loyalty to Syria, Iraq, Egypt, or a city; and independence nationalism, the demand for a sovereign Arab state or states.

How cultural revival became political debate is one of the most contested questions in the historiography. An older account, associated especially with George Antonius's The Arab Awakening (1938), traced an almost continuous line from the Nahda and from episodes such as the anonymous placards posted in Beirut and Damascus in 1880 to the Arab Revolt. More recent historians, among them Hasan Kayalı, Rashid Khalidi, and Eliezer Tauber, have questioned that teleology. They have pointed to the small size of the early societies, the lack of evidence for the authorship and influence of episodes such as the 1880 placards, and the persistence of Ottoman and Islamic loyalties. They have also emphasized that Arab political identity took shape within, and partly in response to, the Ottoman constitutional order, rather than outside it.

Cultural revival fed political debate in specific ways. Arguments about the Arab contribution to Islamic civilization, about Arabic as a language capable of expressing modern science and administration, and about the standing of the Arabs in an empire whose official language was Turkish provided the vocabulary of later claims. Arabic was already the language of primary education and religious instruction in most Arab provinces, while Turkish was required for higher state service. After 1908, as the press expanded and parliamentary politics made language an issue of public rights, these background conditions made the use of Arabic in schools, courts, and administration a political matter. The move from cultural to political Arabism was therefore gradual and uneven, and it was shaped above all by the events of 1908–1913 and by fears of Turkification. Most cultural Arabists before 1914 did not conclude that Arabs should leave the empire.

Division III · Section 54.13

Early Arab Political Thinkers and Competing Visions of the Nation

Najib Azoury is the figure most often cited for an early explicitly political Arab program, and he deserves careful treatment. Born in the Syrian lands in the early 1870s into a Christian family, he served in the Ottoman administration in Jerusalem before leaving for Europe around 1904. In Paris he founded a Ligue de la Patrie Arabe, published Le Réveil de la nation arabe dans l'Asie turque in 1905, and in 1907–1908 edited a journal called L'Indépendance arabe. The book argued that an Arab national awakening was under way in the Asian provinces of the empire, called for the separation of spiritual and temporal authority, and sketched an Arab state in the lands between the Tigris and Euphrates valleys and the Suez isthmus, with a spiritual leadership in the Hijaz. It also warned that Arab and Jewish national movements in the region would come into conflict.

Historians disagree about how much weight to give Azoury. For some, he was an early articulator of Arab nationalism whose ideas anticipated later developments. For others, he was a marginal figure with little organized following in the Arab provinces, whose works were read mainly in French and in European circles and whose financing and motives have been questioned. What is clear is that his program was one among several, that he was an outlier in calling for separation from the empire at that date, and that most reformers in Damascus, Beirut, Baghdad, and Cairo neither adopted nor endorsed it. Treating him as the voice of "the Arab nation" in 1905 would project a later outcome backward.

Other thinkers and activists advanced very different proposals. Sulayman al-Bustani, an Ottoman Christian from Mount Lebanon who sat as a deputy for Beirut in 1908, argued in the aftermath of the revolution for the unity of the Ottoman state and the equality of its citizens. Muhammad Kurd Ali in Damascus, Rafiq al-Azm and Muhibb al-Din al-Khatib in Cairo, and the Islamic reformers discussed in §§54.20–54.22 argued for different mixtures of constitutional reform, Arabic language rights, provincial autonomy, and religious renewal. Some stressed Arab distinctiveness within a continuing empire, some envisioned autonomy for particular provinces, and some foregrounded Muslim solidarity. Reading them as stages on a single path to independence misrepresents the options perceived at the time, and students should compare what each thinker actually asked for rather than assuming that a shared interest in "Arab" themes implied a shared goal.

Division III · Section 54.14

The Arab Political Press and Transregional Networks

Newspapers, petitions, letters, student associations, and emigrant communities connected Damascus, Beirut, Jerusalem, Baghdad, Cairo, Istanbul, Paris, and other centers. The telegraph allowed provincial petitioners to appeal directly to the capital and journalists to report events within hours; steamships and, increasingly, railways carried newspapers, students, and merchants; and the Ottoman post and customs authorities attempted, with imperfect success, to censor what circulated. After 1908 a growing proportion of the Arabic press shifted from cultural and literary topics to explicitly political questions of administrative rights, language, representation, and relations with the CUP. This distinction between cultural publishing and political journalism is important for historians, who must decide whether a given paper was intended to educate, to entertain, to sell, or to mobilize opinion.

Beyond the empire's borders, Syrian and Lebanese emigrants formed a transregional public. Hundreds of thousands, by common estimates, left the Syrian lands between the 1860s and 1914, settling in Egypt, the Americas, and elsewhere, where they founded newspapers and clubs that fed ideas and funds back to the homeland. Students in Istanbul and Paris formed their own associations. Cairo occupied a distinctive position. Under British occupation yet intellectually connected to the Ottoman Arab world, it offered Arab and Ottoman activists, including the Syrian reformers who had moved there, a place to publish and organize outside direct Ottoman administration. Papers such as al-Muayyad and al-Manar reached readers in the Arab provinces, and the Decentralization Party formed there in 1912. At the same time, Cairo's press was subject to British influence and to the surveillance of Egyptian authorities, and the city also hosted rival currents, including supporters of the British administration and Egyptian nationalists.

Paris offered a different setting. Its relative freedom and the presence of Arab students allowed al-Fatat and later the 1913 Congress to organize and to address French opinion, but this very contact exposed activists to charges of seeking foreign backing, a charge Ottoman authorities exploited. Historians draw on a wide range of evidence to reconstruct these networks: surviving issues of newspapers, Ottoman and foreign police and consular reports, the minutes and correspondence of societies, and memoirs such as those of Izzat Darwaza. Each type has a bias. Police reports tend to exaggerate conspiracy, memoirs often magnify early commitments, and newspapers speak for particular editors and readerships.

Division III · Section 54.15

Arab Deputies, Provincial Representation, and the Politics of Language

After 1908, Arab deputies and activists generally turned to concrete administrative questions. Should Arabic be used in primary and secondary schools and in local courts and administration? Should Arab officials be appointed in Arab provinces, and should Arab soldiers serve nearer home? How should provincial councils and governors share authority, and how should taxation and public works be allocated? What did equality within the empire mean when Turkish was the language of the central government, of the army's command, and of most higher state schools, and when the senior bureaucracy was disproportionately Turkish-speaking? These were not abstract issues. They affected the careers of educated Arabs, the dignity of the local languages and elites, and the daily dealings of ordinary people with courts and tax collectors.

The linguistic question had several layers. Arabic was already the language of instruction in most primary schools and of religious learning in the Arab provinces, but it had little standing in secondary and higher state schools and in the central administration. Arabic-speaking litigants complained that proceedings and documents in a language they did not know disadvantaged them. In the Chamber, deputies pressed for the recognition of Arabic. The demand was cultural but also practical: it affected who could qualify for official posts and who was excluded from them. Conscription added another grievance. Arab conscripts were often posted to distant fronts, notably to Yemen and the Balkans, and the system of paying an exemption fee favored wealthier families; reformers asked that service be performed in home provinces except in emergencies.

A provincial administration law of 1913 enlarged the competence of provincial general councils on paper, but reformers judged its implementation insufficient, and the debates on it illustrate how decentralization could be framed in legal terms within the existing constitutional order. For most reformers these questions mattered more immediately than independence. They could be addressed by legislation and negotiation, and they connected directly to the livelihoods and status of notables and officials. They also gave Arab Ottomans allies: Armenians, Greeks, Albanians, and others had parallel grievances, and the Arab Congress itself expressed solidarity with Armenian reform demands (§54.17). To understand why the Arab movement before 1914 looked so much like a movement for administrative rights, students should keep in mind the concreteness of these grievances.

Division III · Section 54.16

Arab Political Societies: Brotherhood, al-Fatat, al-Ahd, and Related Networks

A number of organizations emerged between 1908 and 1914, though accounts differ on some founding dates and details. The Arab-Ottoman Brotherhood Society (Istanbul, 1908) sought cooperation between Arabs and Turks within the constitutional framework and was closed in 1909. Al-Muntada al-Adabi, the Literary Club in Istanbul, functioned as a cultural and social meeting place for Arab students and officials. Al-Qahtaniyya, formed in Istanbul in about 1909 by Arab officers and intellectuals, promoted a dual Turkish-Arab monarchy on the Austro-Hungarian model, but was reportedly dissolved by the authorities soon after. Al-Fatat, the Young Arab Society, emerged from a Paris-based student group established in 1911, initially known as the Society of Speakers of Ḍād (the Arabic letter traditionally associated with the Arabic language). Some later accounts trace its beginnings to 1909. The secret society moved its headquarters to Beirut in 1913 and Damascus in 1914 and had limited membership. Al-Ahd, associated with the Ottoman Arab officer Aziz Ali al-Misri, drew largely on Arab officers in the army and was established in 1913 (its formation is sometimes dated to 1914 in later accounts). The Ottoman Party for Administrative Decentralization (§54.17) was public and reformist.

These groups differed in public or secret character, membership, goals, and reach. Some were literary clubs, some reformist parties, some conspiratorial circles of a few dozen members. Their members did not share a unified revolutionary program, and several organizations aimed at reform inside the empire. Historians such as Eliezer Tauber have stressed how small and varied many of these societies were, and later nationalist accounts often exaggerated their size and coherence. The surviving evidence comes largely from memoirs and later testimony, so claims about membership and intentions require caution.

Division III · Section 54.17

Decentralization, the Beirut Reform Movement, and the Arab Congress of 1913

The Ottoman Party for Administrative Decentralization was founded in Cairo in December 1912, with Rafiq al-Azm as president and a membership including Rashid Rida and Muhibb al-Din al-Khatib. Its program called for provincial administrative autonomy within the empire, use of local languages, and greater participation of local people in government. In Beirut, a reform society formed in the winter of 1912–1913 around prominent Muslim and Christian notables, including figures from the Beirut municipal and commercial elites, and published a reform program for the vilayet of Beirut, including a larger role for Arabic and local councils. The authorities closed it in 1913. Reform societies also appeared elsewhere, among them Basra, where reform proposals reflected local concerns.

In early 1913, leaders of al-Fatat proposed a congress of Arab reform societies in Paris. The Decentralization Party agreed to participate, and the First Arab Congress met at the Société de Géographie with formal sessions on 18–23 June 1913 (some chronologies give the wider span as 17–24 June). It had some two dozen official delegates, with a Muslim and Christian membership drawn mainly from Ottoman Syria, and it was chaired by Abd al-Hamid al-Zahrawi. Observers included Zionist representatives. Its resolutions were modest and reformist: administrative decentralization in the Arab provinces; effective Arab participation in the central administration; recognition of Arabic in the Chamber of Deputies and as an official language in Arab provinces; military service in home provinces except in extreme necessity; and "radical and urgent" reform generally. It also expressed solidarity with Armenian demands for reform.

The Ottoman response combined condemnation and negotiation. Provincial officials organized counter-petitions of loyalty from notables. In July 1913, representatives of the Congress negotiated in Istanbul with CUP leaders, who agreed in principle to some Arab demands on Arabic in schools and on Arab employment, while decentralization itself was largely sidestepped; the concessions were poorly implemented, and accounts differ about whether they were deliberately withdrawn. The Congress should not be misrepresented as a declaration of independence. Its public platform sought equality and reform within an Ottoman framework, and the negotiations show that both sides saw a bargain as possible.

Division III · Section 54.18

Regional Political Experiences: Syria, Iraq, the Hijaz, and Yemen

Arab political claims differed sharply by region, and the same label "Arab provinces" concealed very different conditions. Syria, understood in the Ottoman sense to include Damascus, Aleppo, Beirut, and (with qualifications) Palestine, had the densest network of reform societies, newspapers, and active notables, and was the main source of delegates at Paris. Within it, cities differed. Damascus combined a conservative religious establishment with a circle of reformist scholars and journalists; Beirut, with its mixed Muslim and Christian merchant elites, its schools, and its press, was a center of reform politics; Aleppo and the interior towns had their own notable families and concerns. Mount Lebanon had been governed since 1861 as a special autonomous district under a Christian governor appointed with European approval, and Lebanese Christians were divided over Ottoman reform, Arab identity, and European, especially French, protection. The Beirut Reform Society, in which Muslim and Christian notables participated, demanded reforms for the Beirut province in 1913.

Iraq (Baghdad, Basra, and Mosul) faced different circumstances. Its population included Sunni and Shi'i Arabs, Kurds, Turkmen, Jews, Christians, and tribal confederations, and the Shi'i holy cities of Najaf and Karbala were centers of religious authority with ties to Iran, where a constitutional revolution had unfolded between 1905 and 1911. Basra notables such as Sayyid Talib al-Naqib pursued local interests, including relations with the British in the Gulf, and a reform society in Basra in 1913 reflected local demands for autonomy. Iraqi deputies and local societies engaged with the debate on decentralization, but Iraqi politics was distinct from Syrian politics and did not simply follow it. Studies of the region stress the importance of tribal, sectarian, and commercial divisions and of the Ottoman government's concern with the Gulf and with British influence.

In the Hijaz, Sharif Hussein, appointed Emir of Mecca in 1908, held a distinctive religious and dynastic position. His relations with the Ottoman government were shaped by disputes over the authority of the Ottoman governor in Hijaz, the autonomy of the holy cities, taxation, and the railway, whose planned extension to Mecca threatened the income of camel owners and the Sharif's own influence. His sons Abdullah and Faisal sat as deputies in the Ottoman Chamber, which shows that his family was integrated into imperial politics even as tensions developed. Evidence on the relationship comes largely from Ottoman and British records and later memoirs, which require careful comparison. In Yemen, Ottoman rule had faced recurring rebellion under the Zaydi imam; the agreement reached with Imam Yahya at Da'an in 1911 recognized his authority in much of the highlands, including over Zaydi legal and religious institutions, in return for nominal Ottoman suzerainty. In Asir, the Idrisi and the Ottomans competed for influence, and the Italian war of 1911–1912 added a Red Sea dimension.

These regions had different relationships with Istanbul, different social structures, and different goals. Treating the "Arab provinces" as a single political constituency obscures that many Arab communities, including Shi'i and Christian communities and tribal groups, had interests that were not captured by Arabist programs. It also shows why the Arab Congress, drawn largely from Syria and the Decentralization Party in Cairo, could not claim to speak for every Arab Ottoman.

Historical Vocabulary
Arabism and Decentralization

Arabism ('urūba) in this period covered a spectrum from pride in the Arabic language and heritage to demands for Arab political rights, and only for some activists a demand for sovereignty. Decentralization (lā-markaziyya) meant transferring administrative powers from Istanbul to provincial bodies, without necessarily leaving the empire.

The modern word "nationalism" can obscure these distinctions. The same person might support Arabism, decentralization, Ottoman loyalty, and Islamic reform at the same time. Conversely, Turkification was a charge made by critics against CUP policy; historians debate how consistently the CUP pursued it before 1914.

Historical Debate

Did Arabism before 1914 Primarily Seek Reform within the Empire or Independence from It?

The question: Was the Arab movement before the war primarily a movement for reform and equality inside the Ottoman Empire, or already a movement aimed at separation?

Interpretation A — Reform within the empire: Many historians argue that most politically active Arabs sought reform, decentralization, language rights, and representation, and that separatist demands were held by a small minority until the war and its repression changed conditions. They point to the public platform of the 1913 Congress and to the continuing negotiations with the CUP.
Interpretation B — Growing separatist current: Others emphasize secret societies, early writings such as Azoury's, and contacts with foreign powers as evidence that some activists were already contemplating independence, and see the open reformist language as partly tactical.

Evidence: Both depend on a limited body of sources: public resolutions and press, Ottoman and foreign reports, and memoirs often written after 1918, when independence had become a political reality and writers had reasons to emphasize early commitment.

Why it matters: The answer affects how one understands the war: whether Arab politics was transformed by wartime events, including repression and diplomacy (Chapter 55), or merely revealed an existing separatism. Most current scholarship stresses a plurality of positions, with reformist views predominant before 1914, rather than two clean camps.

Student task: Identify what additional evidence, such as private correspondence or membership records, would help distinguish tactical moderation from sincere reformism, and explain the limits of memoirs as evidence.

Division IV · Section 54.19

The Ottoman Caliphate, Pan-Islamism, and Imperial Solidarity

Abdülhamid II gave new emphasis to the Ottoman sultan's title of caliph. The claim was not new: Ottoman rulers had used the title for centuries, and the treaty of Küçük Kaynarca in 1774 had been interpreted as recognizing a religious authority over Muslims outside Ottoman territory. What changed in the later nineteenth century was the systematic use of this authority as an instrument of policy. Facing European expansion into Muslim lands, from the French in Algeria and Tunisia to the British in Egypt and India and the Russians in Central Asia, the palace presented the sultan-caliph as the protector of Islam's holy places and, rhetorically, of Muslims everywhere. This was a response to a real political need, since many Muslim communities under colonial rule looked to the Ottoman sultan, and since the claim strengthened the sultan's standing among his own Muslim subjects.

The program had several instruments. The palace patronized Sufi shaykhs and scholars, including figures such as Abu al-Huda al-Sayyadi of Aleppo and Shaykh Zafir al-Madani, who became intermediaries between the court and Arab religious networks. It supported mosques, religious schools, and publications, sent emissaries and cultivated contacts with Muslim communities abroad, and used the language of Islamic solidarity in diplomacy and in education. Pan-Islamism in this usage was a political strategy of solidarity centered on the Ottoman ruler, useful against European pressure and against secessionist tendencies at home. It was not a single ideology. Reformers and conservatives, Arabs and Turks, interpreted it differently, and there is continuing debate among historians about how far it was a coherent policy as opposed to a flexible rhetoric adapted to circumstances.

The Hijaz Railway shows these elements working together. Announced in 1900 and financed substantially by contributions from Muslims across the world, supplemented by Ottoman taxes and by the labor of army units, the line from Damascus reached Medina by 1908, a distance of more than a thousand kilometers. German engineers played a major role in its construction. It eased the pilgrimage, strengthened Ottoman communications and military reach, and symbolized imperial care for the holy cities. It also altered local economies. Camel owners and Bedouin groups who had profited from transporting pilgrims lost income, and tribal attacks on the line occurred. The planned extension to Mecca was never completed, partly because of technical and financial constraints and partly because the Sharif of Mecca opposed measures that would reduce his autonomy.

For Arab provinces, the caliphate remained a powerful source of legitimacy, which is one reason many Arabs framed reform as renewal within Islam and empire rather than separation. Yet pan-Islamism also had critics. Reformers such as al-Kawakibi saw the Hamidian use of religion as a prop of despotism, and the emphasis on Islam sat awkwardly with the Ottomanist promise of equality for non-Muslims. After 1908 the CUP, despite its secular reputation, continued to use Islamic symbols and language. The evidence for the effectiveness of these policies is mixed: palace records, the Islamic press, and the memoirs of religious figures show considerable engagement, but they cannot easily measure the loyalty of ordinary Muslims.

Division IV · Section 54.20

Jamal al-Din al-Afghani, Muhammad Abduh, and the Politics of Islamic Reform

Jamal al-Din al-Afghani (1838/39–1897) was an itinerant political thinker whose life is difficult to reconstruct, in part because he cultivated his own image. Although he presented himself as an Afghan, scholars have concluded that he was born in Iran and educated in a Shi'i environment, and that he concealed this background to appeal to Sunni audiences. He traveled through Iran, India, Egypt, Europe, and the Ottoman lands. In Egypt in the 1870s he gathered disciples, including the young Muhammad Abduh, and urged resistance to foreign domination and to despotism; he was expelled in 1879. In India he wrote a critique of materialist philosophy. In Paris in 1883 he engaged in a public exchange with Ernest Renan over Islam and science, and in 1884, with Abduh, he published the journal al-'Urwa al-Wuthqa, which called for Muslim unity against European encroachment and was banned in Egypt and India. After involvement in Iranian politics, including the opposition to the tobacco concession of 1891, he was invited to Istanbul in 1892 and lived there under the sultan's observation until his death in 1897, shortly after the assassination of the Iranian shah by a man who claimed to be his follower.

Muhammad Abduh (1849–1905) was a different kind of figure. Educated at al-Azhar, he participated in the Egyptian political upheavals of 1881–1882 and was exiled, spending several years in Beirut, where he taught and wrote and influenced Syrian scholars. After his return to Egypt he served as a judge and, from 1899, as Grand Mufti, and he worked to reform religious education at al-Azhar, the administration of religious endowments, and the court system. His theological writings, such as Risalat al-Tawhid, argued for the compatibility of reason and revelation, for reopening independent interpretation (ijtihad) rather than relying on inherited commentary, and for returning to the example of the early Muslim community (the salaf). He cooperated with the British-dominated administration on practical reforms, which his critics regarded as accommodation to colonial rule and his defenders as pragmatism.

The two men should not be merged. Al-Afghani's emphasis was on political agitation, anti-imperial unity among Muslim states, and personal networks of activists. Abduh's was on gradual educational and religious renewal, the legal and institutional reform of Islamic learning, and a more cautious attitude toward political activism. Both inspired followers who disagreed about the Ottoman state, the caliphate, and cooperation with Europeans. Their influence reached Arab reformers across the region: in Damascus, scholars such as Tahir al-Jaza'iri and Jamal al-Din al-Qasimi were associated with a reform-minded current often called the Damascene Salafi movement, and in Cairo Abduh's circle shaped the intellectual world of Rashid Rida. The term "Salafi" in this period refers to a modernist movement of renewal and should not be confused with later uses of the label. For sources, historians rely on the writings of both men, on the periodicals they produced, on letters, and on the accounts of disciples, which sometimes portray them as more coherent than they were.

Division IV · Section 54.21

Rashid Rida, al-Manar, and Islamic Renewal

Muhammad Rashid Rida (1865–1935) was born near Tripoli, in the Syrian lands, and received a traditional religious education, including exposure to Sufi practice, before being drawn to reformist ideas by reading al-'Urwa al-Wuthqa. He moved to Cairo in 1897, became a disciple of Muhammad Abduh, and founded the journal al-Manar in 1898. It appeared for decades and became one of the most influential Islamic periodicals, reaching readers from the Ottoman provinces to South and Southeast Asia and combining legal opinions (fatwas), theological arguments, commentary on current events, and a Qur'anic commentary that grew out of Abduh's lectures. Through al-Manar Rida presented Abduh's ideas but also developed his own, with a stronger emphasis on the authority of the early community and on the dangers of both Westernization and rigid traditionalism.

Before 1914 Rida's politics were those of an Arab Muslim reformer who looked to Ottoman Islam but criticized Ottoman despotism. He denounced the Hamidian regime and welcomed the constitution of 1908, hoping it would permit religious and educational reform. As the CUP moved toward centralization, he grew disillusioned, and he became one of the founders of the Ottoman Party for Administrative Decentralization in Cairo in 1912 and a supporter of Arab reform demands. In the same period he established a school and society in Cairo, the Society of Call and Guidance, intended to train missionaries and scholars, and he sought Ottoman support for it with limited success. These activities show that he combined religious reform, a commitment to the unity of Muslims, and a concern for Arab representation within the empire.

Interpretation must remain centered on the prewar period. Rida's later treatise on the caliphate, written after the First World War and the abolition of the Ottoman sultanate, reflects postwar conditions and cannot be used to project a fully developed position back onto his early career. Before 1914 he did not call for the abolition of the Ottoman caliphate or for the separation of the Arab provinces; he argued for reform of the Ottoman state and for a greater role for Arabs and for religious scholars. The principal sources are al-Manar itself, his correspondence, and his later memoirs, which should be read with attention to date, since he revised his positions in response to events.

Division IV · Section 54.22

Abd al-Rahman al-Kawakibi and Competing Ideas of Islamic and Arab Political Authority

Abd al-Rahman al-Kawakibi (1855–1902) was a journalist, jurist, and Ottoman official from Aleppo. He edited an early local newspaper, held positions in the provincial administration, and came into conflict with the authorities. After arrests and lawsuits he left for Egypt around 1899, where he published his work in the Cairo press and reportedly traveled. He died in Cairo in 1902, and later accounts attributed his death to poisoning by Hamidian agents, a claim that cannot be verified from reliable evidence. His two major works were Tabā'i' al-istibdād (The Nature of Despotism), published in installments in 1900 and later as a book, and Umm al-Qura, published in 1899–1900 in al-Manar and later as a book.

Tabā'i' al-istibdād analyzes despotism as a social and psychological system: how tyranny corrupts religion, undermines knowledge, distorts the economy, and degrades the morality of rulers and subjects. It does not name the Ottoman sultan in every passage, but contemporary readers, and later scholars, have understood it as a critique of Hamidian rule and of the wider political culture. Umm al-Qura takes the form of the proceedings of an imagined congress of Muslim delegates meeting in Mecca to diagnose the decline of the Muslim world and propose reform. In some readings it advocates a spiritual caliphate vested in a member of the Quraysh tribe, with the caliph's authority confined to religious matters, which would separate religious leadership from the Ottoman sultanate.

Historians caution against reading al-Kawakibi as a straightforward Arab nationalist. His critique of despotism and his emphasis on the reform of Muslim societies were central, and he addressed Muslims at large; scholars differ over the extent to which he envisaged an Arab political entity, and over whether the Arab caliph he proposed was a practical program or a rhetorical device. After his death he was claimed by different currents, including Arab nationalists, Islamic reformers, and critics of authoritarian rule, which means that later interpretations must be distinguished from his own writing.

Comparing al-Kawakibi with al-Afghani, Abduh, Rida, and Azoury shows how reformers differed over the Ottoman caliphate. The sultan-caliph's supporters considered it the legitimate center of Muslim unity. Abduh was less engaged with the question of the caliphate than with education and law. Rida before 1914 sought reform of the existing Ottoman institution. Al-Kawakibi, in one reading, proposed an Arab spiritual caliphate, and Azoury a separate Arab political state. Differences also concerned the sources of legitimate authority, the role of religious scholars, and the relationship between Arab identity and Muslim solidarity. Islamic reform, Ottomanism, and Arabism were not necessarily mutually exclusive, but their adherents could disagree profoundly.

Division V · Section 54.23

Ottoman Palestine before Political Zionism: Society, Land, and Administration

This division concerns political movements that later became central to the modern Middle East. The aim is to reconstruct the perspectives of Jewish immigrants, long-established Jewish communities, Palestinian Arab residents, Ottoman officials, and organizations without projecting the later conflict back onto the nineteenth century.

Palestine was a recognizable geographical, historical, and religious region, but not a single Ottoman province with the boundaries of the later British Mandate. After a brief period in 1872 when Jerusalem was made a province, it was reconstituted by 1874 as an independent district (mutasarrifate) answerable directly to Istanbul, while the northern districts of Nablus and Acre belonged to the province of Beirut after the 1888 reorganization, and other areas were attached to neighboring provinces. The population was predominantly Muslim, with significant Christian communities (Greek Orthodox, Latin, Greek Catholic, Armenian, and others) and a Jewish minority. Population estimates for the late Ottoman period are uncertain, but around 1900 the total is commonly put at roughly six hundred thousand, with Muslims forming a large majority, Christians about a tenth, and Jews a smaller share concentrated in Jerusalem and a few towns. In Jerusalem itself, Jews formed the largest single religious community by the later nineteenth century, according to most estimates.

The region had distinctive urban centers. Jerusalem was a religious and administrative city whose economy depended partly on pilgrimage; Jaffa was the principal port and the center of a citrus industry exporting to Europe, linked to Jerusalem by a railway built in 1892; Haifa grew after the construction of a railway link to the Hijaz line; Nablus was known for soap and cotton; Gaza exported grain; Hebron, Acre, Safed, and Tiberias had their own trades and communities. The countryside held villages of cultivators, many of them poor and indebted, as well as semi-nomadic and Bedouin groups. A system of village headmen (mukhtars), tax farming, and the collection of tithes tied villagers to the state and to local notables.

Land was governed by the Ottoman Land Code of 1858 and subsequent legislation. Much land was classified as state-owned land whose cultivation rights could be held by individuals, and many villages used a system of communal tenure (musha') in which fields were periodically redistributed among households. Registration of title (tapu) was often avoided by peasants who feared taxation and conscription, which allowed notables, moneylenders, and absentee landowners to have land registered in their names, sometimes without the cultivators' knowledge. Notable families such as the Husaynis, Khalidis, Nashashibis, and Dajanis held municipal, religious, and judicial offices, and villagers and townspeople petitioned the central government in growing numbers. Historians use Ottoman tax and land registers, shari'a court records, consular reports, travel accounts, and the local press to reconstruct this society, and each has limits. Registers are concerned with taxation, consuls with foreign interests, and travelers with what they expected to find.

Division V · Section 54.24

Jewish Communities, European Antisemitism, and the Origins of Modern Zionism

Jews had lived in Palestine continuously, with communities in Jerusalem, Safed, Tiberias, Hebron, and Jaffa whose members included Sephardi and Mizrahi families long resident in the region and Ashkenazi newcomers, many dependent on charitable support from abroad known as haluka. This "Old Yishuv" was largely religious in orientation, was not a national political movement, and many of its members were wary of Zionism. Jews also lived across the Ottoman Empire, in Salonika, Istanbul, Izmir, Baghdad, and Aleppo, where communities had their own languages, including Ladino and Judeo-Arabic, and their own political positions. Many were Ottomanist, and the chief rabbi of the empire after 1909, Haim Nahum, opposed political Zionism and supported Ottoman unity.

Modern Zionism arose from the situation of Jews in Europe. Persecution in the Russian Empire, including the pogroms of 1881–1882 and the restrictive May Laws, rising political antisemitism in Central and Western Europe, and the example of other national movements led some Jews to conclude that emancipation had failed or was insufficient. Leon Pinsker's Auto-Emancipation (1882) argued for Jewish self-determination, and the Hovevei Zion societies encouraged settlement in Palestine, as did a pioneering group of young students known as Bilu. In Western Europe, the Dreyfus affair in France in the 1890s deeply affected Theodor Herzl. Attachment to Zion also had a long religious dimension, expressed in liturgy and pilgrimage, which different currents interpreted differently.

Zionism was always plural. Religious Zionists, who organized as Mizrachi in 1902, sought to combine settlement with observance. Cultural Zionists, among them Ahad Ha'am, stressed a Jewish cultural and spiritual revival in Palestine and criticized political Zionism's diplomacy. Political Zionists, led by Herzl, sought an internationally recognized charter. Labor Zionists combined socialism with settlement. Many Jews rejected Zionism altogether, preferring emancipation within existing states, socialism and the Bund, assimilation, or religious anti-Zionism, which organized in groups such as Agudat Israel from 1912. Early immigrants also included Yemenite Jews, who began arriving in the early 1880s for their own reasons. Jewish opinion was never uniform, and Jewish communities should not be portrayed as uniformly Zionist.

Division V · Section 54.25

Theodor Herzl, the Zionist Congress of 1897, and Political Organization

Theodor Herzl, a Viennese journalist, published Der Judenstaat in 1896, arguing that antisemitism could not be cured by assimilation and that the "Jewish Question" required a political solution through a Jewish state. His thinking had been affected by the Dreyfus affair, which he covered as a correspondent in Paris. Within a year he convened the First Zionist Congress in Basel, held in August 1897 after plans to meet in Munich were abandoned. Roughly two hundred delegates attended. The Congress founded the World Zionist Organization and adopted the Basel Program, which declared the movement's aim to be a home for the Jewish people in Palestine secured under public law. In a diary entry afterward Herzl wrote that he had founded the Jewish state in Basel, a remark that illustrates the movement's aspirations rather than its achievements.

Institutions followed: the Jewish Colonial Trust (1899) as a financial instrument, the Jewish National Fund (1901) to purchase land held in trust for the Jewish people, and the Anglo-Palestine Company, which operated as a bank in Jaffa. Herzl pursued diplomacy with the Ottoman sultan, the German emperor, and British and other officials. He visited Istanbul in 1896 and again met Sultan Abdülhamid II in 1901, offering assistance with the Ottoman public debt in exchange for a charter permitting Jewish settlement in Palestine. The sultan rejected the proposal, though he indicated willingness to accept Jewish refugees in other parts of the empire. Later negotiations explored territory in the Sinai peninsula, which came to nothing, and in 1903 a British offer concerning East Africa, which was debated at the Sixth Congress and later rejected. Herzl died in 1904.

The movement then divided over strategy. "Political" Zionists emphasized the quest for a charter. "Practical" Zionists favored settlement and institution-building in Palestine regardless of formal permission, and by 1907 a "synthetic" Zionism associated with Chaim Weizmann sought to combine both. A Zionist office in Istanbul, led for years by Victor Jacobson, lobbied Ottoman officials and politicians after 1908. Students should distinguish the aspirations and language of the movement from what it had accomplished by 1914: it had an international organization, financial institutions, and a growing settlement infrastructure, but no charter, no sovereignty, and a small share of Palestine's population. Primary sources include the proceedings of the congresses, Herzl's diaries and letters, and Ottoman and diplomatic archives, which read the movement differently from its own publications.

Division V · Section 54.26

Jewish Immigration, Agricultural Colonies, and the Development of the Yishuv

The First Aliyah, conventionally dated 1882–1903, brought immigrants, mainly from the Russian Empire and Romania, who founded agricultural settlements such as Rishon LeZion and Zikhron Ya'akov, building on earlier colonies like Petah Tikva, established by members of the Old Yishuv in 1878, and Mikveh Israel, an agricultural school founded in 1870 by the Alliance Israélite Universelle. Many colonies relied on the philanthropy of Baron Edmond de Rothschild, whose administrators supervised them closely and promoted vineyards and wine, a policy that bred dependency and conflict with the settlers. Estimates suggest the Jewish population rose from about 25,000 to somewhat over 50,000 during this period. Settlers struggled with unfamiliar farming conditions, malaria, and finances, and many colonies relied on Arab labor. Not all immigrants were Zionists; some migrated for religious or economic reasons, and some later left.

The Second Aliyah (1904–1914) brought younger immigrants, many influenced by socialist ideas, in the aftermath of the failed Russian revolution of 1905. Estimates are uncertain, but tens of thousands arrived and a substantial proportion left again. They built labor organizations such as Ha-Poel Ha-Tza'ir and Poalei Zion, defense groups such as Hashomer, founded in 1909, and cooperative settlements, including Degania, founded in 1910. Tel Aviv began as a Jewish suburb of Jaffa in 1909. The Palestine Office, established in Jaffa in 1908 under Arthur Ruppin, coordinated land purchase and settlement and worked with the Jewish National Fund. Institutions such as the Herzliya Gymnasium (1905), the Bezalel art school (1906), and Hebrew-language newspapers formed a growing cultural infrastructure. Hebrew was revived as a spoken language, helped by figures such as Eliezer Ben-Yehuda and by Hebrew-language schools; a "language war" in 1913 over the language of instruction at the Technion in Haifa showed that cultural questions remained contested.

The Yishuv was divided by ideology, language, origin, and religious observance. Ashkenazi and Sephardi, Old and New Yishuv, religious and secular, labor and farm-owning groups all had different interests, and relations with Yemenite immigrants raised questions of labor and status. Some Zionists adopted an Ottoman orientation. David Ben-Gurion and Yitzhak Ben-Zvi, for instance, studied law in Istanbul in 1912–1914 in the hope of working for Ottoman citizenship and a Jewish role within the empire. By 1914 estimates of the Jewish population range roughly from 60,000 to 85,000 within a total population of several hundred thousand. These figures are approximate and come from varied sources.

Division V · Section 54.27

Land Purchase, Rural Communities, Labor, and Local Encounters

Land acquisition was central to Zionist settlement, and the evidence shows a complicated picture. Ottoman restrictions on land sales to foreign Jews were issued from the 1880s onward, but enforcement varied and transactions were often conducted through intermediaries, local notables, or by changing the nominal buyer. Many sellers were absentee landowners, such as wealthy families based in Beirut, or local notables and moneylenders who held registered title to land that peasants cultivated; others were Palestinian landowners selling for reasons of debt or profit. By 1914 land owned by Jews has been estimated at on the order of two percent of Palestine's area, a small fraction of the whole but concentrated in particular districts. Buyers included the Jewish National Fund, individuals, and companies such as the Palestine Land Development Company founded by Ruppin.

When a registered owner sold, tenant cultivators without title could lose access to land they had worked for generations, sometimes with compensation and sometimes without. The best-documented example is the transaction at al-Fula in the Marj Ibn Amir (Jezreel) in 1910, when land belonging to the Sursock family of Beirut was sold for Jewish settlement. Attempts to take possession and displace tenant cultivators met local resistance; the disputed transaction provoked press coverage and, in 1911, a parliamentary intervention by Ruhi al-Khalidi concerning Zionism. Registered-title claims could support demands for land transfer, but villagers and Ottoman officials contested the validity and enforcement of particular transactions. The gap between registered title and cultivators’ rights was a central issue of the period.

Labor questions further shaped local relations. Early colonies employed Arab workers, including seasonal laborers from the Hauran and local villagers, and Arab guards. Second Aliyah labor Zionists increasingly advocated hiring Jewish labor (the "conquest of labor"), a policy whose consequences were debated within Zionism itself, while some farmers preferred cheaper Arab or Yemenite workers. Different settlements adopted varied practices. Gershon Shafir's research on land and labor has emphasized how these strategies shaped the early relationship of settlers and the existing population, and Yuval Ben-Bassat's study of petitions shows how villagers used the Ottoman bureaucracy to protest land and administrative issues, with the central government often responding.

Claims of uninterrupted harmony or uninterrupted hostility are both unsupported. There was trade, employment, and everyday cooperation, and there were also disputes over grazing, crops, and guarding, violent incidents, and political protest. Because many sources were written by officials, settlers, or notables, the voices of villagers appear mainly through petitions and later testimony, which means historians must triangulate Ottoman archives, Zionist records, consular reports, and the press.

Division V · Section 54.28

Palestinian Arab Responses, Ottoman Petitions, and Political Journalism

Palestinian Arab responses developed through several channels. As early as the 1890s notables in Jerusalem petitioned the central government to stop Jewish immigration and land sales, and villagers and townspeople sent petitions to Istanbul complaining of land sales, evictions, and the activities of settlers. Local officials reported tensions, and the press carried disputes. Yusuf Diya al-Khalidi, a former deputy and mayor of Jerusalem, wrote in March 1899 to the chief rabbi of France, Zadok Kahn, for transmission to Herzl, recognizing Jewish historical ties and the legitimacy of Jewish suffering but warning that a Jewish political project in Palestine would meet resistance from the population and the Ottoman state, and urging that Palestine be left alone. Herzl replied on 19 March 1899, assuring him that non-Jewish inhabitants would not be expelled and would benefit. Historians read the exchange differently: some see an early prescient Palestinian warning that revealed Zionist assumptions about the local population, others stress its limited immediate effect.

The press expressed growing concern after 1908. Najib Nassar's al-Karmil, founded in Haifa in 1908, and Filastin, founded in Jaffa in 1911 by Isa al-Isa and Yusuf al-Isa, criticized Zionist land purchase and its implications, and Nassar published a book on Zionism around 1911. In the Ottoman parliament deputies such as Ruhi al-Khalidi and Sa'id al-Husayni raised the matter. The Arab Orthodox movement, in which lay Arab Christians challenged the Greek-dominated hierarchy of the Orthodox church, also shaped the political and cultural world in which Palestinian Christian journalists wrote. These expressions combined Ottoman citizenship, Arab political debate, local attachment, and opposition to aspects of Zionist settlement.

Responses within the Zionist movement were also varied. Ahad Ha'am had warned as early as 1891 about the treatment of the Arab population, and in 1907 Yitzhak Epstein published an essay arguing that the "hidden question" of the Arab inhabitants had been neglected. Such voices were not decisive in shaping policy, but they show that Zionists were not uniform in their attitudes. Neither a fully formed mass nationalist movement nor an absence of meaningful opposition should be assumed for the period before 1914: Palestinian political consciousness was emerging within an Ottoman setting, and its later forms cannot be read back as complete.

Division V · Section 54.29

Ottoman Policy toward Zionism and Palestine on the Eve of War

Ottoman policy began restrictively. From 1882 the government limited Jewish immigration and land purchase in Palestine, concerned about foreign intervention, nationalist aims, and the empire's experience with Balkan separatism. Regulations restricted the entry of Jewish pilgrims and settlers, imposed time limits on visits, and sought to prevent Russian Jews in particular from settling, while Abdülhamid rejected Herzl's proposals and remained open to Jewish refugees settling elsewhere in the empire. Enforcement, however, was inconsistent. Foreign consuls, acting under the capitulations, protected their nationals, bribery and loopholes such as naturalization undermined restrictions, and provincial officials in Jerusalem and Beirut sometimes interpreted instructions differently from the capital. Scholars have therefore described Ottoman policy as marked by contradictions, a mix of strict regulation and weak implementation, with important differences between what was decided in Istanbul and what occurred on the ground.

After 1908 the constitutional government faced pressure from several directions. Arab deputies and the Arabic press pressed for the enforcement of restrictions, while Zionist organizations lobbied in Istanbul, and Jewish Ottoman communities and politicians argued for the loyalty of Ottoman Jews. Some Unionists distrusted Zionist political aims; others believed Jewish capital and skills could benefit the empire. Zionist leaders adopted a cautious Ottomanist stance in public, and the Arabic press often alleged that Zionists exerted undue influence over the Istanbul press and politics, an accusation that is difficult to substantiate. By 1914 the central government had tightened some regulations on foreign Jewish entry and purchase, but they remained unevenly enforced.

By 1914 the Zionist organization had built institutions but remained dependent on Ottoman tolerance and external support; Palestinian Arab political networks had begun to organize opposition through petitions, the press, and parliament; and the existing communities, Muslim, Christian, and Jewish, were affected in varied ways. The Balfour Declaration of 1917, British occupation, and the mandate are outside this chapter, and students should not read them back as foreordained. Instead, the evidence shows a situation in which several outcomes remained possible and in which Ottoman policy, local politics, and international diplomacy all continued to shape events.

Historical Vocabulary
Zionism and the Yishuv

Zionism was the modern Jewish national movement that sought a national home or state for Jews in Palestine; it took religious, cultural, political, and labor forms. The Yishuv (Hebrew for "settlement") denotes the Jewish community of Palestine; the "Old Yishuv" was the pre-Zionist religious community and the "New Yishuv" the settlers of the Aliyot. An Aliyah ("ascent") is a wave of Jewish immigration to Palestine.

Not every Jew in Palestine was a Zionist, and not every Jewish political position was Zionist. Because the word "Zionism" carries strong associations today, students should use it with the historical specificity of the period.

Everyday Life and Social History

Four Ways of Living through the Late Ottoman Order

An urban voter in Damascus or Beirut in 1908 or 1912 might have been a landowner, merchant, or professional enfranchised under a restrictive indirect system, reading the press in coffeehouses and following disputes over appointments and language. A newspaper reader in a town such as Jaffa or Haifa learned of debates over land and immigration through a press with growing circulation, often read aloud to those who could not read.

A rural Palestinian petitioner, usually the voice of a village community, might dictate a petition through a local scribe, appealing to Istanbul against an eviction, a tax assessment, or a dispute over land; the document survives because officials recorded it. A Jewish immigrant might be an Eastern European farmer in a colony, a worker in a cooperative, or a religious scholar in Safed or Jerusalem, and each lived differently, facing disease, financial insecurity, and an unfamiliar environment. Women's labor in households, farms, and workshops across all communities is poorly documented in the political sources and must be reconstructed from other evidence.

Division VI · Section 54.30

Ottomanism, Arabism, Islamic Reform, and Zionism: Political Futures before 1914

By 1914 five major frameworks competed to define the political future of the Ottoman Arab lands. They differed in their constituencies, in the institutions they proposed, and in the amount of influence they had. Ottomanism and constitutionalism had the backing of the state and of the CUP but were strained by centralization, war losses, and disputes over language and appointments. Arabism had a growing press, societies, and parliamentary presence but remained mostly reformist and limited in organized strength. Islamic reform and pan-Islamism had wide religious appeal and intellectual prestige but gave different answers about the caliphate and authority. Zionism had a developing organization and settlement but was a small minority in Palestine, dependent on Ottoman and international conditions. Palestinian Arab opposition was emerging through petitions, press, and parliament.

These frameworks were not all mutually exclusive. An individual could support Ottoman constitutionalism and Arab language rights, or Islamic reform and decentralization. Others conflicted directly, especially over Palestine. Their relative strength also varied by region and by social group. In 1914 none of the futures was settled, and the empire's collapse, the wartime repression and diplomacy of Chapter 55, and the postwar settlements were not inevitable. The war transformed a field of open possibilities, and the following chapters examine how.

Chapter Summary

This chapter examined how Ottoman subjects and emerging political movements imagined different futures for the Arab provinces between 1876 and 1914, and why demands for imperial reform, decentralization, religious renewal, national recognition, and territorial settlement competed before the First World War.

Ottomanism offered common citizenship as a basis for imperial unity, building on the Tanzimat, the Nationality Law of 1869, and the Young Ottomans. The constitution of 1876 and the first parliament, suspended in 1878, established a precedent in which promised equality coexisted with strong sultanic powers. Under Abdülhamid II, political repression coexisted with schools, communications, and a bureaucracy that created new literate publics.

In the Arab provinces, local notables, officials, religious authorities, and tribal leaders negotiated power with the state. Loyalty to the dynasty, to the state, to provincial interests, and to political participation were distinct, which explains why constitutional ideas could be welcomed without any thought of secession.

The Young Turk revolution of 1908 restored the constitution and raised hopes across the empire, including among Arabs. The counterrevolution of 1909 and subsequent amendments shifted power toward cabinet, army, and the CUP. The Balkan Wars and the 1913 coup consolidated CUP rule, and perceived centralization and Turkification pushed many Arab reformers from enthusiasm toward organized pressure, although negotiations with the CUP continued.

Arabism developed from cultural revival into political claims over language, appointments, representation, and military service. Thinkers such as Azoury offered distinct programs, and societies such as al-Fatat, al-Qahtaniyya, and al-Ahd varied in size and aims. The Decentralization Party and the Arab Congress of 1913 are best understood as reformist rather than separatist, though historians debate the balance of views.

Regional differences were significant. Syria hosted the densest reform networks; Iraq had its own concerns; the Hijaz involved Sharif Hussein's distinct position and the Hijaz Railway; Yemen saw negotiation and conflict between Istanbul and local rulers. The Arab provinces were never a single constituency.

Islamic reform offered another set of answers. Pan-Islamism supported imperial solidarity, while al-Afghani, Abduh, Rida, and al-Kawakibi differed on political activism, education, the caliphate, and authority. Islamic reform, Ottomanism, and Arabism were not necessarily exclusive.

In Palestine, Ottoman administrative geography, land law, and society formed the setting for the Zionist movement's emergence from European antisemitism and nationalism. Jewish immigration, agricultural colonies, and institutions grew, while Palestinian Arabs responded through petitions, the press, and parliament, and Ottoman policy combined restriction with weak enforcement. The evidence supports neither a story of simple harmony nor one of a fully formed conflict.

The chapter's central conclusion is that in 1914 several political futures remained open. These frameworks differed in strength and sometimes overlapped. The war, treated in Chapter 55, would transform the conditions in which they competed.

Key Terms

Ottomanism
The proposal that all subjects share equal citizenship in the Ottoman state regardless of religion or ethnicity.
Tanzimat
The era of Ottoman legal and administrative reform from 1839, discussed in Chapter 50.
Young Ottomans
Writers and officials of the 1860s–70s who argued for constitutional government rooted in Ottoman and Islamic tradition.
Kanun-i Esasi
The Ottoman constitution of 23 December 1876, amended in 1909.
Midhat Pasha
Reforming statesman and principal architect of the 1876 constitution.
Hamidian regime
The rule of Abdülhamid II (1876–1909), marked by palace government, censorship, and expansion of schools and communications.
Notables (a'yan)
Locally influential families who mediated between provincial society and the state.
Young Turks
The loose coalition of opponents of Hamidian rule, including the CUP, whose composition and aims varied.
Committee of Union and Progress (CUP)
The organization that led the 1908 revolution and dominated government after 1913.
Centralization
Concentration of administrative authority in the capital, favored by many Unionists.
Decentralization
Devolution of administrative powers to provinces, the central demand of Arab reformers.
Turkification
The accusation that the CUP privileged Turkish language and officials; its extent before 1914 is debated.
Counterrevolution of 1909
The April 1909 mutiny in Istanbul, suppressed by the Action Army and leading to Abdülhamid's deposition.
Freedom and Accord Party
Opposition party formed in 1911 against CUP dominance.
Law of Associations (1909)
Ottoman statute restricting association by ethnic or national designation after the constitutional revolution.
Arabism
A spectrum of ideas from cultural pride in Arabic to demands for Arab political rights or sovereignty.
Ottoman Party for Administrative Decentralization
Reform party founded in Cairo in 1912.
Al-Fatat
Secret Arab student society formally founded in Paris in 1911, with antecedents sometimes dated to 1909, that helped organize the 1913 Congress.
Al-Ahd
Society of Arab officers associated with Aziz Ali al-Misri, dated 1913 or 1914.
Arab Congress of 1913
Paris meeting of Arab reformers that issued reformist resolutions on decentralization, Arabic, and representation.
Pan-Islamism
Strategy and ideology of Muslim solidarity, used by Abdülhamid II to bolster imperial loyalty.
Caliphate
Institution of leadership of the Muslim community, claimed by the Ottoman sultans.
Hijaz Railway
Damascus–Medina line completed in 1908.
Islamic reform (Salafi modernism)
Movement of Afghani, Abduh, and Rida seeking religious renewal in response to modern conditions; the label "Salafi" here differs from later usages.
Dar al-Da‘wa wa-l-Irshad
Rashid Rida’s Cairo school for training religious guides and preachers, active from 1912.
Mutasarrifate
Administrative district headed by a mutasarrif; Jerusalem became an independent district by 1874 following a short-lived provincial arrangement in 1872.
Tapu
Ottoman land title registration under the Land Code of 1858.
Musha‘
Forms of village landholding in which cultivation shares could be distributed or periodically reallocated among households.
Petition
Formal appeal to Ottoman authorities, an important channel for rural and urban voices.
Zionism
Modern Jewish national movement seeking a national home in Palestine, in religious, cultural, political, and labor forms.
Aliyah
Wave of Jewish immigration to Palestine; the First is dated 1882–1903 and the Second 1904–1914.
Yishuv
The Jewish community in Palestine, comprising Old and New Yishuv.
Jewish National Fund
Zionist institution founded in 1901 to acquire land.
Basel Program
The 1897 statement of aims of the World Zionist Organization.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows the Arab world on the threshold of war with several unsettled political possibilities. Its principal lesson is that identity and loyalty were layered, and that political outcomes later treated as natural were in 1914 still contested.

It also reveals larger processes: the transformation of cultural revival into political demands, the interaction of imperial reform and provincial interests, and the intersection of Ottoman, Arab, Islamic, and Zionist projects in a shared geography. Cairo, Paris, Istanbul, Damascus, Beirut, and Jaffa were connected nodes in a single political field.

Chapter 55 will show how wartime policy, repression, diplomacy, and the Arab Revolt transformed this field. The questions raised here about language, citizenship, representation, sovereignty, religious legitimacy, and Palestine did not disappear, and they continued to shape the history of the modern Arab world in the chapters that follow.
