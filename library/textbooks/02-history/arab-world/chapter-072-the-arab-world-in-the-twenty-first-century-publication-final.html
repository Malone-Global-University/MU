Chapter Opening

On March 22, 1945, representatives of six states signed a pact in Cairo establishing the League of Arab States; Yemen signed on May 5, becoming the seventh founding member. More than eight decades later the League has twenty-two members, stretching from the Atlantic coast of Morocco to the Gulf, from the Mediterranean to the Horn of Africa and the Indian Ocean. This final chapter asks what, historically, connects those twenty-two societies, and what does not.

A note on dating. Because this chapter treats events still unfolding as this edition was prepared, it observes a fixed historical cutoff of November 7, 2026. Statements about the present describe conditions as documented by the sources named, on the dates given. Statistics are tied to the institution that produced them, the geography it covered, and the date it was issued. Later developments belong to a future revised edition, not to silent changes in this one.

The pact of 1945 is a useful starting point because it already contained the tension that organizes this chapter. Its signatories declared a common bond and committed themselves to cooperation, yet the text was equally concerned to protect the independence and sovereignty of each member. Eight decades later, an Arab world that shares a language of high culture, overlapping religious and historical memories, dense family and labor networks, and a vast Arabic-language media space also contains monarchies with some of the world's largest sovereign wealth funds, republics struggling with debt, a transitional government in Damascus, a divided Sudan at war, and a Palestinian population living under conditions that no single label adequately describes.

The preceding eleven chapters of Part XI and Part XII traced independence, Arab nationalism, oil, revolution, war, and the uprisings of 2010–2011 and their aftermath. This chapter does not retell them. It asks what the region looks like once those histories are brought together, and it extends the narrative through the extraordinary developments of the half-decade after 2020: the Abraham Accords of 2020, the Saudi–Iranian agreement of 2023, the war in Sudan beginning in April 2023, the Hamas-led attack of October 7, 2023 and the Gaza war that followed, the fall of Bashar al-Assad's government on December 8, 2024, and the regional war that began in February 2026 and disrupted Gulf energy exports and shipping through the Strait of Hormuz.

The chapter is organized in five divisions of four sections each. The first examines sovereignty and conflict, the second the Gulf and the international order, the third population, family, migration, and cities, the fourth water, climate, technology, and culture, and the fifth governance, reconstruction, comparison, and the meaning of the Arab world itself. Throughout, it is guided by a central argument: the contemporary Arab world is neither a single political system nor a collection of unrelated states. It is a historically interconnected region whose societies share significant linguistic, cultural, religious, economic, and institutional relationships while following increasingly varied national trajectories.

Evidence for a chapter of this kind differs from evidence for earlier periods. Historians of the twenty-first century work with official government reports, the outputs of international organizations such as the United Nations and the World Bank, court opinions, treaties, survey data, satellite imagery, journalism, and digital sources. Each has strengths and limits. A government development report records stated priorities and claimed performance; a humanitarian statement records operational need, not a census; an economic forecast records assumptions, not outcomes. Students will repeatedly be asked what a source can establish, what it cannot, and whose perspective it represents.

The chapter also carries forward the rules established in Chapter 1. "Arab" is not a synonym for "Muslim," "Middle Eastern," or "Gulf." Millions of citizens of Arab states are Amazigh, Kurdish, Nubian, Somali, Assyrian, Armenian, or members of other communities, and many Arab citizens are Christians, Jews, Druze, or members of other traditions. Arabization and Islamization, treated as separate processes in Chapter 24, remain separate in the present. And nothing about the twenty-first century was inevitable from the seventh, or the nineteenth, or even the twentieth.

The chapter ends the textbook with a question rather than a prediction: what is the Arab world, historically, and how has that idea been made and remade? By the end, students should be able to explain how a region of shared history and divergent outcomes came to be, and to read the next headline about it with the care that this book has tried to model.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Distinguish the Arab world from the Middle East, North Africa, the Islamic world, and the Arabic-speaking diaspora, and explain what League membership does and does not establish.
  2. Analyze the interacting dimensions of the Palestinian question in the 2020s, including legal, territorial, humanitarian, and diplomatic issues, while distinguishing documented events from contested interpretations.
  3. Explain the difference between the collapse of an authoritarian government in Syria and the construction of a legitimate postwar state.
  4. Assess how rival armed institutions, failed transition, resources, and external involvement interacted in Sudan's war after 2023.
  5. Evaluate Gulf development strategies by distinguishing announced objectives, completed projects, and independently measured outcomes.
  6. Explain why energy geography makes Gulf states both powerful and exposed, using the 2026 disruption as a dated case.
  7. Compare youth employment, women's legal status, migration, and urban life across different Arab societies without treating any category as uniform.
  8. Interpret water scarcity, climate stress, and food security as outcomes of both physical conditions and institutional choices.
  9. Describe how satellite media and digital platforms created a transnational Arabic public sphere shaped by state regulation.
  10. Compare contemporary governance and reconstruction challenges across several states using a stated analytical framework.
  11. Evaluate primary evidence, including treaties, court opinions, and institutional reports, for scope, date, perspective, and limits.
  12. Connect the contemporary Arab world to the long history traced in Chapters 1–71 without projecting modern identities backward or treating present outcomes as inevitable.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. What makes the Arab world a coherent historical subject, and why is the same subject also not a single political system?
  2. Why can legal findings, diplomatic recognition, and humanitarian reporting all be accurate and still leave a political question unresolved?
  3. What distinguishes the fall of a government from the creation of a functioning state?
  4. Why do transitions fail when armed institutions keep independent claims to resources and authority?
  5. How can the same energy resources produce both state capacity and strategic vulnerability?
  6. Why might educational expansion fail to produce employment, and how does that affect political expectations?
  7. How should historians handle statistics from regional categories that do not match the membership of the Arab League?
  8. In what ways has digital communication connected Arab societies while states continue to regulate it differently?
  9. What counts as evidence of political accountability, and how can historians distinguish reform from administrative strengthening?
  10. How can a historical idea such as "the Arab world" be real without being timeless or uniform?

Historical Context

This chapter is the endpoint of a long argument. Part I described the lands before the Arab world: the arid geography and water strategies examined in Chapters 2 and 3 that still shape settlement today. Part II surveyed the Mesopotamian, Egyptian, Levantine, North African, and Arabian civilizations that this textbook has refused to call "Arab." Parts III and IV followed the rise of Islam and the Arab-Islamic conquests, after which Arabic became a language of government, religion, and learning across a vast area. Chapters 24 and 25 onward then separated Arabization from Islamization and traced the Abbasid, Fatimid, Maghrebi, Mamluk, and Ottoman worlds in which Arabic coexisted with Persian, Turkish, Amazigh, Coptic, Syriac, Kurdish, and other languages.

Parts IX and X explained how reform, the Nahda, European expansion, the First World War, and the mandate system built the modern state system, and Part XI traced independence, Nasser's pan-Arabism, the oil revolution, and the wars of 1948, 1967, and 1973. Part XII's earlier chapters followed political Islam, the Lebanese war, the Gulf crises, the peace processes, and the uprisings of 2010–2011 and their consequences. The shorthand that students should carry into this chapter is: the borders and institutions of the modern Arab states are twentieth-century creations built on much older societies, and the idea of Arab unity has repeatedly been invoked, institutionalized in the League, and contested by the realities of separate sovereign states.

Three structural continuities recur. First, aridity and water remain defining constraints, as Chapter 3 showed. Second, hydrocarbons since the mid-twentieth century have produced very unequal fiscal resources among states and a large labor migration within the region. Third, contested sovereignty, whether between mandatory powers and nationalists, between states and armed movements, or between the Palestinian people and the occupying state, has remained a recurring source of crisis. The chapter's task is to show how those continuities intersect with new forces: demographic youth bulges, global energy transition, digital communication, climate change, and a multipolar international order.

A boundary rule applies. Events already narrated in Chapters 61, 66, 68, and 70 (the Nakba, 1967, Lebanon, Oslo) and in Chapter 71 (the uprisings and civil wars through 2020) are referenced, not retold. The new narrative concentrates on 2020–2026 and on synthesis.

Sovereignty, Conflict, and the Changing Political Geography of the Arab World

Section 72.1

The Contemporary Arab World: States, Regions, Institutions, and Historical Connections

In ordinary usage the "Arab world" names the states in which Arabic is the principal language of government, education, and public life and whose governments belong to the League of Arab States. In 2026 the League has twenty-two members: Algeria, Bahrain, Comoros, Djibouti, Egypt, Iraq, Jordan, Kuwait, Lebanon, Libya, Mauritania, Morocco, Oman, the State of Palestine, Qatar, Saudi Arabia, Somalia, Sudan, Syria, Tunisia, the United Arab Emirates, and Yemen. The list shows at once why the term needs care. Comoros, Djibouti, and Somalia are Arabic-using only in part of their national life, and Somalia's principal language is Somali. Palestine is a member although its territory is under occupation and its institutions lack full sovereign control. Syria was suspended in 2011 and restored to regular participation in 2023. Membership is a political fact about governments, not a measurement of what populations speak or how individuals identify.

The Arab world must also be distinguished from neighboring categories. The Middle East is a geopolitical label that includes Iran, Turkey, and Israel. North Africa is a geographical region that includes the Amazigh-speaking Maghreb but also, in some definitions, areas with different histories. The Islamic world is a religious and civilizational category that includes Indonesia, Pakistan, and Nigeria, and the Arab world includes sizeable Christian and other non-Muslim communities. The Arabic-speaking diaspora extends far beyond the League's territory. Chapter 1 established these distinctions; this chapter relies on them whenever a statistic or a headline conflates them.

Within the League's territory, historians and statisticians usually recognize several subregions. The Maghreb (Morocco, Algeria, Tunisia, Libya, Mauritania) looks to the Atlantic and Mediterranean and has long Amazigh, Andalusi, Ottoman, and French or Italian colonial histories. The Nile Valley (Egypt and Sudan) centers on a river that has structured settlement since antiquity. The Mashriq (Iraq, Syria, Lebanon, Jordan, Palestine) comprises the Fertile Crescent societies whose religious and ethnic diversity is exceptional. The Arabian Peninsula and Gulf includes Saudi Arabia, Yemen, Oman, and the five smaller Gulf monarchies. The Horn of Africa and island societies (Somalia, Djibouti, Comoros) connect the Arab world to East Africa and the Indian Ocean.

Internal diversity extends well beyond geography. Amazigh peoples form a large part of the population of Morocco and Algeria and are present in Libya, Tunisia, and Mauritania; Morocco recognized Tamazight as an official language in its 2011 constitution. Kurds live in Iraq and Syria, with an autonomous region in northern Iraq. Nubians live along the Nile in Egypt and Sudan. Assyrians, Armenians, Turkmen, Circassians, Jews (in dramatically reduced numbers from the mid-twentieth century), Copts, Maronites, Druze, Yazidis, Sunnis, Shi'a, Ibadis, and other communities form the real social fabric. Arabic itself is lived as a spectrum of regional dialects, with Modern Standard Arabic as a shared written register.

Institutional connections are real but limited. The League provides a diplomatic forum, a secretariat in Cairo, and affiliated specialized bodies; its decisions generally require member consent and it has rarely enforced them. Other connections are informal and often stronger: shared television and music, labor migration within the region, family and commercial networks, pilgrimage to Mecca and Medina, religious scholarship at institutions such as al-Azhar, and a common stock of literary and historical references.

Finally, the boundaries, flags, anthems, and national histories that students now take for granted are historical products. As Chapters 54 through 60 showed, the frontiers of Iraq, Syria, Lebanon, Jordan, Palestine, and the Gulf states were drawn or consolidated in the twentieth century; those of North Africa reflect French, Italian, Spanish, and British colonial arrangements. They are no less real for being recent, but they are not expressions of a timeless uniform civilization. The remainder of this chapter keeps both truths in view: the connections are historical, and so are the divisions.

The contemporary map also conceals considerable differences in the relationship between state borders and political authority. Iraq's federal constitution recognizes a Kurdistan Region; Somalia's federal arrangements coexist with unresolved questions about Somaliland; the Comoros has its own island-based institutional history; and Palestinian self-government operates within an unresolved framework of occupation and limited jurisdiction. These are not deviations from a supposed standard Arab state. They illustrate how state formation emerged from multiple imperial, colonial, nationalist, and local processes. Historians should consequently identify the relevant institution before assigning policies or statistics to an entire population. A statement by the Arab League, a Gulf Cooperation Council declaration, a municipal regulation, and a decision of a particular government have different legal authority. Treating them as interchangeable is one of the easiest ways to mistake regional connection for political uniformity.

Historical Vocabulary
"Arab" and the Arab World

"Arab" has meant different things in different centuries. In pre-Islamic and early Islamic sources it referred to particular Arabian peoples and later to the language-speaking conquest elite. Under the Abbasids and later it increasingly named a linguistic and cultural identity, and in the late Ottoman period and after it acquired a modern political meaning through Arab nationalism. Today it can denote language, cultural participation, ancestry, political solidarity, citizenship in a League state, or some combination, and individuals within the same state may answer differently.

The "Arab world" is thus a historical and political concept, not a bloc with a single will. Using it responsibly means avoiding two errors: treating every resident of a League state as identifying as Arab, and treating the region as having one economic, political, or religious character.

Section 72.2

Palestine, Israel, Gaza, the West Bank, and Unresolved Sovereignty in the 2020s

The Palestinian question has run through this textbook since Chapter 54, and the earlier chapters (61, 66, 68, 70) described the Nakba, the occupation that began in 1967, the Lebanese war, and the Oslo process. By the 2020s the situation that Oslo was meant to be a transition from had hardened into one of fragmented territory. The West Bank is divided into areas of differing Palestinian and Israeli administrative and security control; East Jerusalem was annexed by Israel in a step not recognized by most states; Israeli settlements, regarded as unlawful by most of the international community and disputed by Israel's governments, expanded in the West Bank and around Jerusalem; and the Gaza Strip, after Israel's 2005 withdrawal of settlers and troops, came under Hamas control from 2007 and under a blockade imposed by Israel and, along its border, Egypt. The Palestinian Authority administered parts of the West Bank, with the legitimacy of its institutions weakened by the absence of national elections since 2006 and by its own divisions with Hamas.

On October 7, 2023, Hamas-led fighters crossed from Gaza into southern Israel, killing approximately 1,200 people according to Israeli authorities, most of them civilians, and taking roughly 250 hostages. The attack was condemned by many governments as a deliberate assault on civilians. Israel responded with a large-scale air and ground campaign in Gaza. Humanitarian and health data from the territory, compiled by the Gaza Ministry of Health and treated by UN agencies as broadly reliable while Israeli officials have disputed aspects of it, recorded deaths in the tens of thousands by late 2025, mass displacement of most of the population, destruction of a large share of housing, hospitals, schools, and water systems, and severe food insecurity. In August 2025 the Integrated Food Security Phase Classification, a UN-backed technical system, confirmed famine conditions in part of the Strip; the Israeli government disputed the finding. Counting methods, categories (combatant versus civilian), and the effects of access restrictions on verification remain subjects of dispute, and students should always identify the counting institution and date.

A ceasefire arrangement announced in October 2025 led to the release of the living hostages held in Gaza and a reduction in the intensity of hostilities. It did not end violence. UN humanitarian reporting through 2026, including OCHA's situation reports of September and November 2026, continued to record strikes, civilian casualties, restrictions on aid, and severe problems of shelter and health care, and the UN's political briefings in October 2026 described the situation as extremely fragile. Humanitarian crossings were closed at the start of the regional war with Iran on February 28, 2026, according to Human Rights Watch, a reminder that Gaza's conditions depended on events far beyond it. Whether the ceasefire framework could produce durable governance, security, and reconstruction arrangements remained unresolved at the cutoff.

Behind the war lie questions that every plan must answer: the borders of a Palestinian state and the status of settlements, the status of Jerusalem, the claims of Palestinian refugees and their descendants, security arrangements, who governs Gaza, and how Palestinians can exercise self-determination. Israeli and Palestinian positions on these questions, and the positions of other governments, differ widely and are not reducible to a single formula.

International law has played an unusually prominent part. On July 19, 2024 the International Court of Justice issued an advisory opinion, requested by the UN General Assembly, on the legal consequences of Israel's policies and practices in the Occupied Palestinian Territory (examined in the Primary Source Window below). Students must distinguish this from other proceedings: the ICJ's separate contentious case on Genocide Convention obligations brought by South Africa, the ICJ's advisory role in general, and the International Criminal Court's work on individual criminal responsibility, including arrest warrants issued in 2024. An advisory opinion is authoritative legal reasoning but not an enforceable judgment against a party.

Diplomatically, the Abraham Accords of 2020 had shown that some Arab governments were willing to normalize relations with Israel without a Palestinian settlement. The Gaza war complicated further normalization and revived two-state diplomacy. In 2025 several states, including France, the United Kingdom, Canada, and Australia, formally recognized the State of Palestine, and a UN conference on two-state implementation took place. Statements of support are not the same as implementation: recognition does not itself change control of territory, borders, or security. Chapters 61, 66, 68, and 70 should be reviewed for the earlier history.

A history attentive to civilians must also examine what a temporary reduction in hostilities does and does not repair. Students should distinguish a ceasefire's military provisions from access to clean water, functioning sanitation, rehabilitation of hospitals, school continuity, recovery of livelihoods, and the restoration of property records. Even when people return to a neighborhood, damaged buildings and unexploded ordnance may prevent safe residence. Food-security classifications depend on specified locations, periods, and indicators; the IPC's August 2025 famine determination concerned Gaza Governorate rather than every part of the territory simultaneously. In the West Bank, the 2026 OCHA reporting on settler violence, access restrictions, and disrupted olive harvesting shows that humanitarian conditions cannot be inferred solely from a Gaza ceasefire. These different crises demand separately dated evidence and attention to Palestinian and Israeli civilians' rights and safety.

Map Study

Palestine and the Changing Political Geography of the Eastern Mediterranean

Use dated maps from credible institutional sources, such as the UN Office for the Coordination of Humanitarian Affairs (OCHA) for the occupied territory and an atlas for the wider region. Identify:

  • The 1949 armistice line (the Green Line) and the areas occupied in 1967
  • The West Bank's administrative divisions, settlement locations, and barrier route as shown on the map's date
  • East Jerusalem and the jurisdictional lines around it
  • Gaza's crossings, and any military lines or restricted zones shown on the specific map's date
  • The relation of the territory to Egypt, Jordan, Lebanon, Syria, and the Mediterranean

Do not treat temporary military control lines as final international borders, and note the map's date and source before drawing any conclusion.

Section 72.3

Syria after the Assad Government: Political Transition, Reconstruction, and National Reconciliation

Chapter 71 followed the Syrian conflict through 2020, by which time front lines had largely stabilized: the government, backed by Russia and Iran, held most major cities; opposition-held territory remained in the northwest around Idlib, dominated by Hayat Tahrir al-Sham (HTS); and Kurdish-led forces held much of the northeast. Between 2021 and 2024 the war was relatively frozen while the country's economy deteriorated under sanctions, currency collapse, and corruption, and while several Arab governments moved toward re-engagement with Damascus, culminating in Syria's return to the Arab League in 2023.

That stalemate ended abruptly. In late November 2024 an opposition offensive from the northwest, led by HTS, took Aleppo and then moved south with little resistance. Russian and Iranian capacity to support Damascus had been reduced by their other commitments and by Israeli action against Iran's allies. On December 8, 2024 the Assad government collapsed and Bashar al-Assad left Syria for Russia. The speed of the collapse surprised nearly all observers and illustrates a theme of this chapter: regimes may be strong in appearance and brittle in reality.

The new authorities, led by Ahmed al-Sharaa, formerly the HTS leader, formed an interim government and in early 2025 he was named transitional president. A constitutional declaration set a transitional period. Violence against civilians on the coast in March 2025 and in the southern Suwayda region in mid-2025 reminded observers how fragile intercommunal trust remained, and negotiations over integrating the Kurdish-led forces of the northeast produced agreements whose implementation remained contested. An indirect electoral process for a People's Assembly took place in October 2025 for most of its elected seats, and by July 2026 UN officials described the establishment of the Assembly and a constitutional court as an institutional milestone while warning of continuing insecurity. The United States, European states, and the Gulf states moved to ease sanctions and support recovery; Turkey became a major influence in the north; and Israel occupied additional territory in the southwest and conducted strikes, citing security concerns, while Russian forces' status and Iranian influence were sharply reduced.

The central problems are institutional. Syria must integrate numerous armed groups into a unified military and police; restore public services, electricity, water, and the currency; reform courts and security services that were instruments of repression; and write constitutional arrangements credible to Alawites, Druze, Kurds, Christians, Sunni Arabs, women, and others. It must also confront the legacy of mass detention, disappearance, chemical attacks, and other wartime abuses documented by Syrian and international organizations. Transitional justice, meaning the processes of truth-seeking, accountability, reparation, and institutional reform after mass abuse, is difficult because thousands of families are still searching for the missing, because perpetrators were widespread, and because newly empowered forces have themselves been accused of abuses. Millions of Syrian refugees abroad also face decisions about return that depend on security, property, documentation, housing, and livelihood.

External actors have distinct interests: Turkey seeks border security and influence; Israel seeks to prevent hostile forces near its border; Gulf states seek stability and investment opportunities; Russia and Iran seek to preserve what remains of their positions; the United States combines counterterrorism and sanctions policy; and the United Nations supports a "Syrian-led" process. As of October 2026 the UN continued to describe Syria as in a fragile but real political transition requiring stronger institutions, inclusive participation, transitional justice, and economic recovery. The historical question is the one the chapter's title poses: what is the difference between the collapse of an authoritarian government and the creation of a legitimate, secure, and functioning postwar state?

The transition presents a problem of institutional sequencing as well as leadership. A provisional assembly or constitutional tribunal can create a visible administrative framework, but legitimacy also depends on who selects members, whether excluded communities can challenge decisions, whether judges are independent, and whether armed formations obey civilian law. The October 2026 UN briefing by Deputy Special Envoy Claudio Cordone emphasized inclusive political dialogue, accountability, constitutional development, economic recovery, and women's participation. Those criteria offer historians a way to test progress rather than treating the existence of a newly named institution as proof of democratic consolidation. Regional perspectives differ: displaced Syrian families may prioritize return, housing documents, and information about missing relatives; municipal workers may emphasize salaries and functioning utilities; communities threatened by armed violence may place physical security first. None of these priorities alone defines a completed national settlement.

Sudan's War, State Fragmentation, and the Wider Crisis of Sovereignty

Sudan's modern crisis grew from an unfinished transition. After mass protests removed Omar al-Bashir in April 2019, a joint civilian–military authority governed under an agreement of that year, but the generals retained control of the security forces, large parts of the economy, and the armed institutions. On October 25, 2021 the army chief Abdel Fattah al-Burhan, supported by the commander of the Rapid Support Forces (RSF), Mohamed Hamdan Dagalo ("Hemedti"), took power, ending the civilian partnership. Street resistance continued, and negotiations on a new framework in late 2022 stalled over the integration of the RSF into the army and the timetable for doing so.

The two forces had different histories. The Sudanese Armed Forces (SAF) were the state's regular military. The RSF grew out of the Janjaweed militias used by the government in Darfur in the 2000s and was formalized in 2013; it developed independent commercial interests, including gold, and foreign connections. On April 15, 2023 fighting erupted in Khartoum and spread rapidly. The capital, Omdurman, Darfur, and Kordofan became battlegrounds; the SAF eventually recaptured much of Khartoum in 2025, while the RSF consolidated control over most of Darfur, including El Fasher in late 2025, after a prolonged siege that drew widespread reports of mass killing. The country was effectively divided between rival authorities, with international mediation by regional and Western actors producing repeated but unimplemented truce efforts.

Violence has had pronounced ethnic and regional dimensions in Darfur and Kordofan, with reports of targeted attacks on non-Arab communities such as the Masalit, and widely documented sexual violence. But communities were not uniformly aligned with armed factions, and local people have been victims of both sides. The war has destroyed hospitals, markets, universities, archives, and infrastructure in Khartoum and elsewhere, produced famine conditions in places, and made humanitarian access dangerous and expensive.

Displacement figures require precision. As of November 6, 2026, a joint UNHCR–IOM statement described Sudan as the site of the world's largest displacement crisis and estimated that about 15.4 million people urgently needed emergency shelter and basic household items, while available resources allowed the agencies to prioritize roughly one in ten. That is a figure for need, not for the number displaced. Students should distinguish people displaced within Sudan since April 2023, people displaced earlier and still displaced, refugees who crossed into Chad, South Sudan, Egypt, and elsewhere, and returnees to areas such as Khartoum; these categories overlap and must not be added together carelessly.

Sudan's case is part of a wider pattern in the region. In Yemen, the Houthi movement controls the capital and much of the populated north, while the internationally recognized authorities and other forces hold other areas. In Libya, rival administrations in the east and west have coexisted since 2014, with UN mediation continuing in 2026. In Iraq and Lebanon the formal state coexists with powerful armed organizations. What these cases share is not a common cause but a common problem: when armed institutions retain independent claims over territory, revenue, and violence, political transitions can stall or collapse.

A comparative account of Sudan must keep the institutional history of armed authority visible. The Rapid Support Forces developed from earlier paramilitary arrangements associated with the Darfur wars and entered the transition with an independent chain of command and economic interests. The Sudanese Armed Forces retained established military institutions and a competing conception of state authority. After the 2021 military takeover, disputes about integrating the RSF into a unified force intensified amid failed civilian–military bargaining. The April 2023 outbreak of war was thus neither a sudden ethnic eruption nor simply a contest between two personalities. It emerged from unresolved questions about the monopoly of force, economic assets, accountability for earlier abuses, and civilian authority. Humanitarian figures must likewise separate the 15.4 million requiring emergency shelter and household supplies in the November 2026 joint statement from the different numbers of people internally displaced, refugees abroad, and returnees.

Sudan's War after April 2023

Long-term causes

Decades of centralized rule from Khartoum, earlier wars in Darfur and the south, reliance on irregular militias, military control of the economy, and a state that never settled the relation between its armed forces and civilian politics.

Short-term causes

The 2019 revolution's incomplete bargain, the 2021 military takeover, and the failure of 2022 negotiations over security-sector integration and the timetable for placing the RSF under army command.

The outbreak of fighting between the SAF and RSF on April 15, 2023.

Gulf Transformation, Economic Diversification, and the International Order

Section 72.5

The Gulf Monarchies in Transformation: Development Strategies, Institutions, and Social Change

The six Gulf Cooperation Council states share dynastic rule, hydrocarbon wealth, and large non-citizen workforces, but they differ sharply. Saudi Arabia is by far the largest, with a large citizen population and a state budget tied closely to oil revenue. The United Arab Emirates is a federation whose emirates have different resource endowments: Abu Dhabi holds most of the oil, while Dubai built its economy on trade, logistics, finance, and tourism. Qatar combines one of the world's largest natural-gas export sectors with a very small citizen population and substantial international investments. Kuwait has an elected parliament with real powers, though its emir suspended parliament in 2024 for a period. Bahrain, with limited oil and a large Shi'a majority governed by a Sunni dynasty, has long relied on financial support from neighbors. Oman has more modest reserves and different regional relationships. These differences in fiscal capacity, population structure, and political institutions affect what each can attempt.

The most prominent program is Saudi Arabia's Vision 2030, announced in 2016 under Crown Prince Muhammad bin Salman. It seeks to reduce dependence on oil revenue by developing tourism, entertainment, logistics, mining, manufacturing, and technology; to expand private-sector and women's employment; to modernize the public sector; and to attract investment through a national wealth fund. Visible changes include the opening of cinemas and concerts, the 2018 permission for women to drive, the relaxation of some gender-segregation rules, large urban and giga-projects, and the award to Saudi Arabia of the 2034 men's football World Cup and, as the Expo 2030 host, Riyadh. Statistics published by the government show growth in non-oil activity and women's labor-force participation. Independent analysts, however, note that oil revenues still dominate the budget, that some megaprojects have been scaled back or delayed, and that outcomes should be tested against independent data. The Vision 2030 annual report is thus best read as an official record of priorities and claimed performance, not as an independent audit.

The UAE has pursued a diversified commercial model for longer, becoming a hub for aviation, ports, finance, tourism, and, more recently, artificial intelligence investment. Qatar's strategy rests on gas, a sovereign wealth fund, global sports and media presence, and a foreign policy of mediation, including in Gaza and in the 2026 Iran war's ceasefire diplomacy. Kuwait, Bahrain, and Oman face tighter fiscal constraints and more difficult reform politics. Oman's debt reduction and its Vision 2040 program have been pursued with notable caution.

Social change has been real and unevenly distributed. Women's educational attainment is high across the Gulf and their workforce participation has risen, especially in Saudi Arabia and the UAE, while guardianship-related legal constraints have varied by country and have been relaxed in some. Migrant workers, who form the majority of the labor force in several states, have been subject to the kafala sponsorship system, which tied residence to employers; Qatar and Saudi Arabia introduced reforms around 2020–2021 easing exit and job-change rules, though human-rights organizations continue to report weak enforcement and abuse. Entertainment and public culture have expanded, religious institutions have been brought under closer state direction, and governments have shifted the terms of the bargain with citizens: less guaranteed public employment, more expectation of private-sector work.

The key analytic caution is that wealth, infrastructure, and administrative modernization do not automatically signal democratization. Gulf governments have often combined economic opening with tight political control, and the relation between citizens and states has been negotiated through distribution, consultation, and in some cases coercion rather than through competitive elections.

A useful contrast begins with public finance. Kuwait's parliamentary traditions have created recurring struggles over budgets, appointments, and the distribution of oil income; Qatar's export earnings depend heavily on natural gas and liquefaction capacity; the UAE combines the oil wealth of Abu Dhabi with Dubai's trade, tourism, logistics, and financial services; and Oman and Bahrain confront tighter fiscal constraints than their wealthier neighbors. Their sovereign investment institutions, labor-market policies, and citizenship arrangements therefore differ materially. This variation matters when interpreting the word diversification. New hotels or financial districts can expand services while the public budget remains exposed to hydrocarbon revenues or state-directed capital spending. Likewise, a rising female employment rate among nationals may coexist with unequal conditions for low-paid foreign workers who operate many essential services. A serious assessment needs economic, social, and institutional evidence, not the visual appearance of modernization.

The Vision 2030 report is a valuable government account precisely because it reports explicit objectives, program categories, and performance indicators. It records progress in non-oil activity and private investment and describes initiatives in tourism, logistics, digital services, and employment; these claims should be distinguished from outside evaluation of public spending, productivity, fiscal risks, and sectoral competitiveness. In 2026 the Gulf security shock exposed why the resilience of an economy cannot be inferred from its share of non-oil output alone. Hotels, aviation, financial services, and industrial supply chains may be classified as non-oil industries yet remain exposed to disruptions in shipping, confidence, and international travel. Students should ask whether a reported gain reflects a lasting productivity increase, a government-financed project, a cyclical change, or a statistical reclassification. Each has a different implication for the country's longer-run economic position.

Section 72.6

Oil, Natural Gas, Energy Security, and the Strategic Geography of the Gulf

Hydrocarbons remain the foundation of Gulf public finance and a major element of world energy trade. Production is concentrated around fields and facilities in eastern Saudi Arabia, southern Iraq, Kuwait, Abu Dhabi, and Qatar's North Field, and the infrastructure that moves it includes pipelines, export terminals such as Ras Tanura and Ras Laffan, refineries, and fleets of tankers and liquefied-natural-gas carriers. Much of this trade must pass through the Strait of Hormuz, only a few dozen kilometers wide at its narrowest. Before the 2026 war roughly a fifth of world oil consumption and a large share of internationally traded LNG passed through it.

Other chokepoints matter as well. The Red Sea and the Suez Canal connect Asian and European markets; Bab el-Mandeb at the Red Sea's southern end became hazardous when Houthi attacks on shipping from late 2023 diverted vessels around Africa and sharply reduced Suez Canal revenues, a significant loss for Egypt. The Mediterranean ports of Alexandria, Tangier-Med, and Beirut, and the Atlantic-facing and Red Sea ports of Saudi Arabia and Sudan, tie Arab economies to wider networks. Pipelines such as Saudi Arabia's East-West line and the UAE's route to Fujairah offer partial bypasses, but their capacities are limited.

The February 2026 war showed how exposed this system is. After US and Israeli operations against Iran began on February 28, 2026, Iranian actions closed or severely restricted shipping through Hormuz, and infrastructure, including energy and public facilities in several Gulf countries, was damaged. The World Bank's October 2026 update described significant regional economic disruption, noting that, unlike previous energy shocks, this one hurt oil exporters severely; the IMF made a similar assessment. An April temporary ceasefire was followed by a June memorandum of understanding reached after Pakistani-led mediation; that framework collapsed amid renewed fighting and expired in August, while limited strikes continued and shipping did not normalize. The World Bank's October baseline projected a gradual reopening beginning late in 2026, not a settlement already achieved. This is a dated development, not a permanent condition, and students should check subsequent sources.

Parallel to these events is a long-term shift in energy systems. Solar and wind power, electric vehicles, hydrogen and ammonia, and industrial decarbonization are expanding globally. Gulf states have invested heavily in renewable electricity and in new fuels while remaining among the world's lowest-cost oil and gas producers; Arab importers such as Egypt, Jordan, Morocco, and Tunisia face different exposures through fuel subsidies and import bills. The central question is how the same resources that support development and state capacity create exposure to market and geopolitical shocks.

The strategic geography of oil is also the geography of supply-chain concentration. An export terminal cannot deliver if tankers cannot safely reach it; a refinery cannot operate normally if imported inputs, insurance, or power are interrupted; and petrochemical manufacturing often depends on both feedstock and maritime access. Some producers possess pipeline routes to the Red Sea or Gulf of Oman, but the capacity of alternatives is limited and varies by operator and commodity. The Strait of Hormuz is therefore not merely a symbol of geopolitical vulnerability: restrictions can force production cuts, raise transport and insurance costs, and affect airlines, ports, manufacturing, and food imports. Countries that import fuel may face higher prices, while exporters deprived of shipping routes may lose income despite elevated market prices. Historians should distinguish threatened closure, actual shipping volume, naval blockade, commercial risk aversion, and physical damage to energy facilities.

The 2026 episode provides an unusually clear dated case. U.S. and Israeli strikes on Iran began on February 28, followed by Iranian retaliation and severe restrictions affecting traffic through Hormuz. An April ceasefire was followed by a June memorandum of understanding intended to support further negotiations, but the arrangement collapsed and expired in August, and limited strikes subsequently resumed. The British parliamentary research service and U.S. Congressional Research Service describe these stages separately; neither treats the June agreement as a durable peace treaty. The World Bank's October update projects a 2026 contraction in its MENAAP statistical region and an even sharper shock in the GCC economies. Those are forecasts based on assumptions, not final measured annual output or a statistic for all Arab League states. The lesson is to connect precise chronology with the correct scope of economic evidence.

Section 72.7

Regional Rivalries, Diplomatic Normalization, and the Changing Balance of Power

Arab governments have varied interests and cannot be reduced to a single side in a religious or civilizational rivalry. Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Qatar, Egypt, and others pursue distinct strategies toward Iran, Turkey, Israel, the United States, Russia, China, the European Union, and neighboring African states. Their positions have also shifted over time.

The Abraham Accords, signed in Washington on September 15, 2020 by Israel with the UAE and Bahrain, were followed by Morocco's agreement in December 2020, in the context of US recognition of its claim to Western Sahara, and by an announcement from Sudan that was not fully implemented. They broke with the long-standing Arab position that normalization should follow a Palestinian settlement, as embodied in the Arab Peace Initiative of 2002. Saudi Arabia did not join. After October 2023 further normalization became politically harder for several governments, though existing relationships largely persisted.

Another realignment came on March 10, 2023, when Saudi Arabia and Iran announced, after talks in Beijing mediated by China, an agreement to resume diplomatic relations and reopen embassies. China's role, a notable Chinese mediation in the region, signaled a changing balance of external influence. Yet the agreement restored relations; it did not resolve rival strategic interests in Yemen, Iraq, Lebanon, or the Gulf, and the war of 2026 tested the arrangement severely.

External powers have shifted as well. The United States remains the principal security partner of many Gulf states, with bases and arms sales, but US priorities and reliability have been questioned, particularly given disputes over Gaza and Iran. China is the largest trading partner of many Arab states and a growing investor and diplomatic actor. Russia's role was diminished in Syria in 2024 though it retains military and energy relationships. Turkey became an active regional power through Syria, Libya, Iraq, and Somalia. The European Union is a major trade and migration partner with limited security influence.

The wars in Gaza, Syria, Sudan, and the Gulf have reshaped these relations. Longstanding rivalries, whether Saudi–Iranian, Turkish–Egyptian, or between Gulf states over Sudan and Yemen, continue, but partnerships have become more flexible and transactional. The section's lesson is both continuity in rivalry and growing complexity in partnership.

Regional diplomacy increasingly combines security competition with selective cooperation. The UAE's commercial and diplomatic relationships may not mirror those of Qatar or Oman; Saudi Arabia's approach to Iran involves border and maritime security as well as questions about Yemen, investment, and religious diplomacy; and Egypt's calculations often reflect the Red Sea, Nile Basin, and domestic economic pressures. Iraq occupies a distinctive position between Iran, the Arab Gulf, Turkey, and the Levant. These overlapping relationships complicate any map dividing the region into two permanent camps. The 2023 Saudi–Iranian–Chinese statement promised restored relations and implementation of earlier agreements, while carefully avoiding any guarantee that all proxy, territorial, or ideological disputes had disappeared. Subsequent crises reveal the limits of normalization without nullifying the importance of diplomatic channels.

International relationships have also become more issue-specific. A government may acquire American defense equipment, export hydrocarbons to Asian markets, finance infrastructure with investors from several countries, and mediate a regional dispute without accepting the foreign-policy agenda of any one partner. China's trade and mediation roles are not equivalent to the military commitments of the United States; Turkish border interests are not identical to Iranian strategic calculations; and European aid, migration agreements, and energy purchases entail distinct incentives. Historians should examine concrete treaties, troop deployments, investment flows, and diplomatic decisions before using broad terms such as alliance or pivot. Even the Abraham Accords, launched in 2020, are best analyzed through the particular legal and commercial arrangements made by each participant and through the unresolved Palestinian question, rather than a claim of comprehensive regional peace.

Section 72.8

Uneven Development: Economic Growth, Debt, Trade, and the Changing Social Contract

Arab economies fall into recognizable groups. Gulf oil exporters hold large fiscal and financial resources and import much of their labor. Larger oil-importing countries, including Egypt, Morocco, Tunisia, and Jordan, depend on remittances, tourism, manufacturing, agriculture, foreign aid, and borrowing. Conflict-affected economies, such as Syria, Yemen, Libya, Sudan, and the Palestinian territories, have suffered destruction, collapsing currencies, and disrupted trade. Lebanon's financial collapse after 2019 shows how a once-sophisticated economy can fail when banking and fiscal systems are overextended.

The decade has brought a sequence of shocks: the COVID-19 pandemic of 2020, which hit tourism, remittances, and oil prices; the global inflation of 2021–2023 and food and energy price spikes after Russia's invasion of Ukraine in 2022, especially significant for Egypt and others dependent on imported wheat; high interest rates that raised debt-service costs; the regional wars of 2023–2026; and the 2026 Hormuz disruption. Many governments responded with borrowing, subsidy changes, currency devaluation, privatization of state-owned enterprises, and appeals to international lenders and wealthy Gulf partners. Egypt, for example, received large investment commitments from Gulf states and an expanded IMF program in the mid-2020s.

Aggregate growth tells only part of the story. Income and opportunity are distributed unevenly across households, regions, age groups, and occupational classes; informal employment is large; and the gap between capital cities and rural or peripheral regions is wide. Questions of trade, ports, manufacturing, logistics, and digital industries are therefore also questions of who benefits.

The World Bank's October 2026 regional update, titled From Divide to Opportunity: AI, Jobs, and Growth, projected that output in its MENAAP region would contract by about 2.1 percent in 2026 after growing about 3.3 percent in 2025, with the GCC contracting more sharply and then, under its baseline, rebounding in 2027. Students must note its scope: MENAAP includes Iran, Afghanistan, and Pakistan and omits some Arab League members, so its aggregate cannot be read as a statistic for the twenty-two members. Its projections also rest on assumptions about the reopening of Hormuz, which may not hold.

The central economic problem is whether governments can build more resilient and productive economies while providing jobs, essential services, and opportunities for social mobility, in other words, renewing a social contract under stress.

Economic exposure is distributed unevenly within countries as well as among them. A civil servant whose salary is denominated in local currency may confront imported food inflation differently from an exporter receiving foreign exchange; a household with members working abroad may rely on remittances but remain vulnerable to employment rules in the destination country. In Lebanon, banking collapse and currency instability transformed savings and public services; in Egypt, debt servicing and foreign-exchange needs constrained budgets even as infrastructure expanded; in Morocco and Tunisia, tourism, agriculture, and manufacturing connect local livelihoods to European demand and climatic conditions. Fiscal reform may stabilize government accounts while imposing costs on low-income consumers if subsidies are withdrawn without effective protection. The relevant historical question is not whether reform occurred but who paid for it, who gained access to new opportunities, and which institutions could protect those left exposed.

Regional economic numbers require particular care. The World Bank's October 2026 MENAAP outlook includes Afghanistan and Pakistan, which are not Arab League members; the ILO's Arab States labor grouping differs from the full Arabic-speaking region; and UNDP's statistical regional categories are not identical to those of the League. A regional average can therefore obscure both group composition and extreme national variation. The 2026 MENAAP projection of minus 2.1 percent output growth is a regional forecast, not a measured Arab-world GDP decline. Equally, Saudi Arabia's official non-oil growth indicator cannot be assigned to all Gulf economies. Students should record the institution, year, denominator, geographical coverage, and forecast status every time they use a numerical claim. Those are not technical decorations; they determine whether a historical comparison is meaningful.

Historical Vocabulary
Social Contract

In the Arab context, the "social contract" usually refers to the implicit post-independence bargain in which governments provided education, public employment, subsidized food and fuel, and basic services in return for political acquiescence. Chapters 62 and 64 described its construction in republics; Gulf monarchies built a variant on hydrocarbon rents.

The term describes a pattern of expectations, not a literal contract. Its erosion under debt, population growth, and subsidy reform helps explain protest movements and why economic reform is also political.

Section 72.9

Youth, Education, Employment, and the Future of the Social Contract

The Arab world is a young region by global standards, though age structures differ: the populations of Yemen, Iraq, Sudan, Somalia, and Palestine are very young, while Tunisia, Lebanon, and the Gulf citizen populations are older and the Gulf's total populations are shaped by migration. A large cohort of young people entering adulthood can be an economic opportunity or a source of strain, depending on whether jobs and institutions expand to receive them.

The post-independence states made education a central promise. Chapters 49 through 64 traced the creation of national school systems and universities and the guarantee of public-sector employment to graduates. Enrollment expanded dramatically, and literacy and women's schooling rose across the region. By the 1990s and 2000s, however, public payrolls had become difficult to sustain, private sectors in many countries remained small or dependent on the state, and graduates found that credentials did not translate into jobs matching their expectations.

Students should therefore distinguish attainment from opportunity. Enrollment figures measure schooling, not skills; graduate unemployment can be higher than unemployment among the less educated; informal work is widespread; and wages and mobility depend on connections as well as competence. Differences between urban and rural areas, between citizens and non-citizens, and between men and women are large. Emigration of doctors, engineers, and other professionals is a response and a loss. International Labour Organization reporting in 2026 identified the Arab States and Northern Africa among world regions with exceptionally high youth unemployment, though students must note that the ILO's subregional groupings are defined by its own categories and do not match League membership exactly.

Young people have not been passive. Student associations, start-ups, volunteer networks, professional networks on digital platforms, and local civic initiatives have grown, and the youth-led protests of 2010–2011, 2019 in Sudan, Algeria, Lebanon, and Iraq, and later movements show political expectations shaped by education. The UNDP's Arab Human Development Report 2022, which examined recovery after the pandemic, linked governance, economic opportunity, and social resilience, and is a useful source for the interaction among those factors.

The historical question is how education and demographic change transformed expectations about what governments should provide, and why economic institutions have struggled to meet those expectations. The answer lies partly in the structure of economies built around public employment and rents, partly in the pace of population change, and partly in political choices about education quality, labor markets, and private enterprise.

The contrast between a diploma and a livelihood is among the most important social transformations of the last half century. Governments across the region expanded public schools and universities as part of post-independence nation-building. In many countries, graduates expected a place in administration, teaching, or state enterprises. Yet demographic growth, fiscal constraints, and changes in recruitment reduced the number of such positions relative to demand. Private employers frequently required skills, networks, experience, or mobility that qualifications alone did not confer. The resulting mismatch is not merely a statistical unemployment rate: it affects marriage timing, household formation, geographic migration, political expectations, and trust in educational institutions. Students should compare labor-force participation, unemployment, informal work, and the share of young people neither employed nor studying, rather than substituting one measure for another. The ILO's 2026 assessment puts 2025 youth unemployment at 26.2 percent in its Arab States grouping and 22.6 percent in Northern Africa, two distinct statistical populations.

Education systems also face divergent language and skills demands. A university may teach in Arabic while private employers need French or English for finance, technical design, or international commerce. Vocational pathways are often affected by the status hierarchy between academic and technical credentials, and rural students may lack affordable transport or internet access. Some Gulf governments finance scholarships and training linked to nationalization of employment; elsewhere families bear large expenses for private tutoring. Labor migration can supply skills and remittances but may drain public hospitals or engineering departments of scarce professionals. Neither entrepreneurship campaigns nor artificial intelligence investment can substitute for functioning contract enforcement, competitive markets, and reliable basic education. A meaningful social contract depends on whether young people can translate schooling and work into predictable income, housing, health care, and the ability to plan their lives.

Section 72.10

Women, Family, Citizenship, and Social Transformation

Women's lives across the Arab world vary by country, class, region, legal status, and religion, and a single "Arab woman" does not exist. Women now outnumber men in universities in many countries, and women have become doctors, judges, engineers, ministers, scholars, journalists, and entrepreneurs. Yet participation in paid work remains among the lowest in the world by global comparison, parliamentary representation is uneven, and unpaid care work falls disproportionately on women, as the UN Women and ESCWA regional gender assessment documents.

Legal frameworks matter. Family law, often derived from interpretations of Islamic law or from the personal-status systems of recognized religious communities, governs marriage, divorce, custody, and inheritance. Tunisia's 1956 personal-status code and later reforms went far; Morocco's family code was revised in 2004 and further reform proposals were advanced in the 2020s; Gulf states have codified family law with varying degrees of change; and Lebanon retains separate religious personal-status courts. Nationality laws in many countries limit women's ability to pass citizenship to children, with consequences for stateless or foreign-born children. Law, however, is not the same as practice: women's access to courts, property, and credit depends on enforcement, social expectations, and economic independence.

Regional variation is large. In the Gulf, women's labor participation and public roles have risen quickly in recent years, but within systems in which guardianship rules and political participation remain limited. In North Africa, women have long participated in professions and politics but face high unemployment. In the Levant and Nile Valley, urban-rural and class differences are acute. In conflict-affected communities in Syria, Gaza, Sudan, and Yemen, women have been heads of households, caregivers, humanitarian workers, and targets of sexual violence, and displacement has reshaped family responsibilities for children and elderly relatives.

Women have also been central actors in protest and civil society: in Sudan's resistance committees, Algeria's Hirak, Lebanon's 2019 protests, and the humanitarian and professional organizations of Syria and Palestine. Avoiding a uniform portrait means acknowledging both measurable constraints and the many forms of agency, and recognizing that legal liberalization does not produce identical outcomes in different societies.

Women's legal position cannot be inferred from one celebrated reform or national ranking. Morocco's 2004 family-code changes sought to alter marital rights and responsibilities within a specific legal system; Tunisia has its own post-independence family-law history and subsequent debates about equality; and Saudi Arabia's changes to travel rules and labor participation occurred within a different institutional and legal framework. Across countries, nationality laws can affect whether women transmit citizenship to spouses or children, while procedures for marriage, divorce, guardianship, property registration, and inheritance vary. Even an enforceable right may be difficult to exercise where legal assistance is expensive or where administrative officials exercise discretion. Historians should ask whether a change appeared in a statute, was implemented by courts, or affected the daily experience of women of different incomes and locations. Such distinctions prevent celebratory or dismissive generalization alike.

Education and paid employment have changed family strategies, yet unpaid care remains central. A professional woman may have access to university training and salaried work but still assume responsibility for children or older relatives when affordable care services are unavailable. Conflict can intensify those burdens: widows and displaced households must navigate lost documents, unsafe transport, disrupted schools, and uncertain property rights. Migrant domestic workers, including women from Africa and Asia, occupy another position in the social economy; their legal status and labor protections cannot be represented by statistics for female citizens. Political participation also extends beyond parliamentary seats, embracing local charities, unions, teaching, medicine, journalism, and civic mobilization. The historical question is how formal institutions, economic opportunity, and family responsibilities jointly shape autonomy, not whether any one reform has transformed all women's lives.

The effects differ even within a single household or generation. Older women may have experienced limited access to secondary school but possess extensive commercial and community networks, while their daughters hold university degrees yet confront housing costs and competitive employment. Rural women engaged in agriculture may work long hours without being recorded as salaried workers; urban entrepreneurs may be formally registered but lack affordable credit. Survey design, definitions of employment, and the invisibility of unpaid labor shape the numbers used to describe these experiences. For comparative study, students should assemble at least one legal indicator, one educational indicator, and one employment indicator, each with a source and observation year. No single percentage can stand for gender equality, and no country has one uniform female experience.

Section 72.11

Migration, Refugees, Migrant Labor, and the Arab Diaspora

Migration is an old feature of Arab history, from the Arab-Islamic conquests and Mediterranean trade to Lebanese emigration to the Americas in the late nineteenth century and Palestinian displacement in 1948. Modern categories need distinguishing: voluntary labor migrants, professionals, refugees (people who have crossed an international border and have protection claims), asylum-seekers, internally displaced persons, returnees, and diaspora communities established over generations.

Intraregional movement is substantial. Since the oil boom of the 1970s, millions of Egyptians, Sudanese, Yemenis, Jordanians, Palestinians, and others have worked in the Gulf, alongside very large numbers from South and Southeast Asia. ESCWA's 2025 research on migration emphasizes the continuing significance of such movements, a corrective to the assumption that Arab migration flows principally toward Europe. Movement toward Europe, North America, and Australia is nonetheless important, including professional migration from Lebanon, Egypt, Syria, and North Africa, and irregular crossings of the Mediterranean.

Migrant workers in the Gulf are in many states the majority of the labor force while remaining outside citizenship. Recruitment systems, fees, contracts, and the sponsorship (kafala) rules that tied workers' residence to employers produced dependence and vulnerability; reforms since 2020 in Qatar and Saudi Arabia have eased some restrictions, though enforcement, wage theft, heat exposure, and housing conditions remain subjects of reporting by international organizations and researchers. Remittances are a major source of income for origin countries.

Displacement from wars has been vast. Syria's war created millions of refugees, primarily in Turkey, Lebanon, Jordan, Iraq, and Egypt, and millions more displaced inside Syria; some returned after December 2024, but return depends on security, property, services, and documentation. Sudan's war created the world's largest displacement crisis, with large numbers fleeing to Chad, South Sudan, Egypt, and elsewhere. Palestinians are displaced across generations in Gaza, the West Bank, Jordan, Lebanon, and Syria, and Gaza's war displaced most of its population within the Strip. Yemen and Iraq have long-standing displacement populations.

Host societies absorb costs and benefits unevenly. Lebanon and Jordan, small economies, have hosted proportionally very large refugee populations; labor markets, housing, schools, and politics have been strained, and public debate about refugees can be intense. Diaspora communities maintain businesses, cultural associations, universities, political advocacy, and transnational families, and are important in language preservation and in funding and shaping debate at home. The central problem is how migration connects Arab societies across borders while exposing inequalities in citizenship, opportunity, and protection.

The geography of labor migration reflects the twentieth-century growth of oil economies and earlier histories of Indian Ocean and Mediterranean exchange. Workers from Egypt, Sudan, Jordan, Yemen, and other Arab states joined migrants from South and Southeast Asia in the construction, domestic service, health, transport, and hospitality sectors of the Gulf. Their situations vary by employer and national law. The term kafala commonly describes sponsorship arrangements that historically tied residence to an employer, but labor reforms have differed between states and legal change does not guarantee effective enforcement. Recruitment fees, passport retention, wage delays, overcrowded housing, and occupational injury present particular risks for vulnerable workers; other migrants enter highly paid professional markets. A sound account should distinguish low-wage temporary migration from settled citizenship, and the rights of workers from the economic needs of employers. It should not assume all noncitizens share identical conditions.

The diaspora extends well beyond recent wage employment. Arab-origin families in Brazil, Argentina, the United States, Canada, France, and West Africa may have roots reaching back generations and differ substantially in language, religion, class, and political identity. Some retain Arabic or develop institutions around religious and cultural heritage, while others are connected chiefly by family histories or commercial networks. Remittances can support relatives' education or housing, but they may also reflect exclusion from decent work in the country of origin. Students should examine how associations, investment, journalism, and artistic production sustain transnational ties without assuming that expatriate communities speak with a single political voice. Diaspora identification may change as successive generations reinterpret ancestry, language, and national citizenship.

Forced migration raises different legal and moral questions. A person displaced within Sudan is not statistically the same as a Sudanese refugee crossing into Chad or Egypt; a family returning to a ruined neighborhood may remain in acute need after ceasing to count as displaced. Palestinians possess distinct historical refugee institutions and registration arrangements, while Syrians, Yemenis, and others face varying asylum and residency regimes. Host communities also experience pressure on housing, health care, labor markets, and municipal infrastructure; their needs should be acknowledged without portraying refugees as an undifferentiated burden. Humanitarian datasets are often operational instruments assembled from incomplete registrations, access-limited surveys, and changing population movements. The November 2026 Sudan statement illustrates why totals for shelter needs, returns, people displaced internally, and people abroad must remain separate categories rather than being added together.

Section 72.12

Cities, Housing, Infrastructure, and Everyday Life in the Contemporary Arab World

Most Arabs now live in cities. Cairo, with a metropolitan population in the tens of millions, is one of the world's largest urban areas; Casablanca, Algiers, Tunis, Amman, Beirut, Baghdad, Alexandria, Khartoum, Riyadh, Jeddah, Dubai, Doha, and Kuwait City are major centers. Their histories differ: medieval cities such as Cairo, Fez, Damascus, and Aleppo gained colonial-era extensions and then postcolonial growth; Gulf cities grew from modest port towns into metropolises in a few decades.

Contemporary urban landscapes include planned districts, enormous infrastructure and real-estate projects (Egypt's New Administrative Capital east of Cairo, Saudi Arabia's Riyadh expansion and giga-projects, Dubai's towers), historic cores facing both preservation and tourism pressure, informal neighborhoods housing large shares of residents in Cairo, Casablanca, Baghdad, and elsewhere, refugee settlements, and post-conflict areas. Land ownership, planning institutions, municipal finance, and real-estate investment determine who can afford to live where.

Everyday consequences include housing affordability, commuting and congestion, summer electricity cuts in Iraq and Lebanon, water interruptions, waste management, uneven public transport, and access to schools and hospitals. War has destroyed urban infrastructure on a vast scale: Gaza, where OCHA reported in late 2025 that about 91 percent of homes were destroyed or damaged; Syria's Aleppo, Homs, and Damascus suburbs; Khartoum; Sana'a and Hodeidah; Mosul and Benghazi; and parts of southern Lebanon and Beirut, where the August 2020 port explosion destroyed districts. Emergency shelter, which is temporary, must be distinguished from permanent housing reconstruction.

These cities are where contemporary Arab history is lived: in neighborhoods, workplaces, schools, transport systems, and households, and not only in governments.

Compare everyday mobility in a metropolitan capital with life in a provincial town. In Cairo, distance between affordable housing and employment can produce long commutes and complicated reliance on informal transport; in Dubai, high-capacity infrastructure coexists with a labor market divided by citizenship and income; in Beirut, economic crisis and infrastructure failures affect households that once relied on formal banking and public utilities. A resident's access to jobs can depend more on affordable travel than on national employment programs, while an apparently modern apartment may be unsafe if electricity, drainage, or waste collection is unreliable. Urban history therefore requires attention to neighborhoods and service networks rather than skyline photographs. The relevant institutions include municipal councils, water utilities, transport authorities, land registries, ministries, private developers, and informal neighborhood associations, whose jurisdictions often overlap.

Rapid urban development can redistribute both benefits and risk. Investment in airports, commercial centers, or coastal redevelopment may create jobs while raising land prices and displacing tenants; informal settlement residents may enjoy strong social networks despite precarious tenure and deficient services. In Morocco, Casablanca's industrial and transport expansion differs from the historical conservation problems of older medinas; in Egypt, new administrative and residential districts create an additional layer of metropolitan planning rather than automatically resolving pressure on the existing city. Climate stresses are socially uneven as well: households with insulated buildings, dependable electricity, and access to cooling cope with heat differently from those in dense low-income neighborhoods. The same flood can be a temporary inconvenience for one household and a loss of housing or livelihood for another. Students should connect spatial maps with testimony and administrative evidence, not infer wellbeing from infrastructure expenditure alone.

War makes the relationship between land and citizenship especially visible. In Gaza, Syria, Sudan, and parts of Yemen, people have lost housing, records, public offices, and the means to prove ownership or tenancy. Reconstructing an apartment block is not sufficient if families cannot establish claims, reach workplaces, receive electricity, or access schools. A durable recovery must reconcile competing property documents, address unexploded ordnance, protect tenants, and provide transparent mechanisms for public decisions about relocation. Displacement can reshape urban economies as families move into existing districts and host cities expand services under pressure. Conversely, poorly financed rebuilding can entrench inequality by restoring the most commercially valuable areas first. These processes resemble historical patterns of urban reconstruction examined in earlier chapters, but their contemporary technologies and legal institutions create new questions of documentation and accountability.

Housing, Services, and Inequality in the Arab City

Documented patterns allow comparison among households without inventing representative individuals. A middle-income household in a planned district of a Gulf city typically has reliable electricity, desalinated water, and private transport, while lower-paid migrant workers in the same city often live in employer-provided or shared accommodation at a distance from city centers. In Cairo or Casablanca, many families live in informally built neighborhoods that developed without formal planning approval, often with strong community networks but uneven provision of water, sewerage, and public transport. In Beirut and Baghdad, households have adapted to unreliable public electricity by buying from private generators, a cost that falls heavily on the poor. In Gaza, Sudan, and Syria, displaced households live in damaged buildings, schools, tents, or host families' homes, and the form and timing of reconstruction depends on funding, security, and property documentation.

Section 72.13

Water Scarcity, River Systems, Agriculture, and Environmental Governance

Chapter 3 showed that water has structured settlement since antiquity, and it remains the Arab world's defining resource constraint. The principal sources are the Nile (shared by Egypt, Sudan, and upstream states), the Tigris and Euphrates (rising in Turkey and flowing through Syria and Iraq), the deep fossil aquifers of North Africa and the Arabian Peninsula, smaller mountain and coastal systems such as the Jordan, Litani, and Atlas water systems, and increasingly desalinated seawater.

Demand has grown through population increase, agricultural expansion, industrial and urban consumption, and groundwater extraction that in places exceeds recharge. Climate variability adds stress. Transboundary disputes are central: Ethiopia's Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam on the Blue Nile, formally inaugurated in 2025 after years of negotiation, raised Egyptian and Sudanese concerns over flows, particularly during droughts; Turkey's upstream dams on the Tigris and Euphrates have contributed to Iraq's water crises, which also reflect Iraqi infrastructure, agriculture, and governance problems.

Careful vocabulary matters: water scarcity, water stress, drought, poor management, inadequate infrastructure, and unequal distribution are different problems, though they interact. A city may have enough water in principle and still lack it because pipes leak or the grid fails. Responses include desalination (energy-intensive, especially important in the Gulf), wastewater reuse, irrigation reform, regulating groundwater, and financing infrastructure. Agricultural policy is central because farming uses the largest share of water.

ESCWA's tenth Water Development Report (2025) identifies population growth, low water-use efficiency, climate change, conflict, and overextraction as interacting pressures. Historians should avoid determinism: scarcity does not inevitably produce war, and climate change alone does not explain any specific political conflict. Chapter 3's lesson holds: environment shapes the range of possibilities without dictating choices.

Water systems make the dependence of modern states on older geography particularly clear. The Nile sustains dense settlement in Egypt and Sudan while much of its watershed lies beyond their borders; upstream Ethiopian dam development raises questions about filling schedules, electricity generation, drought management, and data sharing. Egypt's population is concentrated around the river and delta, making reliability of agricultural irrigation and municipal supply politically central. In Iraq and Syria, the Tigris and Euphrates originate outside many downstream farming districts and are affected by dams, diversion, changing runoff, and war-damaged infrastructure. A description of a river as a 'national resource' therefore conceals the institutional need for transboundary cooperation. The historical record includes both conflicts and negotiations, and geography alone predicts neither. Agreements on monitoring, reservoir operations, and emergency drought response can matter as much as annual rainfall totals.

The region's groundwater presents an equally important governance problem. North African aquifers can cross borders and refill slowly; in the Arabian Peninsula, extraction of ancient groundwater has supported agriculture but cannot necessarily continue indefinitely at the same rate. Desalination is significant along Gulf and Red Sea coasts, yet requires capital, energy, intake facilities, distribution networks, and management of concentrated brine. Wastewater reuse and leakage reduction can increase effective supply without increasing river flows. These options have different costs and environmental effects, so it is misleading to propose one universal technical solution. Students should distinguish renewable freshwater availability from gross water withdrawals and separate household access from agricultural allocation. ESCWA's tenth regional water report emphasizes the combined effects of climate change, population growth, low use efficiency, conflict, and over-extraction, reinforcing the need to analyze institutions along with hydrology.

The term 'water stress' refers to pressure on available resources, but its numerical expression depends on the measure used. A basin-wide average may hide a city with intermittent piped water or a farming district dependent on expensive groundwater pumping. Poor water quality can make apparently available water unsuitable for drinking, while electricity interruptions can stop pumps and treatment plants even when reservoirs are full. In war-affected communities, damaged pipes and contaminated wells create public-health risks that do not appear in simple rainfall charts. The history of irrigation, canal maintenance, and municipal water supply from ancient Mesopotamia through modern ministries illustrates continuity in the need for collective management. What changes are population density, technical capacity, the political scale of systems, and the consequences when institutions fail to maintain them.

Historical Vocabulary
Water Scarcity, Water Stress, and Aridity

Aridity is a climatic condition of low rainfall (Chapter 3). Water stress and water scarcity are measures of the relation between available water and demand, usually expressed per person; a country can be arid yet manage water well, or less arid and mismanage it. Drought is a temporary shortfall. Confusing these terms produces fatalistic claims that blame nature for problems that policy and infrastructure also create.

Section 72.14

Climate Change, Food Security, Energy Transition, and Environmental Resilience

Rising temperatures, extreme heat, drought, flooding, and changing rainfall affect the region unevenly. The Gulf and Iraq face some of the world's most severe summer heat; Mediterranean and Atlantic North Africa face longer droughts; low-lying coastal cities such as Alexandria and the Nile Delta, and Gulf coastal zones, face sea-level and storm risks; mountain communities and desert cities each have distinct vulnerabilities. Floods in Libya's Derna in 2023, when two dams failed after a storm, killed thousands and showed how weather extremes interact with poor maintenance and political fragmentation.

Food security is a related problem. Many Arab states import large shares of staple grains, so prices and supply routes matter, as the 2022 and 2026 shocks showed. Heat affects public health and energy demand, especially air conditioning; water infrastructure and agriculture feel pressure; and household livelihoods, particularly of farmers and pastoralists, are affected. Environmental hazards are more destructive when planning is weak, housing inadequate, poverty widespread, or war has disrupted services.

Responses include solar and wind power projects in Morocco, Egypt, the UAE, and Saudi Arabia, energy efficiency, desalination, changes in cropping, conservation, and infrastructure investment. Oil- and gas-exporting states balance current petroleum revenue against future energy industries. Students should distinguish announced net-zero commitments and investment programs from independently measured emissions reductions. The question that ends the section is how adaptive capacity differs between wealthy states, lower-income countries, and societies in armed conflict, a difference that makes the same climate shock a manageable stress in one place and a catastrophe in another.

Climate hazards should be examined through exposure and vulnerability, not reduced to a single rising-temperature curve. Extreme heat affects outdoor labor, construction schedules, road surfaces, electricity demand, and the health of older people; flooding can damage informal settlements in cities where drainage has not kept pace with building; prolonged drought can reduce yields and increase dependence on imports. Coastal zones near the Nile delta and North African ports face combinations of sea-level rise, groundwater salinity, erosion, and concentrated infrastructure. Yet the effects vary according to building standards, emergency warnings, public health systems, and households' ability to move or adapt. The same climate event can therefore produce different losses in countries or neighborhoods with similar physical exposure. A historian's task is to explain those institutional differences, not claim that climatic stress alone causes political violence.

Food security also links local agriculture to world markets. Many Arab countries import substantial grain or animal feed, leaving consumers vulnerable to international prices and shipping disruption; irrigated regions may produce high-value crops but demand large quantities of scarce water. Governments choose among subsidies, targeted household assistance, storage, import diversification, crop-policy changes, and investment in agricultural research. Each entails fiscal or environmental trade-offs. A cheap staple subsidy can protect poorer families but may be expensive or poorly targeted, whereas removing it suddenly can expose households to shocks. During the 2026 Hormuz crisis, disruption to shipping and energy trade also affected logistics costs, demonstrating that water, food, energy, and transport cannot be analyzed as separate policy compartments. Effective resilience requires coordination across institutions that often have historically distinct powers and budgets.

Energy transition creates another set of contrasts. Desert sunshine offers significant potential for solar generation, but photovoltaic systems require storage, grids, maintenance, and land or rooftop access. Natural gas can support power production but remains exposed to fuel supply and export-market pressures; hydrocarbon producers face the challenge of financing new sectors with revenues from resources whose long-run demand is uncertain. A renewable-energy target is not proof of completed capacity, and installed capacity is not identical to electricity actually delivered at the moment people need it. The 2025 Saudi development report, international energy datasets, and national utility accounts can be compared, but they use different categories and fiscal assumptions. Environmental history benefits when students distinguish technological possibility from investment decisions, and announced policy from measured performance.

Section 72.15

Satellite Television, Digital Culture, Artificial Intelligence, and the New Public Sphere

Arabic-language media in the late twentieth century was dominated by state broadcasters. The launch of Al Jazeera in 1996 and the satellite channels that followed created a transnational public sphere in which news, debate, and entertainment crossed borders. By the 2010s, social media, video platforms, podcasts, and messaging services moved much political communication online, and the uprisings of 2010–2011 showed their mobilizing power. Since then Arabic-language digital production has grown across journalism, comedy, religious discussion, music, gaming, education, and e-commerce.

Governments have responded with regulation, censorship, surveillance, cybercrime laws, platform pressure, and sometimes internet shutdowns, while also promoting digital government, fintech, and technology entrepreneurship. Disinformation circulates widely during wars, including in Gaza, Sudan, and Syria, and fact-checking is difficult. Different political systems yield different limits on public debate even within a shared Arabic-language space.

Digital infrastructure and access are uneven. Gulf states have among the highest connectivity in the world; conflict-affected and poorer countries have lower access and higher costs; rural and urban communities and men and women differ in use. The International Telecommunication Union's reporting in October 2026 identified continued improvement but persistent gender differences in internet use in its Arab States reporting region, which, as with other international categories, needs to be checked against League membership.

Artificial intelligence, data centers, and digital finance have become new investment priorities, especially in the UAE and Saudi Arabia, and the World Bank's 2026 update examined AI and jobs. Students should distinguish investment in infrastructure from genuine public access and local research capacity, and recognize both opportunity and risk of exclusion, including from Arabic-language models and datasets. Arabic digital production connects people across borders while national regulation continues to shape debate.

Modern Arabic public communication has moved through several overlapping stages rather than one sudden digital revolution. Satellite television expanded transnational debate in the 1990s; online newspapers and forums offered new spaces for discussion; smartphones and video platforms made participation cheaper and more immediate for many users. News organizations, artists, teachers, religious speakers, and activists reach audiences across national borders, yet the resulting public sphere is not unregulated. Broadcasting ownership, licensing, censorship, defamation laws, platform moderation, surveillance, and advertising revenue shape which voices are visible. Audiences also differ by literacy, connectivity, age, urban location, and preferred dialect. The same Arabic hashtag may connect people in several countries while carrying different local political meanings. Students should therefore examine the infrastructures and institutions behind communication, not treat devices as independent agents of social change.

Quantitative evidence reveals both change and limits. The ITU's 2026 regional estimates report internet use in its Arab States category at approximately 74 percent, and a continuing gender gap; its gender series estimates 78 percent for men and 70 percent for women. These figures are survey-based regional estimates and do not describe every member of the Arab League. Affordability and meaningful use vary more than simple network coverage. A person may technically own a smartphone but lack regular data access, private space for study, or the skills required for professional work. Online education can serve rural students when reliable power, devices, and language-appropriate content exist; otherwise it can reproduce disparities. The history of communication thus returns to questions of schooling, income, infrastructure, and public policy explored elsewhere in the chapter.

Digital government and artificial intelligence require similar caution. Online permits, electronic payments, medical records, and customs systems can improve administrative speed and reduce some opportunities for arbitrary interference, while poor identity safeguards or limited appeal mechanisms create new vulnerabilities. Investment in data centers or AI services does not itself establish a broadly skilled workforce or independent innovation capacity. Models trained predominantly on high-resource languages may perform unevenly with regional Arabic dialects, specialized terminology, or smaller languages spoken within Arab states. Questions of privacy, employment, censorship, and public procurement matter alongside technical achievement. The World Bank's October 2026 assessment suggests near-term productivity gains from AI in some occupations but highlights structural gaps; its region is MENAAP, and its conclusions must not be read as uniform predictions for every Arab society.

Section 72.16

Language, Religion, Memory, Literature, and the Diversity of Arab Identity

This section draws together the cultural threads of the textbook. Arabic is characterized by diglossia: Modern Standard Arabic, descended from the classical language and revitalized by the Nahda (Chapter 51), is used in writing, formal speech, and news, while regional dialects, from Moroccan Darija to Egyptian, Levantine, Gulf, and Iraqi Arabic, are used in daily life and increasingly in social media, music, and film. Religious scholarship, Qur'anic recitation, publishing, translation, and the world of literary prizes link readers across borders.

Other languages and cultural traditions are integral. Amazigh languages have gained official recognition in Morocco and Algeria, Kurdish in Iraq, and Nubian, Somali, Syriac-Aramaic, Armenian, and others persist with varying institutional support. Language policy remains contested: debates concern the place of French in North Africa, English in the Gulf, and the status of minority languages in education.

Islam is central to the lives of most people in the Arab world, but it is not a single political program, and Sunni, Shi'a, Ibadi, Sufi, and secular expressions coexist. Christians, notably Copts, Maronites, Greek Orthodox, and others, Druze, Yazidis, Jews, Baha'is, and non-religious people are part of the region's history, though many communities have declined through migration and conflict.

Film, literature, music, visual arts, theater, architecture, sports, and digital cultural production express both national and transnational identity: Egyptian cinema and song have long reached the whole region; Gulf states now fund museums, festivals, and film industries; and Palestinian, Syrian, Lebanese, Sudanese, and Iraqi artists work under conditions of exile and war. Heritage and memory are political: debates over colonial memory, historical narratives, monuments, archaeological sites damaged in war, and who owns or interprets the past involve states, independent artists, diaspora networks, and new historical scholarship.

The central question is what creates cultural connections across Arab societies and how they coexist with distinct national, ethnic, regional, religious, and political identities. The answer developed here is that connections are real, historically made, and layered, and that they never required uniformity.

Language policy is a practical question of educational access as well as cultural identity. Modern Standard Arabic connects textbooks, news broadcasts, scholarship, and formal administration across national boundaries, while everyday conversation employs diverse spoken varieties. In the Maghreb, Amazigh languages have long literary and oral traditions; French remains influential in aspects of higher education and professional life; and English is important in many Gulf labor markets and technical institutions. Kurdish, Syriac, Nubian, Somali, Comorian languages, and many others preserve histories that cannot be described as dialects of Arabic. Children may encounter one language at home, another in formal schooling, and a third in employment. Differences between these registers can create opportunities for multilingual competence or barriers to learning depending on how institutions teach and assess students.

Religious communities also connect the region through different networks and legal arrangements. Sunni and Shi'a traditions, Ibadi scholarship, Christian churches, Druze communities, Jewish heritage, and other practices have histories older than many modern state borders. Religious belonging may shape education, family law, pilgrimage, charitable institutions, and local calendars without determining a uniform political stance. State policies toward religious institutions range widely, and official categories can conceal dissent within communities. For historians, the relevant evidence includes architecture, manuscripts, school records, legal texts, artistic representations, and the memories of families who migrated or were displaced. The history of Jewish communities in North Africa and the Middle East, for example, cannot be reduced to their greatly diminished contemporary numbers; their earlier institutions, trade, and cultural production remain part of the regional record.

Cultural heritage can be both a source of connection and a subject of dispute. Archaeological sites, medieval cities, colonial buildings, and contemporary museums may carry different meanings for governments, residents, displaced communities, and scholars. Restoration of a historic district can preserve craftsmanship while turning homes into expensive tourist accommodation; official commemorations may celebrate some histories and marginalize others. Film, popular music, poetry, novels, calligraphy, fashion, and digital art allow competing interpretations to circulate, often across diasporic networks. A concluding history should acknowledge that 'Arab culture' does not mean a single canon. It is a field of shared references, translation, borrowing, disagreement, and creative reinvention, in which people can participate through language or identification without accepting a single political program.

Section 72.17

Governance, Political Legitimacy, Civil Society, and the Continuing Demand for Accountability

Contemporary Arab governments take several institutional forms: hereditary monarchies (Morocco, Jordan, and the Gulf states), presidential or semi-presidential republics, a federal republic with powerful sectarian arrangements in Iraq, a confessional power-sharing system in Lebanon, transitional governments in Syria and Libya, and divided authorities in Sudan and Yemen. Each has constitutions, legislatures, courts, security institutions, and in some cases parties; the formal text does not reveal where power lies. In many states, security services, ruling families, the military, and business networks hold decisive influence regardless of what parliaments do.

The protest movements of Chapters 70 and 71 left a continuing legacy without being retold here. Mass protests in Sudan, Algeria, Iraq, and Lebanon in 2019 demanded accountability, and while several did not produce lasting institutional change, the demand persists. Civil society, including professional associations, women's organizations, trade unions, humanitarian networks, independent journalists, local civic groups, and diaspora advocacy, continues to operate, often under legal restrictions. Political repression, corruption, inadequate services, financial crisis, and disputes over public resources shape the conditions in which it works.

Legitimacy cannot be measured by elections alone: competitive elections may be limited by restrictions, while governments without elections may claim legitimacy through performance, religion, or dynastic tradition. And accountability is not synonymous with efficiency: a government may deliver services well without being answerable to citizens, and an accountable one may deliver poorly. Historians should therefore distinguish formal participation from actual distribution of power.

Political legitimacy has several dimensions that cannot be reduced to constitutional labels. A monarchy may possess a consultative assembly and extensive administrative capacity while reserving key decisions to the ruler; a republic may conduct competitive local elections but retain concentrated executive and security power; a transitional authority may promise broad inclusion yet lack effective control over armed forces or courts. Comparing these systems requires separate measures of legislative independence, judicial review, accountability for abuses, freedom of association, and predictable delivery of services. A fast passport office is evidence of administrative effectiveness, not necessarily of citizens' ability to change policy or challenge officials. Conversely, contested elections may demonstrate political participation while public finance remains opaque. Historians should examine how specific institutions actually operate rather than treating words such as reform, stability, or democracy as self-explanatory.

Civil society survives through varied forms. Lawyers challenge administrative decisions, medical associations defend professional standards, neighborhood committees organize basic services, and trade unions negotiate working conditions; women's organizations, journalists, and religious charities perform different functions depending on the country. Some operate with official support, others under restrictive licensing or threat of prosecution, and many occupy a space between cooperation and dissent. In post-conflict settings the most urgent institutions may be local networks delivering education, identifying missing persons, or mediating property disputes. Their work is historically significant because it shows how public authority extends beyond central governments, but their existence alone does not prove a pluralistic political order. The relevant questions are whether they can organize independently, obtain resources, represent members, and hold authorities accountable without retaliation.

The 2010–2020 uprisings examined in Chapter 71 changed the repertoire of political action without producing a single regional outcome. Some governments responded with constitutional amendments or administrative modernization; others relied on surveillance, prosecution, armed force, or negotiated power-sharing. By the 2020s fiscal stress and digital technologies added new challenges: online complaint portals might increase responsiveness, but they might also generate data accessible to coercive agencies; cash transfers might protect households, but their allocation could reproduce patronage. Public confidence depends on consistency between stated rights and daily practice. Students should compare institutions over time, identify who can demand remedies, and note whose experiences are excluded from official performance reports. This is more revealing than ranking countries on one undifferentiated measure of governance quality.

Section 72.18

Post-Conflict Recovery, Reconstruction, and the Uneven Rebuilding of States

Wars discussed in Chapters 68–71 and in this chapter have left enormous reconstruction needs in Syria, Sudan, Iraq, Yemen, Libya, Lebanon, and the Palestinian territories. These needs are not of one kind. Physical reconstruction (housing, hospitals, schools, ports, power stations, roads, water networks) differs from humanitarian relief, from institutional recovery (courts, police, administration), from political reconciliation, from security-sector reform, and from transitional justice. Money alone addresses only some.

Other obstacles include corruption risks in contracting, land and property claims where records were destroyed or documents lost, public debt, sanctions, unexploded ordnance, missing persons, and the documentation displaced families need to return. The difference between internationally recognized governments and the authorities that actually control territory or administer services complicates who may sign contracts and receive funds. International financial institutions, the UN, regional development funds, national governments, private investors, and community organizations all play parts, with different priorities.

Iraq's experience since 2003, Lebanon's after 1990 and again after 2020, and Syria's after 2024 show that rebuilt buildings do not guarantee inclusive institutions. The central problem is whether reconstruction can create reliable public services and inclusive institutions without reproducing the political and economic structures that contributed to earlier crises.

Reconstruction begins with the restoration of safety and legal recognition. A family cannot rebuild securely if the route home contains unexploded ordnance, if a checkpoint prevents passage, or if a lost deed cannot be replaced. Syria's transition faces these problems alongside reform of detention and security institutions; Sudan's rival military authorities contest the basic conditions for nationwide administration; Lebanon's damaged public finance complicates infrastructure investment; and Gaza's destruction has produced extraordinary housing, medical, and sanitation needs. Each case has a different history of ownership, public authority, and war damage. It is therefore misleading to speak of one regional reconstruction program. The sequence of security, registration, temporary shelter, public-service restoration, and long-term housing differs among places and cannot always be neatly separated.

Money alone does not resolve disputes over who controls contracts and public institutions. A donor can fund a water-treatment plant, but someone must maintain the intake, pay staff, supply chemicals, provide electricity, and ensure equitable distribution. A repaired school cannot teach effectively if teachers have fled, salaries are unpaid, or children lack safe transport. Private investors may demand property certainty while displaced tenants require protections against eviction. International sanctions and banking restrictions may complicate otherwise legitimate procurement, even when humanitarian exemptions exist. These administrative details shape whether capital spending translates into social recovery. Historians should compare public tender records, municipal budgets, aid-monitoring reports, and residents' testimonies, distinguishing announced commitments from works actually completed and functioning.

Transitional justice adds an obligation not captured by the language of rebuilding. Families of the disappeared may seek truth and records before they can pursue compensation; survivors of torture or sexual violence may need protection and independent investigation; rival communities may disagree about memorials, prosecutions, or institutional vetting. Neither a political settlement nor an amnesty automatically creates trust, while trials without due process can reproduce grievances. The destruction of archives makes evidence preservation particularly important. Community-level rebuilding can succeed in limited ways amid national disagreement, but its durability is threatened if armed groups continue to control revenues or force. A careful chapter should avoid presenting a new skyline as proof of reconciliation. Reconstruction is a historical struggle over authority, memory, distributive choices, and whose losses are officially recognized.

Section 72.19

Comparing the Contemporary Arab World: Divergent National Trajectories and Shared Historical Structures

Comparison requires a framework that does not rank societies on a single scale of "modernization" or "success." This section compares trajectories along eight dimensions: state capacity, public finance, economic opportunity, social mobility, access to services, political participation, climate resilience, and the effects of international relationships. Four caveats apply. Each trajectory is internally varied; national averages hide regional and class differences; evidence is dated; and categories do not map neatly onto international datasets.

Broadly, Gulf energy exporters pursue diversification with large fiscal resources but exposure to energy disruption and reliance on migrant labor. North African states manage differing combinations of reform, demographic pressure, tourism and agricultural exposure, and ties to Europe. Levantine societies are affected by conflict, migration, and unresolved sovereignty. Nile Valley societies confront demographic density, water dependence, and political pressure. The Horn of Africa and island states have distinct geographic, transnational, and fiscal conditions. The shared historical structures that cut across these groups include the legacies of colonial and mandate borders, resource-based public finance, the social contract, a young population, water constraint, and connected media and labor networks.

Comparison becomes defensible only when unlike cases are placed on explicitly stated measures. The UNDP Human Development Report 2025 offers one internally consistent dataset for 2023, combining life expectancy, education, and income into the Human Development Index. Its Table 1 reports HDI values of 0.940 for the UAE, 0.900 for Saudi Arabia, 0.754 for Egypt, 0.710 for Morocco, 0.695 for Iraq, 0.511 for Sudan, and 0.404 for Somalia. These are dated composite estimates, not 2026 measures, and they do not directly measure democracy, freedom, or the quality of humanitarian access. They do demonstrate why a single Arab-world average can conceal striking contrasts in public health, schooling, and income. The comparative teaching table below also records a life-expectancy component, so students can distinguish a composite index from one of its inputs.

These contrasts arise from histories, not immutable national characteristics. The UAE and Saudi Arabia have benefited from large hydrocarbon-linked fiscal resources and international service industries, although the distribution of benefits between citizens and migrant workers differs. Egypt combines a large population, a long administrative history, extensive education, and serious employment and financial constraints. Morocco's agriculture, manufacturing, and tourism connect it to weather and European demand, while Iraq's petroleum wealth has coexisted with political conflict and uneven infrastructure. Sudan's 2023–2026 war devastated services and displaced people; Somalia's institutions reflect distinct histories of state fragmentation, regional administration, and Indian Ocean commerce. An HDI snapshot cannot explain these pathways alone; it must be read alongside historical events and institutional evidence.

A useful comparison therefore proceeds in stages. First establish a shared observation year and common definitions; next identify a country's political institutions, economic structure, environmental conditions, and recent conflicts; finally test whether a numerical difference corresponds to the explanation proposed. Some important societies cannot be represented safely by recent comparable series because war has disrupted data collection. Student tables should mark missing observations rather than invent them or silently replace them with older figures. The point of comparison is not to discover which population is most 'modern.' It is to identify the different combinations of capabilities, vulnerabilities, choices, and historical constraints that shape social experience. Such analysis is more faithful to the textbook's central claim than a single ranking of states.

Section 72.20

What Is the Arab World? Historical Continuity, Pluralism, and the Future of a Regional Idea

This textbook began by asking how the societies and territories now associated with the Arab world developed over time. The answer has been layered. Part I showed lands shaped by aridity, rivers, oases, and mobility. Part II showed civilizations in Mesopotamia, Egypt, Nubia, the Levant, North Africa, and Arabia that were not Arab and cannot be called so. Late antiquity brought Byzantine and Sasanian frontiers, and the rise of Islam in Arabia produced the first Arab-Islamic polity. Conquest created an empire in which Arabic became the language of government and, gradually and unevenly, of daily life; Arabization and Islamization proceeded separately, as Chapter 24 showed. The Abbasid, Fatimid, Maghrebi, Mamluk, and Ottoman worlds were cosmopolitan and plural. The nineteenth century brought reform, the Nahda, and European empire; the twentieth brought mandates, nationalism, independence, oil, and war. The twenty-first has brought uprisings, collapse, rebuilding, and new rivalries.

The long history of the lands and peoples of the region is therefore not the same as the history of Arab nationalism or of the modern Arab state system. The ancient societies were not already Arab, and the modern idea of an Arab nation is a product of the late Ottoman and mandate eras. Yet neither can the connections be dismissed: language, literature, religion, trade, pilgrimage, and memory created networks that outlasted empires.

The League of Arab States is one institutional expression of that connection. Its pact emphasizes cooperation while safeguarding each member's independence and sovereignty, and its history shows both the value of a regional forum and the limits of its enforcement power. The tension between national sovereignty and regional cooperation, between cultural identification and political diversity, and between continuity and change remains unresolved.

"Arab" itself can mean language, cultural participation, historical memory, political solidarity, citizenship, or personal identification, and no one answer is universal. An Amazigh Moroccan, a Kurdish Iraqi, a Copt in Egypt, a Palestinian in the diaspora, a Gulf citizen, and a Sudanese Nubian may each relate differently to the term. The Arab world cannot be reduced to political institutions alone, and it cannot be assumed that every person in a League member state identifies the same way.

The Arab world is therefore best understood as a historically constructed, continually changing field of relationships among states, societies, languages, religions, economies, environments, and diasporas. Its present was made by choices, accidents, and contests that could have gone otherwise, and its future is not written here. What this book offers is not a prediction but a way of reading: with attention to evidence, to difference, and to the long time in which present arguments take shape.

The history told in these volumes began long before either the Arabic language or the League of Arab States occupied the place they do today. The civilizations of Mesopotamia and Egypt, the maritime societies of the Levant, the peoples of North Africa, and the commercial networks of Arabia possessed their own political orders and identities. The rise of Islam and the spread of Arabic changed patterns of government, scholarship, trade, and religious life, but neither process erased all earlier languages or institutions. Under the Abbasids, Fatimids, Mamluks, Ottomans, and other dynasties, the region was repeatedly connected to political and cultural worlds extending beyond its present borders. The history of the Arab world must therefore be written as a succession of transformations, not as the gradual unveiling of a fixed ancient essence.

European imperialism, Ottoman reform, the mandate system, independent state-building, pan-Arabism, and petroleum development subsequently gave the region new boundaries and institutions. The Pact of the League of Arab States, signed by six governments on March 22, 1945 and by Yemen on May 5, attempted to formalize solidarity while preserving each member's sovereignty. That bargain helps explain both the persistence and the limits of regional cooperation. It did not abolish national citizenship or align every government's interests; nor did political rivalry erase cross-border language, cultural production, kinship, pilgrimage, labor, and education. The region's institutions are real because people founded and use them, but their authority is always historically and legally specific.

The twenty-first century illustrates the consequences of that layered history. Gulf cities have become centers of trade and capital; North African societies negotiate multilingualism, agriculture, migration, and economic reform; Palestinians continue to confront unresolved sovereignty; Syria and Sudan have faced profound crises of state power; and communities across the region respond differently to climate risk, technology, and demands for accountability. Migrants and diasporas connect these histories beyond the League's political map. Historical understanding requires both a common field of inquiry and an insistence that no one state, ethnic identity, religious current, social class, or governing institution can stand for all its people. The most important conclusion of seventy-two chapters is therefore a method: identify the period, place, actors, documents, and competing interpretations before drawing a regional conclusion.

That method should also discipline judgments about the future. A treaty may offer cooperation without settling political disagreement; a statistical improvement may conceal exclusion; a battlefield victory may leave a society's institutions shattered; and new technologies may extend both opportunity and control. None of these relationships guarantees the next outcome. The responsibility of a historical textbook is to explain the choices and inheritances that shape possibilities, not to prescribe a destiny. The Arab world remains a meaningful historical region because its peoples and institutions are connected through language, memory, exchange, politics, and shared problems. It remains diverse because those connections have never required a uniform identity or common course. That tension, rather than a prediction, provides an appropriate end to the book.

It was written by governments, not peoples: six governments signed in March 1945, Yemen signed in May, and their treaty did not represent every perspective within the much larger Arabic-speaking world.

  • From empires and caliphates to sovereign nation-states created in the twentieth century
  • From agrarian and pastoral economies to oil, services, and urban economies
  • From manuscript and oral culture to satellite and digital media
  • From the ruler-and-subject relation to citizenship claims and, unevenly, new forms of civic organization

What Stayed the Same

  • Aridity and the importance of rivers, oases, and groundwater
  • The region's role as a crossroads of trade and ideas linking Africa, Asia, and Europe
  • Diversity of peoples, languages, and religions
  • Tension between central authority and local or armed power
  • Movement of people through trade, pilgrimage, labor, and exile

Who experienced change most directly? Urban residents, migrants, young people, and those living through war experienced rapid change. Who experienced greater continuity? Rural and pastoral communities often experienced continuity in livelihood pressures, though their circumstances also changed. Why the pattern matters: It cautions against both timeless essentialism and the idea that the past has no weight in the present.

Chapter Summary

This chapter concluded the textbook by bringing the region's political, economic, social, environmental, and cultural histories into a single synthesis, observing a fixed cutoff of November 7, 2026. Its central argument is that the contemporary Arab world is neither a single political system nor a collection of unrelated states but a historically interconnected region whose societies follow increasingly varied national trajectories.

Division I examined sovereignty and conflict. The Arab world was defined through language, culture, and League membership, with the cautions that League membership is not identity and that the region contains non-Arab and non-Muslim communities. The Palestinian question in the 2020s involved fragmented territory, the October 7, 2023 attack, the devastating Gaza war, an October 2025 ceasefire that did not end violence, the ICJ's 2024 advisory opinion, and diplomatic recognition initiatives distinct from implementation.

Syria showed that the collapse of an authoritarian government on December 8, 2024 is not the same as the creation of a legitimate, secure state, and Sudan showed how a transition can fail when armed institutions retain competing claims. UN and humanitarian sources documented fragility in Syria's transition and the world's largest displacement crisis in Sudan, with careful distinctions required among categories of need and displacement.

Division II examined the Gulf and international order. Gulf monarchies differ markedly, and Saudi Arabia's Vision 2030 exemplifies diversification whose results must be separated from official claims. Hydrocarbons and chokepoints make Gulf states powerful and exposed, as the 2026 war and Hormuz disruption showed. Regional rivalries and normalizations, including the Abraham Accords and the Saudi–Iranian agreement, show continuity in rivalry and growing complexity of partnership.

Uneven development, debt, inflation, and the erosion of the social contract defined the economic picture, with the World Bank's October 2026 update offering dated evidence whose MENAAP scope must be respected. Division III turned to people. Youth populations, educational expansion, and constrained labor markets reshaped expectations; women's education and legal status advanced unevenly; migration within and beyond the region connected societies while exposing inequalities of status; and cities embodied both megaproject investment and informal, war-damaged, and refugee housing.

Division IV examined water, climate, technology, and culture. Water scarcity is a product of both physical conditions and management, with transboundary disputes on the Nile and Tigris–Euphrates. Climate change compounds vulnerability, especially where institutions are weak or conflict has intervened. Satellite and digital media created a transnational Arabic public sphere regulated differently by each state, and language, religion, memory, and the arts expressed both shared and plural identities.

Division V examined governance, reconstruction, and comparison. Political legitimacy cannot be measured by elections alone, and accountability differs from efficiency. Reconstruction requires more than capital, including property documentation, security, justice, and inclusive institutions. The comparative study showed five divergent trajectories and the shared historical structures beneath them.

The final section returned to the textbook's founding question. The long history of the region's lands and peoples is not the same as the history of Arab nationalism or the modern state system, and "Arab" has multiple meanings. The League of Arab States embodies both connection and sovereignty.

Continuity and change were visible across the whole book: aridity, crossroads, diversity, and the tension between central and local power persisted, while empires, economies, media, and citizenship changed.

The chapter also modeled a method: tying every statistic to an institution, geography, and date; distinguishing government claims, independent measurement, humanitarian need, forecasts, and legal findings; and refusing both essentialism and inevitability.

The chapter ends, as the textbook does, without prediction. The Arab world is a historically constructed and continually changing field of relationships, and understanding how it was made is the best preparation for understanding what happens next.

Key Terms

Arab world
A historical and political concept for states and societies connected by the Arabic language, culture, and institutions such as the League; not a single bloc and not coextensive with Arab identification.
League of Arab States
Regional organization founded in Cairo in 1945; twenty-two members in 2026.
Pan-Arabism
Political ideology advocating Arab unity or solidarity, strongest in the mid-twentieth century.
Sovereignty
A state's claim to supreme authority over its territory and independence from outside control.
Occupation
Effective control by a state of territory outside its recognized borders, governed in international law by specific obligations.
Advisory opinion
A legal opinion given by the ICJ at the request of a UN organ; authoritative but not a binding judgment between parties.
Green Line
The 1949 armistice line separating Israel from the West Bank, used as a reference in debates about borders.
Abraham Accords
2020 normalization agreements between Israel and the UAE and Bahrain, later joined by Morocco; Sudan announced participation.
Transitional justice
Processes of truth-seeking, accountability, reparation, and institutional reform after mass abuse.
Internally displaced person
A person forced from home who remains within their own country, distinct from a refugee.
Refugee
A person who has crossed an international border and has a recognized claim to international protection.
Rapid Support Forces (RSF)
Sudanese paramilitary force that grew from Darfur militias and fought the army from 2023.
Rentier state
A state financed chiefly by external rents such as oil and gas rather than domestic taxation.
Vision 2030
Saudi Arabia's national program, launched 2016, to diversify the economy and reform institutions.
Chokepoint
A narrow strategic passage, such as Hormuz or Bab el-Mandeb, through which trade is concentrated.
Normalization
Establishment of regular diplomatic relations between states previously in conflict or without relations.
MENAAP
The World Bank's statistical region of the Middle East, North Africa, Afghanistan, and Pakistan; not equivalent to the Arab League.
Social contract
The implicit bargain between rulers and citizens over services and political acquiescence.
Youth bulge
A demographic pattern in which young people form an unusually large share of the population.
Kafala
Sponsorship system tying migrant workers' residence and employment to a specific employer, reformed in some Gulf states after 2020.
Diaspora
A population living outside its place of origin but maintaining ties to it, often across generations.
Informal settlement
Urban housing built without formal planning approval, often housing large populations.
Water stress
A condition in which demand for water approaches or exceeds available supply.
Transboundary water
A river or aquifer shared by two or more states, requiring cooperation or producing dispute.
Desalination
Removal of salt from seawater or brackish water to produce fresh water, energy-intensive and widespread in the Gulf.
Digital divide
Unequal access to or use of digital technology across income, place, gender, or language.
Diglossia
The coexistence of a formal written register and everyday spoken varieties, characteristic of Arabic.
Legitimacy
The perceived rightfulness of a government's authority, resting on consent, tradition, performance, or other grounds.
Reconstruction
Rebuilding of physical, institutional, and social structures after war, distinct from humanitarian relief.
Data vintage
The date and source of a statistic, essential for interpreting it.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how the long history traced across seventy-one chapters weighs on the present without determining it. Water, oil, borders drawn in the twentieth century, languages and religions with deep roots, and the memory of earlier unions and losses are all present in the headlines of 2026, yet each is mediated by choices made by governments, movements, and families.

The chapter also reveals larger processes: the strain of the social contract, the relation of armed institutions to states, the dependence of regional economies on a few maritime passages, and the formation of a transnational Arabic public sphere. It connects regions that earlier chapters treated separately, showing, for example, how a regional war touched Gulf finance, Egyptian trade, Gazan aid, and Sudanese supply lines.

Many continuities survived: the importance of water, the crossroads position, plural societies, and the contest between center and periphery. Many things changed: the political forms, the economies, the media, and the demographic scale of the region.

Finally, the chapter closes the textbook by returning to its opening method: describing the lands, the peoples, and the identities carefully, in their own times, without projecting later categories backward. The Arab world is a story still being made, and the best use of this book is as a foundation for reading what comes next with evidence, humility, and attention to the diversity of those who live it.
