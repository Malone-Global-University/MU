Part IX — Reform, Nahda, and European Expansion

Algeria, Tunisia, and the Expansion of European Empire

A History of the Arab World: From Ancient Crossroads to the Modern Arab World

Chapter Opening

On 5 July 1830 the ruler of Algiers, Dey Husayn, concluded a written convention with the commander of a French expeditionary army that had landed on the coast three weeks earlier. The French side promised that Muslim worship would remain free and that the property, trade, and persons of the inhabitants would not be harmed. Within a generation, a large part of that promise had been broken, and the capture of a single city had become a conquest of a country.

The convention of 1830 is the best starting point for this chapter because it shows what the invasion of Algiers was supposed to be and what it became. The French government of Charles X sought a quick victory that would repair its prestige and settle a long dispute with the ruler of Algiers. It had no plan for a settler colony or for a war that would last decades. Yet by the 1840s France had more than a hundred thousand soldiers in Algeria, by the 1870s hundreds of thousands of European settlers were arriving, and by 1914 northern Algeria was administered as part of France, with a settler minority holding political power over a Muslim majority that lacked citizenship.

A little more than half a century after Algiers fell, a different drama unfolded in the neighboring regency of Tunis. In May 1881, in a palace outside the capital, Tunisia's ruler, the bey, signed the Treaty of Bardo with a French general whose troops had already crossed the frontier. The bey kept his throne, his court, and his title. But within two years, under the Convention of La Marsa of 1883, the real direction of his government passed to French officials. Tunisia was not annexed. It became a protectorate, a formally separate state governed under French supervision.

This chapter examines both conquests and the colonial systems they produced. It asks how French military power, financial intervention, and settler colonization transformed two societies of the central and western Maghreb. It asks how Algerians and Tunisians responded through armed resistance, state-building, religious organization, emigration, negotiation, petitioning, and the adaptation of their own institutions. And it asks why two neighboring countries, with overlapping histories and shared religious and linguistic traditions, experienced such different forms of imperial rule. Algeria became a settler colony incorporated into the French state. Tunisia kept its dynasty and its administration in outward form while decisive authority passed to France.

The chapter's chronological scope runs from the late Ottoman and Husaynid periods through 1914. It is not a history of French Algeria from 1830 to 1962, nor of Tunisian nationalism in the twentieth century. Chapter 60 treats the interwar political movements of North Africa, and Chapter 63 treats decolonization, including the Algerian War and Tunisian independence. Chapter 52 explains how the colonial orders were created and how they worked by the eve of the First World War. Chapter 53 turns to the separate pressures on Egypt, Sudan, Libya, and Morocco.

The historian of this subject faces a problem of sources. The richest archives are French: military dispatches, administrative reports, legal codes, parliamentary debates, and the memoirs of officers and settlers. These records document French policy in detail, but they present the conquest in terms of security, order, and progress, and they often describe Algerians and Tunisians only as populations to be pacified or governed. Algerian and Tunisian voices survive in Arabic chronicles, the writings of Abd al-Qadir and his circle, Khayr al-Din's political treatise, the Tunisian chronicle of Ibn Abi Dhiaf, petitions, legal records, religious endowment documents, oral poetry, and later oral histories. The chapter uses both kinds of evidence and asks, of every source, who produced it and for whom.

By the end of the chapter students should be able to explain why the French occupation of Algeria turned from an expedition into a conquest, how land law and settlement reshaped Algerian society, how the Tunisian protectorate worked, and why historians stress that colonial rule was neither inevitable in its form nor uniform across North Africa. They should also see that the societies conquered were not static or passive. Algerians and Tunisians were already living through political, religious, and economic change when Europeans arrived, and they shaped the colonial era as well as suffering under it.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Describe the political, social, and religious order of the Ottoman Regency of Algiers and of Husaynid Tunisia before French intervention.
  2. Explain the diplomatic, financial, and domestic political reasons for the French expedition of 1830, and distinguish the invasion of Algiers from the later decision to conquer the territory.
  3. Analyze Abd al-Qadir's emirate as an exercise in state-building and religious leadership as well as armed resistance.
  4. Assess the methods of the French army under Bugeaud and the historical debate over how to characterize colonial violence in Algeria.
  5. Trace how land law, confiscation, and the appropriation of endowments dispossessed Algerian communities and enabled European settlement.
  6. Distinguish French nationality, full citizenship, religiously defined personal status, and the indigénat in colonial Algeria, and compare the legal positions of Muslims, Jews, and Europeans.
  7. Evaluate the causes and consequences of the Algerian famine of the 1860s and the Mokrani Revolt of 1871.
  8. Explain how Tunisian reform, taxation, debt, and European rivalry eroded the autonomy of the Tunisian state before 1881.
  9. Interpret the Treaty of Bardo and the Convention of La Marsa and explain how the protectorate transferred authority while preserving beylical forms.
  10. Compare Algeria and Tunisia in land policy, legal status, economy, education, and resistance, and explain the divergence between settler colony and protectorate.
  11. Identify Algerian and Tunisian responses to colonial rule, from armed revolt and emigration to petition, journalism, and boycott.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did a limited naval and military expedition in 1830 become a conquest lasting decades, and how much of that outcome reflected deliberate planning rather than improvisation?
  2. What political and religious resources allowed Abd al-Qadir to build a state in the 1830s, and why did it ultimately fail?
  3. How did land law function as an instrument of colonial power, and how did its intended effects differ from its real ones?
  4. How should historians weigh ecological shocks against colonial policy when explaining the famine of the 1860s?
  5. Why did Tunisia's reforming government fall into debt and lose its autonomy before the French invasion, and was occupation inevitable?
  6. What was the difference in practice between annexation and a protectorate, and why did France choose different systems for neighboring territories?
  7. How did Algerians and Tunisians of different regions and social positions experience, resist, or accommodate colonial rule differently?
  8. What do French official sources reveal and conceal, and how can Arabic and oral sources correct their perspective?

Historical Context

By the early nineteenth century the central Maghreb had been connected to the Ottoman Empire for about three centuries, but connection did not mean direct rule. As earlier chapters showed (Chapters 39, 44, and 47), the Ottoman provinces of North Africa had become largely self-governing regencies. In Algiers, Tunis, and Tripoli, military elites, local dynasties, merchants, and religious scholars negotiated the daily business of government, while the sultan in Istanbul was acknowledged in prayer and diplomacy and consulted on major questions. This autonomy shaped how the French approached the region, since they dealt with Algiers and Tunis as separate states, and it shaped how the regencies responded, since neither could call on the full resources of the empire.

The nineteenth century also changed the Mediterranean balance of power. The Napoleonic wars had ended the old pattern in which Mediterranean powers treated corsairing and ransom as part of regular diplomacy, and European states increasingly regarded the regencies' maritime practices as illegitimate. Europe's industrial economies pulled North African grain, wool, olive oil, and hides into wider commercial circuits, and European merchants, consuls, and bankers gained influence at the courts of Algiers and Tunis. At the same time, France sought to recover prestige after 1815 and then, under the July Monarchy and the Second Empire, to build a colonial empire in compensation for lost possessions. Chapter 49 and Chapter 50 described parallel pressures on Egypt and the Ottoman center; this chapter follows them westward.

Students should keep two cautions in mind. First, colonial rule was not the unfolding of a single plan. French policy shifted with each regime in Paris, with the ambitions of army officers and settlers, and with Algerian and Tunisian action. Second, Algerian and Tunisian societies were not frozen before the French arrived. Both had experienced administrative reform, religious revival, commercial change, and political conflict. The chapter therefore begins with these societies as they were, and it keeps their institutions in view throughout, including the Sufi orders, scholarly networks, tribal confederations, urban merchant families, and Jewish communities whose fortunes colonial rule would reshape in very different ways.

Section 52.1

Algeria before 1830: The Regency, Its Peoples, and Its Political Order

The Ottoman Regency of Algiers took shape in the sixteenth century, when corsair captains and Ottoman backing brought the central Maghreb into the imperial orbit. By the late eighteenth century its government rested on the odjak, the corps of Ottoman-descended soldiers, whose officers chose the ruler, the dey, from among themselves. The dey governed from the Kasbah of Algiers with a small council and a treasury fed by customs, taxes, tribute, and, in earlier periods, the profits of maritime raiding. Outside the capital the regency was divided into three provinces, or beyliks, ruled by beys of Oran in the west, Titteri in the center, and Constantine in the east. Each bey commanded garrisons and collected taxes, and his authority reached as far as his troops, his tribal allies, and his negotiating skill allowed.

The countryside was not a uniform field of subjects. Government relied on "makhzen" tribes that received privileges in exchange for military service and tax collection, while other communities paid taxes irregularly or only under pressure. In the mountains of Kabylia and the Aurès, many communities ran their own affairs through village assemblies and customary law and recognized the regency at a distance. Arabic-speaking and Amazigh-speaking populations lived side by side, and many people were bilingual. Pastoralists, cultivators, and townspeople were bound by trade and kinship. Sufi orders such as the Qadiriyya, Rahmaniyya, and Tijaniyya ran lodges, or zawiyas, that taught, mediated disputes, sheltered travelers, and often held endowed land. Their shaykhs could command loyalty beyond any single tribe, a fact that would matter greatly after 1830.

Towns such as Algiers, Constantine, Tlemcen, and Mascara had merchants, artisans, scholars, and mixed households of Ottoman soldiers, their descendants called kuloghlis, and local families. Jewish communities lived in the coastal towns and the interior, and some Jewish merchant houses, notably the Bacri and Busnach families, played major roles in the grain trade and in diplomacy with Europe. The population as a whole is not known precisely; modern estimates for 1830 commonly fall in the range of two to four million people. Students should treat any single figure with caution, because the regency never conducted a census. What is clear is that the French invaded a functioning, if decentralized, political society, with its own institutions of law, taxation, religion, and learning. The fall of Algiers would remove its apex while leaving most of its social foundations in place.

Section 52.2

Why France Invaded Algeria: Diplomacy, Debt, and the Conquest of Algiers

The immediate quarrel concerned money. During the French Revolutionary wars, the Bacri and Busnach houses supplied grain on credit to French armies, and the resulting claims were disputed for decades. Husayn Dey had a personal and financial stake in the matter and pressed France for settlement. In April 1827, after a heated audience with the French consul, Pierre Deval, the dey struck the consul with a fly-whisk, an episode French officials treated as an insult to France. Whether the blow was a calculated provocation or a lapse of temper is unclear from the sources, and the incident was less a cause than a pretext. Paris severed relations and ordered a naval blockade of Algiers, which lasted three years but harmed French commerce as much as Algerian.

Domestic politics gave the dispute its force. Charles X's government was unpopular and faced a hostile Chamber of Deputies. A victorious campaign promised prestige, and some ministers hoped it would help the regime survive the coming elections. Strategic and commercial motives mattered too: the French navy wanted bases, Marseille merchants hoped for expanded trade, and the idea that Europe should end the regencies' maritime practices had wide support. An expedition of roughly thirty-seven thousand troops sailed in May 1830 and landed at Sidi Ferruch, west of Algiers, in mid-June. It defeated the dey's forces and the contingents sent by the beys and tribes, and on 5 July Husayn capitulated.

Even at this stage there was no settled plan for what France would do with Algiers. The July Revolution overthrew Charles X within weeks, and the new July Monarchy inherited a conquest it had not planned. The new government wavered for years between retaining a coastal enclave and committing to the interior. The army, which had its own interest in careers and glory, and the first merchants and speculators who followed it, pushed events toward occupation. This is why historians distinguish the invasion of 1830, a limited punitive operation with a political motive, from the conquest that followed, which was a series of decisions made in Algiers and Paris over many years. The primary source below shows what France promised at the outset.

Primary Source Window

The Convention of Algiers, 5 July 1830

Creator: Negotiated between Dey Husayn and the French commander, General de Bourmont · Date: 5 July 1830 · Type: Diplomatic capitulation agreement, preserved in French official publications and archives

Context: The convention was concluded in the Kasbah of Algiers after the French army had surrounded the city. It set the terms on which the dey and his troops surrendered the city and its fortifications. The text exists in French; students should consult a verified translation and compare it with Arabic or Ottoman sources where available.

Paraphrase of the principal terms: the dey surrendered the Kasbah and the city's forts and harbor to France; he and his soldiers were allowed to leave with their personal property; and, in the article most often discussed by historians, the French commander pledged that the practice of the Muslim religion would remain free and that the liberty, religion, property, commerce, and industry of the inhabitants would not be harmed, with their women respected.

Analysis: The document is valuable as evidence of what the French command promised and of the language of protection in which the occupation began. It also shows the nature of the surrender: it was an agreement between two commanders, and it did not record the consent of the broader population or of the beys. Within months, French authorities seized the regency's treasury, appropriated buildings, and moved against religious endowments. Their defenders argued that the convention bound them to respect individual property but did not bind them to preserve the regency's public and endowed property, and critics, including some French observers, charged that the promises had been breached. A further limitation is perspective: the surviving text is French, and Algerian understandings of its terms are known mainly from later protests and writings.

Questions for students:

  1. What does this source say, and what did the French command promise?
  2. Who created it, and for what purposes might each side have accepted these terms?
  3. What can historians learn from it about the beginning of the occupation?
  4. What can historians not establish from this source alone about how the promises were kept?
Section 52.3

The Fragmentation of Ottoman Authority and the First Algerian Responses

The capture of Algiers removed the center of a political system that depended on one capital's ability to tax, supply, and command. In the weeks after the surrender the garrisons of the beys, the tribal contingents, and the corps of janissaries dispersed, and authority fell back on regional and local leaders. The Bey of Titteri, based at Médéa, first accepted and then broke with French authority, and in late 1830 General Clauzel's forces marched on Médéa and then withdrew. The Bey of Oran, Hasan, negotiated with France, and by 1831–1832 French troops held Oran, though the surrounding tribes were not subdued. Bône (Annaba) and Bejaia (Bougie) fell in 1832 and 1833. French control for several years remained largely a string of coastal and near-coastal footholds.

The political vacuum produced competing responses. Some urban notables, merchants, and former officials of the regency accepted the occupation, whether from calculation or fear, and some Algerians served in auxiliary units. Others emigrated, especially from Algiers and Tlemcen, to Tunis, Morocco, or the Ottoman East. Many tribal and religious leaders looked for a new leader who could organize defense and uphold Islamic law. In the east, Hajj Ahmad, the Ottoman-appointed Bey of Constantine, kept control of his province, used the language of Ottoman loyalty and of jihad, and prepared for a French attack. In the west, the tribes around Mascara turned to the family of a respected Qadiri shaykh, Muhyi al-Din, whose son was to become the central figure of the first decades of the war.

It would be a mistake to describe these early responses as a single national movement. Algerians in 1830 did not share a modern national identity, and their loyalties lay in tribe, region, religious order, and household as much as in the idea of a common homeland. Yet there was a shared vocabulary of religious obligation and local defense, and it provided common ground for new forms of political organization. The next section examines the most ambitious of these, and Chapter 54 will return to the later development of national political identities in the Arab world.

Section 52.4

Emir Abd al-Qadir: Resistance, State-Building, and Political Authority

Abd al-Qadir ibn Muhyi al-Din was born in 1808 near Mascara into a family of scholars and Sufi leaders who traced descent from the Prophet. He received a thorough religious education, traveled with his father on pilgrimage to Mecca and visited Baghdad and Damascus, and acquired a reputation for learning and piety. When the tribes of the west sought a leader in 1832, his father declined for reasons of age, and Abd al-Qadir was given the pledge of allegiance, the bay'a, in November 1832. He later took the title amir al-mu'minin, "commander of the believers," a style that asserted religious as well as political authority, and he cultivated the support of tribal leaders through a mix of persuasion, marriage and kin ties, and military success.

His project went beyond fighting. He established capitals and fortified depots at inland places such as Tagdemt, Mascara, and Saïda, appointed deputies, or khalifas, over large districts, and organized a regular army alongside tribal levies. He introduced a system of taxes grounded in Islamic law, such as the tithe and alms tax, in place of the more arbitrary levies of the regency. He set up arms workshops, stored grain, and appointed judges and officials. He corresponded with the Moroccan sultan and with European governments, and he presented himself to the French as the ruler of a state, not a rebel. His emirate was also contested. Some tribes resisted his taxation and centralization, rival religious families disputed his legitimacy, and his rule depended on the loyalty of local leaders whom he could not simply command.

Historians read his career through several kinds of evidence. French military reports describe his campaigns and administration but through the eyes of an adversary. His own correspondence, his religious writings, and the later biography written by his son Muhammad, Tuhfat al-za'ir, preserve an Algerian and Arab-Islamic view. French observers, including some officers who negotiated with him, admitted his abilities, and their writings helped shape his later reputation. Modern Algerian historiography has often treated him as a founding figure of national resistance, while recent research emphasizes the particular religious, tribal, and regional structure of his state and avoids reading later nationalism into it. Both readings agree that he created the most durable political alternative to French rule in the first generation of the conquest.

Section 52.5

The Treaties of Desmichels and Tafna, and the Conquest of Constantine

In 1834 the French commander at Oran, General Desmichels, concluded an agreement with Abd al-Qadir that recognized his authority over most of the interior of the province of Oran while giving France the main ports. Both sides had reasons for the arrangement: France wished to reduce the costs of the war, and the Emir gained recognition and access to arms and trade. The agreement quickly produced disagreement. The French text and the Arabic text could be read differently on matters of sovereignty, and the Paris government disavowed arrangements it believed gave the Emir too much. In 1835 Abd al-Qadir's forces defeated a French column at the Macta marsh, and the French responded with larger operations.

The Treaty of the Tafna, concluded in May 1837 by Bugeaud and the Emir, again recognized his rule over much of the interior of Oran and Titteri and some adjacent districts, while France kept Algiers, Oran, and their hinterlands. For the Emir it was a chance to consolidate his state, and for France it was a means to free troops for the east. Each side regarded the arrangement as temporary. The French government saw it as a pause before further occupation, and Abd al-Qadir used the interval to strengthen his administration and fortifications. The history of the two treaties demonstrates that the French and the Algerians could sign agreements while holding opposite expectations about their future.

Meanwhile France turned to Constantine. A first expedition in November 1836 ended in a costly retreat in winter rains. A second, in October 1837, besieged the city after its governor refused to surrender; the French commander, General Damrémont, was killed, and the city fell after fierce street fighting, with heavy losses among the defenders and civilians. Ahmad Bey withdrew to the Aurès and the south and continued the struggle for years. The fall of Constantine gave France a major interior city of the east, and it removed the last strong Ottoman-era provincial authority.

Section 52.6

Total War: Bugeaud, the Destruction of Resistance, and Colonial Violence

The Tafna peace ended in late 1839, after a French column marched through the mountain pass known as the Iron Gates, which the Emir regarded as a violation of the treaty, and he declared a renewed jihad. France responded by appointing Thomas-Robert Bugeaud governor-general in 1841 and by raising the army in Algeria to more than a hundred thousand men. Bugeaud abandoned the slow, fort-bound method of earlier campaigns in favor of mobile columns that marched light, lived off the country, and attacked the settlements, grain stores, orchards, and herds on which the Emir's tribes depended. The aim was to deprive the resistance of its social base. Bugeaud believed conquest would be secured by settlement, and his motto, "by the sword and the plough," expressed both halves of his policy.

The consequences for civilian populations were severe and are well documented in French sources. Columns burned crops and villages, seized livestock, and forced the flight of communities. In 1843 the Duc d'Aumale's forces captured the Emir's mobile capital, the smala, which sheltered thousands of followers and servants. In June 1844 a French officer, Colonel Pélissier, ordered fires lit at the mouths of caves in the Dahra region where members of the Ouled Riah tribe had taken refuge; hundreds of people died of smoke and asphyxiation, with later estimates ranging from several hundred to more than a thousand. Similar episodes, called enfumades, were repeated the following year. Reports by officers and journalists reached France, and the Chamber of Deputies debated them. Some metropolitan critics condemned the methods, and Alexis de Tocqueville, who accepted the colonization of Algeria, nonetheless criticized the army's burning of harvests and seizure of the unarmed as dishonorable and counterproductive.

Historians approach this violence by distinguishing what the evidence establishes from how it is characterized. The use of scorched-earth tactics, mass destruction of crops and settlements, and the asphyxiation of people in caves is established by contemporary French testimony. Officers defended these practices as military necessity and as the customary conduct of campaigns against populations believed to be harboring fighters; critics called them atrocities. Modern scholars disagree about how to describe the overall policy. Some use terms such as extermination or genocide, while others argue that these terms do not fit French intentions or the available evidence and prefer to speak of a brutal counterinsurgency that was also a war on the population's means of subsistence. Students should be able to state both positions and evaluate them against evidence about intent, scale, and consequences, a task the Historical Debate below invites. Population loss in these decades is hard to measure, because French sources lack reliable baseline data and conflate deaths from war, disease, and famine.

Section 52.7

Abd al-Qadir's Surrender and the Continuing Conquest of Algeria

By the mid-1840s the Emir's position was eroding. The loss of the smala, the destruction of his supply bases, and defections of tribes wearied by the war weakened him. He turned to Morocco for refuge and support, and the Moroccan sultan's tolerance of his presence led France to attack Morocco; at the battle of Isly in August 1844 Bugeaud's army defeated a much larger Moroccan force, and the sultan was forced to abandon the Emir. After a last campaign in the west, and an unsuccessful attempt to make a stand against both French and Moroccan troops, Abd al-Qadir surrendered in December 1847 to General Lamoricière, who promised, on behalf of the French command, that he could go into exile in the East. The French government declined to honor that arrangement. He was held in France, at Toulon, Pau, and then the château of Amboise, until Louis-Napoléon released him in 1852. He settled in Bursa and then Damascus, where he devoted himself to teaching and scholarship and, in 1860, protected many Christians during communal violence, which won him international esteem. He died in 1883.

His surrender did not complete the conquest. Armed resistance continued in other forms and places. Bu Ziyan's defense of the Zaatcha oasis near Biskra in 1849 ended in the destruction of the oasis and the killing of many inhabitants. In the Dahra and the Aurès, popular leaders such as Bu Ma'za drew on religious and local grievances. In Kabylia, the mountain communities held out until Marshal Randon's large campaigns of 1856–1857; the religious figure and resistance leader Lalla Fatma n’Soumer became a symbol of the struggle there, and she was captured in 1857. The Saharan oases fell in stages, with Laghouat taken in 1852 and Touggourt in 1854, and further French expansion across the south continued into the 1880s and beyond, drawing in the Mzab, the Tuareg, and Saharan trade networks. The "pacification" of Algeria was still being enforced in remote districts at the end of the century.

In the end the French conquest of Algeria took about seventy years, from the capture of Algiers to the occupation of the deep south. The war's demographic and social costs, together with the land policies that followed, set the stage for the settler society examined in Division II. Algerian leaders also continued to respond in nonmilitary ways: tens of thousands of Algerians emigrated to Ottoman lands, particularly Syria, after 1847 and again after 1871, creating communities that kept alive the memory of resistance and sometimes shaped Arab politics there.

Section 52.8

Governing French Algeria: Annexation, Departments, and Military Rule

French governance of Algeria was never a single system. In the 1830s Paris treated the conquest as "French possessions in the north of Africa" under a governor-general, with authority concentrated in the army. After the Revolution of 1848 the Second Republic declared Algeria an integral part of French territory and divided the northern settled zone into three departments, Algiers, Oran, and Constantine, modeled on the departments of France. The principle of incorporation, called assimilation, would remain a core French claim for the rest of the colonial period. But it applied unequally: it gave European residents the institutions and rights of French departments while leaving most Muslims under a separate legal and administrative regime.

Territorially, Algeria was divided into "civil territory," where European settlement was concentrated and elected municipal councils existed, and "military territory," where the army governed directly. In the military zones the Bureaux arabes, founded in 1844, were staffed by officers who spoke Arabic and local languages, collected taxes, judged disputes, and supervised tribal leaders. Their reports are among the richest sources for rural Algeria and are also instruments of surveillance. Officers of the Bureaux often saw themselves as protectors of the "natives" against settler greed, and settlers regarded them as obstacles to European expansion. Under Napoleon III in the 1860s, the emperor spoke of an "Arab kingdom" and of protecting indigenous property, but the policy was inconsistent and settler hostility to it was intense.

The fall of the Second Empire in 1870 changed the balance. The Third Republic gradually replaced military rule with civil administration, and in the early 1880s the "attachment" system placed Algerian administrative departments under the relevant ministries in Paris, which in practice increased the influence of settlers in local and national politics. Europeans elected deputies and senators and dominated municipal and departmental councils, while the Muslim majority, with very limited exceptions, did not vote on the same terms. Later reforms created financial delegations in 1898 and gave Algeria a measure of budgetary autonomy in 1900, which strengthened settler control further. By 1914 Algeria was both part of France in law and a settler-dominated society in practice.

Section 52.9

Land Seizure, Property Law, and the Expansion of Settlement

Land was at the center of the colonial transformation. Many forms of tenure existed in precolonial Algeria: privately owned plots, family and tribal lands, communal pastures and agricultural land used by whole groups, state lands of the regency, and endowed properties held for religious and charitable purposes. French officials, trained in a legal culture that favored clear individual title, often considered this complexity obstructive. Early confiscations targeted the lands of the dey's regime, of the departed Ottoman elite, and of rebels. Seizure of endowed property, discussed in the Vocabulary box below, removed a large part of the land and buildings supporting mosques, schools, and charity. Defeated tribes were also required to cede land, or were "cantoned" onto reduced territories while the freed land went to settlement.

The legal framework changed over time and reflected competing goals. The sénatus-consulte of 1863 declared that tribes were proprietors of the lands they permanently occupied, and it set up commissions to delimit tribal territories and divide them into douars. The law was presented as a safeguard for Algerian landholders against arbitrary seizure, and it was linked to the "Arab kingdom" ideas of Napoleon III. Yet the delimitation procedures disrupted customary arrangements, since land used collectively by tribes or in shifting patterns had to be recorded, and they left ambiguities and disputes that officials and speculators could exploit. The Warnier Law of 1873, named for the settler politician Auguste Warnier, went further. It authorized the establishment of individual titles on land held collectively and made it easier to sell, and a series of later measures extended the process. Cultivators who lacked cash, who were in debt, or who held claims shared with relatives were vulnerable, and many lost land to Europeans and to those with cash or credit.

The scale of dispossession is difficult to state precisely and the data are contested, but the broad pattern is clear. After the Mokrani Revolt of 1871, authorities sequestered a very large area of Kabyle and other land as collective punishment; commonly cited estimates run to around 450,000 hectares, together with a heavy war indemnity. By the early twentieth century Europeans owned millions of acres of the best agricultural land, including coastal plains and fertile valleys, while many Algerian cultivators were pushed onto smaller, poorer plots, into the semi-arid steppe, or into wage work. Forest laws extended in 1851 restricted the grazing and wood-gathering on which many communities relied. Historians debate how much of the transfer resulted from direct confiscation and how much from market forces unleashed by the new property rules, but most agree that the law and the state's coercive power together created the conditions in which settler landholding expanded and Algerian landholding shrank.

Historical Vocabulary
Habus (Waqf)

Habus (in the Maghreb; waqf in much of the Arab East) is property, such as land, shops, or buildings, dedicated in perpetuity to a religious or charitable purpose. Its income supported mosques, schools, the poor, and sometimes the descendants of the founder.

Endowments were a major part of the property and social welfare system of precolonial Algeria and Tunisia. Because they could not ordinarily be sold, French officials often saw them as an obstacle to a land market, and in Algeria the colonial state seized or reorganized many of them from 1830 onward.

The term matters because the fate of endowments shows how colonial land policy affected religious learning and charity as well as agriculture. "Charitable trust" is a rough equivalent, but it understates the central role of endowments in the economic life of Maghribi communities.

Section 52.10

European Settlers and the Making of Colonial Algeria

The settler population grew from a few thousand in the early 1830s to roughly 130,000 by 1851, about a quarter of a million by 1872, and some three-quarters of a million by the eve of the First World War, according to French census figures. The French government encouraged settlement with land grants and agricultural colonies, including those established after 1848 for unemployed workers from Paris and, after 1851 and 1871, for political deportees and refugees from Alsace-Lorraine. But French migrants never formed the entire population. Spaniards settled heavily in the west around Oran, Italians and Maltese in the east and in the coastal towns, and many others came from across the Mediterranean, drawn by work and cheap land. A law of 1889 made children born in Algeria to foreign-born European parents French by default, a policy meant to create a larger French-identifying population and reduce reliance on foreign communities.

Settlers were not a uniform group. Large landowners and vineyard proprietors, merchants, officials, small farmers, artisans, port workers, shopkeepers, and the urban poor had different interests, and their political opinions ranged widely. Many early arrivals were poor and suffered from fever, drought, and debt, and some returned to Europe or moved to towns. Europeans also lived overwhelmingly in towns and cities, so that Algiers, Oran, and Bône became largely European in outlook, with municipal governments that defended settler interests. What united them politically was the defense of their status and of a system that gave them rights denied to Muslims. The "colon" lobby, with representation in the Chamber and the Senate, shaped legislation on land, taxation, and local government, and it often opposed measures that limited its influence. Anti-Jewish agitation also grew among Europeans, culminating in the riots of 1898 in Algiers and other towns, and it showed that the settler community was neither uniformly liberal nor uniformly tolerant.

Historical Debate

Conquest, Settlement, and French Intentions in Algeria

The question: Was the French conquest of Algeria driven from the start by a design to dispossess and settle, or did it evolve through improvisation, military initiative, and shifting metropolitan politics into a settler colony?

Interpretation A — Contingent, incremental conquest: One body of scholarship stresses that no coherent plan existed in 1830. The expedition was launched for domestic and diplomatic reasons, successive governments hesitated, and officers and settlers pushed expansion forward. On this view the settler colony emerged from a chain of decisions, local pressures, and the preferences of the Third Republic, rather than from an original blueprint, and French policy contained real disagreements, including Napoleon III's attempts to protect Algerian landholding.
Interpretation B — A settler-colonial logic from an early stage: Another body of scholarship argues that by the 1840s, if not earlier, French policy aimed at the displacement of the indigenous population from the land, and that Bugeaud's war, the seizure of land, and the promotion of settlement were parts of one project. Some scholars in this camp draw on comparative research on settler colonialism and use terms such as extermination or genocide, while others in the broader field object that these terms overstate intent or that population loss resulted mainly from disease and famine.

Evidence: The interpretations rely on the same documents: ministerial correspondence, army reports, parliamentary debates, settlement plans, land legislation, and census data. They differ in the weight placed on stated intentions versus outcomes, on military necessity versus design, and on how to interpret evidence of mortality from war, disease, and famine. Algerian sources, including later oral histories and Arabic chronicles, are used to establish how communities experienced events that French records describe in administrative language.

Why it matters: The question affects how the conquest is remembered and taught, how responsibility is assigned, and whether Algeria is treated as an exceptional case or as one example of a wider pattern in nineteenth-century empires. It also affects how the later struggle for independence is understood.

Student task: Identify two pieces of evidence that favor Interpretation A and two that favor Interpretation B. Then explain whether the two views can be partly reconciled, for example by distinguishing the intentions of 1830 from the policies of the 1840s and 1870s.

Section 52.11

Agriculture, Labor, Commerce, and the Colonial Economy

The settler economy was built on commercial agriculture for export and on the integration of Algeria into French markets. Grain remained important, but wine became the signature product. After the phylloxera epidemic devastated French vineyards in the 1870s, Algerian viticulture expanded from a few thousand hectares to well over a hundred thousand by 1900, and wine became a major export. Other products included esparto grass, cork, olives, citrus, and tobacco. Mining of iron and phosphates, railways, ports, roads, and public works developed, often financed by French capital, and a customs union with France from the 1880s tied Algerian trade to the metropole. Algiers, Oran, and Bône grew as ports and commercial centers, and towns such as Sétif and Sidi Bel Abbès arose around agricultural settlement.

For Algerian communities the effects were uneven and often harmful. Many cultivators lost the best land and shifted to marginal plots or to the semi-arid steppe, and pastoralists faced lost grazing under forest laws and settler fencing. Some Algerians worked as sharecroppers on European estates, for instance as khammès who received a fifth of the crop, or as seasonal wage laborers, and others migrated to towns or, later, to France. A minority of Algerian landowners, merchants, and notables adapted to the new markets and prospered, and some invested in vines or grain. French taxes on Muslims, including the "Arab taxes" that continued Ottoman-era levies in modified form, fell heavily on rural communities and contributed to debt and dependence on moneylenders. Economic growth in the colonial sector therefore coexisted with a decline in the living standards of much of the indigenous rural population, and this contrast would shape the grievances of the twentieth century.

Section 52.12

Famine, Epidemics, and Demographic Upheaval in the 1860s

The crisis of 1866–1868 is one of the gravest episodes in the history of nineteenth-century Algeria. A series of environmental shocks struck a rural population already weakened by two generations of war, land loss, and heavy taxation. Drought, swarms of locusts, and a harsh winter ruined harvests and herds, and the resulting shortage of food was followed by epidemic disease, including cholera and typhus. French relief measures were limited and late, and the authorities were slow to recognize the scale of the disaster. Rural families sold livestock and tools, migrated to towns in search of food, and in many areas faced severe hunger. Missionary and administrative reports, army accounts, and later Algerian memory all record the suffering, although each has its own limits.

French census figures suggest a fall in the Muslim population from roughly 2.7 million in the mid-1860s to about 2.1 million in 1872, but these figures should be used carefully, because census methods were imperfect, the 1871 revolt and its repression also affected the numbers, and estimates of famine deaths vary widely, from several hundred thousand upward. Historians therefore avoid any single "official" figure. They emphasize that the death toll cannot be explained by drought alone. Colonial land policy, the loss of grain reserves and pasture, fiscal pressure, the commercialization of agriculture, and the inadequacy of relief all helped determine who died. The famine also fed political consequences: it deepened distrust of French rule, shaped the context of the revolt of 1871, and influenced debates in France about the future of the colony.

Section 52.13

The Mokrani Revolt of 1871 and Its Consequences

The great rebellion of 1871 began in Kabylia and spread across much of eastern and central Algeria. Its immediate setting was the French defeat in the Franco-Prussian War and the fall of the Second Empire in September 1870. The collapse of imperial authority, the withdrawal of troops for the war in France, and the turmoil of the new Republic encouraged hope that French power could be challenged. Settlers meanwhile pressed for the end of military rule and the Bureaux arabes, and the government of national defense in Paris introduced civil measures, including the decree granting French citizenship to Algerian Jews, that many Muslim leaders resented. Years of famine, heavy taxation, and land loss deepened rural anger.

The revolt's first leader was Muhammad al-Mokrani, the head of an aristocratic family of the Majana region who held a high local office under the French and who had personal grievances about the curtailment of his authority. In March 1871 he resigned his post and rose in revolt, and the movement quickly gained the support of Shaykh al-Haddad, the elderly head of the Rahmaniyya order in Kabylia, who called for jihad in April. The involvement of the Sufi network gave the revolt a mass base. Over two hundred tribes are commonly said to have joined at the height of the rebellion, and fighting spread from Kabylia to the Hodna, the Aurès, and the Constantine region. Al-Mokrani was killed in battle in May, and the revolt was gradually crushed by French forces under Admiral de Gueydon, the governor-general, and by columns of troops brought from France.

The suppression was harsh and its consequences lasting. The French imposed a collective war indemnity of tens of millions of francs, sequestered large areas of land, deported hundreds of rebels, including some sent to the Pacific island of New Caledonia, and imprisoned many leaders. The Rahmaniyya and local elites were weakened, and traditional political structures in Kabylia were damaged. The confiscated land became available for European settlement, and the Warnier Law of 1873 soon facilitated further transfers. After 1871, no large rural revolt on this scale occurred in northern Algeria until the twentieth century, and settlers gained enough power to dominate Algerian politics. Historians read the revolt as both a last great traditional rebellion and a turning point in the consolidation of settler colonialism.

Section 52.14

Colonial Citizenship, the Crémieux Decree, and the Indigénat

French law in Algeria separated nationality from citizenship. A sénatus-consulte of 1865 declared Muslims and Jews French subjects, with the right to serve in the army and administration, but not citizens. A Muslim could apply for full French citizenship only by accepting French civil law and giving up the personal status governed by Islamic law, a condition most regarded as an abandonment of their religious community's law. Very few did so before 1914. Muslims therefore remained "subjects," subject to French sovereignty yet outside the political community of citizens that elected deputies and senators. The separate legal category fixed a hierarchy at the center of colonial Algeria.

The legal position of Algerian Jews took a different course. The Crémieux Decree of October 1870, issued by the provisional government in the wake of the Second Empire's fall, collectively naturalized the Jews of the northern departments, some thirty-five thousand people, as French citizens, though not the Jews of the Saharan Mzab. The decree reflected the republican belief that Jews could be assimilated, and it responded to the efforts of French Jewish leaders and the community's consistory institutions. It also created a gulf between Jews and Muslims who had lived in similar circumstances, and it provoked settler antisemitism and Muslim resentment. Settlers of European origin, by contrast, enjoyed citizenship, or acquired it easily through naturalization laws such as that of 1889. Within each group, individual status varied: Muslims who served the state or had French education could be treated differently, and many Jews experienced social exclusion despite citizenship.

In 1881 a code of exceptional administrative penalties, the indigénat, was adopted and subsequently renewed. It listed infractions, such as disrespect to officials, unauthorized travel, and failure to pay taxes, that local administrators could punish without ordinary trial, with fines and short imprisonment, and it allowed collective penalties and sequestration of property. The code gave local officials wide discretion over Muslims' daily lives, and it embodied the principle that "natives" should be governed by different rules. Historians treat it as a defining institution of colonial Algeria because it combined the rhetoric of French law with the reality of arbitrary administrative power. Chapter 60 and Chapter 63 trace how demands for the abolition of these distinctions became central to Algerian politics.

Historical Vocabulary
Indigénat

The indigénat (from indigène, "native") was a system of exceptional administrative punishments applied to colonial subjects, formalized for Algeria in 1881. It allowed administrators to impose penalties without trial for offenses defined specifically for indigenous people.

It must be distinguished from nationality, which Muslims held as French subjects, and from citizenship, which gave political rights and was tied to French civil law. A person could be a French national yet subject to the indigénat.

The term matters because it shows how colonial law combined universalist language with legal inequality. Modern equivalents such as "criminal code" or "minority status" are misleading, since the indigénat applied to the majority population and operated outside regular courts.

Section 52.15

Religion, Education, Family, Language, and Everyday Life under Colonial Rule

Colonial rule altered but did not erase Algerian religious and cultural life. The conversion of mosques into churches or military buildings, as with the great Ketchaoua mosque in Algiers, and the seizure of endowments, weakened religious institutions. The state took control of the appointment of religious officials and the supervision of mosques, and it reorganized Muslim courts so that qadis retained authority over personal status and family law, such as marriage, divorce, and inheritance, while other matters passed to French courts. In Kabylia, French authorities gradually replaced customary law with French law in the 1870s and 1880s. Sufi lodges, Quranic schools, and scholarly networks nevertheless continued, often with reduced resources, and they remained the main institutions of religious education and community life.

French schooling for Muslims expanded slowly. Officials created a few state-run Muslim colleges, or médersas, from 1850 to train judges and clerks, and later schools for Muslim children, but under the 1880s educational laws the great majority of Algerian children received no French schooling, and by 1914 only a small percentage were enrolled. Settlers often feared that educated Muslims would challenge their position. Arabic and Amazigh languages remained the languages of home and community, and French became the language of administration, higher education, and advancement, producing a small bilingual elite. The restrictions on Arabic teaching and the privileging of French shaped later cultural politics.

Women and families lived within these changes in varied ways. Family law remained under Islamic or customary rules, but dispossession, migration, and war altered household structures and economies. Women's labor in agriculture, weaving, and household production continued, while French officials and missionaries often described Algerian women in stereotyped terms. Algerian communities responded in several ways: some emigrated, some withdrew into religious and community institutions, some used French courts and petitions to defend rights, and some sent sons to French schools while preserving Islamic values at home. Section 52.16

Husaynid Tunisia: Government, Society, and the Regency of Tunis

Tunisia in the early nineteenth century was governed by the Husaynid dynasty, founded in 1705 by Husayn ibn Ali, who established the hereditary rule of the beys. Like the dey of Algiers, the bey acknowledged the Ottoman sultan in prayer and diplomacy but acted as the effective ruler. The dynasty governed from Tunis and the nearby Bardo palace with a court of ministers, many of them of mamluk origin, and with a Turkish-descended military and administrative elite alongside long-established Tunisian families. The regime was more centralized and more continuously dynastic than that of Algiers, and its rule extended across a country whose population was perhaps a million and a half.

Tunisian society combined an urban culture centered on Tunis, with its merchants, craftspeople, and scholars, and the agricultural Sahel with its olive groves, the cereal districts of the north and the Majerda valley, the pastoral and oasis south, and the island of Djerba. Tunis's great mosque-university, the Zaytuna, trained jurists and teachers who staffed courts and schools. A significant Jewish community lived in Tunis and other towns, divided between old-established families and a group of Livornese merchants, and growing numbers of Italians, Maltese, and French merchants and artisans settled in the capital. European consuls had established privileges under the system of capitulations, which exempted their nationals from local courts and gave consular representatives influence at court. The state depended on taxes, including the poll tax called the mejba, on customs, on state monopolies, and on revenues from olive oil, grain, and tribute. Tunisia's trade with Europe expanded, and the increasing role of European finance would become central to its later history.

Section 52.17

Reforming Tunisia: Ahmad Bey, the Fundamental Pact, and the Constitution

Tunisia's reform movement paralleled that of the Ottoman Tanzimat described in Chapter 50 and the Egyptian state-building of Chapter 49, but it had its own course. Ahmad Bey (r. 1837–1855), impressed by Muhammad Ali's Egypt and by the French army, built a modern-style military with European instructors, opened a military school at the Bardo, and sought to create industries and a stronger fiscal base. In 1846 he abolished slavery in Tunisia, one of the first rulers in the Muslim world to do so by decree, though slavery's practical end took time and the ruling circles' dependence on mamluk officials continued. His costly projects strained the treasury, and they set a pattern of ambition that exceeded revenue.

Under his successors the reforms took legal form. In 1857 Muhammad Bey promulgated the Fundamental Pact, which guaranteed security of person and property and equality before the law for all subjects regardless of religion, partly in response to European pressure after the execution of a Jewish man in a case that outraged European opinion. In 1861 Muhammad al-Sadiq Bey issued a constitution, the first in the Arab world, which created a Grand Council, limited the bey's powers, and set rules for ministers and taxation. The constitution was suspended in 1864 after the revolt described below. Meanwhile the statesman Khayr al-Din, a former mamluk of Circassian origin who wrote a political treatise advocating reform and engagement with Europe, headed a reforming ministry in the 1870s, and he helped found the Sadiqi College in 1875, which taught modern subjects alongside Arabic and Islamic learning. The chronicler Ahmad ibn Abi al-Diyaf, a Tunisian official, left an important account of the period from a court insider's view, and he is a key source for reform and its limits.

Tunisian reform should not be read as imitation of Europe alone. Reformers drew on Islamic jurisprudence, Ottoman precedents, and local political traditions, and many argued that reform was consistent with Islamic law. Yet reform also created problems: new institutions required revenue, and revenue required taxation or borrowing, a dilemma the next two sections explain.

Section 52.18

Taxation, the Revolt of 1864, and the Crisis of Tunisian Reform

The costs of the army, the court, and the new institutions were heavy, and the prime minister Mustafa Khaznadar's government met them by raising taxes and borrowing. In 1863 the poll tax was doubled, and in 1864 rural communities rose in a revolt associated with Ali bin Ghedhahem, a tribal leader of the northwest who emerged as spokesman for the grievances of the tribes. The rebels called for the abolition of the doubled tax and attacked government officials. The revolt spread through much of the interior and the Sahel, and it was suppressed by government forces and by a policy of divide-and-rule, together with heavy collective penalties. The bey suspended the constitution, which many rural people had associated with the new burdens.

The revolt exposed a gap between the capital's reformers and the rural population on whom the reforms were financed. Reform presented as modernization could mean greater demands on cultivators and herders, and it could weaken traditional local leaders without offering new forms of representation. Bad harvests, famine, and epidemics in 1867–1868 caused severe suffering, and the combined effects of revolt, repression, and disease weakened the state's finances and its legitimacy. Historians differ over how to weigh the revolt's economic and political grievances, and over whether the reforms were doomed or could have succeeded with better management, but all accept that the crisis of the 1860s left Tunisia financially and politically exposed.

Section 52.19

Debt, International Financial Control, and European Rivalry

The Tunisian government borrowed heavily in Europe in the 1860s, including a large loan in 1863 on terms that gave lenders very high effective returns. By 1867 it could not meet its obligations, and in 1869 it accepted an international financial commission composed of French, British, and Italian representatives, with authority to supervise revenue and ensure debt service. The commission reduced the state's financial independence and made its budget a matter of European oversight. It also put the three powers in a position of rivalry, since each sought commercial, railway, and landholding concessions and influence at court.

Students should be cautious about treating debt as a simple cause of conquest. Financial control was a means of pressure, but the French decision to invade in 1881 also arose from strategic concern about Tunisia's position beside Algeria and the central Mediterranean, from competition with Italy, which had its own community and ambitions in Tunisia, and from diplomatic bargains among the European powers. At the Congress of Berlin in 1878, Britain and Germany signaled that they would not oppose French action in Tunisia, partly to ease France's disappointment over other matters. Competing French and Italian railway and land companies, as well as French consuls such as Théodore Roustan, turned commercial disputes into political crises. Tunisian rulers and ministers tried to play the powers against each other, and the sultan in Istanbul offered some diplomatic support, but the Ottoman state could not provide effective protection. Tunisia's loss of autonomy thus resulted from the interaction of internal fiscal weakness and external power politics, not from a single cause.

Section 52.20

The French Invasion of 1881 and the Treaty of Bardo

In the spring of 1881 the French government of Jules Ferry used reports of raids by tribes from the Khumir region across the Algerian frontier as the justification for military intervention. It claimed that the bey could not control his border and that France must act to protect Algeria. The incidents were real in the sense that tribal movements and disputes occurred along the frontier, but many historians regard the pretext as exaggerated and the decision as shaped by wider strategic goals, including fear of Italian influence in Tunisia, the value of a position facing Sicily and the central Mediterranean, and the wish for prestige after earlier setbacks. In April French columns crossed from Algeria while naval forces landed at Bizerte, and by May the army stood near Tunis.

On 12 May 1881 Muhammad al-Sadiq Bey signed the Treaty of Bardo with General Bréart in the palace of Qasr al-Said. It allowed French military occupation of points deemed necessary for order, placed Tunisia's foreign relations under French representation, and obliged the bey to consult France before concluding international agreements. It also promised reforms and the continuation of the bey's rule. Italy, which had hoped to establish its own influence in Tunisia, protested bitterly, and the episode contributed to its decision to join the Triple Alliance with Germany and Austria-Hungary in 1882. The primary source below, with the Convention of La Marsa, shows how the formal language of the agreements preserved the bey's position while shifting real power.

Section 52.21

Tunisian Resistance, Military Occupation, and the Convention of La Marsa

The treaty did not end the war. Many Tunisians rejected the bey's agreement, and resistance spread through the interior and the south in 1881–1882. Fighting at Sfax in July 1881 was especially fierce: French naval bombardment preceded an assault on the city, and its defenders and civilians suffered heavy losses. French forces occupied Kairouan in October 1881 after tribal resistance, and they advanced into the south. Leaders such as Ali ben Khalifa of the Nafat tribe carried on armed opposition for several years, and some resisters withdrew into Ottoman Tripolitania. The bey's authority was too limited and his position too compromised to control the situation, and many resisters saw him as having surrendered the country.

French authorities decided that the treaty did not give them enough control. In June 1883 Ali Bey, who had succeeded Muhammad al-Sadiq, signed the Convention of La Marsa with the French resident minister, Paul Cambon. It obliged the bey to carry out administrative, judicial, and financial reforms deemed useful by the French government, and it gave France decisive influence over the Tunisian state. In 1884, French officials took charge of finance and reorganized the debt. The occupation, treaty, and convention together established a protectorate that left the dynasty in place but placed it under French supervision.

Historical Vocabulary
Protectorate

A protectorate is a political arrangement in which a stronger state takes responsibility for the foreign relations and, in practice, the main internal affairs of a weaker one, which keeps its own ruler and institutions in form. In Tunisia the bey remained head of state, and French officials directed policy through a resident-general.

It contrasts with annexation, as in northern Algeria, where territory was incorporated into the colonizing state's administrative system and where large-scale European settlement was integrated into French departments. A protectorate was usually cheaper and less legally disruptive, but its practical effect could be similar.

The term matters because it describes a legal fiction with real consequences. Students should not treat "protectorate" as meaning that the protected state kept its independence.

Section 52.22

Governing the Protectorate: Beylical Sovereignty and French Power

The protectorate worked through a dual structure. The bey continued to reign, to sign decrees, and to preside over a Tunisian government with a prime minister and ministers, and traditional officials such as the qa'ids (provincial governors) remained. But a French resident-general, who was also the foreign minister of the bey's government, directed policy, and French officials supervised each Tunisian ministry. Local French "controllers" shadowed the qa'ids and had decisive influence over rural administration. The French also reorganized finance, the police, public works, education, and the legal system, and they created separate French courts for Europeans while Tunisian courts continued to handle matters among Tunisians.

The arrangement allowed France to claim that it was respecting Tunisian sovereignty and that it governed with cooperation of local elites. It also limited the cost and legal complexity of administration, because the system used existing officials and structures. For Tunisians the effect was mixed. Many officials, merchants, and landholders accommodated themselves to the new regime and gained from it, while others felt that the bey had become a figurehead and that real power lay with foreigners. The distinction between form and substance, which is central to this chapter, was visible in the daily operation of government: the bey's seal was needed for decrees, but the decisions were made elsewhere.

Section 52.23

Land, Agriculture, European Settlement, and the Colonial Economy

French administrators introduced a land registration law in 1885, modeled on a system of registered title, which made it easier to establish clear ownership and attract European investment. Colonists obtained large tracts of land, including state domain and, after reforms in the 1890s, endowment property that could be leased on long terms. French settlers acquired several hundred thousand hectares by the early twentieth century, though the numbers were far smaller than in Algeria, and Italians formed a larger European community than the French for decades, working in agriculture, fishing, construction, and trade. Tunisian smallholders and tribal communities lost land and grazing, and some turned to wage labor or to planting arrangements in the Sfax region.

The protectorate built railways and ports, developed the arsenal and harbor at Bizerte, and developed mining, particularly of phosphates in the Gafsa region from the late 1890s. Olive oil, cereals, wine, and phosphates became important exports, and the tax system continued to burden rural Tunisians. The scale and mechanisms of European control differed from Algeria. Tunisia was never legally incorporated into France, French settlers never held the same political control, and the preservation of Tunisian institutions meant that dispossession was somewhat less sweeping. Yet the economy was reoriented toward French interests, and Tunisian farmers and artisans faced competition and restrictions. Historians therefore describe the protectorate as a system of indirect but effective economic domination.

Section 52.24

Tunisian Society under the Protectorate

Religious and educational institutions survived in modified form. The Zaytuna remained the center of Islamic learning, and reforms to its curriculum in the late nineteenth century widened its teaching, while the Sadiqi College continued as a modern school with a bilingual curriculum. French and "Franco-Arab" schools expanded, though they enrolled a small fraction of Tunisian children, and Arabic-language Quranic schools remained the main form of education for most. Jewish communities experienced the protectorate in different ways: some, especially in Tunis, gained from French education and economic opportunities, while others remained poor. Tunisian women's lives varied by region and class, and while family law remained under religious courts, urban and rural women experienced economic change through household production, wage labor, and migration.

Tunisian society experienced both continuity and transformation. The bey's court, the religious elite, and many older institutions persisted, and Tunisia's urban culture, with its markets, guilds, and mosque schools, adapted to new conditions. At the same time, a growing number of Tunisians were educated in modern schools, worked in the colonial economy, or read newspapers, and a new social layer of educated men emerged, some of whom would form the Young Tunisians. Rural communities faced pressure on land and taxes, and in the south and the steppe the state's presence grew through roads, garrisons, and administrators. The combination of institutional continuity and social change is the key to understanding early twentieth-century Tunisia.

Everyday Life and Social History

Households and Communities under Colonial Rule

Rural Algeria. After 1870, many rural families in the north lived on reduced land holdings, often as sharecroppers, smallholders, or seasonal laborers. Families managed risk by combining farming, herding, weaving, and seasonal work, and by relying on kin and religious networks. Administrators of the indigénat and tax collectors were part of daily life, and so was the need for travel permits and the fear of collective penalties. Women's work in agriculture, water and fuel gathering, and textile production sustained the household economy, and the loss of land and herds changed what families could produce and sell.

Colonial towns. In Algiers, Oran, and Bône, European neighborhoods with cafés, shops, and municipal institutions grew beside older Muslim quarters and Jewish quarters. Language, schooling, and legal status marked daily boundaries, even where residents worked, traded, and lived near each other. Many Europeans were workers or small shopkeepers rather than rich landowners, and many Muslims worked as day laborers, servants, or small traders.

Tunisian communities. In Tunis, the medina's markets, guilds, and religious institutions continued, while a European quarter grew along new avenues. In the Sahel, olive cultivation tied families to planting contracts and the market. In the south and on Djerba, trade and migration continued, and in the countryside the presence of controllers, taxes, and settlers altered local politics. Because most surviving written sources come from officials and urban elites, historians use oral histories, court and endowment records, and material culture to recover the experiences of rural families, women, and poorer townspeople.

Section 52.25

The Young Tunisians and the Growth of Anti-Colonial Public Protest

By the turn of the century a group of young, educated Tunisians, many graduates of the Sadiqi College and of French schools, began to press for reform and equal treatment in public. Known as the Young Tunisians, they included figures such as Ali Bach Hamba and Bashir Sfar. They demanded better education, a share of administrative posts, equality in taxation and justice, and respect for Tunisian cultural and religious institutions, and they argued within the framework of the protectorate that France should fulfill its own stated ideals. In 1907 they launched the newspaper Le Tunisien in French, followed by an Arabic edition in 1909, and they used the press to articulate demands to both French and Tunisian audiences.

Protest also took more popular forms. The Jellaz affair of November 1911 began as a dispute over the registration of a Muslim cemetery in Tunis, and it led to riots and a violent response by the authorities; its intensity was heightened by anger over the Italian invasion of Ottoman Libya that year. In early 1912 a boycott of the Tunis tramway, set off by the death of a child in a tramway accident and by wider grievances, mobilized many Tunisians for weeks. The government declared a state of siege, banned the Young Tunisian press, and exiled or restricted several leaders. These episodes show that organized public protest had emerged before 1914. They should be treated as early responses to the colonial order, not as the whole of Tunisian nationalism. The Destour party and the movement toward independence belong to Chapters 60 and 63.

Section 52.26

Algeria and Tunisia under French Empire: Conquest, Colonial Rule, and Historical Consequences

Algeria and Tunisia illustrate how one imperial power could create two different systems in neighboring societies. Algeria was conquered by long war, annexed in law, settled by Europeans in large numbers, and governed under a legal hierarchy that placed Muslims in a subordinate position. Tunisia was occupied by a shorter campaign after a period of financial and diplomatic pressure, retained its ruling dynasty and administration in form, and was governed through French supervision with a smaller European settler population. The differences came from several factors: the lack of a plan in 1830 and the military's central role in Algeria, the existence in Tunisia of a stronger, more centralized state with a long dynastic tradition, the changed international setting of 1881, French fear of costly conquest and of Italian rivalry, and the lessons of Algeria itself.

The similarities were also real. Both systems reoriented economies to French interests, favored European landholding and commerce, limited Muslim political participation, and used coercion against resistance. In both cases colonial intentions diverged from outcomes: the sénatus-consulte of 1863 was intended partly to protect Algerian tenure and instead helped destabilize it; the protectorate was meant to preserve Tunisian institutions and instead hollowed them out. Within each country, different groups had different experiences, as Muslim elites, rural cultivators, Jews, and the several European communities faced distinct opportunities and constraints. Algerians and Tunisians also acted: they fought, emigrated, petitioned, published newspapers, preserved religious institutions, and adapted to French schooling and markets on their own terms.

By 1914 the colonial systems in place shaped what followed. In Algeria the unresolved questions of citizenship, land, and settler power would be central to nationalist politics and to the war of independence examined in Chapter 63. In Tunisia the contradiction between the bey's formal sovereignty and French control would shape the nationalist movements of Chapter 60. Chapter 53 turns to Egypt, Sudan, Libya, and Morocco, where European pressure produced still other forms of rule.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how France created two different colonial systems in the central Maghreb. Algeria, an autonomous Ottoman regency with a decentralized society of tribes, towns, Sufi orders, and Jewish and Muslim communities, was invaded in 1830 after a dispute over debt and diplomacy. The conquest was not planned in advance, but it became a long war that ended the regency, created a settler colony, and reshaped Algerian society.

Algerian responses were varied. Local leaders, religious networks, and tribes resisted, negotiated, accommodated, or emigrated. Abd al-Qadir built an emirate that combined religious authority, taxation, and military organization, and signed treaties with France that each side understood differently. Under Bugeaud, France adopted total war, destroying crops, villages, and in some episodes killing people sheltering in caves. His surrender in 1847 did not end the conquest, which continued in Kabylia, the Sahara, and elsewhere for decades.

After conquest, settler colonialism transformed Algeria. France incorporated the north into its administrative structure through departments, while settlers of French, Spanish, Italian, Maltese, and other origins gained land and political power. Land laws such as the sénatus-consulte of 1863 and the Warnier Law of 1873, together with confiscation and the appropriation of endowments, shifted property to Europeans and weakened Algerian cultivators. Commercial agriculture, especially vineyards, grew, while many Algerian families became poorer and more dependent.

The famine of 1866–1868 and the Mokrani Revolt of 1871 were major turning points. The famine resulted from drought, locusts, and epidemic disease acting on a population already weakened by war, land loss, and taxation, and its death toll remains debated. The revolt was the last great rural rebellion in the north; its suppression led to land sequestration and consolidated settler power. Legal categories, including the 1865 sénatus-consulte, the Crémieux Decree of 1870, and the indigénat of 1881, created a hierarchy among Europeans, Jews, and Muslims.

Tunisia followed a different path. Under the Husaynid beys it was a self-governing state with its own dynasty and institutions. Reforms under Ahmad Bey, the Fundamental Pact of 1857, the constitution of 1861, and the ministry of Khayr al-Din sought to strengthen the state, but they required revenue that provoked the revolt of 1864, and borrowing led to bankruptcy and an international financial commission in 1869. Debt did not by itself cause conquest: French strategic concerns, Italian rivalry, and European diplomacy were decisive.

France invaded Tunisia in 1881, citing frontier incidents, and imposed the Treaty of Bardo, then, after resistance, the Convention of La Marsa in 1883. The protectorate preserved the bey and many institutions but placed real authority in the hands of French officials. French settlement, land policy, railways, ports, and mining reoriented the economy, though on a smaller scale of dispossession than in Algeria. Tunisian society changed through education, economic transformation, and new forms of protest, and the Young Tunisians, the Jellaz affair, and the tramway boycott showed organized opposition before 1914.

The comparison between the two countries shows that colonial rule was shaped by the preexisting societies, by contingent decisions, and by the international context. Both systems were coercive and unequal, but they worked through different mechanisms. Throughout, Algerians and Tunisians acted as political agents, resisting, adapting, emigrating, petitioning, and preserving institutions.

The colonial structures in place by 1914 shaped the later history of both countries. The settler colony in Algeria produced unresolved questions of land, citizenship, and political power that fed nationalism and the war of independence, while the protectorate in Tunisia created a contradiction between formal sovereignty and foreign control that framed the nationalist movement. These developments are taken up in Chapters 60 and 63, and the wider context of European expansion continues in Chapter 53.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how empire was built in North Africa not as a single design but through a sequence of decisions, pressures, and responses. The French conquest of Algeria and the occupation of Tunisia were not inevitable outcomes of European power. They resulted from specific political choices, military practices, financial weaknesses, and diplomatic bargains, and they met resistance and adaptation at every stage.

The chapter also reveals larger processes. Settler colonialism turned land, law, and citizenship into instruments of power, while the protectorate demonstrated that sovereignty could be preserved in form and emptied in substance. Both processes recurred elsewhere in the Arab world, including the Mandate states and Palestine examined in later parts of the book.

Many continuities survived beyond 1914: debates over land, language, and identity; Sufi and scholarly networks; migration between North Africa and the wider Muslim and French worlds; and the memory of conquest. The next chapter turns east to Egypt, Sudan, Libya, and Morocco, where European expansion followed other paths and the Arab world's western and eastern halves were drawn into a common imperial era.
