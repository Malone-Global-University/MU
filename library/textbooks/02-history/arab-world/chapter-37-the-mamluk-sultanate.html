Part VII — The Late Medieval Arab World
37

The Mamluk Sultanate

A History of the Arab World: From Ancient Crossroads to the Modern Arab World

Chapter Opening

Chapter 36 ended with a regime that had survived. The Mamluks had defeated a Mongol army in Galilee, installed a sultan in Cairo, and taken control of Egypt and Syria together. This chapter asks the harder question: how does a regime of this kind turn survival into durable government? Its rulers were, for the most part, men who had been born outside Islam, bought as boys, trained as soldiers, and freed by masters who themselves had begun life the same way. Their state lasted for more than two and a half centuries.

Consider a single building as a way into the problem. In the Cairo Citadel, the great fortress rising above the city on the Muqattam spur, the sultan Qalawun (r. 1279–1290) is said by later Egyptian historians to have lodged a large body of newly purchased soldiers in existing towers of the fortress, which served as their quarters. Those soldiers became known as the Burjiyya, "the Tower men." Their presence illustrates the machinery of a political system. A ruler bought young men from beyond the frontiers of the Islamic world, converted and trained them, bound them to himself by personal loyalty, and placed them at the center of his government. Within a generation or two, some of those men governed provinces, commanded armies, and made and unmade sultans.

This chapter treats that system as the central subject of the Mamluk Sultanate's history. Its subject is the political and institutional history of the mature sultanate from roughly 1260 to the later fifteenth century: how it recruited and reproduced its ruling class, how sultans and emirs competed within recognizable rules, how Egypt and Syria were governed as one strategic system, how the regime fought the last Frankish states and the Mongol Ilkhanate, and how it conducted diplomacy with neighbors from the Golden Horde to Byzantium to Aragon. It also asks how a regime of military slaves-turned-rulers convinced others that its rule was legitimate.

A common impression holds that Mamluk history was a long sequence of palace assassinations, a story of violent and unstable men seizing power. There is evidence for such an impression: many sultans were deposed, killed, or reigned for only months. This chapter argues that such an impression, taken alone, misleads. Violence at the top of the system coexisted with remarkable continuity in government. Provincial administration, civilian bureaucracy, courts, endowments, and the recruitment of new soldiers continued to function through changes of sultan. Instability among the elite and durability of the state were two features of the same system, not contradictory ones.

The chapter also resists the opposite temptation: to describe the Mamluks only as a bridge between the Crusades and the Ottoman conquest. Part VII of this textbook gives the late medieval centuries independent treatment, and this chapter follows that principle. For much of the period the Mamluk Sultanate was among the largest, wealthiest, and most militarily capable states of the eastern Mediterranean and the Islamic world. It defeated the Mongols repeatedly, ended the mainland Crusader presence, and exercised suzerainty over the Hijaz, claiming prestige through the protection and provisioning of Mecca, Medina, and the pilgrimage routes. It should be understood on its own terms.

The evidence for Mamluk political history is unusually rich. Chroniclers such as Ibn ʿAbd al-Zahir (a chancery official and biographer of early sultans), al-Maqrizi, Ibn Taghribirdi, and others wrote at length about court politics. Chancery manuals and administrative treatises describe offices and protocol. Waqf (endowment) deeds, inscriptions on buildings, coins, and diplomatic correspondence preserved in Arabic, Latin, and other languages supplement the chronicles. Yet most narrative sources were written by members of the literate civilian elite or by men attached to military households, and many later historians, writing in the fifteenth century, looked back on earlier periods with their own preoccupations. Students should therefore treat each account as evidence to be weighed.

The chapter's boundaries are deliberate. It builds on Chapter 36, which explained how Mamluk rule emerged between the 1240s and 1261, and it assumes the reader knows what a mamluk was. It does not retell the Crusades (Chapters 34–35) or the Mongol destruction of Baghdad (Chapter 36). It leaves the social and commercial life of Cairo, Damascus, and the countryside to Chapter 38, the Black Death to Chapter 41, the strategic landscape of about 1500 to Chapter 42, and the Ottoman conquest to Chapter 43. Its task is the mechanics of Mamluk rule.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how military slavery continually reproduced the Mamluk ruling elite through purchase, training, manumission, and household loyalty.
  2. Distinguish the legal and social status of "mamluk" from any single ethnic identity, and explain why the sons of mamluks (awlad al-nas) normally did not inherit their fathers' status.
  3. Describe the political relationship between sultan and emirs, and explain why no sultan governed independently of the military elite.
  4. Analyze succession, deposition, and political violence as structural features of a system lacking a universally binding hereditary rule.
  5. Assess the Qalawunid era as an attempt to combine mamluk elite politics with dynastic succession.
  6. Describe how Cairo and the Syrian provinces, especially Damascus, functioned together as a single political and military system.
  7. Explain how Mamluk rulers constructed legitimacy through military defense, the Abbasid caliphate in Cairo, Sunni legal patronage, public justice, and protection of the holy cities.
  8. Trace the campaigns that ended the mainland Frankish states, culminating in the fall of Acre in 1291, and the continued warfare against the Ilkhanate after Ayn Jalut.
  9. Evaluate Mamluk diplomacy with the Golden Horde, Byzantium, Italian maritime powers, Aragon, and Armenian Cilicia as evidence that the sultanate operated within a wide international system.
  10. Interpret the Bahri–Burji periodization critically and explain why it should not be read as an abrupt ethnic or institutional revolution.
  11. Evaluate competing historical interpretations of Mamluk stability and durability, and the limits of "decline" narratives.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How did a regime of former military slaves become a durable state rather than a short-lived military seizure of power?
  2. Why did the absence of ordinary hereditary succession for the military elite make constant recruitment of new soldiers necessary, and what consequences followed?
  3. In what ways was the sultan both the head of the state and a first among competing emirs?
  4. How did the Mamluk regime govern Egypt and Syria together, and why was Syria simultaneously a frontier, a recruiting resource, and a base of political power?
  5. What could an Abbasid caliph, who held no governing power, still contribute to a sultan's legitimacy?
  6. Why did Mamluk campaigns against the Franks and the Mongols differ in character, and what did each reveal about the sultanate's strengths?
  7. How should historians weigh narrative sources that emphasize palace violence against evidence of administrative continuity?
  8. What does the transition from "Bahri" to "Burji" actually describe, and what does it risk concealing?
  9. Why might narratives of Mamluk "decline" be an unreliable guide to the fifteenth-century sultanate?

Historical Context

By 1261 the Mamluk regime in Cairo had come through a decade of upheaval. The Ayyubid dynasty founded by Saladin (Chapter 35) had ended in Egypt after the death of al-Salih Ayyub in 1249 and the crisis that followed, in which his mamluk regiments seized power. Shajar al-Durr, Aybak, and Qutuz (treated in Chapter 36) were the principal figures of that transition. The Mongol sack of Baghdad in 1258 had destroyed the Abbasid caliphate in Iraq, and the Mongol advance into Syria had brought the sultanate's survival into question until the victory at Ayn Jalut in 1260. Baybars then took the sultanate, and an Abbasid claimant was installed as caliph in Cairo shortly afterward. These events belong to Chapter 36 and are only referred to here.

The practice of recruiting military slaves was far older than the Mamluks. Abbasid caliphs had used Turkic slave soldiers from the ninth century (Chapters 25 and 30), the Ayyubids had relied on mamluk regiments, and Saladin's own household was built on such loyalties (Chapter 35). What was unusual in 1260 was not military slavery but its position at the apex of the state. Military-slave elites had founded ruling houses before, the Ghaznavids being a well-known earlier example. What was distinctive in Egypt and Syria after 1250 was that the mamluk military elite itself occupied the apex of the state rather than serving an Ayyubid ruling household.

The new state inherited three geographical settings from its predecessors: Egypt, with its rich agricultural base along the Nile and its Mediterranean and Red Sea ports; Syria, with its cities, fortresses, and long frontier facing the Mongol-ruled East and the Frankish coast; and the Hijaz, whose holy cities lay along pilgrimage routes that Egyptian and Syrian rulers had long sought to protect. Egypt provided revenue and manpower. Syria provided strategic depth, the principal military frontier, and a second tier of governing authority. The Hijaz provided prestige.

Students should keep two cautions in mind. First, "Mamluk" is a status, not a people: it designates a particular legal and social-military condition. Second, to avoid collapsing the entire period into a single pattern, this chapter distinguishes among its phases: the foundational reigns of the 1260s and 1270s, the Qalawunid era to 1341, the period of powerful emirs and weak sultans in the later fourteenth century, and the Circassian-dominated sultanate from the 1380s onward.

Section 37.1

From Ayn Jalut to a Mamluk State

In the weeks after Ayn Jalut, the sultanate had won a battle but had not yet become a state. Its rulers controlled Egypt and, increasingly, Syria, but the rules for succession, for dividing revenue among the military, for governing Syria from Cairo, and for justifying their rule to a Muslim population were unsettled. Qutuz had been killed on the way back to Egypt, and Baybars had taken the throne. His position was not secure: other emirs had claims, Syrian governors had their own followings, and the Mongols were certain to return.

Baybars therefore inherited an opportunity and a problem. The opportunity was the prestige of the victory and the removal of the most acute Mongol threat. The problem was that a regime founded by the killing of a predecessor offered no precedent for how a sultan should be chosen or how his authority should be recognized. Much of the early history of the sultanate can be read as a series of improvisations that hardened into practice: the installation of an Abbasid caliph in Cairo, whose role is examined in 37.7; the setting of military ranks; the assignment of revenue-yielding land to soldiers; and the building of communications between Cairo and Damascus.

Historians therefore distinguish between the emergence of Mamluk rule, the subject of Chapter 36, and its institutionalization, the subject of this chapter. The two are separated not by an event but by the gradual realization that the regime would endure and by the construction of routines that allowed it to do so.

Section 37.2

Baybars and the Consolidation of Power

Baybars (r. 1260–1277), originally a Kipchak Turkic military slave who had risen in Ayyubid service, spent his reign converting a precarious victory into durable rule. His first tasks were domestic: neutralizing rival emirs, disciplining Syria, and establishing his own household as the core of the army. Syrian governors who had been installed by earlier regimes or who had other ambitions were removed or brought into line. Cairo and Damascus were bound more closely through an expanded system of relay stations, couriers, and signals known collectively as the barid, which allowed news of invasions or revolts to reach the sultan with remarkable speed. Fortresses in Syria were repaired, garrisoned, and placed under officers loyal to the sultan.

Baybars also developed routines of government. He appointed chief judges from each of the four Sunni legal schools in Cairo, a measure discussed in 37.7. He held public sessions to hear complaints, a practice that bound ruler and subjects in a recognizable ideal of justice. He sponsored building, including mosques, colleges, and fortifications, and supported pilgrimage by dispatching the covering for the Kaaba (kiswa) to Mecca. His reign also saw the first sustained campaigns against the Frankish coast, examined in 37.8, and an active diplomacy with the Mongol Golden Horde and with Byzantium, examined in 37.9.

One of the principal sources for Baybars's reign is the biography written by Ibn ʿAbd al-Zahir, a senior official of the chancery who served the sultan. The work is valuable because its author had access to the documents and ceremonies he describes, but it is also a work of praise, composed by a man whose career depended on the regime. Readers should treat it as a source for how Baybars wished to be seen and for how a close observer understood the state, not as a neutral record.

When Baybars died in Damascus in 1277, the state he had built continued, but the lack of any secure rule of succession immediately reasserted itself. His son was proclaimed, then pushed aside within a few years, and the throne passed after a brief interval to Qalawun in 1279. The pattern of a strong founding ruler followed by contest over his legacy would recur throughout Mamluk history, and was a feature of the system rather than an accident of Baybars's death.

Section 37.3

Military Slavery as a Political System

The heart of the Mamluk system was the way the ruling elite replenished itself. Boys and young men were purchased, mostly from regions beyond the frontiers of the Islamic world: during the Bahri period principally from the Kipchak steppe north of the Black Sea, and later increasingly from the Caucasus, where Circassians were prominent. The slave trade that supplied them is treated in Chapter 38; what matters here is the political use made of those who arrived. On arrival, recruits were converted to Islam, taught Arabic and the rudiments of religion, and trained in riding, archery, and the use of the lance and sword. Training took place under supervision in barracks, often in the Citadel, and combined discipline with systematic instruction.

Upon completing training, a mamluk was freed (manumitted) by his master, called his ustadh. Manumission did not dissolve the bond; it transformed it. A freed mamluk remained attached to his former master and to his master's household by loyalty, patronage, and obligation, and was typically given a place in the army and, as he advanced, a rank and an assignment of revenue. Mamluks who had been trained and freed together developed strong ties of solidarity known as khushdashiyya, which became a basis of political alliance. Loyalty ran both vertically, toward the master, and horizontally, toward one's cohort.

This system was not simply a method of recruiting soldiers. It made a political order. A sultan who had risen through the mamluk ranks was himself a former slave and the master of the largest household, whose mamluks were his most dependable supporters. Great emirs likewise had households of their own. When a sultan died or was deposed, the loyalty of his mamluks did not automatically transfer to a successor; their allegiance was to the household and the person of the master. Succession struggles were therefore, in large part, contests among competing households. This fact helps to explain both the violence of succession and the speed with which a new sultan, once established, could rebuild a loyal following by purchasing and promoting a new generation of his own mamluks.

Why Mamluk status was not ordinarily hereditary

Mamluk status depended on having been born outside Islam, enslaved, and then freed into the military elite. The sons of mamluks were freeborn Muslims, called awlad al-nas ("children of the people"). They were often wealthy, educated, and well connected, and some held posts in the army or administration. But they normally did not enter the mamluk military hierarchy on the same terms as their fathers, and the major military commands remained overwhelmingly in the hands of first-generation mamluks. As a result, a mamluk could not simply pass his rank to his son, and the elite could be sustained only by continuing to import new recruits. This feature distinguished the sultanate from a conventional hereditary aristocracy, and explains why the regime depended throughout its history on the flow of military manpower from outside its borders.

Students should be careful about the word "Mamluk" itself. It names a legal and military condition, not an ethnic group. Early elites included many Kipchak Turks, later ones increasingly Circassians, and other origins appear as well. Ethnic or regional background could shape alliances, but it did not define membership in the system.

Section 37.4

Sultans, Emirs, and the Politics of Succession

At the top of the military hierarchy stood the sultan. Beneath him were the emirs, ranked by the size of the units they commanded; the highest emirs were commanders of a large body of troops, with others at intermediate and lower levels. Senior emirs held the great offices of the court and army. Among them were the commander-in-chief (atabak al-ʿasakir), the head of the sultan's household administration, officers in charge of the sultan's weapons and personal protection, the dawadar ("inkwell-bearer"), a powerful court official associated with royal correspondence, incoming mail, orders, and ceremonial duties, and, at various times, a deputy of the sultan (naʿib al-saltana). The sultan's authority over these men was real but conditional. He could not rule without their cooperation, and a sultan who offended enough of them could be removed.

Succession illustrates how this worked. There was no rule requiring that a sultan's son succeed him, and none that bound the emirs to accept any candidate. When a sultan died, his senior emirs deliberated and chose a successor, with the choice often shaped by the balance of household power. Sometimes they chose the late sultan's son, particularly if he was young and could be controlled by a regent; sometimes they chose one of their own. Deposition and assassination were common means of ending a reign when the balance shifted.

Historians have long debated how to interpret this pattern. The chronicles record many reigns that lasted only months and many violent endings. But the same chronicles also show that when a sultan fell, the army, the provincial governors, the courts, and the bureaucracy continued to operate. The violence was concentrated at the apex of the system and was resolved by an established set of participants who knew the rules of the contest. The Historical Debate feature later in this chapter examines the question directly. The central point for now is that succession by negotiation among military elites was the form that a lack of hereditary rule necessarily took, not a symptom that the state was constantly on the verge of collapse.

Section 37.5

The Qalawunid Experiment

Qalawun (r. 1279–1290), another Kipchak by origin, took power in 1279 after a period of struggle that followed Baybars's death. Over the next eleven years he built a strong regime, defeating a Mongol force near Homs in 1281 and taking the Frankish stronghold of Tripoli in 1289. He is also notable for the scale of his purchase of new mamluks, whom he placed in the towers of the Citadel as the Burjiyya, and for founding in Cairo a large complex of hospital, school, and tomb that became a model of royal patronage.

Qalawun's significance for this chapter lies in what he tried to do about succession. By arranging for his son to be designated heir, and by building a household large and loyal enough to support that arrangement, he attempted to combine mamluk politics with a degree of dynastic succession. His son al-Ashraf Khalil (r. 1290–1293) succeeded him, captured Acre, and was assassinated by emirs who feared his ambitions. Khalil's younger brother al-Nasir Muhammad then took the throne as a boy, was displaced, restored, and displaced again before achieving the stable rule examined in 37.10. For roughly nine decades, with several interruptions, descendants of Qalawun supplied most of the sultans until the 1380s.

The Qalawunid era shows both the possibility and the limits of dynastic rule within the Mamluk system. Descent from Qalawun conferred real prestige and gave emirs a convenient focus for compromise. But descendants remained dependent on the support of powerful emirs and could be, and often were, reduced to figureheads. The dynasty was therefore not an escape from mamluk politics but a form that mamluk politics took for much of the fourteenth century.

Section 37.6

Egypt and Syria as One Political System

The Mamluk Sultanate governed Egypt and Syria as one strategic unit with two centers of gravity. Cairo, with the sultan, the court, and the central administration, was the capital. Damascus was the principal Syrian political and military center, and its governor was the most senior provincial official. Syria beyond Damascus was divided into several governorships, centered on cities and strongholds such as Aleppo, Tripoli, Hama, Safad, Karak, and Gaza, whose precise status and number changed over time. Each had a governor, usually an emir appointed by the sultan, with military forces and administrators under his authority.

The reason for this arrangement was geography. Syria was the front line against the Mongol Ilkhanate along the Euphrates frontier, and the base from which coastal and northern operations were launched. Egypt supplied the revenue and the bulk of the sultan's household troops. Holding the two together demanded rapid communication, which the barid and the road network provided, and a way of preventing Syrian governors from becoming independent. The sultan therefore moved and replaced governors regularly, kept their families or officers under watch, and relied on the relative equality of rank among them to prevent any single governor from dominating the region.

Even so, Syria was often a theater of political crisis. Governors of Damascus sometimes rebelled or backed rival candidates for the throne, and Syrian forces could be decisive in succession contests. Syria was thus at once a frontier, a reservoir of military manpower, and a power base for ambitious men. Chapter 38 treats Syrian and Egyptian society and economy; here the aim is to grasp the structure of government.

Cairo: the capital of the sultanate

In Cairo, the Citadel was the center of political life. The sultan lived and held court there, and the major mamluk barracks and the principal offices of state were located there or nearby. The central administration included a vizier or equivalent officials responsible for finance, a chancery that produced diplomatic and administrative correspondence, and a set of offices managing the sultan's private properties and household. Beside them were the four chief judges of the Sunni legal schools and the Abbasid caliph, whose positions are discussed below. Cairo was also a great commercial and cultural city, but this chapter confines itself to its role as the seat of government.

Damascus and provincial government

The governor of Damascus, often called naʿib, was the sultan's chief representative in Syria. He commanded the Damascus garrison, oversaw other Syrian governors in matters of defense, administered justice, and communicated with Cairo. His power depended on the sultan's confidence, and the relationship between the two could be cordial or tense. Because the post carried real military capacity, it was also a springboard to the sultanate for men who could not obtain it in Cairo. The Mamluk provincial system was therefore a means of extending Cairo's authority and a place where that authority could be tested.

Section 37.7

Caliph, Law, and the Languages of Legitimacy

A regime of former slaves whose rule rested on force needed more than force to be accepted. Mamluk sultans sought legitimacy in several overlapping ways. The first was military: a ruler who defeated Mongols and Franks showed that he defended the Muslim community. The second was the recognition of a caliph. After 1261 the sultanate hosted an Abbasid caliph in Cairo, descended from the Baghdad line. He did not govern. But by formally investing a sultan, the caliph conferred on him a recognized place in the tradition of Islamic rulership. Historians should avoid the extremes of calling the Cairo caliph a mere puppet and of treating him as the spiritual head of all Muslims. He possessed symbolic and religious-political legitimacy; the sultan possessed effective governmental and military power. The two roles were distinct, and each needed the other. On at least one occasion, a caliph was briefly raised to the sultanate itself, in 1412, an episode that was not repeated and which shows the exceptional nature of any merging of the roles.

The third source of legitimacy was patronage of the Sunni religious establishment. Baybars appointed chief judges for each of the four schools of law (Shafiʿi, Hanafi, Maliki, and Hanbali), recognizing the pluralism of Sunni legal practice. Sultans and emirs founded colleges, mosques, and hospitals, supported scholars through endowments (waqf), and appeared in public as defenders of Islam. The institutional and social life of this religious establishment belongs to Chapter 38; here its political function matters. Scholars gave the sultan's rule the stamp of religious acceptance, and in return gained protection and resources, though their relationship with rulers was never wholly comfortable and some scholars criticized the regime openly.

A fourth element was justice. Sultans and senior officials held sessions (the mazalim, sometimes held in a "house of justice," dar al-ʿadl) at which petitioners could present grievances, and the ideal of an accessible and fair ruler was a staple of Mamluk political writing. Whether justice was always done is another question, but the public performance of it contributed to the regime's authority.

Finally, the sultanate claimed a special role in the protection of Mecca, Medina, and the pilgrimage routes that led to them. Sultans sent the kiswa for the Kaaba, secured and provisioned the routes, and had their names invoked in the Friday sermon in the holy cities. The Hijaz was governed locally by Meccan sharifs under Mamluk suzerainty. Chapter 40 treats Arabia itself; for this chapter, the importance of the Hijaz is that service to the holy places gave the sultanate prestige and a claim to leadership among Muslim states.

Section 37.8

The End of the Frankish Mainland

The Frankish states of the Levant, whose history is traced in Chapters 34 and 35, were reduced to a narrow coastal strip after Saladin's victories and the Third Crusade. In the 1260s, Baybars set about eliminating what remained, with campaigns that illustrate the growth of state capacity as much as military talent. Between about 1265 and 1271 his forces took Caesarea, Arsuf, Safad, Jaffa, and Antioch, and the great fortress of Krak des Chevaliers. These operations were not simply battles but coordinated campaigns that required logistics, siege engineering, and the control of territory behind the lines. Where a place was captured, garrisons and administration were installed so that the gains would be held.

Baybars's campaigns also show that Frankish weakness was partly a matter of fragmentation. The remaining Latin states and military orders were divided among themselves and dependent on reinforcements from Europe that arrived irregularly. Mamluk rulers, by contrast, could draw on the revenue of Egypt and Syria and on a standing army. Qalawun continued the process with the capture of Tripoli in 1289. The fall of Acre in 1291, examined in the Cause and Effect feature, completed it.

Al-Ashraf Khalil besieged Acre in the spring of 1291, and the city fell on 18 May after a determined defense. The remaining coastal positions, including Tyre, Sidon, Beirut, and others, were abandoned or taken within weeks. The Mamluks systematically dismantled many of the fortifications to prevent any return. This effectively ended the mainland Crusader-state era. But it did not end Latin interest in the eastern Mediterranean: the island kingdom of Cyprus remained in Latin hands, and Italian merchant communities continued to trade in Mamluk ports under agreements negotiated with the sultans, a matter returned to in 37.9.

Section 37.9

The Mongol Frontier and Mamluk Diplomacy

The idea that Ayn Jalut ended the Mongol threat is a persistent misconception. The Ilkhanate, the Mongol state in Iran and Iraq, continued to contest Syria for decades. In 1277 Baybars defeated a Mongol force at Elbistan in Anatolia, a victory that exposed how far the Mamluk army could project power but also, because he withdrew soon afterward, how hard it was to sustain. In 1281 Qalawun's army defeated another Mongol force near Homs. The most dangerous moment came under the Ilkhan Ghazan, who invaded Syria in 1299, defeated a Mamluk army near Homs, and for a short time occupied Damascus in early 1300. The Mongols withdrew, and Mamluk authority was restored. A further invasion was decisively beaten at Marj al-Saffar (also known as Shaqhab), south of Damascus, in 1303, and this victory is generally regarded as the end of the main phase of Ilkhanid attempts to conquer Syria.

Peace followed a long period of mutual exhaustion and negotiation. In 1323 the sultanate concluded a settlement with the Ilkhan Abu Saʿid, and the Ilkhanate itself fragmented after 1335. The northern frontier, once the most threatened region of the sultanate, became relatively stable. Students should not give every campaign equal weight: the point of the sequence is that the Mamluks were forced to defend Syria repeatedly, that they could do so, and that the end of the conflict came through a combination of military success and diplomacy.

Diplomacy as well as war

The sultanate's international relations were far wider than its wars. With the Mongol Golden Horde, which was in conflict with the Ilkhanate and whose rulers eventually adopted Islam, the Mamluks maintained recurring diplomatic contact from the time of Baybars. These contacts had a military logic, since both states opposed the Ilkhans, and also an economic one, since the steppe was the principal source of mamluk recruits; the commercial side is treated in Chapter 38. Passage of envoys and slave shipments through the Byzantine-controlled straits required agreements with the Byzantine emperor, and the sultans corresponded with Constantinople accordingly. Treaties with Italian maritime republics and with the kingdom of Aragon regulated trade and the position of merchants in Mamluk ports. These documents survive in several languages and are valuable evidence of how the sultanate was perceived: as a power that could be negotiated with on equal terms.

Armenian Cilicia and the northern frontier

To the north lay the Christian kingdom of Armenian Cilicia, which had allied with the Mongols and was therefore treated by the Mamluks as an enemy. Baybars, Qalawun, and later sultans raided and campaigned in Cilicia repeatedly, and al-Nasir Muhammad's forces captured the port of Ayas in 1337. The kingdom's independent existence ended when Mamluk forces captured its capital, Sis, in 1375. The significance of Cilicia for this chapter is its connection to Mamluk strategy: it controlled passes and ports on the sultanate's northern flank and could serve as a base for Mongol or other hostile powers.

Section 37.10

Al-Nasir Muhammad and the Height of Qalawunid Rule

Al-Nasir Muhammad ruled in three separate periods: first as a child (1293–94), then from 1299 to 1309, and finally from 1310 until his death in 1341. His first two reigns were marked by control by powerful emirs, and by the military threat of the Ghazan invasions. In 1309 he withdrew from the sultanate in the face of the emirs' dominance, and returned in 1310 with the support of Syrian governors and part of the army. His third reign, lasting more than three decades, is the longest and most stable of the Qalawunid era.

Over these years, al-Nasir Muhammad reduced the power of the great emirs who had dominated him, promoted his own mamluks, and tightened control over the army and revenue. A cadastral survey of Egyptian lands conducted around 1315 (the rawk) reassessed and redistributed the revenue assignments that supported the military, strengthening the sultan's share. His reign saw the settlement with the Ilkhanate, extensive building in Cairo, and vigorous diplomacy across the eastern Mediterranean and the steppe. He married into the ruling house of the Golden Horde and received embassies from distant rulers.

Al-Nasir Muhammad's reign shows what the Mamluk system could do under a determined ruler: mamluk household politics, careful management of the emirs, and a stable fiscal base could produce strong sultanic authority. It also shows the system's limits. Much of his authority depended on his personal ability to balance the emirs, and when he died in 1341 no mechanism existed to preserve it. His descendants held the throne for decades, but real power moved to the emirs.

After 1341, the pattern of powerful emirs and weak sultans became pronounced. A succession of sons and grandsons of al-Nasir Muhammad occupied the throne, some as children, some briefly, some for years. The Black Death of 1347–49, which struck Egypt and Syria in the middle of this period, is treated in Chapter 41. Government nonetheless continued: governors were appointed, armies mustered, and campaigns launched, including the capture of Sis in 1375. Weak sultans did not mean a weak state, because the institutions were carried by the emirs and officials who staffed them.

Section 37.11

From Bahri to Burji: Barquq and the Circassian Ascendancy

Later chroniclers and modern historians divide the sultanate into two periods, the Bahri and the Burji. The labels come from the names of the regiments: the Bahriyya, the early mamluk corps of the Ayyubid sultan al-Salih Ayyub, and the Burjiyya, Qalawun's mamluks housed in the Citadel towers. The conventional division places the shift around 1382, when the Circassian emir Barquq took the throne, and associates the earlier period with Turkic (largely Kipchak) elites and the later with Circassians from the Caucasus.

The division is useful but imperfect. Many Burji emirs were not Circassian, many Bahri emirs were not Kipchak, and the Burjiyya who gave the later period its conventional name were Qalawun's tower-housed mamluks. "Burji" describes their institutional quartering, not an ethnicity, although Circassians were already prominent among them. Recruitment of Circassians had been growing for decades, and the political networks that brought Barquq to power had been building within the Qalawunid sultanate. The change was therefore gradual in its elite composition, and in its institutional features the sultanate remained recognizably the same: military slavery, Cairo-centered rule, Syrian provinces, revenue assignments for soldiers, and a continuing search for legitimacy.

Barquq (r. 1382–1389, 1390–1399) had been purchased as a boy and rose through the ranks to become a leading emir under a Qalawunid child-sultan. In 1382 he took the throne himself. In 1389 he was displaced by a coalition of emirs and imprisoned in the fortress of Karak, but escaped and, with the support of Syrian forces, regained the sultanate in 1390. His restoration and the establishment of his authority after 1390 mark a real shift in the composition of elite networks. He died in 1399, leaving the throne to his young son Faraj. Shortly afterward, in 1400–1401, the conqueror Timur invaded Syria, captured Aleppo, and sacked Damascus before withdrawing, a crisis that tested the sultanate during a succession and that it survived.

Section 37.12

The Fifteenth-Century Sultanate

The Burji sultanate of the fifteenth century continued the patterns of the preceding period. Succession was typically decided among the senior emirs. Many reigns were short, though several were long. Households of mamluks remained the building blocks of politics, and sultans continued to buy and promote their own recruits. This chapter selects rulers who illustrate the system rather than cataloguing every reign.

Barsbay (r. 1422–1438) is notable for a strong, centralizing rule and for campaigns against the Latin kingdom of Cyprus, whose ships had raided the Egyptian and Syrian coasts. After several expeditions in the mid-1420s, Mamluk forces defeated the Cypriot king and brought the island into a tributary relationship with the sultanate. His reign also saw a heightened state role in commerce, a subject treated in Chapter 38. Barsbay's reign shows that sultans of the fifteenth century were capable of effective military and fiscal policy and of projecting power in the Mediterranean.

Qaitbay (r. 1468–1496) ruled for nearly three decades, longer than almost any other sultan. He was a major builder, sponsoring fortifications, including a fort at Alexandria, and religious and civic works across the sultanate. He maintained diplomatic relations with European, Asian, and African rulers. He also faced a new rival in the Ottoman Empire, whose expansion in Anatolia brought the two states into a protracted war from 1485 to 1491 over the Anatolian-Syrian frontier and neighboring principalities. The 1491 settlement essentially restored the prewar territorial position. The Mamluk Sultanate thus entered the sixteenth century as an established state with a developed system of government, confronting a rising power on its northern frontier. How that confrontation unfolded belongs to Chapters 42 and 43.

Section 37.13

Limits of Mamluk Power, Durability of Structure

The sultanate faced real constraints. Succession disputes recurred and sometimes weakened the state at critical moments. The reliance on imported manpower meant that disruptions of the slave trade or rising costs of purchase could strain the system. Military recruitment, training, and revenue assignment all had to be sustained against fiscal pressure, especially after the demographic and economic shocks examined in Chapter 41. Technological change, including the growing importance of firearms in the later fifteenth century, challenged an army built around mounted archers and household cavalry. Frontier pressure from Timur in 1400–1401 and from the Ottomans in the war of 1485–1491 tested the system.

Yet these problems should not be turned into a narrative of inevitable decline. Many of them were chronic features of the system for centuries, and the sultanate repeatedly adapted. Its institutions survived the Black Death, Timur's invasion, and decades of factional struggle. A historian who reads Mamluk history backward from the Ottoman conquest of 1516–1517 will see decay everywhere; one who reads it forward from 1260 will see a state that regenerated its ruling class, governed two large provinces, defeated major adversaries, and outlasted most of its contemporaries. The Mamluk Sultanate was one of the major states of the late medieval Islamic world, and its history deserves to be understood in its own right.

The deepest lesson of the chapter may be that the sultanate endured not despite the unusual structure of military slavery and elite competition but partly because of them. Those institutions continually regenerated a ruling military class, while civilian administration, provincial government, religious legitimacy, and an effective Egyptian-Syrian strategic system continued to function. Instability at the palace did not necessarily mean the collapse of the institutions.

Historical Vocabulary
Mamluk, Khushdashiyya, and Awlad al-Nas

The Arabic word mamluk means "owned" and refers to a slave. In the context of this chapter it denotes a particular kind of slave: a young man purchased from outside the Islamic world, trained as a soldier, converted to Islam, and eventually manumitted into a military elite. The word is not an ethnic label, and not every slave was a mamluk; domestic and agricultural slaves belonged to different categories.

Khushdashiyya names the bond among mamluks who had been bought, trained, and freed by the same master. These bonds could be as important as the tie to the master himself and shaped political alliances. Awlad al-nas ("children of the people") names the freeborn sons of mamluks, who were Muslims by birth but who normally did not enter the mamluk hierarchy on the same footing as their fathers.

The modern English word "slave" can mislead here. A mamluk was legally a slave until manumission, and the institution depended on forcible removal and sale. But in practice a mamluk's later position could be one of great power, and a sultan who rose through the mamluk ranks was himself a former slave (hereditary heirs, especially during the Qalawunid era, were freeborn). The terminology reminds us that the political and legal categories of the medieval Islamic world did not always match modern assumptions about slavery and rule.

Historical Vocabulary
Iqtaʻ and the Halqa

An iqtaʻ was an assignment of the revenue of a specified area, usually agricultural land, to a military officer or soldier in return for service. It was not ownership of the land: the holder had the right to a share of its revenue, and the assignments could be reassigned by the sultan. The system was used by earlier regimes, including the Ayyubids, and the Mamluks adapted it to support their army.

The halqa was a body of troops, distinct from the sultan's own mamluks, that included freeborn soldiers, awlad al-nas, and others. Its members also received revenue assignments. The existence of the halqa shows that the Mamluk army was not composed solely of mamluks, and the revenue system that sustained it was a key point of connection between the military and the agricultural economy, discussed in Chapter 38.

Because the iqtaʻ tied the soldier's income to land, periodic surveys and reallocations, such as that of about 1315, were important instruments of sultanic power. A sultan who could redistribute assignments could reward loyalty and weaken rivals. For students, the key point is that revenue arrangements were as much a political instrument as an economic one.

Context: Al-Maqrizi wrote more than a century after the events he describes concerning Qalawun's reign. His Sulūk is a major chronicle of the sultanate, and his Khitat, a topographical work, is a major source for the physical and institutional history of Cairo. He was a member of the civilian scholarly class, not a soldier, and he lived under the Burji sultanate, so his account of the earlier period reflects both older material and the concerns of his own time.

Paraphrase (not a quotation): In the Sulūk, al-Maqrizi reports that Qalawun lodged his purchased mamluks in the towers of the Citadel, and that they were therefore called the Burjiyya, from the Arabic word for tower.

Analysis: The passage is valuable for showing how, in the memory of a fifteenth-century Cairene writer, the physical organization of the Citadel was bound up with the political organization of the sultan's household. Architectural study suggests that Qalawun used existing towers as permanent quarters rather than building them as barracks. Separately, al-Maqrizi's Khitat (al-Mawāʿiẓ wa-l-iʿtibār bi-dhikr al-khiṭaṭ wa-l-āthār, Cairo: Dar al-Tahrir li-l-Tabʿ wa-l-Nashr, vol. III, p. 48) supports the related claim that Qalawun intended this new group as a loyal protective force for himself and his descendants; students may read the two works together. It also illustrates how the name of the later Burji period derives from an institutional fact rather than from ethnic identity. Its limitations are significant. It is a later account rather than a contemporary record. It reflects the perspective of a civilian scholar rather than a soldier or a mamluk. It tells us little about how the recruits themselves experienced training, or about the many recruits who left no mark in chronicles. Students should read it alongside chancery, chronicle, and archaeological evidence.

Historical Debate

Was the Mamluk Sultanate Politically Unstable?

The question: The chronicles record frequent depositions, assassinations, and brief reigns. Does this record show a state in chronic instability, or does it describe the ordinary operation of an unusual but durable system?

Interpretation A — Chronic instability: This view emphasizes the sheer number of violent changes of ruler, the absence of a secure succession rule, the destructive consequences of factional struggle for provincial order, and the vulnerability these created in moments of external threat such as the Ilkhanid invasions of 1299–1300 and Timur's attack in 1400–1401.
Interpretation B — Elite turbulence within a durable system: This view emphasizes that violent contests were confined mainly to the highest military elite, that provincial administration, civilian bureaucracies, courts, and endowments continued through changes of sultan, that the sultanate defeated major external enemies repeatedly, and that the state survived for over two and a half centuries.

Evidence: Both views rely on the same chronicles, but read them differently. Narrative sources tend to dramatize palace events and may exaggerate their consequences; administrative sources, such as waqf deeds, chancery documents, and records of appointments, show continuities that chronicles often neglect. The two kinds of evidence answer different questions.

Why it matters: If the sultanate was chronically unstable, its longevity needs a special explanation, and a decline narrative becomes plausible. If instability was structural and compatible with durable institutions, as the better evidence suggests, then the endurance of the regime becomes easier to understand. The strongest conclusion is that elite instability and state durability coexisted.

Student task: Identify two kinds of evidence that would help determine whether a change of sultan disrupted the work of provincial administration, and explain what each might show.

Multiple Perspectives

Living Under the Mamluk System

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing what historians have inferred from many sources. They are not quotations from named individuals, and the groups did not hold equal power.

A newly purchased mamluk trainee: Removed from home and family, enslaved, and placed in barracks, the young recruit faced discipline and the prospect of advancement through loyalty to his master. His position was one of legal dependence, but the path before him could lead to rank and wealth.
An awlad al-nas son of a mamluk emir: Born free into a wealthy household, he enjoyed education and connections but not the military rank his father held. His place in the system depended on the favor of the elite and on finding a role in the army's lesser ranks, administration, or scholarship.
A civilian bureaucrat in Cairo: Working in the chancery or the financial administration, he provided the literacy and expertise on which the military rulers depended, but held no command of troops and could be dismissed or punished when a faction fell.
A provincial notable in Damascus: Local scholars and merchants dealt with governors sent from Cairo, whose authority they needed to accommodate. They could benefit from the stability the regime offered, and also suffer from its exactions or from the effects of factional struggles.
An Abbasid caliph in Cairo: Honored with a title of great symbolic weight, he lived under the sultan's control. His investiture of a ruler mattered, but his own power was limited to the legitimacy he could confer.
A Frankish or Armenian envoy: Dealing with a power that could negotiate treaties and could also campaign effectively, he faced an interlocutor that was neither a petty ruler nor an easily intimidated one.
Continuity and Change Over Time

The Sultanate under Baybars (c. 1265) and under Barquq (c. 1390)

What Changed

  • The composition of the elite shifted gradually, with Circassians increasingly prominent alongside, and then more than, Kipchak Turks
  • The Frankish mainland states and the Ilkhanid threat, central concerns of the 1260s, had been resolved
  • The sultanate had passed through a long Qalawunid era and the demographic shock of the Black Death (Chapter 41)
  • Factional networks and household alliances had reconfigured around new groups

What Stayed the Same

  • Military slavery as the means of reproducing the ruling elite
  • Cairo-centered rule with Damascus as the principal Syrian center
  • Revenue assignments (iqtaʻ) supporting the army
  • Abbasid caliph in Cairo, Sunni legal patronage, and the claim to protect the pilgrimage
  • Succession decided among military elites rather than by fixed rule

Who experienced change most directly? Members of the military elite felt the shift in composition and alliances most directly, since their careers depended on it. Who experienced greater continuity? Rural cultivators, urban artisans, and scholars experienced many of the same obligations and institutions under both rulers. Why the pattern matters: The comparison shows that a change of elite composition need not mean a change of system.

Cause and Effect

The Fall of Acre, 1291

Long-term causes

The consolidation of Mamluk power under Baybars and Qalawun; the progressive reduction of Frankish territory; the fragmentation of the Latin states and military orders; dependence on irregular European reinforcement.

Short-term causes

The accession of al-Ashraf Khalil, who inherited his father's preparations; the capture of Tripoli in 1289; breakdowns in truces with the Franks of Acre; and a large siege army and engines assembled in the spring of 1291.

Event and immediate effects

Acre fell on 18 May 1291 after a determined defense. Within weeks the remaining coastal positions were abandoned or taken, and the Mamluks dismantled many fortifications to prevent a return.

Long-term consequences

The mainland Crusader-state era ended and the eastern Mediterranean's political map changed. But Latin Cyprus remained, Italian merchants continued to trade in Mamluk ports, and crusading ideas persisted in Europe.

Historical complexity: The fall of Acre cannot be reduced to a single cause or to the personality of one sultan. It was the result of decades of Mamluk institutional consolidation, Frankish weakness, and the circumstances of a particular siege. Nor was it the end of Latin involvement in the region: it changed the form of that involvement.

Everyday Life and Social History

Inside a Mamluk Military Household

For a young recruit, life began with removal from the community of his birth and arrival in Egypt, where he was purchased by or for a master. In barracks, he studied the Qurʾan, Arabic, and the basic duties of a Muslim, and trained for years in horsemanship and weapons under instructors who were themselves usually mamluks. Discipline could be harsh. Comradeship developed among the recruits of a single master, and on completing training they were manumitted and entered the army, usually in a junior rank. Advancement came through the master's favor, service in campaigns, and alliance with other members of the household.

A senior mamluk's own household could be large, including his purchased mamluks, servants, wives and concubines, children, and dependents. Mamluks often married, sometimes into the families of other emirs or local notables, and their children became awlad al-nas. Those children grew up in a different world from their fathers: born free and Muslim, raised in comfort, and often educated, but excluded from the main military hierarchy. Evidence for the daily lives of mamluks and their families comes largely from chronicles, biographical dictionaries, and waqf documents, which tend to emphasize elite men. The experiences of women in these households, and of the many recruits who did not rise, are much harder to recover.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how the Mamluk Sultanate, a regime of former military slaves, built a durable state ruling Egypt and Syria from about 1260 to the later fifteenth century. Beginning from the settlement that followed Ayn Jalut, it traced the transformation of a victorious but unsettled regime into an institutionalized state with routines of administration, communication, and justice.

The central mechanism of the system was military slavery. Recruits were purchased from outside the Islamic world, converted, trained, and manumitted, and remained bound to their masters and cohorts by loyalty. Because the sons of mamluks normally did not enter the same status, the elite depended on continuous recruitment, and political life revolved around competing households. "Mamluk" was a status, not an ethnic group.

Succession and political violence were structural consequences of this system. There was no universally binding hereditary rule, so rulers were chosen and removed through negotiation and force among emirs. The Qalawunid era was an attempt to combine mamluk politics with dynastic succession, and for much of the fourteenth century descendants of Qalawun held the throne, though often under the control of powerful emirs.

Egypt and Syria were governed as a single strategic system, with Cairo as the capital and Damascus as the principal provincial center. Communications, regular rotation of governors, and the sultan's control of revenue assignments held the system together, though Syria remained at once a frontier, a recruiting ground, and a base for ambitious emirs.

Mamluk rulers constructed legitimacy through several means: victory over Mongols and Franks, the hosting of an Abbasid caliph in Cairo, patronage of Sunni law and learning, public justice, and the protection of the holy cities. The caliph held symbolic and religious-political legitimacy but not governing power.

Military campaigns reduced and then ended the mainland Frankish states, with the fall of Acre in 1291 as the culmination. The Mongol Ilkhanate, in contrast, was a continuing threat after Ayn Jalut, and the major invasions of 1299–1303 were defeated before a settlement in 1323. Diplomacy with the Golden Horde, Byzantium, Italian maritime states, Aragon, and Armenian Cilicia shows that the sultanate operated in a broad international system.

Al-Nasir Muhammad's long third reign demonstrated that strong sultanic authority was possible within the system, while the period after 1341 showed that government could continue under weak sultans. The shift from the Bahri to the Burji period, conventionally dated to Barquq's accession in 1382, reflected a gradual change in elite composition rather than an abrupt revolution.

In the fifteenth century, sultans such as Barsbay and Qaitbay governed an established state, campaigned in the Mediterranean, conducted wide diplomacy, and faced the rising Ottoman power on the northern frontier. The sultanate confronted real limits, including succession disputes, dependence on imported manpower, fiscal strain, and changing military technology, but these should not be read as proof of inevitable decline.

The historiographical lesson is that elite instability and state durability coexisted. The Mamluk Sultanate lasted for more than two centuries because its institutions regenerated a ruling military class while civilian administration, provincial government, religious legitimacy, and the Egyptian-Syrian strategic system continued to function.

The chapters that follow build on this foundation. Chapter 38 examines the economy, society, and religious life of the sultanate. Chapter 41 treats the Black Death. Chapter 42 surveys the Arab world around 1500, and Chapter 43 turns to the Ottoman conquest.

The ruler of the Mamluk state, head of the largest mamluk household, who held effective political and military power.
Iqtaʻ
An assignment of the revenue of a given area to a soldier or officer in return for military service, not ownership of land.
Halqa
A body of troops distinct from the sultan's own mamluks, including freeborn soldiers and awlad al-nas.
Naʿib al-saltana
A deputy of the sultan, including the senior provincial governor in Damascus.
Atabak al-ʿasakir
The commander-in-chief of the army, one of the highest offices of the sultanate.
Dawadar
Literally "inkwell-bearer": a powerful court official associated with royal correspondence, incoming mail, orders, and ceremonial duties, often an important emir.
Vizier
A senior civil official, associated with finance and administration, whose standing varied across the period.
Barid
The relay system of couriers and stations linking Cairo, Damascus, and other centers.
Mazalim
Sessions in which the ruler or his officials heard complaints and grievances.
Dar al-ʿadl
A "house of justice," a setting for public hearings by the sultan or his deputies.
Investiture
The formal conferral of authority; in this chapter, the caliph's recognition of a sultan.
Waqf
A pious endowment of property whose income supports a designated purpose, such as a school or hospital.
Kiswa
The covering of the Kaaba, sent annually from Mamluk Egypt as a sign of its role in the pilgrimage.
Bahri
Conventional label for the earlier period of Mamluk rule, c. 1250–1382, from the Bahriyya regiment.
Burji
Conventional label for the later period from 1382, from the Burjiyya, Qalawun's tower-housed mamluks.
Kipchak
Turkic peoples of the Pontic-Caspian steppe, an important source of early mamluk recruits.
Circassian
People from the northern Caucasus, increasingly prominent among later mamluk recruits.
Ilkhanate
The Mongol state in Iran and Iraq, the sultanate's chief eastern adversary.
Golden Horde
The Mongol state of the Pontic-Caspian steppe, a diplomatic partner and a source of recruits.
Rawk
A cadastral survey and reallocation of revenue assignments in Egypt, notably around 1315.
Chancery
The office producing official correspondence and documents, whose staff also wrote some of the period's histories.
Sharif of Mecca
The local ruler of the Hijaz under Mamluk suzerainty.

Studying a Mamluk Sultan and His Household

This project asks you to investigate one sultan or senior emir of the Mamluk period and to reconstruct the household and alliances that supported him.

  1. Choose a figure. Select a sultan or emir discussed in this chapter, such as Baybars, Qalawun, al-Nasir Muhammad, Barquq, Barsbay, or Qaitbay.

Chapter Reflection

The Mamluk Sultanate matters because it shows that a state can be durable without being orderly in the ways modern readers expect. Its rulers came to power by unconventional means and kept it through a system that made succession a negotiation. Yet its institutions outlasted countless individual rulers. Understanding why requires looking past the palace and attending to the households, offices, provinces, courts, and endowments that carried the work of government.

The chapter also reveals how the sultanate connected different regions. Cairo and Damascus were bound in a single system; the steppe supplied recruits; Italian and Aragonese merchants traded in its ports; the Hijaz gave it prestige; and Mongol, Armenian, Byzantine, and Ottoman neighbors shaped its strategy. The Mamluk state was an active participant in the politics of the late medieval world.

Several of its features shaped what followed. The Ottoman conquest inherited an Egypt and Syria with sophisticated administration and strong regional identities, treated in Chapters 43 and 44. Patterns of household politics, military revenue arrangements, and relationships between rulers and religious scholars left traces in later centuries. And the sultanate's long-lasting claim to guard the holy cities is a reminder that Arabia's religious significance has repeatedly drawn the attention of powerful states.

Within the larger history of the Arab world, the Mamluk Sultanate offers a lesson in how to read political history. Violence among elites and the endurance of institutions are not opposites. Students who carry that lesson forward will be better prepared to read the chapters ahead, from the social and economic life of Mamluk society in Chapter 38 to the transformations that culminate in the Ottoman age.
