Chapter Opening

In the early 1690s a Damascene scholar and Sufi teacher, ʿAbd al-Ghani al-Nabulusi, set out on a journey that carried him through Syria, Egypt, and the holy cities of the Hijaz. He prayed in great mosques, visited tombs, consulted learned men, and wrote an account of what he saw. His itinerary was not a tour of an "Ottoman Empire" as a political unit. It was a journey through a web of religious and intellectual relationships that stretched across provinces, languages, and legal schools, and that no single office in Istanbul could fully direct.

Al-Nabulusi (1641–1731) belonged to a scholarly world that had existed long before the Ottoman conquests of 1516–1517 and that continued to reproduce itself for two centuries afterward. In his lifetime a student could leave a Syrian town, study in Damascus, travel to Cairo to hear lectures at al-Azhar, make the pilgrimage to Mecca and Medina, and return home with certificates authorizing him to teach what he had learned. A Christian cleric in Aleppo could correspond with Rome or Istanbul while teaching his parishioners in Arabic. A rabbi in Safed could write legal works read by Jewish communities from Salonica to Cairo. A Zaydi scholar in the Yemeni highlands could claim, on the basis of learning and lineage, the right to lead a political community that recognized no Ottoman authority at all.

This chapter asks how religious institutions and communities organized learning, legal life, social relationships, and cultural identity across the diverse societies of the Ottoman Arab lands, principally between the sixteenth and eighteenth centuries. Its governing argument is that imperial authority and religious authority overlapped without being identical. The sultan, the learned hierarchy of Istanbul, and provincial officials appointed judges, funded institutions, confirmed endowments, and favored particular legal traditions. Yet most religious practice, most teaching, and most communal organization were sustained by local scholars, Sufi shaykhs, mosque congregations, families of learned notables, Shi'a jurists, Christian clergy, Jewish rabbis, and the property-holding patrons whose endowments paid for them all.

The chapter also insists on internal diversity. "Sunni Islam" was not a single institution: it contained four legal schools, competing devotional styles, and learned families with long local roots. "Shi'a Islam" included Twelver communities in Iraq, Bahrain, and Jabal ʿAmil, and the Zaydis of Yemen, whose imams stood outside the Twelver tradition entirely. "Christian" and "Jewish" each covered many churches, rites, languages, and histories. Throughout, the chapter avoids two familiar distortions: the image of Ottoman pluralism as unbroken harmony and the image of non-Muslim and minority life as constant persecution. The documentary record shows cooperation, rivalry, negotiation, hierarchy, and sometimes violence, varying by place and period.

The chapter relies on evidence quite different from that of political narrative. Sharia court registers record waqf deeds, estate inventories, and disputes; collections of fatwas show how scholars reasoned about daily problems; biographical dictionaries preserve the careers of learned men and, less often, women; manuscripts and their ownership notes trace the movement of books; church and synagogue records, travelers' accounts, and shrine architecture complete the picture. Each type of source reflects the interests of those who created it, and the chapter points out what each cannot reveal.

The chapter is bounded by its neighbors. Chapter 44 explained provincial administration and the court system as an arm of government; Chapter 45 examined cities, commerce, and the logistics of the pilgrimage; Chapter 47 will turn to the political power of provincial households; Chapter 48 will examine the first Saudi state and the religious movement of Muhammad ibn ʿAbd al-Wahhab; and Chapters 49 through 51 will take up nineteenth-century reform and the Nahda. Here the focus is on what believers believed, taught, endowed, and practiced, and on the institutions through which they did so.

By its end, students should be able to explain how learning moved across provincial boundaries, how endowments sustained religious life, how Sufism formed part of mainstream scholarship rather than standing outside it, and why historians now question the idea of a fixed "millet system" in the sixteenth century. They should also be able to compare the distinct religious environments of Cairo, Damascus, the Hijaz, Iraq, Yemen, North Africa, and the Christian and Jewish communities that lived alongside Muslim majorities.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Distinguish imperial religious authority (the sultan, the shaykh al-Islam, the learned hierarchy) from the local authority of provincial scholars, Sufi teachers, and communal leaders.
  2. Explain how the four Sunni legal schools coexisted in the Arab provinces and why Ottoman preference for Hanafi jurisprudence did not make Arab Muslims Hanafis.
  3. Differentiate the roles of the mufti, who issues a legal opinion, and the qadi, who delivers a binding judgment.
  4. Trace how knowledge circulated through teaching circles, madrasas, certificates of authorization (ijaza), manuscripts, and pilgrimage between Cairo, Damascus, the Hijaz, Iraq, and the Maghreb.
  5. Analyze the legal structure and social functions of waqf endowments, including the roles of founders, administrators, beneficiaries, and women.
  6. Describe the principal Sufi traditions and explain why Sufism and formal scholarship were closely intertwined.
  7. Interpret debates within Sunni Islam over devotional practice and legal interpretation, using al-Nabulusi as a case study.
  8. Compare the religious and scholarly worlds of Twelver Shi'a Iraq, Zaydi Yemen, and Ismaili and other Muslim communities.
  9. Characterize the principal Christian and Jewish communities of the Arab provinces and their relationships with their own clergy, with one another, and with Ottoman officials.
  10. Evaluate the historiographical critique of the "millet system" and assess what evidence supports a more locally varied account.
  11. Assess the kinds of evidence historians use to reconstruct religious life and the perspectives each source leaves out.
  12. Contextualize religious continuity and change from the sixteenth to the eighteenth century and connect them to the political and religious developments of Chapters 47 and 48.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How far did the Ottoman state shape religious life in the Arab provinces, and where did its influence stop?
  2. Why might a scholar's authority rest on a chain of teachers and certificates as much as on an official appointment?
  3. How did legal-school diversity function in practice in cities where the ruling elite followed one school and most inhabitants another?
  4. In what ways did endowments connect piety, property, family interest, and public welfare?
  5. Why should Sufism be treated as part of mainstream Muslim learning rather than as a separate or marginal phenomenon?
  6. How did Shi'a, Zaydi, Ismaili, Christian, and Jewish communities maintain their own institutions while living under Ottoman or Ottoman-adjacent rule?
  7. What does the absence of a single, uniform "millet system" in the sixteenth century change about how historians describe Ottoman pluralism?
  8. How should historians weigh evidence from court registers, biographical dictionaries, and travelers' accounts when each reflects a different viewpoint?
  9. What differences between Cairo, Damascus, Baghdad, the Hijaz, Tunis, and Sanaa explain why religious life took different forms across the provinces?

Historical Context

When the Ottomans conquered Syria and Egypt in 1516–1517, they entered lands in which religious institutions were already centuries old. Cairo contained a vast number of mosques, madrasas, Sufi lodges, and endowed properties dating back to the Fatimid, Ayyubid, and Mamluk periods (Chapters 31, 35, 37, and 38). Damascus and Aleppo had comparable foundations. The great shrines of Mecca, Medina, and Jerusalem drew scholars and pilgrims; the Shi'a shrines of Najaf and Karbala, the Zaydi scholarly tradition of the Yemeni highlands, the Maliki legal culture of the Maghreb, and the Coptic, Orthodox, Syriac, Armenian, Maronite, and Jewish communities all had their own long histories. The early modern period did not begin from a blank slate.

Ottoman rule altered this landscape without replacing it. The state appointed senior judges, confirmed or founded major endowments, built mosques and charitable complexes, and expected loyalty from religious elites. Its official legal tradition was Hanafi, and its learned hierarchy, the ilmiye, culminated in the shaykh al-Islam of Istanbul. But the Arab provinces had been governed under Mamluk and earlier rulers who also patronized, appointed, and supervised religious institutions, and Ottoman administrators generally preferred continuity to disruption. Chapter 43 described the conquests, and Chapter 44 explained how governors, taxation, and courts worked. This chapter examines the religious and intellectual life within that framework.

Historians have long debated how far the Ottomans "Turkified" or "Ottomanized" Arab religious culture. The current consensus is more nuanced: an Ottoman imperial religious culture, expressed in architecture, official ranks, and the Turkish language of administration, existed alongside a vigorous Arabic scholarly culture whose centers were Cairo, Damascus, the Hijaz, and the Maghreb. Arab scholars participated in both. Over time, learned families in many cities acquired hereditary access to local posts and built networks that bridged provincial and imperial spheres.

Two cautions guide the chapter. First, presentism: nineteenth-century arrangements, such as a formally organized communal hierarchy for each non-Muslim religion, or the sharply defined sectarian identities of later nationalism, must not be projected backward onto the sixteenth century. Second, institutional oversimplification: religious communities were internally varied, and institutions that look unified in later textbooks were often loose networks whose boundaries shifted with place, patronage, and politics.

Historical Evidence: How Do We Know?

Religious life in the Ottoman Arab provinces can be reconstructed from several families of evidence, and each shapes what historians can say. Sharia court registers (sijills) survive in large numbers for Cairo, Damascus, Aleppo, Jerusalem, Tunis, and other cities. They record waqf deeds, marriages, inheritances, debts, sales, and disputes, and they preserve the voices, in formulaic legal language, of Muslims, Christians, and Jews, including many women. Their limitation is that they record only what came to court, and they reflect legal categories rather than inner belief.

Fatwa collections preserve scholars' answers to questions posed by ordinary people. They show what problems worried believers, but the questions were often edited, generalized, or stripped of names. Biographical dictionaries, such as those compiled in Damascus by members of the Ghazzi family and later by al-Muhibbi and al-Muradi, document the careers, teachers, and writings of learned men and some women. They are rich in names and connections, but they celebrate their subjects and favor the educated elite of the compilers' own cities.

Manuscripts, their marginal notes, colophons, and ownership statements reveal who copied, owned, and read books. Estate inventories list the books owned by deceased individuals. Architecture and inscriptions reveal patrons and purposes. Travel accounts, including those written by Muslims such as al-Nabulusi, Christians such as the Syrian archdeacon Paul of Aleppo, and European visitors, offer vivid but selective observations. Church, monastery, and synagogue records and the responsa of rabbis supply the internal perspective of non-Muslim communities, though they survive unevenly. Finally, Ottoman imperial orders (firmans) show how the central government responded to petitions from religious communities.

Students should always ask who made a source, for whom, and who is missing from it. Peasants, bedouin, enslaved people, and most women appear mainly through the filter of others' records. Evidence about Shi'a and Zaydi communities often comes from hostile or distant writers as well as from their own scholarly tradition. The chapter marks these limitations as they arise.

Section 46.1

Religion and Society under Ottoman Rule

The Arab provinces of the Ottoman Empire formed one of the most religiously varied regions of the early modern world. Egypt had a Sunni Muslim majority, a large Coptic Christian minority, and a Jewish community of Rabbanite and Karaite Jews. Syria and Palestine contained Sunni Muslims, Greek Orthodox, Maronite, Syriac, and Armenian Christians, Jews, and communities such as the Druze and Nusayris (later called Alawis). Iraq included Sunni and Twelver Shi'a Muslims, Kurdish Muslims, Jews, and Christians of several churches. The Hijaz hosted a multiethnic scholarly population around the holy cities. Highland Yemen was Zaydi, the lowlands largely Shafi'i, and small Ismaili and Jewish communities lived in the mountains. North Africa was predominantly Maliki Sunni, with Ibadi communities in the Mzab and Jerba, as well as Jewish and, in port cities, European Christian residents and captives.

Ottoman rule inherited this diversity rather than flattening it. The empire did not assign a single religious system to the provinces. In Egypt and Syria, it kept the great endowed institutions of the Mamluk era, left most existing scholars in place, and added its own patrons. In Iraq, it confronted the Shi'a shrines and a Safavid rival that claimed protection over them. In Yemen and the Hijaz, it adapted to local religious elites whose authority long predated its arrival. The sultan's religious claims were substantial, especially after the conquest of the holy cities, but the practical effect was to add an imperial layer to existing religious arrangements rather than to substitute for them.

Religion also structured ordinary social life. Sacred calendars governed markets and holidays; religious law shaped marriage, inheritance, and property; endowments paid for schooling and charity; and membership in a mosque congregation, Sufi order, church parish, or synagogue community supplied identity and mutual support. Yet these categories overlapped. A Muslim merchant might belong to a legal school, a Sufi order, a neighborhood, and a guild at the same time, and Christians and Jews also belonged to the trades, quarters, and courts they shared with Muslims. The chapter therefore treats religious communities as parts of larger social worlds, not as sealed compartments.

The sources that reveal these arrangements also reveal their limits. Court registers show Muslims, Christians, and Jews contracting, litigating, and endowing property, while church and synagogue records, fatwa collections, and travelers' accounts add the viewpoints of communities and individuals the courts did not capture. A historian reading Ottoman Aleppo, for example, finds a city in which Sunni scholars, Sufi lodges, Maronite and Greek Orthodox merchants, Armenian silk traders, and Jewish brokers lived within a few quarters of one another, and in which religious institutions competed for patrons and followers as well as cooperating. The remaining sections develop this picture region by region and institution by institution.

Section 46.2

The Sultan, Religious Legitimacy, and the Ottoman Scholarly Establishment

Ottoman sultans based their legitimacy on several religious claims: leadership of the Sunni Muslim community, the defense of the faith through warfare (ghaza), and, after 1517, service as protector of the holy cities of Mecca and Medina. The sultans also used the title of caliph at times, though the weight of that claim varied and grew more prominent in later centuries. Mosques built in the sultan's name, inscriptions invoking his titles, and the Friday sermon (khutba) recited in his name in every province were all ways of expressing sovereignty through religion.

The empire developed a learned hierarchy, the ilmiye, whose members were trained in an ordered ladder of imperial madrasas and appointed to posts as teachers, judges, and muftis. At its summit stood the chief mufti of Istanbul, known as the shaykh al-Islam, whose fatwas on matters of state could affirm or limit official policy. The sixteenth-century jurist Ebussuud Efendi, who held the post under Süleyman, is remembered for aligning imperial law with Hanafi doctrine. Major provincial judgeships, such as those of Cairo, Damascus, and Baghdad, were filled from this hierarchy, and the senior judges often served only a limited term before moving on.

Arab scholars occupied a complicated position. Some pursued careers in the imperial system, learned Ottoman Turkish, and moved among provinces. Many more remained within local scholarly communities, serving as deputy judges, teachers, preachers, muftis, and administrators of endowments. Over the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, local learned families increasingly secured these local positions, sometimes passing them within a family. The authority of Istanbul thus rested on a partnership with local elites: imperial appointees held the highest offices, while local scholars supplied the continuity and expertise on which daily religious life depended. Historians stress that this was an uneven relationship, shaped by the strength of the local scholarly community, the distance from the capital, and the political circumstances of each period.

The conquest of Egypt and Syria brought particular challenges of legitimacy. Under the Mamluks, the Abbasid line of "shadow caliphs" had been housed in Cairo, and the Ottomans ended that arrangement without immediately claiming the caliphal title in any systematic way. They stressed instead their custody of the holy cities, the title "Servant of the Two Noble Sanctuaries," the dispatch of the annual pilgrimage caravan and its ceremonial gift-bearing procession (Chapter 45), and the building of mosques in the imperial style. Sinan Pasha's mosque at Bulaq in Cairo (1571), Sulayman Pasha's mosque in the Citadel (1528), and the Süleymaniyya complex in Damascus are among the monuments that gave visible form to imperial piety. Their domes, pencil minarets, and Turkish inscriptions were immediately recognizable as Ottoman, and they stood among the older Mamluk and earlier buildings of the Arab cities.

The ilmiye also set rules for careers. A judge or teacher who rose through the imperial madrasa ladder moved from post to post, often every few years, and posted deputies (nuwwab) to run the day-to-day work of courts. In the Arab provinces, local scholars filled many of these deputy positions, and over time a family might hold a deputy judgeship, a teaching post at a particular mosque, and the administration of an endowment, in effect creating a local scholarly dynasty. Historians who study these families, such as the Ghazzi and al-Muradi families of Damascus, the al-Khalidi and al-Husayni families of Jerusalem, and the scholarly houses of Cairo, show how they combined local roots with access to imperial offices. Their power rested on both: the sultan's appointment could confirm a position, but the community's recognition of a family's learning gave it staying power. When Istanbul's appointees clashed with local notables, outcomes varied, and imperial authority sometimes yielded to the practical weight of local families.

Imperial and provincial scholars also disagreed on substance. The sultan's jurists issued rulings about taxation, land, and criminal penalties in kanun (sultanic law), which they harmonized with Islamic law as best they could, while provincial scholars sometimes preferred their schools' inherited doctrines. The relation between sharia and kanun was a continuing question in Ottoman legal thought, and Arab scholars participated in this debate through their fatwas and legal commentaries. This chapter treats it as a background condition of religious authority, with provincial administration left to Chapter 44.

Section 46.3

Sunni Legal Schools and the Diversity of Islamic Jurisprudence

Sunni Muslims followed four recognized legal schools (madhhabs), the Hanafi, Maliki, Shafi'i, and Hanbali. These emerged in the early Abbasid centuries (Chapter 28) and by the Ottoman period each was an established tradition with its own texts, teachers, and regional strongholds. The schools disagreed on details of ritual, family law, contracts, and procedure, but they regarded one another as legitimate interpretations of the same law.

The Ottoman state officially favored the Hanafi school, which dominated the empire's central lands, and appointed Hanafi chief judges to the major Arab cities. This preference shaped official courts, endowments, and the ranks of the imperial learned hierarchy. It did not convert the population. In Egypt, most Muslims remained Shafi'i or, in parts of Upper Egypt, Maliki; in Syria and Palestine, the Shafi'i school was widespread, and a Hanbali community, centered on the Salihiyya quarter of Damascus, retained its own scholarly tradition; in North Africa, the Maliki school dominated, with a small Hanafi Ottoman ruling class; in Iraq, Hanafi and Shafi'i traditions coexisted, with Hanbali currents in Baghdad; in the Hijaz, scholars of all four schools taught in the Haram, and the prayer leaders of the Meccan sanctuary represented the different schools.

Courts reflected this diversity. In Cairo, the Hanafi chief judge sent from Istanbul was assisted by deputies representing the other schools, allowing litigants to have their cases heard under the school they followed. In Tunis and Algiers, official Hanafi muftis served alongside Maliki muftis who spoke for most of the population. The system allowed flexibility, since litigants could sometimes choose a favorable doctrine, and it required scholars to know each other's schools. At the same time, legal-school competition could involve rivalry over positions, endowment income, and status. The coexistence of schools was therefore both a feature of religious pluralism within Sunni Islam and a source of institutional negotiation.

Concrete cases show how this worked. In Ottoman Cairo, the chief judge was a Hanafi sent from Istanbul, while the city's four deputy judges represented each school; a Shafi'i litigant could have a marriage or inheritance case heard by the Shafi'i deputy, and a Maliki litigant from Upper Egypt by the Maliki deputy. This meant that the same city could apply different rules on questions such as the guardianship of a bride, the grounds for a wife's request for divorce, or the amount of inheritance a daughter received, depending on the school applied. Scholars debated the doctrines of other schools and sometimes drew on them: Hanafi jurists, for instance, ruled that a cash endowment was valid, while other schools were more reluctant, and a founder might choose a legal setting favorable to the arrangement he wanted.

The Hanbali school was the smallest but had a distinct scholarly community. In Damascus the Hanbalis of the Salihiyya quarter, descended from migrants who had come from Palestine centuries earlier, maintained their own scholarly families, mosque, and traditions of hadith study, and their library holdings preserved texts of Hanbali theology that were read well beyond the school. In Iraq and Najd, Hanbali traditions also persisted, though Najd's later religious politics belongs to Chapter 48. The Ibadi communities of Oman, Jerba, and the Mzab belonged to a legal and theological tradition separate from the four Sunni schools and are treated in §46.17.

Legal-school identity also had social meaning. A family's school could signal its origins, as when Turkish-speaking officials were Hanafi and a local Cairene family Shafi'i, and it affected access to salaried posts, since endowments often named a specific school for teachers or beneficiaries. In Mecca, the presence of imams for each school in the Haram, and the competition among them over the order of prayer and over income, showed how legal-school identity entered the organization of worship. Historians therefore read school affiliation as part of social and institutional history as well as of doctrine.

Section 46.4

Muftis, Fatwas, and Religious Authority in Everyday Life

A mufti is a qualified jurist who issues a non-binding legal opinion, a fatwa, in response to a question. A qadi is a judge who hears a dispute and issues a binding ruling backed by the state. The two roles interacted constantly. Judges often consulted muftis on points of law, litigants brought fatwas to court as supporting evidence, and in the Ottoman system official muftis were appointed to major towns and were sometimes paid for their service. Yet the authority of a fatwa came from the mufti's learning and reputation, not from enforcement.

Fatwas addressed a wide range of questions: how to divide an inheritance, whether a divorce was valid, the legality of a sale or a loan, the obligations of a waqf administrator, the permissibility of a devotional practice or a new commodity such as coffee or tobacco, and the proper conduct of rulers. The Palestinian jurist Khayr al-Din al-Ramli (1585–1671), a Hanafi mufti of Ramla, compiled a collection of fatwas that became widely cited, showing how a provincial scholar's reasoning could reach well beyond his own town. Muftis often reasoned from earlier authoritative texts and balanced doctrine against local custom (ʿurf), which jurists generally treated as a legitimate consideration in applying law.

Chapter 44 explained how courts operated as administrative institutions. Here the interest is in the religious and intellectual reasoning that gave legal decisions their authority. Fatwas show that ordinary people, including women, craftspeople, and peasants, sought scholarly guidance on daily problems, and that scholars adapted inherited doctrine to changing circumstances. They also reveal tension: Ottoman officials sometimes sought fatwas to support state policy, while scholars could answer according to their understanding of the law. The relationship between jurist and ruler was therefore one of mutual dependence and negotiated independence.

Fatwa collections show how this reasoning operated on specific cases. A typical question might ask whether a wife whose husband had disappeared could remarry, how a shared irrigation channel's water should be divided among heirs, whether a waqf administrator who rented property below market value was guilty of breach of trust, or whether a Muslim could pray in a mosque built on land taken wrongfully. The mufti's answer would usually begin from the standard texts of his school and might note a minority opinion or a local custom. Collections by Ottoman jurists such as al-Ramli were organized by legal topic so that later muftis and judges could use them as reference works. The collection of the Istanbul shaykh al-Islam Ebussuud, which circulated widely, shows how an imperial jurist could shape provincial practice on questions like land tenure and endowment management.

The system of consultation also reveals who had access to religious authority. Fatwas were requested by men and women, Muslims and sometimes non-Muslims, and the question could be posed orally or in writing, often in the presence of a scribe. Muftis charged little or nothing for the service in many places, but the social standing of the person asking affected how the question was framed and who read the answer. The distance between a mufti's pronouncement and its enforcement meant that litigants, family elders, and neighborhood notables played a part in turning a fatwa into practice. Fatwas also circulated outside the courts, as in sermons, in correspondence among scholars, and in debates over the permissibility of coffee, tobacco, music, or devotional ceremonies. This section concerns the logic and authority of these opinions; the machinery of provincial courts belongs to Chapter 44.

Historical Vocabulary
Ulama, Madhhab, Mufti, Fatwa, and Qadi

The ulama (Arabic ʿulamaʾ, "those who know") were scholars of religious knowledge: jurists, judges, teachers, preachers, and hadith specialists. They were not a priesthood in the Christian sense, since Sunni Islam has no ordained clergy and authority came from learning and recognition, but they formed a learned class that exercised social influence. A madhhab is a school of legal interpretation. A mufti issues a fatwa, a reasoned opinion; a qadi issues a judgment.

These terms matter because modern equivalents mislead. "Clergy" suggests a hierarchy of ordination, "court" implies a modern separation of powers, and "law" suggests a single code. In the Ottoman Arab lands, religious scholarship, legal practice, and public authority overlapped, but each office had its own basis of legitimacy. The Ottoman state made some muftis and judges officials without turning scholarship into bureaucracy.

Section 46.5

Mosques, Madrasas, and the Organization of Learning

Learning in the Ottoman Arab provinces took place in settings that ranged from formal endowed schools to informal gatherings. The madrasa was an endowed college, often attached to a mosque or tomb, with salaried teachers, stipends for students, and a library or reading room. The mosque teaching circle (halqa) required no building beyond the mosque itself: a teacher sat by a pillar and students gathered around to read a text and hear commentary. Sufi lodges, private homes, and shops also served as teaching spaces.

Education generally began with elementary schooling (kuttab), where children, mostly boys but sometimes girls, learned to read the Qur'an and perhaps to write and calculate. Advanced study focused on Arabic grammar, the Qur'an and its interpretation, hadith, law, theology, logic, and in some centers rhetoric, mathematics, astronomy, and medicine. Students typically read standard texts with a teacher, who explained and commented upon them; mastering a text earned a certificate. Teaching was personal: students chose teachers, and a scholar's prestige depended on his teachers and students more than on the building where he taught.

Patronage mattered. The Ottoman dynasty, governors, army officers, wealthy merchants, and scholars' families founded madrasas and endowed teaching positions, as the next sections show. The imperial madrasas of the empire's core followed a structured ladder, but in the Arab provinces many institutions were older foundations whose arrangements varied. Historians caution against assuming that the Ottoman learned hierarchy replaced them; instead, formal imperial education and informal local scholarship coexisted, and scholars could belong to both. The relationship among teaching spaces, endowments, and certificates is the key to understanding how learning was organized.

Madrasas differed widely in size and prestige. Some were small institutions founded by a single donor, with a few students and one teacher whose salary came from a shop or an orchard; others were large complexes with halls, dormitory cells, a library, and numerous salaried posts. Students in the larger institutions might receive a stipend, a room, and a daily ration of bread or food. Teachers were appointed by the endowment's administrator or by a judge according to the founder's conditions, and the appointment could be hereditary in practice if the founder's deed allowed or if a family succeeded in maintaining its position. Disputes about appointments, salaries, and the use of endowed rooms appear regularly in court records, showing that educational posts were valued as sources of income and prestige.

The curriculum varied by place and teacher, but the foundations were widely shared. Students began with Arabic grammar and morphology, using short texts followed by longer commentaries, and moved to rhetoric, logic, legal theory, law, Qur'an exegesis, hadith, and theology. The study of the "rational" sciences such as logic and theology was well established in Ottoman madrasas, and the imperial curriculum emphasized them more strongly than some Arab centers did, where hadith and law often received greater weight. Advanced students might study mathematics, astronomy, or medicine with specialists. Texts were often read with a teacher who explained, commented, and tested, and students who completed a book received a certificate. Because learning was organized around teachers and texts, a student's biography often reads as a list of the books he read and the men who taught them to him, a fact that biographical dictionaries preserve in detail.

Elementary education reached more people than madrasa education. In towns, neighborhood schools taught children to recite the Qur'an and sometimes to read and write, and endowments often financed them. Girls could attend, though far fewer did, and women of learned families sometimes received instruction at home. Christian and Jewish children learned in church and synagogue schools or from private tutors. This elementary level shaped the literacy of urban artisans and merchants, and it explains the market for books and for practical religious manuals examined in §46.10.

Section 46.6

Al-Azhar and the Scholarly Culture of Ottoman Cairo

Founded under the Fatimids in the late tenth century (Chapter 31), al-Azhar became in the Ottoman centuries the most prominent center of religious learning in Egypt. Its reputation grew in part because Cairo remained a great commercial and political city and because it attracted students from across the Muslim world. Historians caution that al-Azhar's later formal structure, including its modern curricula and bureaucratic administration, did not yet exist. In the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, it was a vast mosque in which many teachers held their own circles, and students studied with the teachers they chose.

Students were organized into residential and regional groups known as riwaqs, sections of the mosque where students from particular regions, including Upper Egypt, the Maghreb, Syria, the Hijaz, Yemen, the Sudan, and the Ethiopian highlands, lived and studied with the support of endowed stipends and food distributions. These communities made al-Azhar a meeting point for Arab, African, and Asian Muslims. Teachers belonged to all four legal schools, though Shafi'i scholars formed the largest group and Maliki and Hanafi scholars were also prominent. By the late seventeenth century, the position of head of al-Azhar (shaykh al-Azhar) had emerged as a recognized office, an indication of a gradual institutionalization.

Eighteenth-century Cairo's scholarly world included figures such as the Maliki jurist and Sufi Ahmad al-Dardir (1715–1786) and Murtada al-Zabidi (1732–1791), an Indian-born scholar trained in Yemen and the Hijaz who settled in Cairo in the mid-eighteenth century and became renowned for his lexicographical and hadith scholarship. The chronicler ʿAbd al-Rahman al-Jabarti (1753–1825), son of an Azhar scholar, is a major source for late Ottoman Cairo. Azhar scholars also took part in public life: as intermediaries between people and rulers, as leaders of Friday prayers, and as petitioners or protesters on occasion. Their influence extended beyond Egypt through students who carried Azhari learning to other regions, as the following sections show.

The organization of student life gives a sense of how the institution worked. Students in a riwaq were typically supported by endowments established for their region or origin; some riwaqs had their own administrators, libraries, and storage rooms, and the heads of the riwaqs represented students in disputes. The Maghribi, Syrian, and Upper Egyptian riwaqs were among the largest, and the presence of riwaqs for students from the Sudan, the Horn of Africa, West Africa, the Hijaz, and Yemen indicates how far al-Azhar's reputation reached. Many students were poor and depended on endowed bread distributions and the generosity of patrons; others came from wealthy families. Learning at al-Azhar usually involved reading commentaries on standard works of grammar, law, and theology with a succession of teachers, and a student might spend a decade or more there before returning home to teach, to serve as a judge or preacher, or to join a Sufi lodge.

The office of shaykh al-Azhar emerged in the later seventeenth century, and tradition identifies Muhammad al-Khurashi (d. 1690), a Maliki scholar, as the first holder. Historians treat this as an emerging office rather than a fully institutionalized bureaucracy: the shaykh represented the scholars and students, administered some of the mosque's affairs, and acted as an intermediary with the governor and the Mamluk-descended military households who dominated eighteenth-century Egypt (Chapter 47). Because the ranks of the Azhari scholars included members of all four schools, appointments were negotiated, and the holder of the office changed with politics and consensus among the scholars.

The late eighteenth century shows al-Azhar as a center of learning whose reach was global. Murtada al-Zabidi, born in India and trained in Yemen and the Hijaz, composed in Cairo his great commentary on the dictionary al-Qamus, the Taj al-ʿarus, and a commentary on al-Ghazali's Ihya ʾulum al-din; he taught students from many regions and received visitors from across the Ottoman world. His circle shows how a scholar from outside Egypt could settle in Cairo and become a central figure, supported by Egyptian patrons and scholars. At the same time, the chronicler al-Jabarti, writing at the turn of the nineteenth century, recorded the deaths and careers of Cairo's scholars, giving later historians a sense of the dense scholarly society of the city. These developments led into the political changes of Chapter 49, which this chapter does not narrate.

Section 46.7

Learning in Damascus, Aleppo, Jerusalem, and Palestine

Damascus preserved a deep scholarly tradition inherited from the Ayyubid and Mamluk periods. Its great mosque, the Umayyad Mosque, and the numerous madrasas and tombs of the old city and the Salihiyya suburb hosted lectures in all four schools. The Ottomans added monumental foundations, notably the Süleymaniyya complex (takiyya), built in the 1550s with a mosque, kitchen, and lodging for travelers, and the Selimiyya complex in the Salihiyya quarter, near the tomb of Ibn ʿArabi. Local scholarly families, including the Ghazzi, the ʿAjlani, and the al-Muradi, produced muftis, judges, and writers across generations and compiled biographical dictionaries that remain essential sources.

Aleppo, the great commercial city of northern Syria, had its own madrasas, such as the Khusrawiyya built by an Ottoman governor in the 1530s, and its Muslim scholars interacted with the city's large Christian and Jewish communities, which also produced their own scholars. In Jerusalem, the al-Aqsa and the Dome of the Rock anchored a scholarly community supported by endowments, and the city's Muslim, Christian, and Jewish holy places drew pilgrims. Palestinian towns such as Ramla, Nablus, Gaza, and Hebron sustained smaller but active scholarly circles. The Hanafi jurist al-Ramli of Ramla, mentioned above, and the Jerusalem-based Husayni and Khalidi families illustrate how local families used learning to hold local office.

Scholars from these cities were connected to wider networks. Damascene and Aleppine scholars traveled to Istanbul, Cairo, and the Hijaz; students from Palestine studied in Damascus and Cairo; and the Hajj caravans (Chapter 45) provided regular opportunities to study with visiting teachers. The result was a scholarly landscape in which towns maintained their own traditions while participating in imperial and Arab-wide circulations of knowledge.

In Damascus, scholarly life revolved around several centers. The Umayyad Mosque hosted teaching circles and housed Qur'an reciters and preachers; the Salihiyya quarter on Mount Qasiyun held the tombs of Ibn ʿArabi and numerous scholars as well as Hanbali and other madrasas; and the Ottoman-built Süleymaniyya and Selimiyya complexes added salaried posts, a kitchen, and lodgings. Damascene scholars compiled biographical dictionaries that preserve the intellectual history of the city: Najm al-Din al-Ghazzi (1570–1651) wrote on the notables of the tenth Islamic century, al-Muhibbi (1651–1699) on those of the eleventh, and al-Muradi (1717–1791) on those of the twelfth. These works record the teachers, texts, offices, and often the personal habits of the city's learned men, and they show families like the Ghazzi, ʿAjlani, Hamzawi, and Muradi sustaining their positions across generations.

Aleppo's scholarly world was shaped by its commercial position and by the presence of a Muslim elite, Ottoman governors, and large Christian and Jewish communities. Its Ottoman-era madrasas, including the Khusrawiyya, and its endowed mosques offered posts for scholars, and the city's families of scholars and notables, including descendants of the Prophet (ashraf), contended over local offices. In Jerusalem, Süleyman's reign saw major work on the city walls (1530s–1540s) and on the tilework of the Dome of the Rock, while the Haram al-Sharif and its endowed teachers, preachers, and Qur'an reciters sustained a scholarly community that also drew visiting Sufis and pilgrims. Jerusalem's Muslim population interacted continually with the Christian and Jewish communities tied to the same holy city.

Palestinian towns provided additional local centers. Khayr al-Din al-Ramli of Ramla, whose fatwas were widely read, held positions as mufti and teacher and was a major figure in Hanafi jurisprudence of the seventeenth century. Nablus, Gaza, and Hebron had their own scholarly families, mosques, and Sufi lodges, and Hebron's Ibrahimi mosque, sacred to Muslims, Jews, and Christians through the figure of Abraham, drew pilgrims and endowments. The scholars of these towns studied in Damascus and Cairo and sometimes taught there, and their careers show the circulation of knowledge between local and regional centers that Chapter 45's discussion of caravan routes and pilgrimage made possible.

Section 46.8

The Hijaz, Iraq, and the Circulation of Religious Knowledge

Mecca and Medina were pilgrimage destinations (Chapter 45), but they were also scholarly centers. Teachers resident in the Haram of Mecca and the Prophet's Mosque in Medina taught hadith, law, and Sufism to students from across the Muslim world, and many pilgrims stayed for months or years to study. Medina in particular became associated with hadith transmission and with Sufi teachers. Ibrahim al-Kurani (1616–1690), a Kurdish-born scholar who settled in Medina, taught students from Arabia, India, and Southeast Asia and wrote works that joined juristic reasoning, hadith, and Sufi thought. His circle included students from the Malay-Indonesian world, such as ʿAbd al-Raʾuf al-Singkili of Aceh, who studied in Medina in the seventeenth century.

The central mechanism of this circulation was the ijaza, a certificate authorizing a student to transmit a text or teach a subject, often tracing a chain of transmission back through teachers to the original author. A student who traveled to the Hijaz might gather ijazas from dozens of teachers and return home with prestige. Scholars also wrote lists of their teachers and texts (thabat or fahrasa), which are valuable records of networks. These chains linked Cairo, Damascus, the Hijaz, Yemen, and the Maghreb in a shared scholarly fabric.

Iraq formed a different scholarly environment. Baghdad, Mosul, Basra, and other cities supported Sunni madrasas, Sufi lodges, and the shrine of ʿAbd al-Qadir al-Jilani in Baghdad, a major Qadiri center that Ottoman rulers patronized after 1534. Iraq's scholars included Hanafi, Shafi'i, and Hanbali jurists and Kurdish scholars with links to the Naqshbandi and Qadiri orders. Iraq also faced political contests with Safavid Iran, which affected patronage and the movement of scholars. The Shi'a scholarly world of Najaf and Karbala, treated below, formed a parallel and partly separate intellectual sphere within Iraq.

The Hijaz's scholars formed a lineage of teachers whose names recur in the chains of transmission of students from many regions. Ahmad al-Qushashi (1583–1661), a Medina-based Sufi and teacher, and his successor Ibrahim al-Kurani taught a tradition that combined hadith transmission, law, and Sufi practice. Their students included the Aceh scholar ʿAbd al-Raʾuf al-Singkili, who returned to Southeast Asia and became a major religious figure there, and later Hijazi scholars such as Muhammad Hayat al-Sindi (d. 1750) and Abu Tahir al-Kurdi (1689–1733), the son of al-Kurani. In 1730–1732 the Indian scholar Shah Wali Allah of Delhi (1703–1762) studied with Abu Tahir and others in the Hijaz, an episode that illustrates how the holy cities formed a point of exchange between the Ottoman Arab world and South Asia. These networks are valuable evidence that "Arab" and "Ottoman" learning was part of a still larger Muslim scholarly world, and that scholarly prestige in the Hijaz could carry weight in India, Southeast Asia, and Africa.

Pilgrimage supplied the occasion. Scholars joined the Syrian and Egyptian caravans to teach and study en route, and some remained in the Hijaz for years, becoming "neighbors" (mujawirun) of the sanctuaries. Cities such as Mecca and Medina had endowed teaching posts, libraries, and Sufi lodges. The Meccan sanctuary housed teaching circles around the Kaʿba, while Medina's scholars taught in the Prophet's Mosque, and the transmission of hadith with unbroken chains of authorities (isnads) gave the holy cities special prestige. Historians also note the economic basis of this scholarly life: pious gifts from the sultan, the Egyptian treasury, rulers elsewhere in the Muslim world, and pilgrims funded stipends, and the dependence on external funding made the Hijaz's scholarly community responsive to the politics of the Ottoman center and the Sharifs of Mecca.

In Iraq, Ottoman rulers after 1534 invested in Sunni religious sites. Süleyman had the tomb of Abu Hanifa in Baghdad, the founder of the Hanafi school, restored and associated with a complex, and the Qadiri shrine of ʿAbd al-Qadir al-Jilani received patronage and was administered by the Gaylani (Kilani) family, whose hereditary position gave them local influence. Baghdad's madrasas and Mosul's endowed colleges supported Hanafi and Shafi'i scholars, while Kurdish scholars in northern Iraq and the Zagros foothills linked Iraq to the wider Kurdish scholarly tradition. Families of notables in Mosul, such as the ʿUmari, combined scholarly and administrative roles, a pattern developed politically in Chapter 47. The Iraqi Sunni world was therefore connected to Istanbul through appointments and patronage, to the Hijaz through the pilgrimage, and to Iran through trade and the long Ottoman-Safavid frontier.

Section 46.9

North African Scholarship: Tunis, Algiers, and the Wider Maghreb

In the Ottoman regencies of Tripoli, Tunis, and Algiers, a Turkish-speaking military and administrative elite governed a population that was overwhelmingly Arabic- and Amazigh-speaking and Maliki in law (Chapter 44). Religious learning therefore developed along two lines: an official Hanafi stream linked to Istanbul and a Maliki stream rooted in the Maghrib's own tradition. Tunis had both Hanafi and Maliki muftis, and the Maliki scholars of the city's Zaytuna mosque enjoyed wide popular respect.

The Zaytuna was Tunis's main teaching mosque, with libraries and endowed teaching posts. Under the Husaynid beys of the eighteenth century, who were semi-autonomous rulers within the Ottoman system (Chapter 47), the Zaytuna received continued patronage and its scholars held the principal religious offices. Algiers and Constantine also had madrasas and numerous Sufi lodges and rural religious centers (zawiyas) that provided teaching, hospitality, and mediation among tribes. The Rahmaniyya order, spread in the Algerian countryside by a scholar trained in Cairo, shows how Azhari training could shape provincial religion far from Egypt.

Morocco lay outside the Ottoman Empire. Under the Saʿdian and later Alawi dynasties, it had its own scholarly center in Fez, whose Qarawiyyin mosque was among the oldest teaching institutions in the Islamic world. Moroccan, Algerian, and Tunisian scholars traveled to and from Cairo and the Hijaz on the pilgrimage, and Moroccan Sufi networks were influential across the Maghreb. Morocco is mentioned here as a neighboring comparative center, not as an Ottoman province. Maghribi scholars formed one of the largest regional groups in Cairo's al-Azhar, linking North Africa to the broader scholarly world.

The relationship between the ruling elite and the local scholars varied by regency. In Algiers, Turkish-speaking officers (the ojaq) formed a military ruling class tied to the Ottoman center, and their Hanafi judges served the capital and the garrison towns, while Maliki scholars, many of them from local families, held positions in the city and countryside. The mosque of Sidi ʿAbd al-Rahman and other shrines in Algiers, and the widely respected zawiyas of the interior, shaped religious life among both urban and rural populations. In Tunis, from the Muradid beys of the seventeenth century to the Husaynid dynasty founded in 1705, rulers gave mosques, madrasas, and libraries to the Zaytuna and its scholars and sought legitimacy from the local ulama, and in the mid-eighteenth century Hanafi and Maliki scholars competed over official posts while cooperating in teaching.

Examples of circulation illustrate the connection with Egypt and the Hijaz. The Algerian scholar Muhammad ibn ʿAbd al-Rahman al-Azhari (c. 1715–1793), from Kabylia, studied at al-Azhar and was initiated into a Khalwati Sufi line in Cairo, then returned to his homeland and spread what became the Rahmaniyya order, which combined scholarly teaching with Sufi discipline and gained a large following among Kabyle villages. Maghribi pilgrims and students also traveled to Cairo and the Hijaz, wrote about their journeys, and collected certificates from teachers they met on the road, and Maghribi pilgrimage accounts (rihla) form an important genre of travel literature. In Tripoli and its hinterland, zawiyas provided teaching and hospitality, and in Fezzan and the Saharan oasis chains, religious networks sustained long-distance trade, as Chapters 32 and 39 showed for earlier periods.

Maliki jurisprudence in this period built on a medieval tradition of commentary and compendia, with works by North African authors read widely. Local custom, including rules on water, pasture, and family property in Amazigh-speaking regions, was often recognized in Maliki practice, so that the written law and local customary law (ʿurf) interacted. The Ottoman regencies did not suppress this arrangement; they used it as a means of governing a society whose religious leaders and tribal elites could not be easily replaced.

Section 46.10

Books, Manuscripts, Libraries, and Intellectual Transmission

Books in the Ottoman Arab world were copied by hand. Professional copyists, scholars, and students produced manuscripts in Arabic, and Ottoman Turkish and Persian texts circulated alongside them. Copying was a mark of study as well as a trade, and a copyist's colophon often records the date, place, and sometimes the exemplar from which the copy was made. Scholars wrote commentaries and supercommentaries on authoritative texts; this layered tradition was a way of teaching, testing, and updating inherited knowledge rather than mere repetition. Libraries attached to mosques, madrasas, and Sufi lodges held endowed collections, and private libraries passed through inheritance and sale.

Book ownership extended beyond scholars. Probate inventories from Cairo, Damascus, and Aleppo record books among the possessions of merchants, craftspeople, and some women. Historian Nelly Hanna has shown that Cairo's middle-class readers owned and traded books on devotional, legal, literary, and practical subjects from the sixteenth to the eighteenth century. Reading, copying, and lending were social activities, and the book trade formed part of the market economy described in Chapter 45.

The Ottoman Arab provinces also had a multilingual intellectual environment. Arabic remained the language of religious scholarship and local administration of law, while Ottoman Turkish served the imperial administration and the higher ranks of the learned hierarchy, and Persian remained a language of literature and Sufism. Scholars with command of more than one language could move between local and imperial spheres.

Printing requires care. Ibrahim Müteferrika established an Ottoman Turkish press in Istanbul in 1727, and Christian communities in the Arab lands began printing Arabic books in the early eighteenth century, notably at Aleppo in 1706 and at the monastery of Shuwayr in Mount Lebanon in the 1730s. Muslim Arabic printing in the Arab provinces developed later, a topic for Chapters 49 and 51. Historians no longer treat the absence of widespread Muslim printing in these centuries as evidence of stagnation. Manuscript production was efficient, reliable, and well integrated into scholarly practice, and the authority of teaching chains meant that a text's value lay partly in the teacher who transmitted it. Printing slowly changed these arrangements, but it did not instantly replace them.

The movement of books reveals networks as clearly as the movement of people. A single manuscript could contain a text copied in Damascus, annotated in Cairo, and owned by a scholar in Tunis, each leaving notes that show who read and valued it. Scholars wrote their own works in many genres: legal commentaries, hadith collections, Sufi treatises, histories, biographical dictionaries, poetry, and travel accounts. The genre of the thabat or fahrasa, a catalogue of the author's teachers and the texts he studied, served as a scholarly curriculum vitae and often preserves data about libraries and teaching circles. The market for such works was not limited to scholars: merchants and artisans commissioned or bought devotional manuals, collections of prayers, and works on dream interpretation, medicine, and practical law.

Libraries also reveal how books were held and lost. Endowed libraries often placed restrictions on removal of books, appointed a custodian, and required periodic inventories, but books were nonetheless borrowed, sold, or stolen, and court records preserve cases of disputes over missing volumes. Private libraries could be large: some scholars held hundreds of volumes, while those of artisans held a handful of devotional works. Christian and Jewish communities also maintained libraries, copying centers, and monastic scriptoria, in Coptic and Arabic in Egypt, Syriac and Arabic in northern Syria and Iraq, and Hebrew and Judeo-Arabic among Jewish communities, and the Aleppo Codex of the Hebrew Bible, preserved in the city's synagogue, illustrates the care taken with sacred manuscripts.

Section 46.11

Waqf: Religious Endowments, Education, and Social Welfare

A waqf (plural awqaf; Ottoman Turkish vakıf) was a charitable endowment in Islamic law. A founder dedicated property, such as shops, farmland, baths, or buildings, so that its income would permanently support a stated purpose. The property was then, in legal theory, no longer owned by the founder and could not be sold or inherited. Waqfs financed mosques, madrasas, libraries, hospitals, soup kitchens, public fountains, tomb complexes, bridges, and the upkeep of caravanserais, and they paid for salaries of teachers, preachers, prayer leaders, Qur'an reciters, and caretakers.

Two broad types existed. A charitable (khayri) waqf served public purposes from the beginning. A family (ahli or dhurri) waqf provided income to the founder's descendants and designated charitable purposes as the eventual beneficiary when the line died out. Family waqfs allowed property to be kept in the family, sometimes protecting it from fragmentation under inheritance rules or from confiscation. Many endowments combined both purposes. In some regions, endowments of cash, with income derived from loans, also existed, a practice that Hanafi jurists, and the Ottoman jurists of Istanbul, accepted while others criticized.

The deed (waqfiyya) specified the property, the purposes, the beneficiaries, and the administrator (mutawalli or nazir), and was registered in court. Historians distinguish the act of founding from the later management of the endowment. Administrators could be family members, professional officials, or scholars, and they were supervised by judges. Mismanagement, embezzlement, and disputes over succession appear frequently in court records. Waqfs could also be exchanged or leased under legal devices, and the actual use of endowed property sometimes differed from the founder's intentions.

Women were active in the waqf system. Studies of court records from Cairo, Aleppo, Jerusalem, and other cities show women founding endowments, administering them, and serving as beneficiaries, though the proportion varied by city and period. Wives, mothers, daughters, and freed slaves established waqfs to provide for their relatives or to support religious institutions. Waqf also offers evidence of imperial presence: members of the Ottoman dynasty, notably Hürrem Sultan, the wife of Süleyman, funded major charitable institutions in the Arab lands, and governors and officers followed suit. In the aggregate, waqfs constituted one of the principal mechanisms of social welfare and of religious and educational support in the Ottoman Arab provinces.

Ottoman cities show the mechanics. A founder, who might be a sultan's wife, a governor, a scholar, a merchant, or a modest widow, appeared before a judge and declared the endowment; the judge verified the founder's ownership and legal capacity, and the scribe recorded the property, its boundaries, the purposes, the beneficiaries, and the conditions of administration. A founder could reserve the post of administrator for himself or herself during life and for chosen descendants afterward, which made the office a family asset. Beneficiaries could include a mosque's staff, Qur'an reciters at a tomb, the poor of a neighborhood, students, relatives, or freed slaves. The deed's conditions were treated as binding, so that jurists argued over how to interpret them when circumstances changed.

The practical difficulties of keeping an endowment productive for centuries explain several legal devices. Because the property of a waqf could not normally be sold, buildings could fall into ruin, and administrators sometimes used long-term leases, including arrangements in which a tenant paid a large initial sum and a small annual rent in return for a secure right to occupy or rebuild (sometimes called the "double rent" or ijaratayn arrangement in Ottoman sources). Judges also permitted exchange (istibdal) of waqf property under conditions designed to protect the endowment's value. These devices kept endowments functioning but could also reduce their real income, and historians evaluate waqf records both as evidence of charity and as evidence of property management, litigation, and loss.

The scale of the system was large. In Ottoman Cairo, Damascus, Aleppo, and Jerusalem, a substantial share of urban real estate was held in waqf, including shops, caravanserais, baths, mills, and apartment buildings, and rural villages and their revenues were endowed as well. Waqf property thus tied together urban commerce and agrarian production: the rent from a market in Aleppo or the revenue of a village in Palestine might pay the stipend of a teacher in a madrasa or the cost of feeding the poor at a shrine. Women's participation appears in the deeds and in the lists of beneficiaries. Studies of Ottoman court records have found women among the founders of numerous waqfs in Cairo, Aleppo, and other cities, often endowing houses or shops for their own families and for charitable purposes, and women also served as administrators and as litigants over endowment rights. Because the proportion of women founders differs across studies and cities, students should avoid a single figure and read the evidence as showing consistent and significant female participation in property and charity.

Non-Muslims also made endowments in the form allowed within their own religious frameworks, and churches, monasteries, and synagogues held property that was protected by law, though Muslim waqf law as such applied to Muslims' endowments. The relationship between community property and the state, including the procedures for repair permits and inheritance of church property, is taken up in §§46.18–46.20.

For the founder, charity at Jerusalem also carried political and religious meaning, associating the dynasty with a city sacred to Muslims, Christians, and Jews.

Cause and Effect

How Endowments Sustained Religious Learning and Welfare

Long-term causes

Islamic legal traditions that allowed property to be dedicated permanently to charitable purposes; a long history of endowed mosques and madrasas from the Abbasid, Fatimid, and Mamluk periods; the absence of a tax-funded public education system; and religious teachings that valued charity as a form of piety.

Short-term causes

Ottoman conquest, which created new patrons among sultans, governors, officers, and notables; the need of provincial elites to display piety and to protect property; and the wealth generated by commerce and by tax-farming in the Arab provinces.

Immediate effects

New and renovated mosques, schools, kitchens, and fountains; salaries for teachers and caretakers; stipends for students; and the formation of large property holdings managed by administrators and supervised by judges.

Long-term consequences

Continuity of religious learning independent of direct taxation; large amounts of urban and rural property placed in endowments; the entanglement of family wealth with religious institutions; and, in later centuries, debates over endowment reform and state control, examined in Chapters 50 and 51.

Historical complexity: Endowments did not simply express piety. They served family interests, status competition, and political loyalty, and their management could be inefficient or corrupt. Yet they also sustained scholarship, charity, and urban life on a scale that the state alone did not provide. Reducing waqf to either pure charity or pure self-interest misses its multiple functions.

Section 46.12

Sufi Orders, Teachers, and Spiritual Networks

Sufism, the mystical dimension of Islam, was a pervasive part of Ottoman Arab religious life. A tariqa ("path") was a method of spiritual training and, by extension, a network of followers who traced their instruction through a chain of masters to a founding figure. A shaykh guided disciples through initiation, devotional practice, and moral discipline, and many shaykhs were also jurists and teachers. Sufi lodges, called zawiyas, khanqahs, or takiyyas depending on region, served as places of worship, teaching, hospitality, and charity, often supported by endowments.

The principal networks in the Arab provinces included the Qadiriyya, associated with ʿAbd al-Qadir al-Jilani of Baghdad (d. 1166) and widespread from Iraq to North Africa; the Shadhiliyya, strong in Egypt, the Maghreb, and Yemen; the Rifaʿiyya and Badawiyya, popular in Iraq, Syria, and Egypt, the latter centered on the shrine of Ahmad al-Badawi at Tanta; the Khalwatiyya, which spread in Egypt and Syria during the Ottoman era, notably through the Damascus-born teacher Mustafa al-Bakri (1688–1749); the Naqshbandiyya, prominent among scholars in Damascus, Iraq, and the Kurdish regions; and the Mevlevi (Mawlawiyya), the order of Rumi's followers, with lodges in Aleppo, Damascus, and Cairo that were closely linked to Ottoman elite culture. Local and regional orders, such as the Rahmaniyya in Algeria, also appeared.

Historians caution against treating each order as a centralized institution with a fixed hierarchy. Many were loose networks of lodges and followers, with branches that adapted to local conditions and that sometimes divided or merged. Individuals often held initiations in more than one order. Sufi shaykhs mediated disputes, offered spiritual counsel to rulers and ordinary people, and ran lodges that served travelers and the poor. The orders also overlapped with scholarly networks: many ulama were Sufis, and Sufi teachers taught hadith and law. Sufism should therefore be understood as part of mainstream religious learning, not as a separate or marginal tradition.

Examples show how orders worked in particular places. The Khalwatiyya, originating in Azerbaijan and Anatolia, was strongly supported by Ottoman elites and reached Egypt and Syria; Ibrahim al-Gulshani (d. 1534), who settled in Cairo soon after the Ottoman conquest, founded a branch that attracted Ottoman officials, Egyptian scholars, and ordinary followers, and later Khalwati reformers such as Mustafa al-Bakri (1688–1749) combined teaching, writing, and travel in Syria, Egypt, and Jerusalem. The Qadiriyya's hereditary custodians at ʿAbd al-Qadir's shrine in Baghdad supported a network of lodges across Iraq, Syria, Egypt, and North Africa. The Badawiyya's center at Tanta in the Nile Delta, built around the shrine of Ahmad al-Badawi (d. 1276), hosted large festivals that mixed devotion with commerce and brought together villagers, townspeople, and Sufi performers. The Mevlevi lodges, with their whirling ceremony and links to the Ottoman court, drew educated and elite members, and Aleppo, Damascus, and Cairo each had Mevlevi lodges associated with Ottoman-era patronage.

Orders were also tied to politics and economy. Lodges received waqf revenue and could hold substantial property; shaykhs often belonged to families that passed leadership by inheritance; and some orders formed ties with occupational groups, so that artisans and soldiers might join particular lodges. In Egypt, the Bakriyya family, who claimed descent from Abu Bakr, exercised hereditary religious influence, patronage, and authority over its own Sufi institutions and endowments before the nineteenth century, an example of how a family could convert religious prestige into institutional authority. This should not be read as a formal office supervising every Egyptian Sufi order: such a centralized post was created only in 1812, under Muhammad Ali, and belongs to later chapters. The Egyptian family should also be distinguished from the Syrian Khalwati teacher Mustafa al-Bakri (1688–1749), mentioned above. Rulers courted shaykhs who commanded popular followings, and shaykhs in turn used their influence to intercede with officials on behalf of followers. Sufism also reached Christians and Jews in the form of shared devotional vocabulary and shared visits to saints, though communities remained distinct.

In the Maghreb and the Sahara, orders often played a social role similar to that of lodges elsewhere but with greater autonomy from the state. Zawiyas offered schooling, arbitration among tribes, and shelter on trade routes, and shaykhs who traced descent from the Prophet or from a founding saint held special prestige. The late eighteenth century saw the emergence of new orders and reform currents in North Africa, including the Rahmaniyya in Algeria and, in the neighboring region, the Tijaniyya, founded by Ahmad al-Tijani (1737–1815) at the end of the century. Their later histories belong to the nineteenth century; here they illustrate the continued creativity of Sufi networks under Ottoman rule.

Historical Vocabulary
Tariqa, Zawiya, Dhikr, Ziyara, and Baraka

A tariqa is a spiritual path and, by extension, the network of followers who practice it. A zawiya is a Sufi lodge or religious center, often built around a saint's tomb. Dhikr ("remembrance") is the devotional repetition of God's names or phrases, performed individually or in groups, sometimes with music or movement. Ziyara is a visit to a shrine or tomb to seek blessing and offer prayer, distinct from the obligatory hajj to Mecca. Baraka is blessing or spiritual benefit believed to emanate from holy persons, places, and objects.

These terms matter because modern English words such as "cult," "brotherhood," or "popular religion" mislead. Many scholars, rulers, and merchants practiced Sufi devotion, and the practices and institutions of Sufism formed part of the learned tradition and civic life. The terms were also sometimes contested, since some scholars criticized particular forms of visitation or ritual, as discussed below.

Section 46.13

Shrines, Saints, Visitation, and Sacred Landscapes

The Arab provinces were dotted with tombs of prophets, companions of the Prophet, members of his family, scholars, and Sufi saints. Cairo's cemeteries, the Umayyad Mosque's shrine of John the Baptist's head, the tomb of Ibn ʿArabi in Damascus, the shrine of ʿAbd al-Qadir al-Jilani in Baghdad, the tomb of Ahmad al-Badawi at Tanta, the numerous tombs of Yemen and North Africa, and the local shrines of villages all drew visitors. People went to shrines to pray, to seek healing, to fulfill vows, to celebrate saints' birthdays (mawlids), and to meet friends and relatives. Mawlids such as that of al-Badawi at Tanta combined devotion, markets, and entertainment and drew large crowds.

Shrine visitation was distinct from the pilgrimage to Mecca treated in Chapter 45. Hajj was obligatory for those able to perform it; ziyara was recommended or customary and varied by place. Visitation also served social purposes: shrines were sites of local identity, patronage, and sometimes political authority. Rulers patronized shrines to show piety and cultivate popular support, as Selim I's patronage of Ibn ʿArabi's tomb in Damascus illustrates, and local leaders derived prestige from descent from saints.

Not all Muslims agreed about shrine practices. Many scholars defended visitation as sanctioned by tradition and valued as a means of remembering the pious dead; others, in the tradition of earlier critics such as Ibn Taymiyya (d. 1328), objected to practices they thought implied worship of anyone but God, such as seeking intercession in certain ways or building over graves. The disagreements were part of a long-running debate within Sunni scholarship, not a divide between Muslims and non-Muslims. Christians and Jews also visited shrines that were sacred to several communities, a point revisited in section 46.21. Historians read shrine practices from waqf records, travelers' accounts, hagiographies, and architecture, with attention to the differing perspectives of patrons, scholars, and pilgrims.

Visitation had a calendar and a landscape. In Cairo, the vast cemeteries known as the City of the Dead contained the tombs of the Prophet's descendants, saints, and scholars, such as the shrine of Imam al-Shafiʿi, which was a focus of pilgrimage and endowment, and the shrines of Sayyida Zaynab and al-Husayn drew crowds at festivals. Damascus's Umayyad Mosque housed a shrine venerated as the head of John the Baptist (Yahya), and sites associated with the Prophet's family and companions attracted visitors. In Baghdad, the tombs of Abu Hanifa and ʿAbd al-Qadir al-Jilani were pilgrimage destinations for Sunnis, while in Najaf and Karbala the Shi'a shrines served their communities. The shrines' keepers, who often belonged to hereditary families, received gifts and managed endowments, and their offices could become objects of competition and litigation.

Sacred landscapes also overlapped across religious boundaries. The prophet Elijah (Ilyas) and the figure of al-Khidr, associated in Muslim tradition with a mysterious guide, were identified in popular practice with Saint George (Mar Jirjis) at sites in Palestine and Syria that Muslims and Christians both visited, and the tomb of Abraham at Hebron was sacred to Muslims, Jews, and Christians, though access to its interior was strictly limited for non-Muslims. Rituals at such places could involve vows, animal sacrifice, healing, and the tying of cloth to trees. Historians use travelers' accounts, endowment records, and anthropological work on later periods with caution, because evidence about shared shrines is stronger for the nineteenth and twentieth centuries than for the sixteenth and seventeenth, and they avoid projecting later patterns of "folk religion" back onto earlier periods.

The Ottoman state's role was ambiguous. Sultans and governors endowed shrines and attended celebrations, such as the Prophet's birthday (mawlid), which the Ottomans promoted as a public ceremony from the later sixteenth century, while officials sometimes tried to regulate disorder at festivals, to limit practices seen as improper, or to collect taxes from visitors and markets. Scholars, including those who defended visitation, also criticized abuses. The result was a landscape of shrines whose use shifted over time with local patronage, political circumstances, and scholarly debate.

Section 46.14

Religious Reform, Criticism, and Debate within Sunni Islam

Ottoman Sunni religious life was not free of argument. Scholars disagreed about legal methodology, the limits of Sufi practice, the place of music and ecstatic ritual, the permissibility of new substances such as coffee and tobacco, and the proper relationship between scholars and rulers. In seventeenth-century Istanbul, a revivalist movement associated with the preacher Kadızade Mehmed condemned many popular and Sufi practices and provoked heated controversy, and its echoes reached Arab cities. Such disputes were part of an ongoing negotiation over what practices were permissible, not a simple conflict between modernizers and traditionalists.

ʿAbd al-Ghani al-Nabulusi of Damascus offers an illuminating case. A prolific author in the Qadiri and Naqshbandi traditions, he wrote on Sufi theory, commented on the writings of Ibn ʿArabi, composed poetry, and defended devotional practices against critics. He also argued on legal and social questions, including the permissibility of tobacco, and wrote travel accounts of his journeys through Syria, Palestine, Egypt, and the Hijaz. Elizabeth Sirriyeh's scholarship has shown how his writings reveal a Sufi-inclined scholarly milieu confident in its learning and engaged with critics, and how his career intersected with Ottoman officials and Damascene society.

The eighteenth century also saw new emphases in hadith study and in calls for renewal of religious practice, in the Hijaz, Yemen, Egypt, and India alike. Historians debate whether these developments represent a coherent "reform movement" or a set of related but distinct trends, and how far they challenged established Sufi institutions. This chapter notes the scholarly debates and leaves the specific political and religious history of Muhammad ibn ʿAbd al-Wahhab and the first Saudi state to Chapter 48. The key point here is that disagreement was a normal feature of Sunni scholarly life, conducted through writing, teaching, and fatwas, and that criticism of particular practices coexisted with deep respect for learning and for the Prophet's legacy.

Controversies over commodities and practices illustrate how debate was conducted. Coffee, which spread from Yemen through Sufi circles, where it was used to stay awake during night devotions, was debated by jurists in Mecca in 1511, when the local governor banned it after consultation with scholars and the Mamluk sultan in Cairo reversed the ban. Tobacco, introduced in the early seventeenth century, was condemned by some scholars and defended by others, including al-Nabulusi, who wrote in favor of its permissibility. The Ottoman state intermittently banned both, with limited effect. These debates were conducted through fatwas and treatises that used the same legal tools, analogy, the principle of harm, and custom, to reach different conclusions, and they show that the scholarly community was dynamic.

The status of Ibn ʿArabi (1165–1240), the Andalusian mystic buried in Damascus, was another subject of contention. Some scholars condemned his doctrines as heterodox, while others, including Ottoman jurists after the conquest of Syria, defended his sainthood, and Selim I's patronage of his tomb reflected that position. Al-Nabulusi wrote extensively defending and interpreting Ibn ʿArabi and other mystics, and his commentary on a work by the Ottoman reformist scholar Mehmed Birgivi shows his effort to place Sufi thought within a framework acceptable to critics. The controversy was not a simple divide between scholars and Sufis, since many of the participants were both. It was also not simply a divide between Istanbul and the provinces: critics and defenders of Sufi practice appeared in both.

Historians of the eighteenth century have debated whether to speak of a "reformist" or "neo-Sufi" movement, a label proposed in the 1960s to describe Sufi orders that supposedly emphasized strict adherence to the Prophet's example and activism, more than ecstatic practices. Later research, including studies by Rex O'Fahey and Bernd Radtke, questioned the coherence of the category. Scholars such as al-Kurani, al-Sindi, al-Zabidi, and al-Bakri did emphasize hadith study, law, and moral discipline within Sufi frameworks, but they differed from one another, and many of their ideas had medieval precedents. This chapter uses the debate as a reminder that internal Sunni criticism and renewal had many forms and that the Wahhabi movement, a particular and distinctive development, is the subject of Chapter 48.

Section 46.15

Twelver Shi'a Communities and the Holy Cities of Iraq

Twelver Shi'a Muslims, who recognize twelve imams descended from ʿAli and Fatima, formed communities in southern and central Iraq, eastern Arabia and Bahrain, and Jabal ʿAmil in southern Lebanon. The shrines of Najaf, containing the tomb venerated as ʿAli's, Karbala, where Husayn was killed in 680, and Kazimayn and Samarra, were the principal sacred centers. Pilgrims came from Iraq, Iran, and the wider region, and scholars taught at the shrines. The shrine cities were administered through endowments, shrine custodians, and local notables, and their income came from pilgrim donations, endowments, and gifts from rulers.

Ottoman sovereignty over Iraq was contested. The Safavid shah Ismaʿil took Baghdad in 1508; Süleyman captured it in 1534 and visited the Shi'a shrines; the Safavids retook it in 1623 until Murad IV recovered it in 1638, and the Treaty of Zuhab (1639) set a long-lasting frontier. Ottoman authorities generally maintained the shrines and tolerated Twelver scholarship while insisting on political loyalty, and the shrine cities enjoyed substantial autonomy. Safavid and later Iranian patronage of the shrines gave Iran influence, so that the shrine cities stood at the junction of Ottoman and Iranian spheres.

Twelver scholarship in these centuries included important links with Jabal ʿAmil and Bahrain. Scholars from Jabal ʿAmil, such as ʿAli al-Karaki (d. 1534) and Baha al-Din al-ʿAmili (1547–1621), served the Safavid state, while the Jabal ʿAmil community continued its own tradition under Ottoman rule. The Bahraini scholar Yusuf al-Bahrani (1696–1772) moved to Karbala and was a leading voice of the Akhbari school, which privileged reliance on traditions of the imams over rational jurisprudence. Akhbari views were strong in Bahrain and the shrine cities into the early eighteenth century; from the 1760s Muhammad Baqir al-Bihbahani (known as Wahid, 1706–1791), working from Karbala, led an Usuli challenge, and Usuli ascendancy followed Yusuf al-Bahrani's death in 1772, strengthening the role of the jurist (mujtahid) as interpreter. This debate within Shi'a scholarship shaped the later history of the Iraqi shrine cities. Conversion of southern Iraq's tribes to Twelver Shi'ism occurred largely in later centuries and is debated by historians, which is why the chapter treats it with caution.

The shrine cities' institutions also deserve attention. Najaf and Karbala had endowed shrines, madrasas, and libraries; hereditary families served as custodians; and scholars and students came from Iraq, Iran, Bahrain, and Jabal ʿAmil. Pilgrimage brought commerce, and pilgrims often sought burial near the imams, so that the cities' cemeteries expanded and the transport of bodies became a trade. The annual commemoration of Husayn's martyrdom on Ashura (the tenth of Muharram) was an occasion for mourning rituals, sermons, and processions, with variation in public display depending on the local political climate. Ottoman governors varied in their treatment: some taxed pilgrims heavily or restricted Shi'a observances, while others protected the shrines and sought good relations with the cities' leaders. Karbala in particular was a site of repeated political tension and later became the target of a major attack from outside Ottoman Iraq, an event belonging to Chapter 48.

The Usuli–Akhbari dispute was a debate about how to know religious law in the absence of the imam. Akhbaris held that the reports of the imams were the basis of law and that rational jurisprudential methods were illegitimate; Usulis held that a qualified jurist (mujtahid) could derive rulings through specific principles of legal theory (usul al-fiqh) and that lay believers should follow a living mujtahid. Akhbari views were influential in the seventeenth and early eighteenth centuries in Bahrain, Iran, and the shrine cities. The Safavid collapse in 1722 and the turmoil in Iran that followed shifted scholars toward the Iraqi shrine cities, where they were outside direct Iranian state control. From the 1760s, Muhammad Baqir al-Bihbahani, called Wahid, led an Usuli campaign from Karbala, writing against Akhbari positions and training disciples. After Yusuf al-Bahrani's death in 1772, Usuli views were dominant, and Wahid's students, such as Muhammad Mahdi Bahr al-ʿUlum (1742–1797) of Najaf, expanded Usuli teaching. The effect was a more prominent role for the mujtahid as an independent authority whose rulings guided the community, with consequences for Shi'a religious organization in the nineteenth century.

Shi'a communities outside Iraq reveal the range of Ottoman policy. In Jabal ʿAmil, Shi'a scholars lived under Ottoman rule and under local rulers, with some scholars moving to Safavid Iran, others staying, and some facing pressure: the jurist Zayn al-Din al-ʿAmili, known as al-Shahid al-Thani, was killed in the mid-sixteenth century in the context of Ottoman suspicion of Shi'a scholars. In Bahrain and eastern Arabia, Twelver communities of Qatif and al-Ahsa maintained scholarly traditions and links to Iraq. The Ottoman state's anti-Safavid propaganda in the sixteenth century, which included fatwas denouncing Safavid religious claims, shaped perceptions of Shi'a communities, but daily relations between Sunnis and Shi'a in towns often involved coexistence and trade. Historians therefore distinguish the political conflict between empires from the religious life of local Shi'a communities.

Section 46.16

Zaydi Islam and Religious Authority in Yemen

The Zaydi school of Shi'a Islam, named for Zayd ibn ʿAli, a descendant of the Prophet who rose against the Umayyads in 740, took root in highland Yemen from the ninth century. Zaydis recognize as imam a learned male descendant of the Prophet through Hasan or Husayn who claims the office through public summons (daʿwa) and rising (khuruj) and demonstrates qualifications in scholarship, piety, and capacity to lead. Unlike Twelver Shi'ism, Zaydism does not hold that the imams are infallible or in occultation, and its jurisprudence has points of contact with Sunni legal reasoning.

Zaydi scholarly culture flourished in teaching centers and in hijras, scholarly settlements whose residents were protected by tribal custom. Teaching networks, libraries, and manuscripts preserved Zaydi law and theology. Tribal support sustained imams: the highland tribes provided military backing, while scholars provided legitimacy. The Ottomans controlled parts of Yemen from 1538 until the Qasimi imams, beginning with al-Qasim ibn Muhammad (r. 1597–1620) and continuing under his successors, expelled them from the highlands by 1635. The religious basis of the Qasimi imamate, grounded in scholarly and lineage qualifications, gave the state religious legitimacy independent of Ottoman appointment. The military chronology belongs to Chapter 44, and the development of regional ruling households to Chapter 47.

Zaydi scholarship interacted with the broader Muslim world. Scholars such as Muhammad ibn Ismaʿil al-Amir al-Sanʿani (1688–1769) drew on hadith scholarship and criticized blind imitation, and Muhammad al-Shawkani (1760–1834), active at the end of this period, argued for independent legal reasoning and moved Zaydi jurisprudence closer to Sunni hadith-based methods. In the lowlands and the Tihama, the Shafi'i school predominated. Yemen thus contained distinct religious regions, whose relations with each other and with the Ottoman state shifted over time.

The Zaydi tradition's doctrines had social consequences. Because the imam had to be a learned descendant of the Prophet through Hasan or Husayn, the community of such descendants (sadah) formed a hereditary elite in Yemen with special status and scholarly roles, and tribal leaders and scholars negotiated the relationship between imam and tribe. The founder of Zaydi rule in Yemen was al-Hadi ila al-Haqq Yahya (d. 911), who established his authority in Saʿda in the 890s, and the Hadawi legal school bears his name. The doctrine of the imamate meant that the imam's legitimacy could be questioned if he lacked qualifications or failed in his duties, and rival claimants sometimes arose, so the imamate was historically a contested office.

The Qasimi imams built their authority on this doctrine. After al-Qasim's rising in 1597, his son al-Mu'ayyad Muhammad (r. 1620–1644) and his successor al-Mutawakkil Ismaʿil (r. 1644–1676) extended Zaydi authority across Yemen, taking cities, including Sanaa, and regions beyond the highlands, which the Ottomans had lost by 1635. These imams drew revenue from the growing coffee trade, taxation, and endowments, and they employed scholars as judges, teachers, and administrators. Their rule had a religious character, but it also relied on tribal alliances and military force, and the lowland Shafi'i population, with centers such as Zabid and its famed teaching mosques and Sufi lodges, experienced it as a political and religious contest. In 1679, under Imam al-Mahdi Ahmad, Yemen's Jews were expelled from highland towns to Mawza in the Tihama, and many returned in the early 1680s, an episode that shows the vulnerability of non-Muslim communities under changing rulers.

Zaydi scholarship in the eighteenth century was engaged with Sunni hadith scholarship. Ibn al-Amir al-Sanʿani (1688–1769) wrote on hadith and on the problem of blind imitation (taqlid), and Muhammad al-Shawkani (1760–1834) of Sanaa, who served as judge under the imams, argued for independent reasoning and drew on Sunni hadith sources, helping to move the Zaydi legal tradition closer to Sunni positions. Yemen's scholars also traveled to the Hijaz, and Yemeni teachers appear in the networks of al-Zabidi and other scholars discussed above. This section's emphasis is on the religious foundations of Zaydi authority; the Ottoman-Zaydi conflict belongs to Chapter 44 and local ruling households to Chapter 47.

Section 46.17

Ismaili Communities, Sectarian Boundaries, and Muslim Diversity

Beyond the Sunni, Twelver, and Zaydi communities, smaller Muslim communities maintained distinct traditions. Ismaili Muslims, descendants of the tradition that had once formed the Fatimid state (Chapter 31), survived in the Yemeni highlands, where Tayyibi Ismailis followed a line of leaders (dais) whose authority extended to communities in India, and in Syria, where Nizari Ismaili groups lived in the mountains. Their religious life centered on closed teaching traditions and local leadership, and their relations with Sunni neighbors and the state varied from tolerated coexistence to periods of tension and taxation. Historians must rely on limited and sometimes hostile sources for these communities.

Ottoman officials and Sunni scholars viewed such communities in different ways, ranging from tolerance of isolated mountain groups to attempts at taxation or conversion, and the communities' local power depended on geography and on tribal or feudal arrangements examined in Chapter 47. The paragraphs that follow separate the communities, because grouping them together obscures their different beliefs, institutions, and relationships with the state.

Students should avoid assuming that modern sectarian categories correspond precisely to early modern communal organization. Religious identity was shaped by local institutions, family ties, regional connections, political circumstances, and teaching, and it could be flexible in practice even where doctrine drew firm lines. Sources about such communities were often written by outsiders, and the communities' own traditions of secrecy or oral transmission limit what can be known. Historians therefore speak cautiously about their numbers, beliefs, and relationships.

Because these communities were often grouped together by outsiders, it is important to distinguish them. Tayyibi Ismailis of highland Yemen followed a line of dais who claimed to represent a hidden imam descended from the Fatimid line; during the sixteenth century the principal seat of Tayyibi leadership shifted toward Gujarat in western India, where the community had grown through Yemeni missions. A succession dispute around 1589–1591 then produced two lines of leadership: the Dawudi line, which remained centered in India, and the Sulaymani line, which remained associated with Yemen and Najran. The names Dawudi and Sulaymani arose from that dispute and should not be applied to the earlier community. Nizari Ismailis in Syria, around Masyaf and the coastal mountains, belonged to a different Ismaili tradition with its own leadership and history. Druze communities in Mount Lebanon, the Shuf, and the Hawran practiced a distinct monotheistic tradition that arose from the Fatimid era, did not accept converts, and divided members into initiated and uninitiated; Druze chiefs held local power, a subject for Chapter 47. Nusayris (later called Alawis) of the Syrian coastal mountains practiced a separate tradition with its own religious leaders, and Sunni jurists and Ottoman officials often treated them with suspicion or as taxable subjects. Ibadis, who belong to neither the Sunni nor the Shi'a tradition, were organized around an elected imamate in Oman, where the Ya'ariba dynasty ruled from 1624 to 1742, and in small communities in the Mzab and Jerba. Yazidis of Sinjar and the Mosul region followed a religion distinct from Islam and were sometimes the object of Ottoman campaigns.

These differences matter for historical reasoning. Ottoman administrators often used simple labels, such as "Kurd," "Druze," or "rafidi," for administrative or polemical purposes, and those labels did not describe doctrine accurately. Historians therefore separate three questions: what members of a community believed and practiced, how outsiders classified them, and how the state taxed or governed them. The first is often hardest to answer, because communities such as the Druze and Nusayris preserved esoteric teachings and left few documents for outsiders, and surviving texts were sometimes written by opponents or later converts.

Section 46.18

Christian Communities and Churches of the Ottoman Arab Lands

Christian communities in the Arab lands belonged to ancient churches with distinct languages, rites, and hierarchies. The Copts of Egypt, whose church was headed by the Patriarch of Alexandria, formed a significant minority; they worshipped with Coptic in the liturgy but spoke Arabic, and many served as scribes and financial administrators in Ottoman Egypt. Monasteries in the Egyptian deserts and Upper Egypt maintained religious and manuscript traditions. The Greek Orthodox (Rum Orthodox) of Syria, Palestine, and Egypt were Arabic-speaking in daily life; the Patriarchates of Antioch, Jerusalem, and Alexandria were linked to the Ecumenical Patriarchate in Istanbul, and Greek clergy often held senior posts, a source of tension with Arabic-speaking laity. In Jerusalem, the Greek Orthodox Brotherhood of the Holy Sepulchre competed with Latin Franciscans and Armenians over the holy places.

The Maronites, centered in Mount Lebanon, had a patriarch who maintained union with Rome and an educational link with the Maronite College in Rome, founded in 1584; monasteries and rural clergy organized parish life, and the Lebanese Maronite Order, founded by Aleppine Maronites in 1695, expanded education and publication. The Syriac Orthodox, with their patriarchal seat near Mardin, and Syriac Catholics formed communities across northern Syria and Iraq. The Armenians maintained an apostolic church with the Patriarchate of Jerusalem and communities in Aleppo and Cairo, and the Church of the East and its Catholic offshoot lived in northern Iraq and Mosul. European Catholic missionaries, including Jesuits, Capuchins, and Carmelites, established missions, schools, and conversions, notably in Aleppo, helping produce the Melkite Greek Catholic church after the Orthodox Patriarchate of Antioch split in 1724.

Church leaders mediated between their communities and the Ottoman state, collecting funds, representing members in court, and requesting permits for repairs through imperial orders. Disputes arose over succession, taxation, jurisdiction, and the holy places, and Ottoman officials sometimes arbitrated, with outcomes that depended on petitions, payments, and political circumstances. Christians also produced intellectual and cultural works: the Aleppo press of 1706 and the Shuwayr press of the 1730s printed Arabic religious books, and chroniclers such as the Maronite patriarch Istifan al-Duwayhi (d. 1704) recorded community history. Regional concentrations, languages, and relationships with Rome and Istanbul differed widely, so no single description fits all Christians.

The ecclesiastical organization of each church deserves separate treatment. The Coptic Orthodox Church was headed by the Patriarch of Alexandria, who resided in Cairo, and by bishops who were mostly drawn from monasteries; its liturgy used Coptic and Arabic, and Arabic manuscripts of theology and law were copied in the monasteries of Wadi Natrun and the Red Sea desert. Lay Coptic notables, especially the scribes and accountants who worked for Ottoman and Mamluk-descended households, played a major role in funding churches and in shaping church politics. In the eighteenth century, Mu'allim Ibrahim al-Jawhari (d. 1795), a Coptic chief scribe in Cairo, was both a high official and a church patron, and his career illustrates how Coptic lay leaders operated within the political system. Catholic missions, including Franciscans, won a few converts, but most Copts remained in the Coptic Orthodox Church.

The Greek Orthodox patriarchates of Antioch, Jerusalem, and Alexandria were linked to the Ecumenical Patriarchate in Istanbul, which often appointed Greek clergy to Arab sees. Arab laity resented the arrangement, and in the seventeenth century, Patriarch Makarios III Ibn al-Zaʿim of Antioch (1647–1672) traveled to Moscow with his son, the archdeacon Paul of Aleppo, who wrote a detailed travel account, a trip that opened a connection with Russian patronage. In 1724 a dispute over succession to the patriarchate of Antioch produced a rupture: the Istanbul-backed candidate, the Greek-born Sylvester, was installed, while Arab supporters of a Catholic-leaning candidate, Cyril (Kirillos) Tanas, formed what became the Melkite Greek Catholic Church in union with Rome. The split divided families and parishes in Damascus, Aleppo, and Mount Lebanon and shows how Ottoman officials, Istanbul's patriarchs, Rome's missionaries, and local Arab laity all affected church politics.

Elsewhere, the Maronites, whose patriarch lived at Qannubin and later at other mountain monasteries, built a church that combined communal leadership, monastic organization, and ties to Rome, culminating in the Lebanese synod of 1736 that regulated church discipline. The patriarch Istifan al-Duwayhi (1670–1704) was also a historian of his community. The Syriac Orthodox patriarchate was centered at Dayr al-Zaʿfaran near Mardin, and the Syriac Catholic line began in the seventeenth century with converts to Rome. In northern Iraq, the Church of the East and the Chaldean Catholic church, which emerged from the Church of the East's divisions, maintained monasteries near Mosul. The Armenian Apostolic Church had a patriarchate at Jerusalem, whose St James Monastery owned property and managed pilgrim lodgings, and Armenian merchants in Aleppo and Cairo supported churches and schools.

Jerusalem's Holy Sepulchre, shared by Greek Orthodox, Latin (Franciscan), Armenian, Coptic, and other communities, was an arena of competition over the right to perform rituals and maintain parts of the church. Ottoman imperial orders in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries awarded or confirmed rights to different communities, and each community used petitions, gifts, and diplomatic support to press its claims. This competition was a feature of Christian life in Jerusalem rather than of Ottoman policy toward Christians in general. The Franciscan Custody of the Holy Land, European Catholic powers, and Orthodox patrons such as Russia all became involved, setting the stage for later disputes in the nineteenth century.

Section 46.19

Jewish Communities, Rabbinic Learning, and Religious Life

Jewish communities in the Ottoman Arab lands included long-established Arabic-speaking Jews (often called Mustaʿrabim in the literature on Egypt and Syria), Sephardic Jews who arrived after their expulsion from Iberia in the 1490s, and, in the Maghreb, communities with long local roots. They worshiped in synagogues, studied in rabbinic academies, and relied on rabbinic courts to adjudicate matters of personal status and commercial disputes among Jews. Communal leaders, including rabbis and lay notables, raised funds, represented the community to officials, and managed charity and education.

Cairo hosted a large community with the prestigious scholar David ibn Abi Zimra (the Radbaz, c. 1479–1573) among its leaders. Safed in Galilee became in the sixteenth century a renowned center of Jewish legal and mystical learning, home to Yosef Karo, author of the code of law Shulhan Arukh, and to the kabbalists Moses Cordovero and Isaac Luria. A Hebrew printing press operated in Safed from 1577, among the earliest Hebrew presses in Asia. Safed's prosperity, linked to the textile trade and immigration, declined in the following century. Jerusalem and Hebron had smaller communities, Aleppo held a famed medieval manuscript of the Hebrew Bible, the Aleppo Codex, in its synagogue, and Baghdad's community was a center of commerce and scholarship. In Yemen, Jews had a distinctive tradition, including the poet Shalom Shabazi (17th century), and suffered a temporary expulsion to Mawza in 1679–1680 under Zaydi rule. In Tunis, two communities, one of local origin and one of Livornese merchants, maintained separate institutions.

Jewish communities were connected across the empire and beyond. The messianic movement of Sabbatai Zevi (1665–1666) spread through Jewish communities from Smyrna to Gaza, Cairo, and Aleppo, and its collapse after Zevi's conversion to Islam left lasting divisions. Correspondence among rabbis, travel of emissaries to raise funds, and shared texts bound communities together. The chapter does not treat any one community, such as Safed, as representing Jewish life across the Arab world. Jews experienced Ottoman rule as a community with legal protections and constraints, as merchants, bankers, physicians, and artisans, and sometimes as targets of local hostility.

Safed's story shows how a small town became a major center. After Ottoman conquest, Sephardic and other Jewish immigrants settled in the Galilee, and the town developed a textile industry, a rabbinic academy, and a mystical circle. Yosef Karo (1488–1575) wrote a commentary on Jewish law, the Beit Yosef, and a condensed code, the Shulhan Arukh, which became widely used in Jewish communities. Shlomo Alkabetz composed a famous hymn welcoming the Sabbath, Moses Cordovero (1522–1570) systematized the kabbalistic tradition, and Isaac Luria (1534–1572) and his disciple Hayyim Vital developed a Lurianic Kabbalah that spread widely through Jewish communities. Safed's press published religious books. The town declined in the seventeenth century because of economic decline and insecurity, but its intellectual legacy endured. In the 1560s, the Jewish financier Joseph Nasi and his aunt Doña Gracia Mendes obtained Ottoman permission to develop Tiberias, an experiment that had limited success.

Cairo's Jewish community preserved a tradition of legal scholarship embodied in the Radbaz (David ibn Abi Zimra), who served as a chief rabbi in the early Ottoman period and whose responsa survive. The community also remembered an episode in 1524, when the Ottoman governor Ahmed Pasha attempted to establish an independent rule in Egypt and was suppressed; the Jewish financier Abraham de Castro, who had been his mint master, was said to have escaped, and the community commemorated its deliverance with a special "Purim of Cairo." Aleppo's Jewish community, also large, produced merchants and brokers and guarded the Aleppo Codex in its synagogue. Baghdad's community, long established, included wealthy merchants and bankers who dealt with the Ottoman governors. Yemeni Jews, known for their distinct liturgical and linguistic traditions, included the poet Shalom Shabazi, and they faced restrictions and periodic expulsions, as in 1679. In Tunis, the community of local origin, known as the Twansa, and the community of Livornese traders, known as the Grana, had separate institutions and were distinguished by language, trade, and communal courts. In Morocco, independent of the Ottomans, Jews lived in separate quarters (mellahs), a pattern distinct from that in Ottoman cities.

The Sabbatian movement shows how news and ideas traveled among communities. In 1665, Nathan of Gaza proclaimed Sabbatai Zevi, a mystic from Smyrna, to be the Messiah, and enthusiasm spread through communities in the Ottoman Empire and Europe. In Cairo, the Jewish treasurer Raphael Joseph Chelebi supported Sabbatai, and in Aleppo, Gaza, and other cities, prayers, fasts, and expectations changed daily life. When Ottoman authorities arrested Sabbatai in 1666, he converted to Islam in Edirne, and many followers were shattered, while some continued to believe in him secretly. Rabbinic leaders afterward showed caution toward mystical enthusiasm. The episode shows that Jewish communities in Arab cities were part of Ottoman-wide and European-wide Jewish networks, not isolated enclaves.

Section 46.20

Legal Status, Communal Authority, and the Problem of the "Millet System"

In Islamic law, Christians and Jews were protected subjects, dhimmis (Turkish zimmi), who were allowed to practice their religions and manage internal communal affairs in return for acceptance of Muslim rule and payment of a poll tax, the cizye (Arabic jizya). Dhimmi status carried protections, including security of person and property and the right to worship, and constraints, including restrictions on building new places of worship, requirements for permits to repair existing ones, regulations on dress and public display, and unequal standing in some legal contexts. These rules were enforced unevenly, with periods of laxity and stringency that depended on local officials and circumstances.

Non-Muslims also used Ottoman courts. Court registers show Christians and Jews bringing cases about property, debts, contracts, and sometimes inheritance, appearing as plaintiffs and defendants, and sometimes litigating against Muslims. Communal authorities, meanwhile, handled many internal matters such as marriage and religious discipline. The mixture of communal and state jurisdiction was flexible, and individuals could choose among forums depending on advantage and circumstance.

A famous textbook scheme describes the empire as organized into "millets," self-governing religious communities headed by officially recognized leaders, such as the Orthodox patriarch, the Armenian patriarch, and the chief rabbi, whose authority was granted by Mehmed II after 1453 and remained unchanged for centuries. Historians, notably Benjamin Braude, have shown that this "millet system" as a systematic institution cannot be traced back to the fifteenth or sixteenth centuries; its origins as a formal structure appear later, and the word millet acquired its modern sense in the nineteenth century. Arrangements in the sixteenth to eighteenth centuries were locally varied, negotiated, and changing. In Arab provinces, communal leaders such as the Coptic patriarch, the Syriac patriarch, or local rabbis exercised authority through custom, petition, and practice, not through a single imperial charter. Bruce Masters has emphasized this variety and the importance of local context. The next feature presents the debate.

The administration of the poll tax illustrates how much depended on communal leaders and local arrangements. The cizye was assessed on adult males according to categories of wealth in many periods, and in practice the collection was often delegated to community leaders or to tax farmers who answered to the state. Communities negotiated the total amount and its distribution, and officials sometimes demanded additional sums, which became a source of grievance. The poll tax records are a valuable source for population and wealth, though their reliability varies, and they were not the same everywhere: the arrangements in Cairo, Aleppo, and Jerusalem differed.

Rules on places of worship show the interplay of law and practice. Islamic jurists generally held that existing churches and synagogues could be maintained but that new ones should not be built, and repair required permission, which could be granted by imperial orders or provincial judges after petitions and sometimes payment. Court registers and imperial orders include requests to repair churches and monasteries and, at times, objections from Muslim neighbors, so that permission depended on local relations and on officials' judgment. In some periods, officials enforced restrictions on dress and on public religious display more strictly, and in others they were relaxed. Non-Muslims could testify and litigate in court, but legal inequality remained in certain matters, such as the limits on a non-Muslim's testimony against a Muslim and rules of marriage and inheritance between religions.

Historical Debate

Was There a Unified "Millet System" in the Early Ottoman Arab Lands?

The question: Did the Ottoman Empire organize its non-Muslim subjects in the sixteenth to eighteenth centuries through a unified system of autonomous religious communities, or did communal organization develop in a more varied, local, and gradual way?

Interpretation A: The classical millet system. Older scholarship, and many popular accounts, described a system established after the conquest of Constantinople in 1453, in which the sultan recognized the Orthodox patriarch, the Armenian patriarch, and the chief rabbi as heads of self-governing communities responsible for law, education, and taxation of their members. This picture gave the empire a rational and tolerant image and supplied a convenient framework for narrating Ottoman pluralism.
Interpretation B: A gradual, locally varied development. Revisionist scholarship argues that the foundational "millet system" was a later construction. Benjamin Braude and others point to the lack of contemporary evidence for a single sixteenth-century charter and show that communal arrangements differed by place, community, and period, with the formal millet structure taking shape in the eighteenth and especially the nineteenth century. For the Arab provinces, Bruce Masters shows that local circumstances, such as the strength of church leaders, the presence of rival missions, and the practices of local governors, shaped how communities related to the state.

Evidence: The debate draws on imperial orders to church and communal leaders, court registers, Ottoman and community chronicles, and the absence of any foundational document for the supposed system. Defenders of a looser version of the older view point to genuine continuities, including the recognition of patriarchs and communal leaders in imperial orders and the delegation of internal matters to communities. Revisionists emphasize variation, negotiation, and the importance of nineteenth-century reforms in creating formal structures.

Why it matters: If the millet system is treated as a timeless institution, Ottoman pluralism appears as a stable design and conflicts appear as exceptions. If arrangements are seen as locally varied and negotiated, then communal life appears as the product of continuing bargaining among communities, clergy, and officials, with different results in Cairo, Aleppo, and Jerusalem. The revisionist view also guards against reading nineteenth-century arrangements backward.

Student task: Identify which kinds of evidence would best test the claim that a given community had a recognized head with defined powers in the seventeenth century, and explain why imperial orders to a patriarch might not by themselves prove a system.

Section 46.21

Religious Communities in Everyday Life: Families, Women, and Neighborhoods

Daily religious life centered on the household and the neighborhood. Households marked births, marriages, and deaths with religious rites; observed fasts, feasts, and prayer times; gave charity; and passed on stories and practices. Neighborhoods often grouped by religion or occupation but were not strictly segregated, and in many cities Muslims, Christians, and Jews lived in adjacent quarters, shared markets, and sometimes lived in the same buildings. Civic and religious calendars intersected, as in Cairo's celebrations of the Prophet's birthday, the Nile's flood, and Coptic and Muslim festivals.

Women participated in religious life in many ways. They attended mosques and shrines, joined mawlid celebrations, listened to preachers, and sought healing and blessings from saints. Some, usually from scholarly or Sufi families, studied with teachers and received ijazas, and a few became known as pious teachers; the Damascene Sufi author ʿAʾisha al-Baʿuniyya (d. 1517) is a notable example from the transition to Ottoman rule. Women founded and managed waqfs, owned property, and appeared in court, as noted in section 46.11. Christian and Jewish women similarly supported churches and synagogues and participated in rites. The evidence about women's religious lives is uneven, since most chronicles and biographical dictionaries were written by men, and historians use court records, endowment deeds, and occasional women's own writings to supplement them.

Interaction among communities included cooperation, competition, and occasional conflict. Muslims, Christians, and Jews did business together, shared some saints' shrines, such as those associated with figures revered in more than one tradition, consulted each other's healers and holy people, and lived through common events like plague and famine. At other times, disputes over property, building permits, conversion, taxation, or perceived insults produced tension, and episodes of violence occurred, often tied to economic or political conflict. Conversion, in both directions, took place for reasons of belief, marriage, and advantage, though conversion from Islam was legally prohibited and rare. Ottoman pluralism was neither a harmony of equals nor a continuous persecution; it was a hierarchical society in which communities lived side by side under rules that favored Muslims and permitted considerable communal life.

Court records illustrate daily relations in detail. Women appear before judges to buy and sell houses, to claim inheritance shares, to demand their dowries, and to register endowments; men and women of all communities complain about neighbors who built walls that blocked light, drains that overflowed, shops that made noise, or doors that opened onto a neighbor's courtyard. Such cases applied the legal doctrine that harm to neighbors must be removed and reveal how a mixed neighborhood handled disputes in practice. Communal institutions also helped: church councils, synagogue boards, and the leaders of Sufi lodges and craft guilds mediated disputes, collected donations, and organized funerals.

Religious life shaped the calendar of work and leisure. Ramadan nights brought storytellers, shadow plays, and gatherings; Christian feasts, such as Easter in Jerusalem, drew large crowds of pilgrims; Jewish holidays brought families to synagogues; and processions, markets, and fairs surrounded saints' days. Wealthier households sponsored charity during these seasons, and endowed kitchens distributed food. Religious boundaries were also visible in marriage, since the law permitted Muslim men to marry Jewish and Christian women under conditions but forbade Muslim women to marry non-Muslim men, and Christians and Jews had rules against marrying outside their communities. Conversion in practice often occurred through marriage or through changing social circumstances, and occasionally through coercion, so that communities guarded their membership.

Violence and tension appear in the sources alongside cooperation. Examples include riots in cities over the repair of a church, the proclamation of a new tax, or accusations against individuals; arbitrary arrests of leaders; and outbreaks of plague, famine, or military occupation that heightened suspicion. Historians resist two oversimplifications: that Ottoman society was a model of tolerance in which communities lived in unbroken peace, and that it was an arena of constant oppression. The evidence supports a picture of a hierarchical, legally unequal, and yet socially intertwined world in which people negotiated their relations daily.

Everyday Life and Social History

Religion in the Rhythms of Ordinary Life

For many people, religion was most visible in recurring routines: the call to prayer or church bells marking the day, the weekly Friday congregation or Sabbath, Ramadan and Lenten fasts, and the seasonal feasts. Craftspeople belonged to guilds that sometimes had patron saints or held religious events, and boys might receive basic education in a Qur'anic school, a church school, or a synagogue school before beginning work. Charity, drawn from waqfs, guild funds, family gifts, and communal collections, provided meals and support for the poor, orphans, and travelers. Healing involved physicians, herbalists, and religious specialists, and many consulted several.

Life events were marked by religious officials: a Muslim marriage contract registered in court, a Christian wedding in a church, a Jewish marriage contract signed before a rabbinic court. Funerals and burials followed religious customs and could involve neighborhood participation. Religious education for girls was more often domestic than institutional, and women's piety often centered on the home, though women visited shrines and attended communal events. Because sources written by ordinary people are scarce, historians rely on court records, inventories, and the observations of travelers to reconstruct these patterns, always aware of what remains hidden.

Section 46.22

Comparing Religious Life across the Ottoman Arab Provinces

Cairo combined a large, well-endowed scholarly establishment centered on al-Azhar, strong Sufi presence, a Hanafi official judiciary and a mostly Shafi'i population, and a significant Coptic and Jewish presence, with a close link to the Hijaz through the Hajj caravans. Syria featured scholarly families in Damascus and Aleppo with strong Sufi connections, Hanafi official institutions, a Shafi'i and Hanbali presence, the Maronites and the Greek Orthodox, and a patchwork of Druze, Nusayri, and Ismaili communities in the mountains. Iraq was divided between Sunni scholarship in Baghdad and Mosul and the Twelver centers of Najaf and Karbala, under a frontier regime shaped by Ottoman-Safavid competition.

The Hijaz functioned as a transregional scholarly meeting place sustained by pilgrimage, endowments, and teachers from many lands. Yemen was divided between Zaydi highlands, whose imams claimed religious authority independent of the Ottomans, and a Shafi'i lowland with a different religious culture. North Africa, in the Ottoman regencies, combined a Hanafi ruling elite with a Maliki society and a strong network of rural zawiyas, with neighboring Morocco developing independently. Christian and Jewish life varied from Maronite mountain monasticism to Coptic scribal administration, Safed's mysticism, and Aleppo's merchant communities.

Several factors explain these differences: geography, which shaped routes and isolation; inherited institutions, which gave each region distinct endowment structures and teaching traditions; local customs and tribal arrangements; patronage, which depended on the wealth and interest of rulers and notables; and the strength of the relationship with Istanbul, which was close in Anatolia-adjacent Syria and weaker in highland Yemen or rural North Africa. Comparison shows that no single model, whether "Ottoman religious policy" or "Arab Islam," explains the diversity, and that the Ottoman state operated through a mosaic of local religious environments.

Comparison also shows that the same institution could play different roles across regions. Endowments funded al-Azhar's riwaqs in Cairo, the Süleymaniyya kitchen in Damascus, Zaydi hijras in Yemen, shrines in Najaf and Karbala, the Zaytuna in Tunis, and church and synagogue properties across the provinces. Scholarly certification linked Cairo to the Hijaz and to India; Sufi networks tied Baghdad, Cairo, and Algeria; and Christian and Jewish communities connected Aleppo to Rome, Moscow, Livorno, and Safed. Differences in the authority of imperial appointments, in the strength of tribal structures, in the number of non-Muslims, and in the extent of urbanization helped determine whether a given province's religious life was dominated by a scholarly elite, a Sufi order, a shrine, a church hierarchy, or an imam.

Section 46.23

Religious Continuity, Change, and the Ottoman Arab Legacy

From the sixteenth to the eighteenth century, the institutional framework of religious life remained remarkably continuous: madrasas, mosques, Sufi lodges, shrines, churches, synagogues, and waqfs persisted, and the practice of learning through personal teaching and certification endured. The Ottoman state added imperial monuments, a learned hierarchy, a preference for Hanafi law, and new patterns of patronage, and local learned families gradually integrated into these structures. Imperial rule thus altered the landscape of religious authority without erasing its local roots.

Change occurred more within intellectual and communal life than in institutional form. Scholarly networks expanded as travel and printing in some communities increased circulation of ideas; debates over Sufi practice, legal method, and hadith study intensified; Christian communities experienced the effects of missionary activity and the creation of Catholic churches; Shi'a scholarship moved toward Usuli dominance; Zaydi scholars experimented with hadith-based reasoning; and the Jewish world felt the shocks of messianic movements. These developments set the stage for the nineteenth-century transformations of Chapters 49 through 51, though the chapter has avoided reading them backward.

The chapter's themes connect to what follows. Chapter 47 will explore how provincial households and local elites, many of them with scholarly and religious ties, gained political power. Chapter 48 will examine the religious and political movement that arose in central Arabia and challenged established practices. The religious institutions described here, including endowments, scholarly families, Sufi networks, and communal leaders, formed part of the social basis of these political changes. The Ottoman Arab legacy in religion lies in this combination of imperial framework and local vitality, which shaped the diverse religious and intellectual life of the Arab world into the modern era.

A caution about periodization applies. Historians sometimes describe the Ottoman centuries as an age of religious conservatism followed by nineteenth-century reform and revival, but the evidence discussed here shows ongoing intellectual activity, institutional adaptation, and debate before the nineteenth century. At the same time, historians avoid reading the eighteenth century only as a prelude to later "modernization," since many scholars and communities then pursued concerns of their own: how to maintain a chain of transmission, how to defend a devotional practice, how to preserve a church's property, or how to teach a text to students. This chapter's task has been to describe those concerns and the institutions that sustained them.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Institutions and Religious Life across Three Centuries

What Changed

  • An imperial layer of patronage, appointments, and monumental architecture was added to existing religious institutions
  • Local learned families increasingly held offices and passed them down
  • Sufi orders such as the Khalwatiyya spread, and scholarly debates over devotional practice and legal method evolved
  • Christian communities began Arabic printing and, in some cases, divided between Catholic and Orthodox churches
  • Shi'a and Zaydi scholarship underwent internal methodological shifts

What Stayed the Same

  • Waqf as the main financial base for religious institutions and welfare
  • Personal transmission of knowledge through teachers and certificates
  • The coexistence of four Sunni legal schools and local scholarly traditions
  • Communal life organized around mosques, churches, synagogues, shrines, and neighborhoods
  • Legal inequality between Muslims and non-Muslims combined with real communal autonomy

Who experienced change, and who continuity: Elite scholars and clergy experienced change most directly, through imperial appointments, new debates, and new connections. Ordinary believers often experienced greater continuity in daily rites, shrine visits, and neighborhood religious life, though they too were affected by shifts in patronage, taxation, and communal leadership. Why the pattern matters: It cautions against two errors: treating the Ottoman centuries as static, and treating them as a preface to nineteenth-century reform.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how religious institutions and communities organized learning, legal life, social relationships, and cultural identity across the Ottoman Arab provinces from the sixteenth to the eighteenth century. Its central argument is that imperial authority and religious authority overlapped without being identical. The Ottoman sultan and the learned hierarchy of Istanbul appointed judges, patronized institutions, and favored Hanafi jurisprudence, but religious life was sustained by local scholars, Sufi teachers, Shi'a and Zaydi jurists, Christian clergy, Jewish rabbis, and the endowments that funded them.

Religious authority among Sunni Muslims rested on learning, certification, and recognition. The four legal schools coexisted, with Ottoman preference for the Hanafi school producing an official Hanafi layer above largely Shafi'i, Maliki, and, in places, Hanbali populations. Muftis issued opinions, judges issued judgments, and the two roles shaped one another. Courts, as Chapter 44 explained, were administrative institutions; this chapter showed the reasoning and authority that gave their decisions religious meaning.

Learning was organized through mosques, madrasas, teaching circles, and the personal relationship of teacher and student. Al-Azhar in Cairo, the scholarly families of Damascus, Aleppo, and Jerusalem, the holy cities of the Hijaz, the Sunni and Shi'a scholars of Iraq, and the Zaytuna of Tunis each formed a distinct center, but they were connected by travel, pilgrimage, correspondence, and certificates. Manuscripts remained the principal medium of scholarship, and Christian communities were the first to print Arabic books in the Ottoman Arab lands. The absence of widespread Muslim Arabic printing should not be read as stagnation.

Waqf endowments supplied the financial foundation for mosques, schools, kitchens, hospitals, and lodges. They tied piety to property and family interest, were administered by supervised officials, and involved women as founders, managers, and beneficiaries. Imperial patrons such as Hürrem Sultan built charitable complexes in Arab cities, while local notables and ordinary families created smaller endowments. Endowments help explain how religious learning and welfare persisted without a modern state education system.

Sufism was part of mainstream religious life, not a marginal phenomenon. Orders such as the Qadiriyya, Shadhiliyya, Khalwatiyya, Naqshbandiyya, and Mevlevi formed loosely organized networks of lodges and followers, and many scholars were Sufis. Shrines and saints' tombs anchored sacred landscapes and local identities, and visitation was distinct from the obligatory pilgrimage. Debates over Sufi practice, devotional innovation, and legal method, illustrated by al-Nabulusi, formed a normal part of Sunni scholarly life.

Other Muslim traditions maintained distinct institutions. Twelver Shi'a communities centered on Najaf and Karbala, connected to Jabal ʿAmil and Bahrain, developed a scholarly tradition in which the Usuli school eventually prevailed. Zaydi imams in Yemen claimed authority through lineage and learning, independent of Ottoman appointment. Ismaili, Druze, Nusayri, and Ibadi communities and the Yazidis maintained their own traditions, often documented through limited and external sources.

Christian and Jewish communities lived under dhimmi status, which combined protection with constraint, and used both communal institutions and Ottoman courts. The Copts, Greek Orthodox, Maronites, Syriac churches, and Armenians, and the Jewish communities of Cairo, Safed, Aleppo, Baghdad, Yemen, and Tunis, differed in language, leadership, and relationships with Istanbul, Rome, and local officials. The familiar "millet system" should not be projected back onto the sixteenth century: scholars such as Benjamin Braude and Bruce Masters show that communal arrangements were varied, negotiated, and locally shaped.

Comparing the provinces showed that geography, inherited institutions, patronage, and relations with the imperial center produced distinct religious environments. Across three centuries, institutional continuity coexisted with intellectual and communal change. The chapter thus prepares for Chapter 47, which turns to provincial power, and Chapter 48, which examines religious and political change in Arabia, while leaving nineteenth-century reform to later chapters.

Key Terms

Ulama

Ilmiye

The chief mufti of the Ottoman Empire, based in Istanbul, whose opinions carried great weight on questions of law and state.

A school of Islamic legal interpretation; the four Sunni schools are Hanafi, Maliki, Shafi'i, and Hanbali.

A qualified jurist who issues non-binding legal opinions in response to questions.
Fatwa
A reasoned legal opinion issued by a mufti, authoritative through the jurist's learning rather than enforced by the state.
Qadi
A judge who issues binding judgments, in Ottoman practice appointed to a court and assisted by deputies.
Madrasa
An endowed college with salaried teachers and stipends for students, often attached to a mosque or tomb.
Halqa
A teaching circle in which students sit around a teacher to read and discuss a text.
Ijaza
A certificate authorizing a student to transmit a text or teach a subject, often tracing a chain of teachers.
Riwaq
A section of a mosque, notably al-Azhar, where students of a given region lived and studied with endowed support.
Waqf
A permanent legal endowment of property whose income supports a stated charitable or family purpose.
Waqfiyya
The deed establishing a waqf, specifying property, purposes, beneficiaries, and administration.
Mutawalli
The administrator of a waqf, supervised by a judge and responsible for carrying out the founder's conditions.
Family waqf
An endowment (ahli or dhurri) providing income to the founder's descendants before passing to charity.
Imaret
An Ottoman charitable kitchen and hospice complex that fed the poor, students, and travelers.
Tariqa
A Sufi path and the network of followers who practice it under a lineage of masters.
Zawiya
A Sufi lodge or religious center, often built around a saint's tomb and offering teaching and hospitality.
Dhikr
Devotional "remembrance" of God through repeated phrases, performed individually or in groups.
Ziyara
Visitation of a tomb or shrine to seek blessing, distinct from the obligatory pilgrimage to Mecca.
Baraka
Spiritual blessing believed to emanate from holy persons, places, and objects.
Mawlid
A celebration of the birth of the Prophet or the birthday festival of a saint.
Usuli and Akhbari
Twelver Shi'a approaches to law: Usulis rely on rational principles of jurisprudence and the authority of a mujtahid, while Akhbaris privilege reports from the imams.

A jurist qualified to exercise independent legal reasoning, central to Usuli Shi'ism.
Zaydi imam
A learned descendant of the Prophet who claims leadership through public summons and demonstrated qualifications.
Hijra (Yemeni scholarly settlement)
A protected settlement of Zaydi scholars sheltered by tribal custom, serving as teaching and legal centers.
Dhimmi
A protected non-Muslim subject, mainly Christian or Jewish, who paid a poll tax in return for protection and communal autonomy.
Cizye (jizya)
The poll tax paid by adult male non-Muslim subjects under Islamic law.

A term for a religious community that acquired its modern sense of a formally organized communal structure mainly in the nineteenth century; its application to earlier periods is debated.
Patriarch
The head of an ancient Christian church, such as the Coptic, Greek Orthodox, Syriac, or Maronite patriarch.
Rabbinic court
A Jewish communal court that adjudicated matters of personal status and disputes among Jews under Jewish law.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a vast and diverse region maintained religious and intellectual life under an imperial government that neither created nor controlled all of it. The institutions examined here, including endowments, teaching networks, Sufi lodges, shrines, churches, synagogues, and legal traditions, were the infrastructure of daily life for millions of people. They reveal that Ottoman rule worked through partnership, negotiation, and inherited structures as much as through command.

The chapter also reveals larger processes. Networks of scholars and texts connected Cairo, Damascus, the Hijaz, Yemen, Iraq, and the Maghreb to one another and to India, Southeast Asia, and Africa. Endowments channeled wealth into education and welfare. Debates over devotion and law, among Sunnis, Shi'a, and others, kept religious thought active. Christian and Jewish communities developed their own scholarship, printing, and leadership, and their relationships with Muslim neighbors and officials shaped the texture of urban life.

These developments shaped later history. The scholarly families, Sufi networks, and endowed institutions described here formed part of the social basis of provincial power in Chapter 47 and of religious and political movements such as that of Chapter 48. When nineteenth-century reformers reshaped education, law, and communal organization, they acted on institutions with deep roots, which explains both the resilience of older structures and the tensions of reform.

Finally, the chapter models a way of reading religious history. Students should be attentive to local variation, wary of projecting later categories backward, and careful about whose voices survive in the sources. Doing so allows a richer understanding of the Arab world's religious diversity than any single label, such as "Islamic civilization" or "the millet system," can provide.
