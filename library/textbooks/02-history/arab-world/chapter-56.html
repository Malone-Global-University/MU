Peace Settlements, Mandates, and New Borders

A History of the Arab World: From Ancient Crossroads to the Modern Arab World

Chapter Opening

In July 1920 French troops crossed the Anti-Lebanon and defeated a small Syrian army at Maysalun, ending a kingdom proclaimed only four months earlier. The Allies had already decided at San Remo who would govern Syria. The episode shows the chapter's subject: how a state system was produced by the collision of external decisions and local claims.

Chapter 55 ended with the Ottoman collapse. This chapter explains what replaced it. The new order emerged in stages: provisional military rule, the Paris conference, the San Remo allocations, the drafting and approval of mandate instruments, treaty arrangements, and gradual boundary demarcation. At each stage Syrians, Lebanese, Iraqis, Palestinian Arabs, Hashemites, Zionists, British, French, and Americans pursued different aims.

Evidence includes treaties and mandate instruments, conference records, the King–Crane petitions, memoranda, memoirs, and press reports. These sources reflect the perspectives of those who produced them, and populations without access to diplomacy are often underrepresented.

The chapter follows five questions. Who exercised authority when Ottoman rule ended? What political futures did local populations and the peace negotiators propose? How were competing claims resolved or enforced, above all in Syria in 1920? How did Allied decisions become mandate institutions? And how were boundaries drawn and then lived with? Each answer shows a different layer of the post-Ottoman order.

It also corrects three common simplifications: that Sykes–Picot drew the modern map, that Versailles created the Arab mandates, and that the Arab populations simply received arrangements made elsewhere. The evidence shows revision of wartime agreements, a gap between Allied decisions and legal entry into force, and persistent local resistance and bargaining.

Historical Context

The Ottoman Arab provinces had been governed from Istanbul for four centuries (Part VIII) and reformed under the Tanzimat (Chapter 50). Chapter 55 narrated the war, the Arab Revolt, the Hussein–McMahon correspondence, Sykes–Picot, and the Balfour Declaration. This chapter begins at the armistice and treats three processes separately: the collapse of Ottoman government, the allocation of international authority, and the negotiation and enforcement of states and borders. Internal histories of Iraq, Transjordan, Syria, Lebanon, and Palestine are reserved for Chapters 57–59.

The Ottoman Arab provinces were diverse. Syria and Lebanon included Muslim, Christian, and Druze communities with a long tradition of urban notable politics; Mesopotamia had Sunni, Shi'i, Kurdish, Christian, and Jewish populations and strong tribal structures; Palestine, part of southern Syria in Ottoman administration, was a mixed society newly affected by Zionist immigration. The Hijaz and the rest of Arabia followed other trajectories. Students should avoid reading the modern states back into these regions.

Evidence for the period comes from treaties, conference minutes, mandate instruments, commission reports, petitions, memoirs, newspapers, and administrative records. Official records emphasize the viewpoint of governments, while petitions and the press offer local voices but reflect organized groups. Memoirs are retrospective and often self-justifying.

Division I. From Ottoman Armistice to Provisional Authority
Section 56.1

The Ottoman Withdrawal and the Political Vacuum of 1918

The Armistice of Mudros was signed on 30 October 1918 aboard a British warship in Mudros harbor on the island of Lemnos, between the Ottoman delegation led by Rauf Orbay and the British admiral Arthur Calthorpe. By then most of the Arab provinces had already been lost in the field. Allied and Arab forces entered Damascus on 1 October, the French navy and Allied troops reached Beirut within a week, Aleppo fell in the last days of October, and a British column had forced the surrender of Ottoman forces at Sharqat on the Tigris, north of Baghdad, in the same week as the armistice. The armistice therefore recorded a military result more than it created one.

Its terms were military, not territorial. The Ottomans accepted demobilization and Allied use of the Straits, gave the Allies the right to occupy strategic points, and agreed to surrender isolated garrisons in the Hijaz, Asir, Yemen, Syria, and Mesopotamia to the nearest Allied commander. Nothing in the text transferred sovereignty. Ottoman titles over the Arab provinces remained legally intact until a peace treaty, which would not be settled for nearly five years. The Allies, who occupied Constantinople itself in mid-November 1918, held the practical power to dictate terms but had not yet agreed among themselves what those terms should be.

The collapse of government on the ground was uneven. In Damascus and Aleppo the Ottoman civil service dispersed or changed allegiance within days, and many Arab officials simply continued in their posts under new masters. In the south and east of the Arabian Peninsula the situation was different. The Ottoman garrison at Medina under Fakhri Pasha refused to surrender and held out until January 1919, an episode that shows how loosely the armistice reached remote areas. In Yemen, Imam Yahya took over the highlands as Ottoman forces withdrew, and in Mesopotamia the British pushed up toward Mosul in the weeks after the armistice, on the argument that its terms allowed occupation of strategic points, while Turkish officials objected that Mosul lay outside the territory the armistice allowed them to occupy.

Students should therefore separate three questions that are often merged: who controlled territory, who held legal title, and who commanded the loyalty of its inhabitants. In late 1918 the answers differed from place to place. The Allies controlled the cities and railways but did not hold sovereignty. The Ottoman state held legal title in theory but no longer governed. And local populations, whose wishes were the subject of rival claims, had experienced four years of requisition, conscription, and famine and were more concerned in many districts with food, security, and the return of family members than with the diplomatic status of their province. The political vacuum was thus both a legal gap and a social emergency.

Section 56.2

The Occupied Enemy Territory Administration

After the capture of Damascus, General Edmund Allenby, the commander of the Egyptian Expeditionary Force, set up an interim system known as the Occupied Enemy Territory Administration (OETA). Its purpose was to govern conquered Ottoman territory until a peace settlement, and it divided the Levant into zones. OETA South covered Palestine and was administered by British officers, beginning under Major-General Arthur Money. OETA West covered the Lebanese coast and the Beirut region and was placed under a French military governor, Colonel Cousse. OETA East covered inland Syria from Damascus northward and was left in the hands of an Arab administration under Faisal, with British liaison officers present. A northern zone, including Cilicia, passed to French control in the months afterward. Mesopotamia was governed separately under its own British administration (§56.4).

The zones reflected a compromise between Allenby's military arrangements and the earlier Anglo-French partition plan, and they were openly described as provisional. The Allies repeatedly stated that occupation did not confer sovereignty. This was consistent with the Hague Regulations of 1907, which held that an occupying power must respect the laws in force in occupied territory and does not acquire title by occupying it. In practice, however, the occupiers made decisions with lasting effects. They changed currencies, appointed officials, controlled railways and ports, and set precedents about public order and land.

The system produced different political experiences. In the south, British military government worked within an environment shaped by the Balfour Declaration and the arrival of a Zionist Commission in 1918, which Palestinian Arabs saw as evidence that the future of the country was being decided without them. On the coast, French officials favored Maronite and other Christian leaders and clashed with Arab nationalists. In the interior, the Arab administration enjoyed the support of nationalist societies but depended on a British subsidy and on Allied troops standing nearby. Each administration thus began building its own network of loyalties and institutions.

The OETA mattered most because it supplied the facts around which the final settlement formed. By the time the peace conference met, three different regimes, each with its own practices, already governed the territory that diplomats were trying to allocate. The later mandates did not begin from a clean slate: they inherited the zones, officials, and expectations created in 1918–19. When Britain withdrew its troops from the interior in late 1919, the balance among the zones shifted again, a change examined in §56.15.

Section 56.3

Faisal's Administration in Damascus, 1918–1919

Faisal, the third son of Sharif Hussein of Mecca, had led the northern Arab forces in the Revolt and entered Damascus on 3 October 1918, two days after Arab and Allied troops had taken the city. He arrived to find a provisional administration already in place, headed by Ali Rida al-Rikabi, a former Ottoman general, and began to build a government from the officials, officers, and nationalist activists around him. Many of the senior figures were Arab officers who had served in the Ottoman army and then joined the Revolt, including a group of Iraqis, while the civil administration drew heavily on Damascene notables and former Ottoman bureaucrats.

The new government established directorates for finance, justice, education, and public security, and it adopted Arabic as the language of administration, reopening schools and courts under Arab officials. Political life flourished: the Arab Club, the secret society al-Fatat, and the Independence (Istiqlal) party provided organization, while Damascus newspapers debated the shape of the future state. In this setting the idea of a united Syria with a representative government acquired institutional form for the first time, even though its jurisdiction was uncertain and its authority rested partly on foreign tolerance.

The administration also faced serious structural weaknesses. It depended on monthly British subsidies that Faisal's ministers considered inadequate, and its revenues from customs and taxes were limited by the postwar slump and by the division of the country into zones. Faisal himself was frequently absent: he sailed to Europe in late 1918, took part in the peace conference at Paris in early 1919, and returned to Damascus only in the spring, leaving his officials to manage a precarious situation. Tensions arose between the officers who came with him and the local notables of Damascus, whose interests and style differed, and the countryside was disturbed by bandits and unrest.

Historians disagree about how to characterize this government. Some treat it as the first modern Arab government in Syria and a real exercise in state-building. Others emphasize that it was a provisional wartime arrangement whose survival depended on external powers and could be ended by them. Both points are supported by evidence. What the experience did establish was a political precedent: when the French later imposed a mandate, many Syrians measured it against a government they had already seen function, and against a form of self-rule they believed had been taken from them by force.

Section 56.4

British Administration in Palestine and Mesopotamia

In Palestine, British officers ran OETA South as a military government under the Hague principle of maintaining the existing legal order. The Ottoman civil and religious courts continued to function, Ottoman land law and the tithe remained in force, and municipal councils kept their roles. British officials added new departments for police, public works, and health, and Egyptian currency replaced Ottoman money. Local notables remained important mediators: Musa Kazim al-Husayni was mayor of Jerusalem until he was dismissed after the Nebi Musa disturbances of April 1920, and the British appointed Ragheb al-Nashashibi in his place. Herbert Samuel's civil administration took over on 1 July 1920.

Even under military government, policy toward Palestine was tied to the Balfour Declaration. Allenby's headquarters had to balance a pledge to support a Jewish national home against the requirement not to alter the status quo in occupied territory. Officers on the ground often distrusted Zionist plans, while the Zionist Commission under Chaim Weizmann advised and pressed the administration. Palestinian Arab leaders protested that the occupation was being used to prepare political changes before any representative consultation, an argument that became central to their case in 1919–20.

Mesopotamia was different. British forces had occupied Basra in 1914 and Baghdad in 1917, and the territory was governed by a civil commissioner and a network of political officers, many of them drawn from the Indian Political Service. Arnold Wilson served as acting civil commissioner from late 1918 until Percy Cox's return in October 1920. The administration relied on Indian-derived law and practice, took direct control of revenue, and adapted Ottoman land and tribal arrangements, including special regulations for disputes among tribes. Wilson also conducted a consultation of notables and tribal leaders in the winter of 1918–19 on the future of the country, but historians question how representative it was.

The two administrations therefore began with different political cultures. Palestine was ruled by a relatively small military and civil staff under an international spotlight and a specific undertaking about a Jewish national home. Mesopotamia was run as an extension of British India with a large military establishment and heavy revenue demands. Both left the legal status of the territory unresolved, which is why these early regimes are best treated as precedents and not as mandates. The grievances they produced, particularly over taxation and foreign rule in Iraq, shaped the revolt of 1920 (§56.32).

Section 56.5

Local Expectations: Independence, Unity, Autonomy, Security

No single political program united the inhabitants of the former Ottoman Arab provinces. In Syria, nationalists in Damascus and Aleppo, organized through societies such as al-Fatat, sought independence and unity under Faisal, while many Christian leaders in Mount Lebanon and Beirut asked for an enlarged and autonomous Lebanon with French protection. Muslim notables in coastal cities such as Tripoli, Sidon, and Beirut often favored union with Syria. Druze, Alawite, and other regional communities had interests of their own, and Armenian survivors of the wartime deportations and massacres sought security and, in Cilicia, French protection.

In Palestine, Muslim-Christian Associations formed in several cities beginning in late 1918, and a Palestine Arab Congress met in Jerusalem in early 1919. These groups opposed the Balfour policy and demanded representative government, though many also saw Palestine as part of southern Syria. Zionist organizations and Jewish communities in Palestine developed their own institutions and expected international support for a national home. In Iraq, leaders ranged from Shi'i clerics in Najaf and Karbala, who opposed foreign rule, to Baghdad Sunni notables and officers who had served the Hashemite cause, to Basra merchants who often accepted British presence. In the north, Kurdish chiefs also pursued varied aims: Shaykh Mahmud Barzinji, whom the British made governor in Sulaymaniyah, rebelled against them in 1919.

Material conditions shaped these hopes. The wartime famine, which had been especially lethal in Mount Lebanon, left towns depleted, and estimates of the death toll vary widely. The Spanish influenza epidemic of 1918–19 added to mortality. Railways, bridges, and telegraph lines were damaged, trade was dislocated, and the currency situation was chaotic. Silk and other export industries had collapsed, and demobilized soldiers, refugees, and unemployed officials returned to depleted communities. These pressures explain the strong interest in security and economic recovery that sat alongside political demands.

The sources for this period include petitions, newspapers, society proclamations, and later memoirs, such as those of participants in the Arab movement. Each has limits. Petitions and press reflect the views of educated, organized groups; memoirs are retrospective; and the views of villagers, women, and nomads appear mainly through the reports of officials. For this reason historians are cautious about claims that a single 'will of the people' existed in 1919. The more accurate picture is of overlapping, sometimes conflicting, aspirations that European diplomats were unwilling or unable to accommodate, setting the stage for the competing claims presented at Paris (§56.6 onward).

Division II. Paris, Self-Determination, and Competing Claims
Section 56.6

Wilson, Self-Determination, and the Ottoman Lands

Woodrow Wilson set out his war aims in the Fourteen Points address to Congress on 8 January 1918. Point Twelve stated that the Turkish portions of the Ottoman Empire should be assured sovereignty, while the other nationalities then under Ottoman rule should be assured security of life and an unmolested opportunity for autonomous development. In later speeches, Wilson insisted that peoples and provinces should not be passed from one sovereignty to another as though they were property, and he treated the consent of the governed as an operating principle of the peace. The United States had never declared war on the Ottoman Empire, which gave Wilson a distinctive but limited position: he could appeal to principle without being bound by the wartime bargains of Britain and France.

Arab leaders and their Allied interlocutors also relied on other wartime statements. The Hussein–McMahon correspondence of 1915–16 (Chapter 55) had been read by Arab leaders as a pledge of independence in a large part of the Arab provinces, while Britain denied that it covered all the areas Arabs claimed. In 1918 Britain gave two further assurances: the Hogarth message to Sharif Hussein in January, and the Declaration to the Seven in June, which said that territories liberated by the Arabs themselves would remain independent and that those occupied by Allied forces would be governed according to the consent of the governed. The Anglo-French Declaration of 7 November 1918 repeated that the Allies sought national governments deriving their authority from the free choice of the indigenous populations.

These statements stood alongside commitments that pointed in another direction. The Sykes–Picot agreement of 1916 divided the Ottoman Arab provinces between British and French spheres, and its text was published by the Bolshevik government in late 1917, forcing British officials to explain it to Hussein and Faisal. The Balfour Declaration of 2 November 1917 promised British support for a national home for the Jewish people in Palestine. Students should therefore avoid treating Allied policy as a single promise that was later betrayed. The Allies had made several overlapping and partly incompatible commitments, and the peace settlement would have to decide which to honor and how to reinterpret the others.

The historical significance of Wilsonian language lies less in its implementation than in its effect on political argument. It gave Arab, Armenian, Kurdish, Zionist, and Lebanese delegations a common vocabulary in which to present claims, and it gave later critics a standard against which to measure the mandates. It also contained ambiguities that the Allies exploited. 'Autonomous development' did not necessarily mean independence; 'self-determination' did not define which communities counted as peoples, or who could speak for them. The American Senate's rejection of the Versailles Treaty and the League, and Wilson's own collapse from illness in the autumn of 1919, removed the one power that might have pressed these principles in the Middle East.

Section 56.7

The Paris Peace Conference and the Eastern Question

The Paris Peace Conference opened on 18 January 1919. Its early work was done by the Council of Ten, which comprised the heads of government and foreign ministers of the United States, Britain, France, Italy, and Japan. From March, the main decisions were taken by the Council of Four: Wilson, Lloyd George, Clemenceau, and the Italian premier Vittorio Orlando. The Covenant of the League of Nations, drafted by a commission chaired by Wilson, was adopted in April and incorporated into the Treaty of Versailles with Germany, signed on 28 June 1919. The peace with the Ottoman Empire, by contrast, was left until later.

Several reasons delayed the Ottoman settlement. The Allies disagreed over how to divide Anatolia and the Arab provinces, and Italy and Greece pressed territorial claims that conflicted with those of other powers. The Greek landing at Smyrna in May 1919, which the Allied leaders authorized, provoked Turkish resistance and encouraged the formation of the Turkish national movement, led from Anatolia by Mustafa Kemal, who reached Samsun on 19 May. Britain and France also had a particular quarrel over Syria, which Clemenceau was reluctant to press while Britain's army still occupied the interior. In March 1919 Wilson proposed sending an inter-allied commission to ascertain local wishes, but Britain and France withdrew from the project, leaving the American commission discussed in §56.12.

Students should note that the Arab provinces were treated as part of a larger Eastern Question about the dismantling of the Ottoman Empire. They were not the central concern of the conference, and they were handled in a series of ministerial meetings and conversations among British and French leaders rather than in plenary sessions. The Arab delegations were heard but were not parties to the decisions. Faisal appeared as a representative of the Hijaz, which had a delegation at Paris, while delegations from Syria, Lebanon, Palestine, and Mesopotamia presented memoranda and lobbied but had no official standing.

The essential distinction is therefore chronological and legal. Versailles supplied the League framework and Article 22, the principle of mandates. San Remo, in April 1920, supplied the allocation of Syria and Lebanon to France and of Mesopotamia and Palestine to Britain. The League Council approved the mandate texts in 1922, and they entered into force only in 1923. Attributing the allocation of the Arab provinces to the Treaty of Versailles collapses three stages of a process that lasted nearly five years.

Section 56.8

Faisal at Paris: The Hashemite Program

Faisal's delegation went to Paris with a case built on the Arab Revolt's contribution to the war and on British wartime assurances. In his memorandum of 1 January 1919 and his address to the Council of Ten on 6 February, he asked for the independence of the Arab regions of Asia, defined roughly as the area from a line running south of Alexandretta and Diyarbakir to the Indian Ocean. He said Palestine, because of its universal character, should be set aside for later consideration between Arabs and Jews. T. E. Lawrence, who accompanied him as an interpreter and adviser, helped to present the case, though Lawrence's own role and accounts of it have been much debated.

The delegation's position was complicated by the structure of the Hashemite movement. Sharif Hussein had proclaimed himself king of the Arab countries in 1916, but the Allies recognized him only as king of the Hijaz, and he opposed concessions made by his son. Faisal had to represent the Hijaz, speak for the Syrian administration at Damascus, and appeal for Iraq, where his brothers Abdullah and Zayd had interests and where Iraqi officers in his service had their own ambitions. His negotiations also relied on British goodwill, which was growing weaker as British policymakers prepared to leave Syria.

Arab unity itself meant different things to different political actors. For some Damascene nationalists it meant a Syrian state including Lebanon and Palestine. For Iraqi officers serving Faisal it meant a connected Arab state with a role for Mesopotamia. For Hijazi leaders it meant the primacy of the Hashemite house. For many Lebanese Christians it meant the opposite of a project they feared would submerge their community in a Muslim-majority Syria. For Palestinian Arabs, the dominant aim was to prevent the Balfour policy while seeking either union with Syria or self-government. Historians therefore treat the Faisal delegation as one important voice among several and not as a single Arab position.

The Paris experience left Faisal with mixed results. He gained a hearing and some sympathy, especially from American opinion, but not recognition of independence. The French government had already made clear that it considered Syria within its sphere. Faisal came to believe that he had little alternative but to seek an arrangement with France, a conviction that drove his negotiations of late 1919 and early 1920 (§56.15) and divided him from the nationalists in Damascus.

Section 56.9

Britain and France: Security, Oil, and Communications

By 1919 Britain and France each had interests that went far beyond the Arab populations whose futures they were deciding. French claims drew on long cultural and commercial ties: protection of Catholic communities, especially the Maronites of Mount Lebanon; schools and missions; the silk trade connecting Lyon to Mount Lebanon; and a large share of the Ottoman public debt, railways, and port concessions held by French investors. The French colonial lobby, represented by figures such as Robert de Caix, advocated control of 'integral Syria', and many French officials regarded Syria as compensation for the war's costs. Other French politicians doubted the expense and were more concerned with Germany and Europe.

British interests centered on strategic security. Palestine and the Sinai protected the approaches to the Suez Canal and Egypt. Mesopotamia and the Gulf protected the route to India and the oil of Persia, and the Royal Navy had become reliant on oil. Mosul was believed to hold petroleum reserves, which Britain wanted to keep out of French hands. In a December 1918 conversation, Clemenceau is reported to have agreed with Lloyd George to give Palestine and Mosul to Britain, effectively revising the Sykes–Picot division. Accounts of the conversation differ in detail, but it indicates that the terms of the earlier partition were already being changed.

The two governments were not monolithic. In Britain, the India Office favored a strong administration in Mesopotamia, the Foreign Office under Curzon had a different outlook from Lloyd George's, and military commanders and treasury officials were concerned about cost. In France, the foreign ministry, the colonial lobby, the army, and parliament had varying priorities. The Anglo-French agreement of September 1919 on the withdrawal of British troops from Syria, and the later oil arrangement at San Remo, were bargains within and between these groups.

Competition and cooperation therefore coexisted. Britain wished to avoid conflict with France over Syria while securing Palestine and Mesopotamia, and France wished to secure Syria while avoiding an open break with Britain. Arab leaders, particularly Faisal, tried to use this rivalry but were more often constrained by it. The outcome showed that the allocation of the Arab provinces depended on imperial bargaining at least as much as on the stated principles of the peace conference.

Section 56.10

Zionist Diplomacy and the Faisal–Weizmann Agreement

The Balfour Declaration of 2 November 1917 gave the Zionist movement a British commitment, and in 1919 its leaders sought to turn that statement into an international settlement. Chaim Weizmann and other Zionist delegates appeared before the Council of Ten, and on 3 February 1919 the Zionist Organization submitted a statement that asked for recognition of the Jewish historic connection to Palestine and proposed boundaries extending well beyond what was eventually granted, including territory toward the Litani River and Mount Hermon. Zionist leaders also cultivated Faisal, who was thought to represent Arab opinion and had wartime links with British officers who favored the Zionist program.

On 3 January 1919, Faisal and Weizmann signed an agreement that welcomed Jewish immigration on a large scale and established cooperation between an Arab state and Palestine. Faisal, however, added a handwritten proviso stating that his acceptance depended on the fulfilment of Arab demands for independence. In a letter to Felix Frankfurter in March 1919, he repeated his sympathy for the Zionist movement on the same condition. Because the condition was not met, historians generally treat the agreement as having lapsed in effect, though its text was later cited by Zionist writers as evidence of Arab-Jewish cooperation, and by Arab writers as evidence that Faisal's promises were conditional.

Faisal could not speak for the Palestinian Arab population. In late 1918 and early 1919, Muslim-Christian Associations were formed in Jerusalem, Jaffa, and other towns, and a Palestine Arab Congress met in Jerusalem. Its delegates protested against the Balfour policy, demanded representative government, and asserted that Palestine was part of southern Syria. Local Arab leaders addressed petitions to the Allies and to the British administration. Their position was weakened by their lack of access to the Paris conference and by the division of the country into British, French, and Syrian spheres.

Jewish opinion was also not uniform. The Zionist Organization had its main support among Jews in Europe and Palestine, but some prominent Jewish figures in Britain, including Edwin Montagu, opposed Zionism, and older Jewish communities in Palestine and other Arab lands had varying attitudes. This section's central point is that the diplomacy of 1919 involved competing national projects, each claiming legitimacy and each seeking support from the great powers. The consequences are developed in §56.33 and Chapter 59.

Section 56.11

Article 22 and the Mandate Principle

Article 22 of the League Covenant emerged from a compromise over what to do with territories taken from the defeated powers. The South African statesman Jan Smuts had proposed in late 1918 that the peoples of the former Russian, Austro-Hungarian, and Ottoman empires be placed under the League's supervision, while the dominions and Japan wanted to annex German colonies and Britain and France hoped to retain control of the areas they occupied. The final text applied trusteeship to colonies and territories that had ceased to be under the sovereignty of the states that formerly governed them, and it described the well-being of their peoples as 'a sacred trust of civilization'.

The article created three classes. Class A applied to communities, formerly belonging to the Ottoman Empire, that had reached a stage of development at which their existence as independent nations could be provisionally recognized, subject to the rendering of administrative advice and assistance by a mandatory until they could stand alone. The text added that the wishes of these communities must be a principal consideration in selecting the mandatory. Class B covered parts of central Africa, where the mandatory would be responsible for administration under conditions guaranteeing certain rights, and Class C covered South West Africa and some Pacific islands, which could be governed as integral portions of the mandatory's territory.

Other provisions set the limits of supervision. The Covenant stated that the degree of authority, control, or administration should be defined by the Council if not previously agreed, that mandatories must submit annual reports, and that a permanent commission should advise the Council. These provisions created an international system of accountability, but they did not give the League the power to impose its views. The mandatory chose the details of administration and exercised control over defense, finance, and foreign affairs.

Arab leaders and others criticized the system at the time. They argued that the phrase 'provisional recognition' offered no timetable, that the stage of development was judged by the victors, and that the wishes of the populations were not consulted in selecting mandatories, in spite of the text. Defenders pointed to the principle of accountability and to the later role of the Permanent Mandates Commission. The question is explored in the Historical Debate feature. The legal framework needs to be kept distinct from the allocation made at San Remo and the later instruments for each territory.

Historical Vocabulary

Mandate and Sovereignty

A mandate was a League-supervised trust held by a Power; sovereignty is ultimate authority. Class A mandates were "provisionally independent" in theory, but the mandatory exercised real control. Autonomy, independence, and administrative jurisdiction must be distinguished, since modern equivalents mislead.

Historical Vocabulary

Autonomy, Independence, and Recognition

Autonomy is authority to manage specified internal affairs while another power retains ultimate sovereignty or external control. Independence is the absence of subordination to an external government, although treaties may limit practical freedom. Provisional recognition in Article 22 described an acknowledged possibility of independent statehood without itself transferring complete control. Recognition by foreign states is a diplomatic and legal act; it must not be confused with a local declaration of independence, effective administration, or the allocation of a mandate.

Section 56.12

The King–Crane Commission of 1919

The King–Crane Commission originated in Wilson's March 1919 proposal for an inter-allied inquiry into the wishes of the peoples of the former Ottoman territories. After Britain and France declined to take part, Wilson sent an American commission headed by Henry Churchill King, president of Oberlin College, and Charles R. Crane, a businessman and diplomat, with a staff of advisers. They traveled through Palestine, Lebanon, and Syria in June and July 1919, receiving delegations and written petitions in cities and towns, and they also visited parts of Anatolia.

Their report to Wilson, completed in August 1919, concluded that most of those who petitioned wanted a single Syria, including Lebanon and Palestine, under a constitutional monarchy led by Faisal. It also found a strong preference for an American mandate, and failing that a British one, with strong opposition to French control. On Zionism, the commissioners reported that the program for a Jewish state, and for unlimited immigration and land purchase, could not be carried out without force, and they recommended that it be reduced and that Palestine remain part of Syria. They proposed a distinct arrangement for Lebanon and special protection for the holy places.

The report is valuable as a record of political opinion in 1919, but it must be read critically. The commission could visit only some places, and a large proportion of the petitions came from organized nationalist groups, which had prepared in advance after learning of the commission's arrival. Lebanese Christian petitioners largely favored an autonomous Lebanon under French protection, and the commissioners' own sympathies and the American framing of their options influenced the questions they asked. Women, villagers, and nomads were underrepresented, and some regions were not visited.

The commission's influence on policy was negligible. Britain and France were not bound by its findings, Wilson's illness and the American political crisis removed its principal backer, and the report was withheld until 1922, when its contents became public. It nevertheless has lasting importance as evidence of the gap between popular preferences in the region and the settlement the Allies adopted. Students should use it as one source among several, alongside the Syrian Congress resolutions, French and British records, and Palestinian and Zionist petitions.

Division III. The Syrian Independence Movement, San Remo, and the Crisis of 1920
Section 56.13

The Syrian General Congress

The General Syrian Congress opened in Damascus in early July 1919. Its delegates were chosen through indirect elections organized by Faisal's administration, with representatives from the interior districts, a smaller number from the Lebanese and Palestinian areas where French or British authorities limited participation, and Hashim al-Atassi, a former Ottoman official from Homs, as its president. The Congress was intended partly to demonstrate to the American commission then in the country that the Syrian population held a coherent political position, and its timing was closely linked to the visit of the King–Crane Commission (§56.12).

The resolutions it adopted on 2 July 1919, sometimes called the Damascus Programme, demanded complete political independence for Syria within its natural boundaries, including Lebanon and Palestine. They called for a constitutional monarchy under Faisal, rejected French claims and the Sykes–Picot and Balfour policies, and opposed Zionist immigration. They asked for American technical and economic assistance for a limited period and, if the United States declined, for British assistance, while refusing any form of French tutelage. The text mixed demands for sovereignty with a willingness to accept aid, a distinction the delegates considered important and the Allies often ignored.

The Congress was both a legislature in embryo and a political pressure group. It met in committees, debated the shape of a constitution, and claimed to speak for the population of Greater Syria. It also strained Faisal's position. When he negotiated with the French in late 1919 and early 1920, the Congress rejected compromises he thought necessary, and its more radical members, many of them linked to the Independence party and the Arab Club, pushed for a declaration of independence. Moderates among Damascus notables, who favored a negotiated settlement with France, were often outvoted or intimidated.

Historians caution against treating the Congress as a fully representative body. Its franchise excluded most of the population, Lebanese participation was contested, and its claims to the whole of historic Syria overlooked communities that preferred other arrangements. Nevertheless it was a genuine political institution, with debates, resolutions, and elected leaders, and it established an argument that Syrians would repeat for decades: that a representative Syrian body had already declared the country's wishes before the French mandate was imposed.

Section 56.14

The Declaration of Independence, 8 March 1920

On 8 March 1920 the Congress, meeting in Damascus, declared the complete independence of Syria within its natural boundaries, including Palestine, and proclaimed Faisal king. The declaration was accompanied by plans for a constitutional monarchy with a parliament and a ministry responsible to it, and a committee drafted a constitution that provided for decentralized administration and guarantees for religious communities. On the same day, Iraqi officers and notables associated with Faisal's circle in Damascus proclaimed Faisal's brother Abdullah king of Iraq, linking the two Hashemite claims to a wider project of Arab states.

The Syrian leaders based their case on several arguments. They cited the principle of self-determination and the wartime assurances of 1918, the provisional recognition of independence for Arab communities in Article 22, and the fact that an Arab administration was already governing the interior. They also argued that the Allies had pledged that the wishes of the population would be the principal consideration. Their opponents answered that the future of former Ottoman territory could be decided only by the peace conference and that no local body could declare sovereignty unilaterally.

The declaration drew mixed reactions in the region. In Mount Lebanon, a council at Baabda declared Lebanese independence within its own historic frontiers later that month, rejecting incorporation into a Syrian kingdom while also seeking French guidance. In Palestine, the proclamation heightened political excitement, and the disturbances in Jerusalem during the Nebi Musa festival of April 1920 occurred in this atmosphere, though they had local causes as well (Chapter 59). The British and French governments declined to recognize the declaration, and the Allies stated that the status of the territories would be settled by the peace conference.

The proclamation reveals the limits of the Syrian position. It created a legal and political claim that could not be enforced against the Allied armies standing nearby, and it hardened French opinion in favor of intervention. At the same time it was an important expression of political will, supported by a broad nationalist coalition, and it remained a reference point in later Syrian arguments about the legitimacy of French rule.

Section 56.15

Faisal, French Diplomacy, and the Failure of Compromise

Faisal's negotiations with France ran through the second half of 1919 and the first months of 1920. In September 1919 Britain and France agreed that British troops would leave inland Syria by the end of the year, with French forces taking over the coastal and Lebanese zones and Faisal's administration remaining in the interior pending a settlement. General Henri Gouraud arrived in Beirut as French High Commissioner in November. In January 1920, after talks in Paris, Faisal and Prime Minister Georges Clemenceau reached an understanding in which France would recognize Syrian independence under French assistance. It proved fragile: Clemenceau left office within weeks, and the successor government under Alexandre Millerand did not feel bound to a compromise that its predecessor had negotiated.

On his return to Damascus, Faisal found that the Congress and the nationalist societies rejected any arrangement that implied a French mandate. He was caught between two forces: the French, who demanded effective control, and Syrian nationalists, who regarded concessions as betrayal. Some of his moderate advisers urged him to accept a French role in exchange for guarantees of Syrian self-government, while the radicals urged him to hold out and prepare for resistance, in the hope that British or American support would emerge.

British policy was also unhelpful to Faisal. London was not willing to protect his government against France and urged him to reach terms with the French. British officials sympathized with Faisal but valued their relationship with France and wished to secure their own claims in Palestine and Mesopotamia. Their withdrawal from Syria removed his strongest guarantee of survival, and the subsidy that sustained his administration was reduced.

The failure of compromise should not be explained simply by personal errors. The parties held incompatible aims: France sought to establish a mandate in Syria, while Syrian nationalists demanded independence on terms that excluded one. They also interpreted the wartime commitments differently, and France held the military advantage. Faisal's decisions can be criticized, but they were made in a situation where each available option had serious costs.

Section 56.16

The San Remo Conference, April 1920

The San Remo Conference met on the Italian Riviera from 19 to 26 April 1920, with British delegates under Lloyd George and Curzon, French delegates under Millerand, Italian representatives under Francesco Nitti, and a Japanese delegate, while the United States attended only as an observer. Its agenda included the Turkish treaty, Germany, and the Middle East. The Arab provinces were settled by a resolution adopted on 25 April, assigning the mandate for Syria (with Lebanon) to France and those for Mesopotamia and Palestine to Britain, and stating that the mandatory powers would be selected by the principal Allied powers.

The resolution on Palestine incorporated the language of the Balfour Declaration, stating that the mandatory would be responsible for putting into effect the declaration of 1917 for a national home for the Jewish people, with the proviso that nothing should prejudice the civil and religious rights of existing non-Jewish communities in Palestine, or the rights and political status of Jews in other countries. The conference also treated Syria and Mesopotamia as provisionally independent under Article 22, subject to the mandates. The boundaries of the mandated territories, it stated, would be determined later by the principal Allied powers.

At the same conference, Britain and France reached an oil arrangement under which France would receive a share, reportedly about a quarter, of Mesopotamian oil production, and the two powers agreed on facilities for pipelines and railways to the Mediterranean through French and British zones. Some historians regard this as evidence that oil and strategic interests were central to the mandate allocations; others caution that mandate decisions had many motives and that the oil agreement was a subsidiary bargain. The point of the arrangement is that the settlement was shaped by hard bargaining between Britain and France.

San Remo is important chiefly because it settled who would exercise authority, but it did not complete the process. Its decisions were taken by the Allied governments alone, without consultation of the populations, and without formal League involvement. The mandates still needed written instruments, approval by the League Council, and the fixing of boundaries. For Syrians, however, San Remo transformed the situation: from this point France possessed an international title it could invoke against the Damascus government.

Section 56.17

The French Ultimatum and the Battle of Maysalun

On 14 July 1920 General Gouraud presented Faisal with an ultimatum. It demanded acceptance of the French mandate, French control of the Rayak–Aleppo railway, the introduction of French-backed currency, the disbanding of the Arab army and the end of conscription, and the punishment of those held responsible for attacks on French personnel and their allies. The deadline was four days. Faisal and his cabinet, aware of the military balance, accepted on 18 July, but French forces had already begun to move, and the French claimed that the reply had come after the deadline or was incomplete.

The resulting confusion contributed to the disaster. Parts of the Syrian army had been demobilized in response to Faisal's instructions, while volunteers and nationalists in Damascus rallied against the ultimatum. The minister of war, Yusuf al-Azma, decided to resist and led a force of regular soldiers and irregular volunteers, a few thousand in number, to the pass of Maysalun on the road from the Bekaa to Damascus. Accounts of the numbers on each side vary, but the French force was much larger, better trained, and equipped with artillery, tanks, and aircraft.

At Maysalun on 24 July 1920 the French forces overran the Syrian defenses in a battle that lasted only a few hours. Al-Azma was killed, and his death made him a national martyr, with a memorial that became a focus of later commemoration. Syrian narratives often portray the battle as a heroic last stand, while French accounts emphasize the ultimatum's rejection and the need to enforce the mandate. Both versions are partly shaped by later political purposes, and the exact circumstances of the telegrams and military orders remain disputed.

The battle's historical significance goes beyond its military result. It showed that the mandate would be enforced by arms, that Faisal's government had no means to protect itself without British support, and that some Syrians preferred resistance to a negotiated surrender even at hopeless odds. Students should avoid treating it as an equal contest; its importance lies in what it revealed about power, legitimacy, and the way the new order was established.

Section 56.18

The French Entry into Damascus

French troops entered Damascus on 25 July 1920, the day after Maysalun. Faisal left the city within days, traveling south, and eventually departed Palestine for Europe. The French dissolved the Arab government and the Congress, imposed military administration, arrested or exiled nationalist leaders, and fined the city. Many nationalists left for Cairo, Transjordan, and elsewhere, where they formed organizations that continued to campaign against the mandate, including the Syro-Palestinian Congress that later presented Syrian and Palestinian grievances in Geneva.

Gouraud and his advisers also began dividing the territory into separate administrative units. In the autumn of 1920 they created autonomous states centered on Damascus, Aleppo, and the Alawite coastal region, and in 1921 added a Druze territory in the south, in addition to Greater Lebanon (§56.19). The arrangement reflected French views that communal differences should be recognized, and it also had the effect of weakening the nationalist movement, which had its strongest base in Damascus. The development of these units is part of the story of Chapter 58.

Reactions among Syrians varied. Nationalists regarded the occupation as a violation of Syrian independence, and some prepared armed resistance in the north and the coastal mountains (§56.31). Many urban notables sought accommodation with the new authorities in order to protect their property and influence, and some religious and communal leaders welcomed the French as protectors. The experience of conquest shaped later political memory, and anecdotes such as the claim that Gouraud visited the tomb of Saladin and spoke to him are widely repeated but their authenticity is uncertain, and historians treat them as part of a later narrative.

For the mandate system as a whole, Damascus mattered because the mandate was established by conquest before the League approved its terms. The French claimed to act under the authority granted at San Remo, but the use of force against a government that had declared independence affected how Syrians, and many observers in other countries, regarded the legitimacy of the mandate. The experience helped create the standard Syrian criticism of the system: that it was a form of imperial rule dressed in the language of trusteeship.

Section 56.19

The Proclamation of Greater Lebanon

On 1 September 1920 Gouraud proclaimed the State of Greater Lebanon at a ceremony in Beirut attended by Maronite Patriarch Elias Hoayek and representatives of other communities. The new state combined the former autonomous district of Mount Lebanon, which had existed since 1861 as an Ottoman mutasarrifate with a largely Maronite and Druze population, with the coastal cities of Beirut, Tripoli, Sidon, and Tyre, the Bekaa valley, and adjacent districts including the Shi'i regions of Jabal Amil. The mutasarrifate had been ruled by a Christian governor appointed by the Ottoman government with the approval of the great powers, and its borders had been narrower.

Several arguments supported expansion. Maronite leaders, notably Hoayek, who led a delegation to Paris in 1919, argued that Mount Lebanon was not economically viable without the coast and the Bekaa, an argument reinforced by the wartime famine, which had made the district dependent on imported grain. They also claimed historic rights to these areas. French officials regarded a strengthened Lebanon as a reliable ally and a counterweight to Damascus. The expanded territory offered ports, farmland, and revenues but included large communities that had not been part of the older district.

Reactions were divided. Many Sunni Muslims in Beirut, Tripoli, and Sidon, along with many Shi'i and some Greek Orthodox communities, preferred union with Syria or resented incorporation into a state in which Christians, especially Maronites, would hold a leading position. Some Sunni leaders boycotted or protested the new arrangements at first. Among Christians, most Maronite leaders supported the larger state, but others preferred a smaller Lebanon or different arrangements. Syrian nationalists regarded the new boundaries as an amputation of Syria. It would be inaccurate to describe the Lebanese as divided simply into a Christian pro-Lebanon camp and a Muslim pro-Syria camp.

The consequences were lasting. Lebanon became a state with an unusually diverse population and a political system based on balancing communities, which developed during the mandate (Chapter 58). The decision to enlarge it also created economic links and disputes between Lebanon and Syria that persisted after independence. Its legitimacy was disputed at its birth and became a continuing feature of Lebanese politics.

Division IV. The Mandate System Becomes an International Order
Section 56.20

From San Remo to League Approval

The mandate system did not come into being on a single date. It developed through a sequence of stages, each of which had a different legal character. First came an Allied political decision, taken at San Remo in April 1920, about which power would administer which territory. Next came the practical establishment of control, by occupation or by force, as at Damascus in July 1920. The mandatory powers then drafted written instruments, which were negotiated among the Allies and, in the case of Palestine, discussed with the United States, which was not a League member but insisted on equal economic treatment and on consultation about the terms. Only after these steps did the League Council approve the texts, and approval was followed by a further delay before each mandate entered into force.

The Council approved the Palestine mandate and the mandate for Syria and Lebanon at its London meeting on 24 July 1922. Disagreements among the Allies over the Holy Places, in which Italy and the Vatican took an interest, and over other provisions helped postpone approval, and the instruments entered into force only on 29 September 1923, after Britain and France had settled their boundary arrangements and the Turkish peace was concluded at Lausanne. The history of the mandates therefore spans more than three years between the San Remo decision and entry into force, and in Iraq's case a different procedure was followed (§56.24).

The League's supervisory body was the Permanent Mandates Commission, established by the Council in 1920 and active from 1921. It consisted of experts who served in a personal capacity, a majority of them from states that did not hold mandates. The commission examined the annual reports that mandatory governments submitted, questioned their representatives, and reported to the Council. Inhabitants of the mandates could send petitions, but these had to be forwarded through the mandatory government, which could comment on them. The commission's published records give historians valuable evidence, though they were produced in a framework in which the mandatory controlled the flow of information.

The limits of supervision are a central point. The commission could criticize, request further information, and make recommendations, but it had no troops, no budget to administer the territories, and no power to dismiss a mandatory. The mandated peoples were not parties to the instruments, which were arrangements between the Allied powers, the mandatory, and the League Council. Whether sovereignty lay with the mandatory, the League, or the local population was a subject of debate among contemporary jurists. These ambiguities allowed the mandatories wide discretion and shaped the political disputes of the interwar years.

Section 56.21

The French Mandate for Syria and Lebanon

The mandate instrument for Syria and Lebanon, approved by the League Council in July 1922, set out France's responsibilities in a document of several dozen articles. It required France to frame an organic law for Syria and Lebanon within three years, in consultation with local authorities, and to encourage local autonomy as far as circumstances permitted. It obliged France to ensure that no part of the territory was ceded or placed under the control of a foreign power, to respect the personal status and religious interests of the various communities, to guarantee freedom of conscience and worship, and to maintain public order. France was permitted to station troops and organize local forces, to control foreign relations, and to supervise finance and public works. Arabic and French were both to be official languages.

The text also described the mandatory's task as facilitating the progressive development of Syria and Lebanon as independent states. This language did not specify a timetable or a method, and it left the interpretation of progress to the mandatory and the League. French officials treated their obligation as one of tutelage, while Syrian nationalists read the text as a commitment to constitutional self-government and territorial integrity. The disagreement over what the instrument required became a constant theme of Franco-Syrian politics.

In practice, the administration was led by a High Commissioner, beginning with Gouraud, who held wide executive and military authority and presided over a structure that combined French officials with local administrations. The French also emphasized communal and regional distinctions, as seen in the separate states created in 1920–21, and they supported a currency and customs system tied to France. The mandate text said little about these matters, but its provisions on religious communities and public order gave a legal basis for French policies.

Comparing the formal promises with practice is central to historical assessment. The text promised preparation for independence, but France's early rule relied on military force, the division of Syria, and limited representative institutions. France argued that the diversity and instability of the region justified its methods. Syrians countered that the French were violating the mandate's spirit and that the divisions were designed to weaken nationalism. The subsequent developments, including the Great Syrian Revolt, the 1926 Lebanese constitution, and the long negotiations over a treaty, belong to Chapter 58.

Section 56.22

The British Mandate for Palestine

The British mandate for Palestine, approved by the League Council on 24 July 1922, was a document of twenty-eight articles. Its preamble recited that the principal Allied powers had agreed that the mandatory should be responsible for putting into effect the declaration made by the British government on 2 November 1917 and adopted by the other powers in favor of the establishment of a national home for the Jewish people. It stated that recognition had been given to the historical connection of the Jewish people with Palestine and to the grounds for reconstituting their national home there, and it added that nothing should prejudice the civil and religious rights of existing non-Jewish communities in Palestine.

The operative articles reinforced this dual structure. Article 2 made the mandatory responsible for placing the country under such political, administrative, and economic conditions as would secure the establishment of the Jewish national home and the development of self-governing institutions, while safeguarding the civil and religious rights of all inhabitants irrespective of race and religion. Article 4 provided for the recognition of an appropriate Jewish agency as a public body to advise and cooperate with the administration, and Article 6 required the administration to facilitate Jewish immigration under suitable conditions and to encourage close settlement of Jews on the land, while ensuring that the rights and position of other sections of the population were not prejudiced. Other articles dealt with nationality, the Holy Places, languages (English, Arabic, and Hebrew), and the protection of religious freedom.

The text's treatment of the Arab population is a central point of historical debate. It referred to the Arabs, who made up close to nine-tenths of the population at the 1922 census, as non-Jewish communities and gave them protections of civil and religious rights, but its political provisions referred to the Jewish national home and to self-governing institutions in general terms. Palestinian leaders therefore argued that the instrument gave political recognition to one community and only civil and religious rights to the other. British officials, in a statement of policy published in June 1922, clarified that they did not aim at making Palestine as a whole a Jewish national home, and that immigration would be limited by the economic capacity of the country to absorb newcomers. The Zionist leadership accepted this statement, while Arab representatives rejected it.

Two dates must be separated. The Council approved the mandate on 24 July 1922, but it entered into force on 29 September 1923, after the settlement of the Turkish peace and Franco-British boundary questions. In the interval, Britain governed Palestine under its occupation authority and the terms of the draft. The mandate's long-term consequences, including its administration, the growth of Jewish and Arab institutions, and the escalating conflict, are the subject of Chapter 59.

Section 56.23

Transjordan and Article 25

The territory east of the Jordan had been divided among Ottoman districts, some administered from Damascus and others dependent on Ma'an, Karak, and Salt. After the fall of Faisal's government in July 1920, the area was left without an effective central authority. In August 1920 British officials, including Herbert Samuel, met local notables at Salt and promised local self-government under British advice. British political officers were stationed in several towns, and local governments emerged, with limited cohesion.

Abdullah, Faisal's brother, arrived at Ma'an in November 1920 at the head of a following from the Hijaz, declaring his intention to march against the French in Syria. British officials feared that this would provoke a clash with France, and in March 1921 the Cairo Conference, convened by Winston Churchill as Colonial Secretary, considered the matter alongside Iraq. After meeting Abdullah in Jerusalem later that month, Churchill agreed that he should administer the territory east of the Jordan for an initial period under British supervision, as a separate unit from Palestine. The arrangement was at first provisional, and its permanence was not assured.

Article 25 of the Palestine mandate allowed the mandatory, with the consent of the Council, to postpone or withhold the application of provisions of the mandate in the territories east of the Jordan. In September 1922 the Council approved a British memorandum under which the provisions relating to the Jewish national home were not applied in Transjordan. This created a different legal and political arrangement from Palestine, though Transjordan remained part of the mandated area and under British supervision. Britain recognized Transjordan as an autonomous administration under Abdullah in 1923.

The arrangement served several British purposes. It created a Hashemite administration that satisfied Britain's obligations to the family, secured a buffer between Palestine, Syria, Iraq, and Arabia, and maintained a land route to Mesopotamia, at low cost. It was also contested by Transjordan's inhabitants, whose tribal leaders and townspeople had varying views on Abdullah's rule. The detailed development of the emirate, including its institutions and tribal revolts, belongs to Chapter 57.

Section 56.24

Mesopotamia: A Distinct British Arrangement

Britain had expected to administer Mesopotamia under a mandate, and the San Remo resolution had assigned it that responsibility. The Iraqi revolt of 1920 (§56.32) changed the political calculation. The cost of suppression, the strength of Iraqi opposition, and criticism at home led British officials to seek a cheaper and politically more defensible form of control. Percy Cox returned to Baghdad in October 1920 and set up a provisional Arab government, headed by the Naqib of Baghdad, with British advisers in key posts.

At the Cairo Conference in March 1921, British officials decided to offer the throne of Iraq to Faisal, who had been expelled from Damascus. He arrived in Basra in June 1921 and was proclaimed king in August following a referendum that British officials reported as overwhelmingly favorable, though historians question its conduct. The choice combined several objectives: to give Iraq a ruler with nationalist credentials who owed his position to Britain, to compensate the Hashemite family, and to reduce British expenditure.

The Anglo-Iraqi Treaty, signed on 10 October 1922, set out the new relationship. It provided that Iraq would be advised by the British High Commissioner on matters affecting British interests and international obligations, that British officials would hold certain posts, and that Britain would provide military support, while Iraq would meet specified financial obligations. The treaty was to last twenty years, though subsequent protocols shortened its duration, and the Iraqi Constituent Assembly ratified it in 1924 only after strong British pressure. The League Council later accepted the treaty arrangements as the basis on which Britain would discharge its obligations.

Iraq's arrangement therefore differed from those in Syria, Lebanon, and Palestine. It was based on a treaty with a nominally sovereign monarchy, and the League did not approve a separate mandate instrument of the same type. Britain continued to exert influence through advisers, the army and air force, and control of finance, so that the distinction between a mandate and a treaty was more a matter of form than of practice. The detailed history of the Iraqi monarchy and its politics belongs to Chapter 57.

Section 56.25

Sèvres, the Turkish National Movement, and Lausanne

The Treaty of Sèvres, signed on 10 August 1920 between the Allied powers and the government of the Ottoman Sultan, was intended to settle the Ottoman question. It required the Ottoman state to renounce its claims to the Arab provinces, recognized the Hijaz as an independent state, acknowledged the separation of Syria and Mesopotamia under mandates, and confirmed the British protectorate in Egypt. It also provided for an Armenian state, a possible autonomous Kurdish region, Greek administration in the Smyrna area, and international control of the Straits. The treaty's harsh terms provoked fierce opposition from Turkish nationalists.

Sèvres never came into force. The nationalist government that Mustafa Kemal had organized in Ankara refused to accept it, and the Turkish War of Independence, including the defeat of Greek forces in 1922, changed the military situation. The Allies agreed to negotiate a new settlement, and the Ottoman sultanate was abolished in November 1922. The Lausanne conference, held in two sessions from November 1922 to July 1923, produced the Treaty of Lausanne, signed on 24 July 1923, which recognized the sovereignty of the Turkish state in Anatolia and eastern Thrace. The Turkish Republic was proclaimed later, on 29 October 1923.

For the Arab provinces, Lausanne mattered because Turkey renounced its rights over them, giving the new arrangements a firmer international legal basis. The treaty also helped clear the way for the entry into force of the Syrian and Palestine mandates in September 1923. It did not, however, settle every frontier question. The status of the Mosul province was left open, to be resolved by negotiation between Britain and Turkey and, failing that, by the League, which led to the 1926 settlement (§56.29).

Students should note the distinction between the territorial settlement for the Turkish state and the separate processes that established the Arab mandates. Lausanne ended the Turkish-Allied conflict and set Turkey's borders in Europe and Anatolia, while San Remo, the League instruments, and the Franco-British agreements determined the Arab territories. The Kurdish provisions of Sèvres were not repeated at Lausanne, which left Kurdish communities divided among several new states.

Division V. Drawing, Negotiating, and Administering Borders
Section 56.26

The Franco-British Agreements of 1920

The San Remo resolutions left the boundaries of the mandated territories to be fixed by the principal Allied powers. Britain and France did so in a convention signed on 23 December 1920, which dealt with the lines between Syria and Lebanon, Palestine, and Mesopotamia, and also with questions of water, railways, and pipelines. It described the Syria–Palestine boundary from the Mediterranean near Ras al-Naqura eastward to the area of Hammat Gader on the Yarmuk, and it outlined the Syria–Mesopotamia line across the desert, leaving details to be marked by joint commissions. These commissions worked from 1921 to 1923 and produced more detailed agreements, including the Paulet–Newcombe arrangements, which refined the line on the ground.

The actual boundaries differed from the spheres sketched in the Sykes–Picot agreement of 1916. That agreement had proposed an international administration for part of Palestine, a French zone including Mosul, and British control of Baghdad and Basra, with an Arab state or confederation in the interior under French and British influence. By 1920 Britain had obtained Palestine and Mosul, France had obtained Syria and Lebanon, and the interior zone had been divided into mandates. Students should therefore treat Sykes–Picot as a wartime proposal that was substantially revised, not as the blueprint of the modern map.

The negotiators weighed geography, railways, water, and strategic calculations. Britain wanted a boundary that secured access to the Jordan headwaters and the Yarmuk, a railway link between Haifa and the Hijaz line, and a route for a pipeline from Mesopotamia to the Mediterranean. France sought to keep the Beirut–Damascus–Aleppo railway and the Rayak junction within its zone. The Zionist Organization lobbied for a northern boundary that would take in the Litani River and the slopes of Mount Hermon, and although it did not obtain them, the line placed Lake Huleh and the Galilee panhandle within Palestine, while some headwater sources remained under French control.

The convention illustrates the way in which the mandate boundaries were made. They emerged from bargaining between two imperial governments, using surveyors' reports and the advice of colonial officials, with little or no consultation of the people who lived along the lines. Their legal basis was a treaty between the mandatories, later recognized by the League, and the details were adjusted over several years. Yet these lines became the frontiers of modern states, and many later disputes over water, pastoral movement, and sovereignty can be traced to the decisions made in this period.

Section 56.27

The Boundaries of Syria and Greater Lebanon

The boundary between Syria and Greater Lebanon was drawn to include in Lebanon territories that had never belonged to the older Mount Lebanon district. Beirut, the main Levantine port and the commercial outlet of Damascus, was connected to the interior by a French-built railway and road, and its merchants traded with both Mount Lebanon and Syria. Tripoli was linked to Homs and Hama through trade in grain, cotton, and soap. Sidon looked toward the Hauran and the south, and the Bekaa valley, one of the main grain-producing areas of the region, supplied both Damascus and Mount Lebanon. The new boundary placed these places in Lebanon and left Syria with fewer ports.

The arguments for and against the line were partly economic and partly political. Supporters of a larger Lebanon, led by Maronite leaders, said that the old district could not feed its population, particularly after the wartime famine, and that the coast and the Bekaa were naturally part of Lebanon. Syrian nationalists and many Muslims in the coastal towns replied that the cities had long been commercial and administrative centers of Syria and that separating them divided the country's economy. French officials supported the larger Lebanon because they considered Lebanese Christians reliable allies and wanted a viable state under French influence.

The new boundary brought into Lebanon large Sunni and Shi'i communities, including the inhabitants of Jabal Amil and the Bekaa, along with Greek Orthodox and other Christian groups. The consequences were felt quickly. Goods that had previously moved within a single economic region now crossed a customs line, though the French attempted to soften this through a system of joint administration of customs and certain other services for Syria and Lebanon. Landowners and merchants with interests on both sides had to adapt, and the question of the boundary remained a political issue in both countries.

Historians reconstruct the effects of the boundary from customs statistics, petitions by merchants and notables, French administrative reports, and the press. These sources show that the line did not have a uniform effect: it favored Beirut and certain Christian regions while burdening Damascus and the northern interior. It also had a political life, since Syrian governments after independence continued to question some of its aspects. The section's key lesson is that this boundary was constructed by decisions of 1920 and became a lasting feature of the region's political economy.

Section 56.28

Palestine, Transjordan, and British Administration

The Ottoman Empire had no single administrative unit called Palestine. Its territory was divided among the independent district of Jerusalem and the districts of Nablus and Acre in the Beirut province, though the name Palestine was in common use in religious, geographic, and scholarly writing, and was used by some Arab newspapers and political writers. The mandate gave the name a precise legal and territorial meaning for the first time, and the boundaries that it adopted were drawn by the British and French governments rather than by any local authority.

The southern boundary with Egypt followed the line from Rafah to Taba that had been fixed in 1906, giving the country access to the Gulf of Aqaba at its southern tip. The northern line, set by the convention of 1920 and refined by the commissions, ran from the Mediterranean to the Galilee and the Yarmuk. The eastern limit was the Jordan Valley, the Dead Sea, and the Wadi Araba, beyond which lay Transjordan. The status of the Aqaba and Ma'an area was initially uncertain, since it had been part of the Hijaz, and it was transferred to Transjordan only in 1925 (Chapter 57).

The relationship between Palestine and Transjordan was one of the most contested aspects of the territorial settlement. Under Article 25, Britain could administer the lands east of the Jordan separately, and from 1922 it did so without applying the provisions concerning the Jewish national home. The High Commissioner in Jerusalem remained the senior British official for both, but the two territories had distinct administrations, laws, and revenues. Zionist leaders criticized the separation because they had expected Transjordan to be open to Jewish settlement, while Palestinian leaders saw it as a division of the country.

The geography of the mandate also shaped its society. The coastal plain, the hill country, the Jordan Valley, and the Negev supported different economies and communities, and the mandate boundaries cut across some existing patterns, especially in the north and the south. It is important to distinguish the geographical understanding of Palestine as a historical region from the boundaries of the mandate's administration, and to avoid projecting the later lines back onto earlier periods. The development of the country under the mandate is treated in Chapter 59.

Section 56.29

Mesopotamia, Mosul, and the Northern Frontier

The Ottoman province of Mosul, comprising the districts of Mosul, Kirkuk, and Sulaymaniyah, was occupied by British forces after the armistice, and it was assigned to Britain at San Remo. Its inclusion in the Iraqi territorial framework, together with Baghdad and Basra, produced a state containing Sunni and Shi'i Arabs, Kurds, Assyrians, Turkmen, Yazidis, and Jewish and other communities. British officials argued that Mosul was needed for the economic viability and defense of Iraq, and many believed that it contained oil reserves, though the major discoveries at Kirkuk came only after the settlement.

Turkey disputed British control. The Turkish National Pact of 1920 claimed the Mosul region, and the Ankara government argued that its population, particularly the Kurds, was closer to Turkey than to an Arab state. Kurdish leaders held varied views. The Treaty of Sèvres had contemplated Kurdish autonomy but never took effect. Some leaders, such as Shaykh Mahmud Barzinji in Sulaymaniyah, rebelled against British authority and sought a Kurdish government, while others accepted Baghdad or preferred Turkish rule. In December 1922 Britain and Iraq issued a declaration recognizing the rights of Kurds within Iraq, but its implementation was limited.

The Treaty of Lausanne gave Britain and Turkey nine months to settle the question by negotiation, failing which it would be referred to the League of Nations. When talks failed, the Council set a provisional line in 1924, and a commission of inquiry visited the province in 1925. The League Council decided in December 1925 that Mosul should remain in Iraq, with conditions concerning Kurdish rights and a longer duration for British responsibility. The settlement was concluded in a treaty of June 1926 among Britain, Iraq, and Turkey, under which Turkey accepted the frontier and Iraq agreed to pay Turkey a share of oil royalties for a period of years.

The Mosul case shows several features of the post-Ottoman settlement. Frontier decisions were still being made years after San Remo, they involved bargaining among great powers and the League, and local populations were consulted only partially. It also shows the complexity of Iraq's population, a point that will matter for the politics of the monarchy examined in Chapter 57. Kurdish communities, in particular, were left divided among several states without the autonomy that some had hoped for.

Section 56.30

Borders and Everyday Life

Borders were not only lines on treaty maps. They became administrative practices that changed how people traded, traveled, and held property. Aleppo offers a clear example. The city had long served as a commercial center connecting northern Syria, Anatolia, and Mesopotamia, but the boundary agreed between France and the Ankara government in October 1921 placed a Syria–Turkey line just north of the city and cut it off from much of its Anatolian hinterland. Its merchants had to reorient trade toward the coast and the south, and the city's economy suffered a long adjustment.

Damascus and the Syrian interior faced new customs and currency arrangements. Goods moving to and from Beirut, Haifa, and Baghdad crossed lines drawn between separate administrations, each with its own tariffs and currency: the Egyptian pound circulated in Palestine, a franc-linked currency in Syria and Lebanon, and the Indian rupee in Iraq for a number of years. Railways such as the Hijaz line and the lines linking Beirut, Damascus, and Aleppo crossed multiple jurisdictions, requiring new arrangements for operation, freight, and passengers. The pilgrimage routes to the Hijaz also passed through several new administrations.

Pastoral communities were also affected. Tribal groups whose grazing ranges extended across what became Syria, Iraq, Transjordan, and Najd had to move through territories controlled by different governments, and the mandate authorities attempted to regulate their movement and taxation. The Franco-British convention and later agreements included provisions concerning nomadic movement and water access, though practice varied and conflicts over grazing and security arose. For many tribal leaders, the new frontiers meant new negotiations with governments over rights to pasture and wells.

Landowners, religious endowments, and families also felt the change. Property held by a single endowment or family could now lie in two jurisdictions, and legal disputes over inheritance, taxes, and status arose. Families with relatives in different mandates experienced travel formalities and passports. Evidence for these experiences comes from customs and court records, petitions, and memoirs, which more often record the experience of merchants, officials, and landowners than of women, herders, and the poor. Border-making thus gradually produced new political communities even where this was not its stated aim.

Division VI. Local Resistance and Negotiated Authority
Section 56.31

Syrian Resistance to French Rule after 1920

French occupation of Damascus in July 1920 did not end armed and political opposition. In northern Syria, Ibrahim Hananu, a landowner and former Ottoman deputy from the Idlib district, led a revolt around Aleppo that had begun in 1919 as French forces took over the north. It gained strength after Maysalun and drew on local fighters, former Ottoman officers, and, at times, assistance from Turkish nationalist forces operating across the frontier. French columns and negotiations with Ankara reduced the revolt, and the October 1921 agreement between France and the Ankara government ended outside support. Hananu was tried by a French court in Aleppo and acquitted in March 1922; his acquittal made him a nationalist symbol.

In the coastal mountains, Salih al-Ali, an Alawite shaykh, led resistance to French rule from the period of occupation of the coast until about 1921. The Alawites, a religious community of the mountains, did not share a single political attitude: some leaders cooperated with the French, who later created an Alawite territory, while others joined Salih al-Ali, who combined opposition to French control with local grievances and ties to Arab nationalists. In the south, the Druze of the Hawran and the Jabal al-Duruz under leaders including Sultan al-Atrash negotiated with and resisted French authority, and on 23 June 1921 an ambush near Quneitra targeted General Gouraud, who escaped unharmed although one of his officers was killed.

These movements shared opposition to French rule but not a single program. Some leaders sought a united independent Syria; others defended local autonomy, resisted taxation and conscription, or sought to preserve the position of their communities. Participants included former Ottoman and Sharifian officers, landowners, tribal and village leaders, and religious figures. Political opposition also took the form of strikes, petitions, and exile organizations in Cairo and elsewhere, which presented Syrian claims to the League and to the press.

The evidence comes largely from French military and intelligence reports, which describe the revolts as banditry or as instigated from abroad, and from Arabic memoirs and later nationalist narratives, which present them as national resistance. Historians therefore read both critically, recognizing that the movements combined national, local, religious, and economic motives. The Great Syrian Revolt of 1925–27, which drew on these precedents but was far larger, belongs to Chapter 58.

Section 56.32

The Iraqi Revolt of 1920

The Iraqi revolt of 1920 grew out of the experience of British occupation. Since 1917 the British had expanded revenue collection, maintained a large military presence, and governed through political officers with wide powers, while Iraqis who had expected self-government after the war saw instead the announcement at San Remo that Britain would hold a mandate. Tribal leaders resented taxation and restrictions on their authority, religious leaders in the Shi'i holy cities objected to foreign rule by non-Muslims, and nationalist groups in Baghdad, including both Sunni and Shi'i participants, organized demonstrations in mosques and political societies.

The revolt began at Rumaitha on the middle Euphrates at the end of June 1920 after the arrest of a local tribal leader, and it spread across the middle and lower Euphrates and into other districts. Muhammad Taqi al-Shirazi, a senior Shi'i religious authority in Karbala, issued rulings that supported demands for Iraqi rights and legitimized resistance, and other clerics in Najaf and Karbala encouraged the movement. Tribal leaders provided most of the fighters. The revolt's strongest areas were in the middle Euphrates, and its participants included a wide range of groups, though it did not cover the whole country and its leadership was not united.

Britain suppressed the movement by October with reinforcements brought from India and with the use of air power. Estimates of Iraqi deaths vary widely, from several thousand upward, and British and Indian casualties and the cost of the campaign were substantial. The scale of the revolt shocked British officials and the British public, and it led to criticism of the administration in Mesopotamia and to a reconsideration of policy.

The revolt's historical significance lies in its effect on the form of British control. Percy Cox returned to Baghdad in October 1920 to form a provisional Iraqi government, and the Colonial Office, under Winston Churchill, looked for a cheaper arrangement through a Hashemite monarchy and treaty relationship (§56.24). Iraqi nationalist narratives later treated the revolt as a founding national movement, while historians have emphasized its local and tribal dimensions as well as its national ones. Its campaigns, leaders, and long-term consequences receive fuller treatment in Chapter 57.

Section 56.33

Palestinian Arab Opposition and Zionist Responses

Palestinian Arab political organization developed quickly after 1918. Muslim-Christian Associations were established in many towns, and a series of Palestine Arab Congresses began in Jerusalem in 1919. The third congress, held in Haifa in December 1920, elected an Arab Executive Committee headed by Musa Kazim al-Husayni, which sent delegations to London in 1921 and 1922 to press for the repeal of the Balfour policy, an end to Jewish immigration, and the creation of a national government. Colonial Secretary Churchill urged the delegates to accept the mandate, but they refused to recognize its legitimacy.

British policy during these years included the appointment of Herbert Samuel as High Commissioner in 1920 and attempts to create institutions for limited self-government. A proposal in 1922–23 for a Legislative Council, with elected and appointed members, was boycotted by most Arab leaders on the grounds that participation would imply acceptance of the mandate and the Balfour Declaration. Violence also marked this period. In May 1921 disturbances in Jaffa and other places killed many Arabs and Jews, and a British commission of inquiry attributed them to Arab fears of Zionist immigration and policy while also criticizing the violence. British authorities temporarily suspended immigration and then restated that it would be limited by the economic absorptive capacity of the country.

Zionist organizations welcomed the mandate framework and used it to build their institutions. The Zionist Commission became the Palestine Zionist Executive, and in 1920 the Jewish community elected an Assembly of Representatives and a National Council. The labor federation Histadrut was founded in the same year, and funds were raised for land purchase and settlement. Article 4 of the mandate provided for an official Jewish agency, a status that gave the Zionist Organization a formal role in the administration's advisory structure.

The two movements therefore responded to the mandate framework in opposite ways. Palestinian leaders boycotted institutions they regarded as legitimizing it, while Zionist leaders participated in and expanded those they could use. Historians debate the consequences of these choices, including whether participation would have given Palestinian Arabs more influence. The longer-term history of Palestinian society, Jewish immigration, British policy, and the conflict is the subject of Chapter 59.

Section 56.34

Negotiation, Local Elites, and Colonial Limits

The mandatory authorities had limited personnel and funds, and they governed through local intermediaries. In Syria and Lebanon the French worked with Maronite church leaders, Sunni and Druze notables, landowners, and some urban merchants, while also fighting nationalists. In Iraq the British strengthened tribal shaykhs through special regulations and relied on Sharifian officers and Baghdad notables to staff the new state. In Palestine they worked through established urban families and, from 1921, through a Supreme Muslim Council for religious affairs. In Transjordan, Abdullah relied on tribal shaykhs and on Circassian and other communities.

These arrangements were not simple collaboration. Local leaders often pursued their own interests, which might include protecting land and status, gaining official positions, or advancing a political cause. Some cooperated at one time and resisted at another. Historians sometimes describe this as the politics of notables, a pattern in which urban elites mediated between the population and the state. The same leaders could be seen by followers as protectors, or as collaborators who had sold out the national cause, and their reputations were contested.

Mandate rule also differed in method. France more often used direct administration and military force and divided territory into separate units, while Britain, in Iraq and Transjordan, ruled through Hashemite monarchies and treaties, though in Palestine it governed directly. These differences shaped how local elites could act. In Iraq, tribal and Shi'i leaders were drawn into the new state; in Syria, many urban nationalists remained outside the official system; in Palestine, the leadership's boycott limited its influence over the administration.

The overall lesson is that imperial authority rested on negotiation with local agency but was not equal to it. Mandatories controlled the army, finance, foreign relations, and the legal framework, so local actors bargained within boundaries they did not set. Students should avoid treating the populations as passive or as uniformly resistant, and should examine each territory's pattern separately, as Chapters 57–59 do.

Comparative Study

British and French Models of Mandatory Authority

France stressed direct administration, a high commissioner, and communal divisions; Britain used high commissioners and in Iraq and Transjordan indirect rule through Hashemite rulers under treaty. Both relied on local officials. Neither model was uniform: Palestine under Britain was direct, with a unique national home clause. The comparison should be tested territory by territory.

Division VII. Comparative Synthesis
Section 56.35

The Post-Ottoman State System

Between 1918 and 1923 the political geography of the Arab East was transformed. Ottoman provinces gave way to separately administered territories whose legal status, boundaries, and political institutions were decided through a sequence of provisional occupation, Allied allocation, international legal instruments, military enforcement, and treaty negotiation. The result was not a single system but a set of different arrangements, and the terms used to describe them need careful distinction.

Sovereignty, independence, territorial identity, and administrative authority did not mean the same thing in every case. Syria had been briefly independent in its own declaration and then governed by France as a mandate. Lebanon was a new territory with an enlarged boundary. Palestine was a mandate with a unique national home provision. Transjordan was an administration under a Hashemite ruler with British supervision. Iraq was a monarchy bound by a treaty to Britain. Historians continue to debate how the settlement should be characterized: as a calculated imperial division, as an improvised compromise among conflicting commitments, or as a stage in the formation of states that local actors also helped shape. The borders were not arbitrary lines drawn in a single meeting, nor were they simply the expression of local identities; they emerged through interaction among imperial interests, international law, military force, and local politics.

The chapters that follow examine how these arrangements worked. Chapter 57 traces the development of Iraq and Transjordan under Hashemite rulers and the rise of Ibn Saud's state in Arabia. Chapter 58 follows French rule in Syria and Lebanon through revolt, institutional change, and the road to independence. Chapter 59 examines the British mandate in Palestine, where Palestinian Arab and Zionist political movements developed within the framework established in 1922.

Chapter Summary

The end of Ottoman rule in 1918 created a political vacuum without settling sovereignty. Allied occupation divided the Arab provinces into zones, and Faisal's Damascus government governed the interior without international recognition.

At Paris in 1919, the Allies debated the Ottoman territories amid conflicting principles: Wilsonian self-determination, wartime promises to Arabs, the secret Anglo-French partition, and the Balfour Declaration. The Covenant's Article 22 created the mandate, a trusteeship that nominally recognized provisional independence for former Ottoman communities while giving mandatories wide authority. Versailles itself did not allocate the Arab lands.

Competing local claims emerged: Faisal's Hashemite program, the Syrian General Congress's demand for a united Syria, Maronite and other advocates of an enlarged Lebanon, Palestinian Arab opposition to the Balfour policy, and Zionist efforts to secure a national home. The King–Crane Commission documented opposition to French control and to unrestricted Zionist immigration, but it did not influence Allied decisions.

The decisive year was 1920. The Syrian declaration of independence in March was followed by San Remo in April, where the Allies assigned Syria and Lebanon to France and Mesopotamia and Palestine to Britain. France enforced its allocation at Maysalun in July, ended Faisal's kingdom, and proclaimed Greater Lebanon in September.

The mandate system took years to establish. The Council approved the Palestine and French mandates in July 1922 and they entered into force in September 1923; Transjordan received a separate arrangement under Article 25; Iraq was governed through an Anglo-Iraqi treaty and a Hashemite monarchy. Sèvres failed and Lausanne replaced it in 1923, leaving Mosul to be settled in 1926.

Boundaries resulted from Franco-British bargaining, geography, railways, water, and strategic judgment, not from Sykes–Picot alone and not from local consent. They gradually became administrative realities affecting trade, pastoral movement, property, and families.

Resistance and negotiation were part of the settlement. Early Syrian revolts, the Iraqi Revolt, and Palestinian political organization showed that mandatory authority was contested, and mandatory regimes relied on local notables, tribal leaders, and religious figures who bargained for their own interests.

By 1923 the Arab East was divided into territories with different legal statuses. Sovereignty, independence, and administrative authority were distinct in each. Chapters 57–59 explain how Iraq, Transjordan, Syria, Lebanon, and Palestine developed within, and against, this framework.

Chapter Reflection

The chapter matters because the post-Ottoman settlement defined the political vocabulary of the modern Arab world: mandates, borders, sovereignty, national home, and independence. Many later disputes, from boundaries to the status of Palestine, return to questions first posed between 1918 and 1923.

It also reveals how states come into being. The Arab state system was neither merely imposed nor merely inherited; it emerged from interaction among imperial decisions, international legal forms, local movements, military force, and negotiation. Students should resist both the claim that the borders were arbitrary lines drawn in one meeting and the claim that they reflected a natural national order.

The settlement connected regions that later chapters treat separately. The Iraqi Revolt shaped British policy in Transjordan and Palestine, French action in Syria shaped Lebanon, and Palestinian and Syrian claims were bound together in 1919–20 before they separated.

Continuities extended beyond 1923: Ottoman institutions, notable families, tribal structures, and economic networks persisted under new rulers, while the legal and territorial framework became the basis of later independence struggles. Chapter 57 turns to the construction of states in Iraq, Transjordan, and Arabia.
