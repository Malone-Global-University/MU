1967, 1973, and the Reshaping of Arab Politics

A History of the Arab World: From Ancient Crossroads to the Modern Arab World

Chapter Opening

Between June 1967 and March 1979, two wars, a long contest over occupied territory, Palestinian political reorganization, and a sequence of agreements changed the political order of the Arab world. This chapter explains why the fighting began, what it did to armies and civilians, how the conflict affected claims to sovereignty and self-determination, and why a bilateral peace agreement ended one interstate confrontation without resolving the wider conflict.

An Egyptian soldier crossing the canal in October 1973, a Palestinian family displaced from a West Bank village in June 1967, an Israeli household mobilized during a holiday, and a Syrian farmer separated from land on the Golan did not inhabit identical political histories. Yet their experiences were linked by decisions made in Cairo, Jerusalem, Amman, Damascus, Washington, Moscow, and international organizations. A textbook must keep human consequences visible even while reconstructing command decisions, diplomatic documents, and military operations.

The governing question is how the wars of 1967 and 1973 reshaped territorial control, Palestinian national politics, Arab leadership, international diplomacy, and the prospects for regional peace. The chapter distinguishes military success from political gain, effective control from recognized sovereignty, an emergency ceasefire from a negotiated disengagement, and a bilateral treaty from a comprehensive settlement. It draws on contemporaneous UN reports, government documents, diplomatic archives, and historical scholarship. Source-study windows below are explicitly editorial paraphrases, not alleged verbatim transcriptions.

Chapter 61 treats the 1948 war and Nakba; Chapter 62 treats Nasser's Egypt and the Suez Crisis; Chapter 64 treats internal changes in revolutionary Arab republics; and Chapter 65 treats Gulf oil and the 1973 petroleum shock. This chapter concentrates on the wars themselves, their occupation and refugee consequences, the rise of independent Palestinian political representation, and the diplomacy culminating in 1979.

Learning Objectives

By the end of the chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Reconstruct the May–June 1967 crisis and identify choices by Egyptian, Israeli, Jordanian, Syrian, Soviet, and American actors.
  2. Explain the Egyptian, Jordanian/West Bank, and Syrian operational fronts of the June war without treating them as identical.
  3. Differentiate military occupation, administration, annexation claims, and internationally recognized sovereign title.
  4. Assess contemporary UNRWA evidence concerning refugees and civilian displacement after 1967.
  5. Analyze the Khartoum resolution and Resolution 242 using the texts and competing interpretations.
  6. Explain the development of the PLO, Fatah, the War of Attrition, and the conflict in Jordan in 1970–1971.
  7. Identify why Egypt and Syria chose a coordinated offensive in October 1973 and how their military objectives differed.
  8. Describe the crossing, Golan battles, Israeli west-bank operation, and the ceasefires in Resolutions 338–340.
  9. Separate battlefield results in 1973 from domestic and diplomatic claims about success or defeat.
  10. Compare Geneva, the 1974 disengagement agreements, and Sinai II with later peace frameworks.
  11. Explain why Palestinian representation and Jordanian political claims became central issues after Rabat in 1974.

Historical Context

The 1949 Arab–Israeli armistice agreements suspended hostilities but did not create a general peace or solve the Palestinian refugee question. Gaza was administered by Egypt; Jordan governed the West Bank and East Jerusalem; Syria and Israel disputed frontier zones and access to water. The 1956 Suez Crisis had brought a UN emergency force onto Egyptian territory and reinforced Nasser's reputation for resisting external pressure. By the mid-1960s, Palestinian armed organizations, Israeli reprisals, Syrian and Egyptian political rivalry, and border disputes interacted to create a volatile security environment.

The Cold War formed another layer of the conflict. Egypt and Syria acquired substantial Soviet military equipment, while the United States developed an increasingly close security relationship with Israel. Yet these governments were not puppets. Egyptian leaders worried about domestic legitimacy and Sinai; Syrian leaders about the Golan; Jordan about state authority and Palestinian representation; Israelis about vulnerability, access to waterways, and the future of their state. From 1967 onward, civilians under occupation and refugees across borders had their own interests, often different from those of the states claiming to speak for them.

The chapter follows a sequence of crisis → war and occupation → attrition and Palestinian political realignment → renewed war → negotiated force separation and bilateral peace. Students should resist reading later outcomes backward into earlier decisions. Sadat did not know in 1973 the detailed terms of the treaty signed in 1979; nor did Israeli and Arab leaders in May 1967 know what territorial or political order would emerge six days later.

Division I — The Crisis and the June 1967 War

Division I · Section 66.1

The Road to War: Border Tensions and the May–June 1967 Crisis

The June 1967 war began in a landscape already shaped by the 1948 Arab–Israeli war and the 1956 Suez Crisis. Armistice agreements had stopped fighting without establishing mutually accepted political borders or resolving the displacement of Palestinian refugees. The frontier between Syria and Israel was especially unstable. Disputes over demilitarized zones, agricultural cultivation, and the diversion of Jordan River headwaters were compounded by exchanges of fire and Israeli reprisals. Armed Palestinian groups, some operating from Syrian, Jordanian, or Lebanese territory, undertook raids that Israel treated as attacks for which neighboring governments bore responsibility. Syria's Ba'athist leadership had its own internal rivalries and reasons to use revolutionary language against Israel. Yet Syrian policy was not uniform: officers disputed both the risks of confrontation and the ability of Egypt to support them.

In November 1966 an Israeli raid on the Jordanian village of Samu, following a land-mine incident, caused extensive damage and casualties. The operation embarrassed King Hussein, who was already under criticism for failing to defend Palestinians in the West Bank. An Israeli–Syrian air battle on 7 April 1967, in which Syrian aircraft were shot down, increased the pressure on Egypt's president, Gamal Abdel Nasser, to demonstrate Arab solidarity. Public threats, broadcasts, and newspaper rhetoric became part of the crisis. A state might have intended a speech to deter an adversary but found that it raised expectations among domestic audiences and allies. These escalating commitments were particularly dangerous because Egyptian and Syrian leaders possessed incomplete information about Israeli planning, just as Israeli officials lacked a clear picture of Arab intentions.

On 13 May the Soviet Union conveyed to Egypt and Syria a warning that Israeli troops were concentrating to attack Syria. The reported concentration was not borne out by the evidence subsequently examined. Soviet motives remain debated: the warning might have reflected faulty intelligence, an attempt to deter Israel, or political calculations within the Soviet alliance. Nasser responded by moving Egyptian units into Sinai. On 16 May Egyptian authorities requested the withdrawal of the United Nations Emergency Force, stationed on Egyptian territory following the 1956 crisis. UN Secretary-General U Thant concluded that he could not leave part of the force in place without Egyptian consent; the withdrawal removed a buffer that had helped reduce border incidents. The UN force was not an independent army capable of imposing its continued presence on the host state.

Egypt announced the closure of the Straits of Tiran to Israeli shipping and strategic cargoes bound for Eilat on 22–23 May. Israel considered freedom of passage a vital interest and had previously warned that renewed closure would be treated as a cause for war. International maritime law, the precise status of the Gulf of Aqaba, and the legitimacy of the Egyptian action became matters of international debate. Within Israel, the government of Prime Minister Levi Eshkol confronted public anxiety, mobilization costs, and pressure from senior military officers who argued that waiting would forfeit the advantage of surprise. Israeli assessments of Arab military capability did not support every claim of an imminent coordinated invasion, but leaders disagreed about how long the dangerous military concentration could be tolerated.

Jordan's position illustrates the regional dilemma. Hussein had sought to avoid an uncontrolled conflict yet faced popular calls for unity with Egypt and anger about Israeli raids. On 30 May he signed a mutual defense pact with Nasser and placed Jordanian forces under a joint-command arrangement. Iraq also entered military coordination discussions. Cairo's radio announced sweeping declarations of confidence, which other leaders could not simply ignore without risking loss of legitimacy. Arab states were not, however, a unified operational coalition. Their armed forces differed in training, logistics, communications, command arrangements, and objectives; neither diplomatic rhetoric nor formal agreements resolved these limits.

President Lyndon Johnson's administration considered proposals for an international maritime response and urged restraint, but it did not secure agreement on a plan that could reopen the straits. United Nations efforts likewise failed to produce a formula accepted by Egypt and Israel. Israel established a national-unity government, including Moshe Dayan as defense minister, shortly before the fighting. On 5 June Israeli aircraft struck Egyptian air bases in an operation planned for speed and surprise. Historians differ in assigning responsibility for the failure of diplomacy, and contemporary officials' memoirs often defend their own choices. What the evidence establishes is a chain of contingent decisions: border escalation, a misleading warning, military deployments, removal of peacekeepers, maritime closure, failed mediation, and an Israeli decision to initiate hostilities. The outcome was not inevitable, even though each decision narrowed the available alternatives.

Historical Debate

Historical Debate: Responsibility for the Outbreak of War in 1967

Interpretation A — Escalation by Egypt and its allies: The troop deployments, withdrawal of UNEF, closing of the Straits of Tiran, and expansion of defense agreements created an acute threat. Supporters stress Israeli security fears and the region’s record of border attacks.

Interpretation B — Israeli choice amid a manageable crisis: Israel launched the opening air attacks, its leaders had differing assessments of an imminent Arab offensive, and some diplomatic alternatives remained possible. Supporters emphasize the distinction between threatening public rhetoric and actual capability.

Interpretation C — Escalatory interaction and misperception: Misleading intelligence, earlier Israeli–Syrian clashes, prestige politics, alliance pressures, and failed mediation narrowed options through decisions on multiple sides.

Research task: Use the U.S. State Department’s contemporary documentary series, Israeli and Egyptian contemporaneous records where available, and UN debates. For each interpretation, separate evidence available before 5 June from retrospective rationalizations. A balanced account should evaluate the evidence, not imply that every explanation is equally persuasive.

Division I · Section 66.2

The Egyptian Front: Air Power, Sinai, and the Collapse of Egyptian Defenses

Israel's opening assault on 5 June 1967 was designed to destroy Egyptian combat aircraft before they could be brought to bear against Israeli cities and ground units. Israeli pilots flew at low altitude toward multiple bases and struck runways and parked aircraft in closely timed waves. The operation, commonly known as Focus, achieved a degree of tactical surprise that left much of Egypt's air force unusable in the first hours. Loss figures vary with whether destroyed, damaged, or subsequently captured aircraft are included, but the essential operational fact is uncontested: Egypt was deprived of the air cover on which a forward defense of Sinai depended. Israel later attacked the air forces of other belligerents, reinforcing its regional air superiority.

The Egyptian army in Sinai was numerically significant but poorly positioned for a rapid, coordinated defense. Its formations included infantry, armor, and artillery; some personnel had experience in Yemen, but the deployment stretched lines of supply and command. Egyptian strategy had shifted under the pressures of May, and commanders struggled to reconcile orders for defense, possible offensive action, and political demonstrations of resolve. The army's nominal strength concealed shortcomings in training, maintenance, communications, and coordination. The sudden loss of air power magnified those weaknesses, enabling Israeli aircraft to strike transport routes and exposed formations while Israeli ground units maneuvered across the peninsula.

Israeli commanders attacked along several axes through northern and central Sinai, including approaches toward Rafah, al-Arish, Abu Ageila–Umm Qatef, and the central passes. The battle at Abu Ageila illustrated the value of combined operations: Israeli forces coordinated armor, infantry, artillery, engineers, and airborne elements to break into elaborate Egyptian defensive positions. Local Egyptian units sometimes fought stubbornly; the speed of the overall defeat should not be mistaken for an absence of resistance. But a field commander who lost contact with headquarters could not easily judge whether neighboring formations were holding. Once Egyptian units began to withdraw, the same desert roads needed for retreat became vulnerable to air attack and congestion.

Egypt's high command, dominated by Field Marshal Abdel Hakim Amer, issued an increasingly general withdrawal order. Poorly transmitted instructions produced confusion over whether units should disengage in sequence or retreat immediately. Men abandoned equipment that could not be transported, while some retreating soldiers died from exhaustion, thirst, air strikes, or capture. Retrospective accounts disagree about the relative weight of operational defeat and the disastrous retreat order. Both mattered: Israeli air and ground performance made continued defense extremely difficult, but command breakdown turned a military reversal into a larger rout. The Sinai road network, distances, and water supply were decisive material conditions, not merely background scenery.

The Gaza Strip, under Egyptian administration since 1948, also came under Israeli control during the war. Gaza's civilian population included residents whose families had lived there for generations and large numbers of Palestinian refugees from the 1948 war. Its capture therefore had consequences unlike those of a largely desert military position. Near the Suez Canal, Israeli forces reached the eastern bank and confronted an Egyptian army now regrouping to the west. The canal became a heavily militarized dividing line. Israel's seizure of Sinai placed the strategic approaches to the Gulf of Aqaba under its control and reversed the frontier arrangement that had followed Suez in 1956.

Egypt's official news reports initially concealed the scale of defeat, in some cases claiming successes that had not occurred. This mattered politically: citizens who were told that the war was going well experienced the later acknowledgment of defeat as a profound breach of trust. Nasser's prestige had rested partly on the claim that post-1952 Egypt could defend Arab dignity and resist external coercion. The war exposed the gap between that aspiration and the actual performance of Egyptian institutions. It also challenged the assumption that quantity of equipment or the size of an army measured effective military power. Air-defense readiness, command discipline, accurate intelligence, logistical resilience, and the capacity to adapt under surprise proved more important than paper totals.

The consequences were not confined to soldiers. Canal-side communities faced bombardment and later evacuation; families waited for news of missing men; refugees and civilian workers moved into unfamiliar administrative systems. Egyptians debated whether defeat reflected individual mistakes, the concentration of power, political favoritism in military promotions, or broader institutional flaws. These explanations were not mutually exclusive. In subsequent years Egypt rebuilt the army and air defenses with a much clearer understanding that restoring the ability to fight Israel required reforms in command, training, and strategy as well as new weapons. Those lessons shaped the War of Attrition and the limited objectives chosen in October 1973.

Historical Vocabulary

Vocabulary: Armistice, Occupation, and Sovereignty

Armistice: An agreement to suspend or regulate hostilities, normally without resolving every territorial or political question. The 1949 agreements established lines that did not necessarily constitute recognized international boundaries.

Military occupation: Effective control of territory by a hostile army without a transfer of sovereign title. Occupation law regulates the conduct of the occupying authority and protection of civilians.

Sovereignty: The legally recognized authority of a state over territory and governmental affairs; it must be distinguished from temporary military control. Annexation is a claim to incorporate territory and can be rejected by other governments.

Student task: Explain why an army’s ability to control a city does not itself answer who possesses lawful sovereignty. Compare East Jerusalem after 1967 with Sinai before 1979.

Division I · Section 66.3

Jordan, the West Bank, and East Jerusalem

Jordan joined the June 1967 conflict in an exceptionally difficult political position. King Hussein governed both the East Bank of the Jordan River and the West Bank, which Jordan had annexed in 1950 in a move that lacked broad international recognition. A large Palestinian population lived under Jordanian citizenship, while many refugees remained in camps and towns. Hussein faced military uncertainty, opposition from Arab nationalist critics, and public expectations that he would not stand aside while Egypt and Syria confronted Israel. The defense pact with Egypt on 30 May committed Jordan to coordination without overcoming the practical limits of shared command or communications.

After Israel's attacks on Egypt began, misleading reports from Cairo suggested that Egyptian forces were enjoying major successes. Jordanian forces opened fire, including artillery fire in the Jerusalem area, and Israeli forces moved against Jordanian positions. Jerusalem's geography made a limited battle difficult: densely populated neighborhoods, religious buildings, and narrow routes through the hills lay close to military lines. Israeli units fought Jordan's Arab Legion for access to strategic hills and the Old City. The battle for Ammunition Hill became especially costly for the opposing soldiers, and Israeli paratroopers entered the Old City on 7 June. Jordanian troops had substantial battlefield experience, but the loss of air cover, pressure at multiple points, and the widening collapse of neighboring fronts undermined their position.

Beyond Jerusalem, Israeli forces advanced into the West Bank and occupied major towns including Ramallah, Bethlehem, Hebron, Nablus, and Jericho. The Jordanian army withdrew across the river as the front disintegrated. The Israeli government's decision to capture and retain the territory had immediate consequences for Palestinians who had little control over the escalation between states. Residents suddenly encountered military orders, checkpoints, new officials, and disruptions to employment and education. Some crossed eastward for safety, while others fled because of fighting, fear, or reports from neighboring communities. The distinction between voluntary departure under danger and forced expulsion is not always recoverable from an individual source; both occurred in the broader history of the war and its immediate aftermath.

For East Jerusalem the consequences were distinct. Israel expanded Jerusalem's municipal boundaries and applied Israeli law and administration to the eastern part of the city after the war. Israeli officials characterized these changes as reunification of the city; the United Nations and most governments did not recognize acquisition of sovereignty by force. The Old City's Jewish Quarter, sites sacred to Jews, Muslims, and Christians, and the surrounding Palestinian neighborhoods became focal points of competing histories and claims. The demolition of the Moroccan Quarter near the Western Wall shortly after the fighting, which displaced its residents, showed how military victory could quickly be translated into changes in urban space. The city's religious significance made local administrative decisions matters of international importance.

Jordan lost territorial control of East Jerusalem in June 1967; it did **not** thereby lose all connection with the city's Muslim holy places. A Jordanian Islamic waqf continued to exercise administrative functions at the Haram al-Sharif/Temple Mount, and the Hashemite custodial role continued to be asserted and later was acknowledged in Article 9 of the 1994 Jordan–Israel peace treaty. Custodianship, day-to-day religious administration, security control, and sovereignty are different legal and political questions. Treating them as one issue obscures why Jerusalem remained contested after the military campaign ended.

King Hussein now governed a state with a changed relationship to Palestinian politics. The West Bank's loss deprived Jordan of territory, resources, and its direct administrative presence west of the river, while many Palestinians displaced in 1967 moved into the East Bank. Jordanian officials still claimed responsibility for West Bank inhabitants and sought avenues for political recovery, but newly influential Palestinian organizations questioned whether Amman should be the principal representative of Palestinian national interests. This tension would intensify as the PLO and Fatah gained strength after the war. It must be distinguished from the experiences of ordinary Palestinian families, many of whom maintained economic, social, and kinship ties across the river even as political institutions divided them.

The events of June demonstrated that the West Bank was not simply a theater of operations between two armies. It was home to an established society with municipalities, farming villages, schools, religious institutions, merchants, and civic networks. A history of its conquest must therefore examine changes in civilian life and competing claims to political representation, alongside movements of tanks and artillery. The crisis helped transform the Palestinian issue from a refugee question managed largely by neighboring states into a central matter of territorial occupation and national self-determination. The effects reached far beyond the six days of fighting.

Division I · Section 66.4

The Syrian Front, the Golan Heights, and the End of the Six-Day War

The Syrian front had been unstable for years before the June war. The 1949 armistice created demilitarized areas without resolving competing land and water claims. Israeli settlement agriculture, Syrian objections, cross-border fire, and disputes over water diversion repeatedly brought military units into contact. Syrian and Israeli leaders often used the frontier to demonstrate resolve before their own publics. Syria's armed forces were influenced by Ba'athist political struggles, and army commanders had to manage the tension between revolutionary exhortations and the practical problem of defending mountainous ground. On the Golan plateau, higher positions gave Syrian artillery an advantage against communities below, but those positions were not invulnerable to maneuver, concentrated fire, or air attack.

When war began on 5 June, Syrian batteries fired on Israeli positions and settlements and Syrian aircraft took part in the fighting; Israel struck Syrian air bases. The full extent of Syria's intended ground offensive remains debated, as do claims made in wartime broadcasts. For several days Israel concentrated most of its mobile strength on the Egyptian and Jordanian fronts. This sequence is essential. An Israeli decision to mount a ground offensive against the Golan late in the war was not the inevitable execution of a single, unchanged plan dating to 5 June. It came after Israel had achieved decisive successes elsewhere and after political and military leaders debated the dangers of opening a more difficult mountain campaign.

On 9 June Israeli units began assaults against heavily defended slopes and approaches to the plateau. Steep terrain, trenches, minefields, fortified positions, and narrow routes made armored movement dangerous. Soldiers fought for individual heights and road junctions under fire. Israeli air superiority helped isolate Syrian defensive positions, while the breakdown of coordination within Syrian command made it hard to reinforce points under attack. Some Syrian units resisted fiercely; others withdrew as the political and military picture deteriorated. Israeli forces reached deeper into the plateau on 10 June, taking Quneitra, and a ceasefire brought large-scale fighting to an end that day.

The capture of the Golan involved more than a change on a military map. Many Syrian civilians left or were displaced, including inhabitants of Quneitra and numerous villages. A small number of Syrian Druze communities remained on the occupied plateau, creating a particular history of residence, political identity, and later resistance. Estimates of displacement and abandoned settlements depend on date and method: contemporary figures, subsequent demographic studies, and government claims do not always define the same population. A rigorous account should therefore identify the source of any precise number rather than combine unrelated totals. Agriculture, grazing, water access, and property rights all changed under new control.

The ceasefire sequence across the various fronts was staggered, rather than a single peace agreement. Military hostilities ended in stages under Security Council pressure, but no diplomatic settlement accompanied the guns' silence. Egypt lost Sinai and Gaza; Jordan lost the West Bank and East Jerusalem; Syria lost the Golan Heights. Israel gained possession of territories several times the size of the state before the war, and its military demonstrated capacity for rapid regional operations. But occupation introduced long-term security, demographic, legal, and moral questions that could not be resolved by victory in a short campaign. Palestinians and Syrians faced changed circumstances without having negotiated the results.

The conflict's official names reflect divergent experiences. Israeli narratives often emphasize the Six-Day War and the vulnerability that preceded it. Egyptian, Syrian, and Palestinian narratives commonly refer to the June defeat or *naksa*, the setback. Neither terminology is an analytical substitute for evidence. The same war produced relief and exhilaration for many Israelis, humiliation and mourning for Arab armies and populations, and a new reality of military rule for people in occupied territory. Serious historical writing must keep those experiences visible while distinguishing them from policy claims made by governments.

The suddenness of the June defeat also generated several different political lessons. Some Arab leaders sought faster rearmament, some pursued diplomatic recovery of territory, and Palestinian movements increasingly argued that their national future could not be entrusted entirely to neighboring armies. Israeli leaders disagreed over whether territory should be retained, negotiated away, settled, or used as leverage for recognition and peace. Those debates opened the longer history examined in the remaining divisions. The outcome of six days was a transformed regional order, not a completed resolution of the Arab–Israeli conflict.

Division II — Occupation, Displacement, and Political Consequences

Division II · Section 66.5

The New Territorial Order: Sinai, Gaza, the West Bank, East Jerusalem, and the Golan

The war of June 1967 placed Sinai, Gaza, the West Bank, East Jerusalem, and the Golan Heights under Israeli control, but these territories did not possess the same prewar political status or thereafter receive identical treatment. Sinai had been sovereign Egyptian territory, whereas Gaza had been administered by Egypt without incorporation into the Egyptian state. Jordan had governed the West Bank since 1948 and formally annexed it in 1950, an annexation recognized by few foreign governments. East Jerusalem had been under Jordanian control. The Golan was Syrian territory. These differences influenced both the legal arguments and the practical institutions of occupation. The popular phrase “territories occupied in 1967” describes their military capture but should not erase their distinct histories.

Military occupation is a factual condition in which an army exercises effective control over territory beyond its state's borders; it does not, by itself, transfer sovereignty. The Hague Regulations of 1907 and the Fourth Geneva Convention of 1949 supplied major legal frameworks for occupation and the protection of civilians. Israel disputed some aspects of how the Geneva Convention applied to the West Bank and Gaza, while maintaining that it respected humanitarian provisions. The International Committee of the Red Cross, United Nations bodies, and most states treated the territories as occupied and the Convention as applicable. Rather than assuming that a legal label settled every practical controversy, historians should examine the actual policies of land control, military courts, civilian administration, property regulation, and movement.

After the war Israel established military administration across most of the occupied territories, with officers exercising extensive authority over security, permits, local government, and some aspects of economic life. Existing Jordanian or Egyptian administrative arrangements did not simply disappear overnight; officials, teachers, judges, and municipal workers often continued under changed authority. In the West Bank farmers depended on access to orchards, wells, markets, and kinship networks across newly controlled boundaries. In Gaza, an already densely populated coastal strip faced heightened problems of jobs, travel, and refugee welfare. In Sinai, strategic military installations, transportation routes, and the canal approached greater importance than large civilian settlements. These differences mattered for economic policy as well as for the possibilities of eventual diplomatic settlement.

East Jerusalem followed a particularly consequential course. Israel expanded the city's municipal boundaries, extended Israeli law and administration to the enlarged area, and treated the city as its unified capital. The international community generally rejected the territorial effects of these measures; Security Council resolutions criticized steps intended to alter Jerusalem's status. Palestinian Jerusalemites faced new residency and municipal systems, while religious institutions confronted challenges over access and authority. Israeli authorities retained an important role for the Muslim waqf in administration of the Haram al-Sharif, even as Israel exercised security control. The religious importance of Jerusalem to Jews, Muslims, and Christians made ostensibly technical urban decisions—roads, building permits, property transfers—objects of political controversy.

Jewish settlement in captured territories began within years of the war and reflected different motivations. Some Israelis advocated security buffers in the Jordan Valley, others sought restoration of religious or historic sites, and still others pursued ideological claims to the West Bank, often called Judea and Samaria by Israelis. Settlement activity also began on the Golan and in Sinai. Israeli governments varied in their priorities and willingness to authorize projects, and the movement expanded over time. International legal criticism centered partly on the Fourth Geneva Convention's prohibition against transfer of an occupying power's civilian population into occupied territory; Israeli governments contested the legal interpretation and advanced historical and security claims. The disagreement cannot be resolved by simply reproducing one government's terminology.

The territorial question also reshaped Arab diplomatic strategy. Egypt primarily sought recovery of Sinai; Syria insisted on restoration of the Golan; Jordan pursued the West Bank; Palestinian organizations increasingly claimed political representation and self-determination for a people spread across several jurisdictions. These goals overlapped but were not identical. In Israel, some leaders hoped occupied territory could be exchanged for formal peace and recognized borders, while others were reluctant to relinquish strategic positions or areas considered historically important. The divergent interests help explain why a military victory did not produce a single negotiable territorial package. The occupation generated new institutions and communities of interest that made later agreements more complicated.

Division II · Section 66.6

Refugees, Displacement, and Civilian Life after the War

For civilians, the six-day war was often experienced as a sequence of alarms, hurried departures, closed roads, and uncertain news rather than a complete military campaign. Families in the West Bank and Gaza heard reports of shelling and advancing troops without reliable information about where relatives were. Villages on the Golan emptied under pressure from combat and fear. Workers who had traveled for employment discovered that new military lines separated them from their homes. Teachers and physicians sought to reopen schools and clinics with damaged supplies and unfamiliar administrative rules. War-related displacement was not a single event with an agreed count: people moved during fighting, after the ceasefire, or in response to subsequent restrictions, and some moved more than once.

UNRWA's 1967 Commissioner-General report, submitted as General Assembly document A/6713, stated that more than 300,000 people had become homeless or left their homes because of the hostilities, including approximately 120,000 people previously registered with the agency as Palestine refugees. This was a contemporary relief assessment, not a universal census covering every Syrian, Egyptian, or Palestinian displacement. Its inclusion of already registered refugees reminds readers that some Palestinian families uprooted in 1948 experienced a second displacement in 1967. These categories must not be added to other figures without checking definitions and time periods. A family recorded as leaving a West Bank camp might already have been counted as a refugee in agency registers.

Many Palestinians crossed the Jordan River into Jordan, where relatives, municipalities, charities, government agencies, and UNRWA attempted to provide shelter. Schools and other public buildings became emergency accommodation; tents and new camps were established or enlarged. Those who remained in the West Bank faced military administration and a new set of conditions for employment and travel. Some displaced people hoped to return immediately. Arrangements for return were contested and often restricted; UN agencies, the International Committee of the Red Cross, Israel, and Jordan dealt with questions of security, registration, and responsibility. Historians should distinguish people who crossed the river in fear and subsequently returned from those who were prevented from reentering. Individual case files may reveal choices and constraints obscured by aggregated statistics.

The Golan produced a related but different history. Syrian civilians fled or were displaced toward Damascus, Daraa, and other places. UNRWA's report noted large numbers departing the occupied Syrian area, though it had a particular mandate to assist registered Palestine refugees among them. Towns, farms, and villages left behind became the subject of competing reports about destruction, access, and eventual disposition. A smaller number of Druze inhabitants remained in northern communities and lived under Israeli control. Their continuing connection to families and communities on the Syrian side of the ceasefire line became a distinctive feature of the region's subsequent social history.

Civilian harm extended beyond visible refugee columns. Canal-side Egyptians experienced bombardment and evacuation over the following years; fishermen and merchants lost access to normal routes; agricultural production suffered where fields became mined or militarized. Palestinian families faced restrictions on movement across Jerusalem and the Jordan Valley, and uncertainty over relatives who had gone to work abroad. Children sometimes changed schools repeatedly, while parents relied on letters, remittances, religious charities, and informal transport networks to remain connected. Such experiences can be reconstructed from oral histories and family papers, but testimonies are memories shaped by later experience. They are strongest when compared with dated relief and administrative records rather than treated as transparent transcripts of a collective experience.

Displacement also had political consequences. Refugee camps became places not only of hardship but also of education, political argument, organization, and debate over the right of return. New generations rejected the assumption that neighboring governments should decide their national future. Host states faced a humanitarian obligation while worrying that armed groups operating from their territory could draw retaliation or challenge sovereign authority. International donors frequently approached refugee assistance as a technical problem of food and shelter, while Palestinian representatives insisted that humanitarian relief alone could not substitute for political rights. The unresolved question of return, restitution, or compensation would remain central long after the immediate emergency ended.

Division II · Section 66.7

Khartoum, Resolution 242, and the Search for a Settlement

The Arab summit at Khartoum, held from 29 August to 1 September 1967, attempted to restore diplomatic coordination after an overwhelming military defeat. Its declaration became famous for rejecting peace with Israel, recognition of Israel, and direct negotiations with Israel. The “three noes” were real, but they were not the summit's entire policy. The participants also emphasized collective diplomatic action to secure Israeli withdrawal, support for frontline states, and the need to coordinate Arab resources. Saudi Arabia and other oil-producing governments agreed to provide financial assistance to Egypt and Jordan, illustrating how inter-Arab diplomacy could continue across differences between monarchies and revolutionary republics. The resolution should be read as a collective statement negotiated among governments with distinct priorities.

Egypt and Jordan faced immediate pressures to recover territory and rebuild armed forces; Syria's ruling faction placed greater emphasis on uncompromising confrontation. Saudi Arabia hoped to preserve Arab solidarity while limiting the political spread of Nasserism. Palestinian leaders and organizations increasingly criticized what they regarded as the failure of Arab armies to secure Palestinian rights. These variations mean that the slogan “no negotiations” did not necessarily foreclose indirect contacts through the United Nations or foreign diplomats. Public declarations had domestic purposes: leaders could not easily admit the depth of military defeat while announcing concessions that their populations might interpret as surrender. Diplomacy therefore developed through language that could sustain different political audiences.

During the summer and autumn the United States, Soviet Union, Britain, and other powers disputed how to formulate a Security Council resolution. The British-sponsored text adopted unanimously on 22 November 1967 became Resolution 242. Its preamble emphasized the inadmissibility of acquiring territory by war. Its operative principles connected withdrawal of Israeli forces from territories occupied in the recent conflict with termination of belligerency and respect for the sovereignty and security of every state in the area within recognized boundaries. The resolution also addressed freedom of navigation, a just settlement of the refugee problem, and measures such as demilitarized zones. Importantly, it was conceived as the basis for a negotiated peace, not as a self-executing agreement specifying every boundary or implementation timetable.

The wording became controversial. The English text called for withdrawal “from territories occupied in the recent conflict,” while the French formulation and the preamble were invoked in debates over the extent of required withdrawal. Arab governments typically emphasized complete withdrawal and inadmissibility of acquiring territory through war. Israeli governments emphasized the absence of the definite article in the English withdrawal clause and the accompanying demand for secure and recognized borders. Drafting records show that sponsors resisted additions and omissions that might undo a negotiated compromise. Historians must distinguish the resolution's actual words, the intentions attributed to its drafters, competing state interpretations, and what later governments accepted or rejected. The controversy was broader than the presence of a single English word.

Another limitation concerned Palestinians. The resolution referred to the “refugee problem” but did not explicitly name Palestinian national self-determination or designate a Palestinian negotiating representative. That language reflected the diplomacy of states in 1967, when many governments still framed Palestinian rights primarily through refugees and the competing claims of neighboring states. Palestinian national movements later argued that the framework could not adequately address their aspirations if it treated them only as refugees rather than as a people with political rights. Israel, Egypt, Jordan, Syria, and the PLO would consequently approach Resolution 242 from different constitutional, territorial, and representational positions.

The United Nations appointed Gunnar Jarring as special representative to explore a settlement. His mission faced a recurring structural problem: Egypt and Jordan wished to know what withdrawal meant before offering final political commitments; Israel wanted credible recognition and security arrangements before agreeing to withdrawal; Syria's approach was more resistant; Palestinians disputed who spoke for them. The superpowers supplied arms and diplomatic pressure but did not command all their regional partners. Resolution 242 survived precisely because it provided a common point of reference while leaving major questions unsettled. It became the starting document for later initiatives, including Resolution 338 after the October 1973 war, but its adoption should not be mistaken for peace.

Political Leadership after Defeat: Egypt, Syria, Jordan, and Israel

Nasser's televised address on 9 June 1967 acknowledged the defeat and announced his decision to resign. Demonstrations calling for him to stay in office followed, and he withdrew the resignation. The reaction is often presented as proof of unbroken personal charisma, but it also reflected fear of political chaos, loyalty to earlier achievements, and the absence of a trusted civilian alternative. The regime still controlled broadcasting and much of organized politics. Egyptians who wanted national recovery did not necessarily endorse every institution that had led them into defeat. The distinction between public affection for Nasser and confidence in the military and administrative apparatus became increasingly important.

The postwar Egyptian government investigated failures within the armed forces. Field Marshal Abdel Hakim Amer, whose influence had grown alongside Nasser's, lost his position and died in September 1967 under disputed circumstances reported officially as suicide. The restructuring of the high command exposed the dangers of personal loyalty networks, fragmented command, and political privilege within the military. New commanders, including Muhammad Fawzi and later Saad el-Shazly, sought more professional training and defensive planning. Nasser also had to address the social burden of mobilization, displaced canal residents, and the fiscal strain of rearmament. Recovery was therefore a state-building effort, not simply a matter of buying new aircraft.

Syria's leadership responded through a struggle inside the Ba'athist government. President Nur al-Din al-Atasi, Salah Jadid, and Defense Minister Hafez al-Assad belonged to a political system already shaped by coups and ideological factionalism. The Golan's loss intensified accusations about military performance and whether revolutionary slogans had encouraged reckless expectations. Assad emphasized military rebuilding and state discipline, whereas rival factions attached more importance to revolutionary mobilization and ideological purity. These differences contributed to the divisions that culminated in Assad's seizure of power in 1970, examined as a domestic political development in Chapter 64. The June war was an important catalyst, but it was not the sole cause of that takeover.

King Hussein's problems were of another kind. Jordan had lost the West Bank, including East Jerusalem, and faced the arrival of people displaced by fighting. The monarchy depended on support from the army, East Bank communities, and many Palestinian citizens, yet politically organized Palestinians increasingly demanded independent national representation. The distinction between Jordanian citizenship and Palestinian national identity became sharper. Hussein sought diplomatic avenues to recover territory, sometimes through channels criticized by Arab rivals, while fearing that armed organizations operating inside Jordan might provoke further Israeli attacks or undermine domestic sovereignty. The crisis of Palestinian guerrilla activity in Jordan developed out of these tensions but cannot be reduced to the king's personal predicament.

Israeli leaders regarded the victory as both a security achievement and an unexpected strategic dilemma. The coalition government contained politicians who differed over the future of Sinai, the West Bank, Gaza, East Jerusalem, and the Golan. Some supported territorial bargaining for peace; others advanced historical, religious, or strategic arguments for retention. The Defense Ministry and military command faced the immediate task of governing large civilian populations, while Jewish and Palestinian groups developed rival ideas of the land's future. Israel's decision to apply its law and administration to expanded East Jerusalem stood apart from the military administration elsewhere. The divergence illustrates why occupation soon became a political project subject to competing domestic pressures.

The defeat altered the intellectual climate of Arab politics. Writers, students, and religious thinkers asked whether institutions had been corrupted by authoritarianism, whether rhetoric had displaced preparation, and whether the failure belonged to Arab socialism, nationalism, or the way individual regimes implemented those doctrines. Some sought democratic reform or more effective military organization. Others promoted Islamic revival or revolutionary armed struggle. No single “Arab reaction” can be reconstructed from these debates. The significance of 1967 lies partly in multiplying competing explanations for a shared shock, and in shifting authority toward new actors, especially Palestinians seeking to organize under their own leadership. The political consequences developed over years rather than appearing complete on 11 June.

Division III — The Years between Wars, 1967–1973

Division III · Section 66.9

The War of Attrition and the Struggle along the Suez Canal, 1968–1970

The June war ended with Israeli forces on the eastern bank of the Suez Canal and Egyptian forces on the western bank. For Egyptians the canal was both a crucial waterway and a visible line of national humiliation; for Israeli commanders the wide water barrier offered a defensible frontier. Neither side regarded the ceasefire line as a permanent international border. Egypt could not dislodge the Israeli army by immediate conventional attack, while Israel could not compel a satisfactory peace merely by occupying territory. The resulting confrontation developed through artillery exchanges and raids in 1967–1968 and expanded into sustained attrition in 1969. Its aim was not necessarily rapid conquest but to impose military and political costs on an opponent over time.

Egyptian forces rebuilt artillery, special operations units, and positions west of the canal. Their attacks sought to demonstrate that occupation of Sinai would not be cost-free and to damage Israeli personnel and fortifications. Israel responded with artillery, raids, and increasingly powerful air strikes, including operations against installations deeper in Egypt. The fortified Bar-Lev Line along the canal's eastern bank represented Israel's effort to make its forward positions defensible. Egyptian artillery sometimes inflicted losses on exposed positions, but Israeli air superiority constrained movement and preparation on the western bank. This unequal balance produced a search for ways to deny Israeli aircraft freedom over the battlefield.

The Soviet Union supplied Egypt with replacement aircraft, surface-to-air missiles, radar equipment, advisers, and eventually personnel who assisted in air defense operations. Egyptian officers sought to integrate these systems into a network rather than depend on isolated batteries. Israel tried to prevent new defenses from becoming operational, and Soviet involvement raised the possibility that local fighting could produce direct superpower confrontation. The exact boundaries between Egyptian and Soviet control of individual units varied and developed over time. Treating all Soviet-supplied equipment as Soviet-operated would exaggerate external control; ignoring Soviet specialists and pilots would underestimate how internationalized the confrontation had become.

The war also disrupted civilian society. Canal cities such as Port Said, Ismailia, and Suez faced attacks and large-scale evacuation. Families were relocated to interior towns where housing, schools, and jobs were not always adequate. Military maps often display the canal as a thin strategic line, but it was also a region of neighborhoods, farms, factories, docks, and families. Israeli settlements and civilian communities near other borders similarly experienced insecurity and alarm. Memorial literature in both countries tended to honor soldiers and sacrifice, while administrative records reveal the less visible burden of displaced workers, interrupted schooling, and reconstruction expenses.

Political objectives were difficult to realize through attrition alone. Egypt wished to show resistance and improve its bargaining position, but Nasser could not easily force Israel into comprehensive withdrawal. Israel sought deterrence and hoped to demonstrate the futility of continued fighting, yet retaliation did not eliminate Egyptian political determination. The Nixon administration, with Secretary of State William Rogers taking a more active diplomatic role, proposed a ceasefire linked to a renewal of negotiations through UN mediator Gunnar Jarring. An earlier Rogers formula for an overall settlement had failed; the 1970 initiative was narrower and designed first to stop the shooting.

Egypt and Israel accepted a ceasefire effective 7 August 1970, commonly described as beginning on 8 August in local reporting. Disputes immediately arose over the movement of Egyptian air-defense missiles into areas close to the canal during the standstill. Israel regarded the alleged deployments as violations; Egypt and the Soviet Union disputed or qualified aspects of the claims. The ceasefire nonetheless largely ended the War of Attrition's intense fighting along the canal. It did not reopen the waterway, restore Sinai, or settle the Palestinian problem. The lessons were asymmetric: Israel learned the costs of holding a static line under sustained fire, while Egypt learned that integrated air defense might enable a future limited crossing. Both drew conclusions that shaped their conduct in 1973.

Palestinian Armed Movements, the PLO, and Black September

The defeat of 1967 profoundly changed Palestinian political organization. The Palestine Liberation Organization had been established in 1964 with important support from Arab governments, particularly Egypt, but its relationship to the people it claimed to represent was contested. Fatah, associated with Yasser Arafat and other organizers, developed independently and argued that Palestinians should conduct their own national struggle. After the Arab armies' defeat, Fatah and other armed organizations acquired supporters who no longer trusted neighboring states to secure Palestinian rights. Political mobilization extended across refugee camps, universities, labor networks, and the diaspora, creating an arena of debate as well as armed activity.

In March 1968 Israeli forces attacked the Jordanian town of Karameh to strike Palestinian bases after cross-border operations. Jordanian troops and Palestinian fighters resisted; Israel withdrew after inflicting substantial damage and suffering losses. Palestinian organizations celebrated the episode as a victory, even though its military outcome cannot be understood without the role of Jordanian forces. Karameh mattered especially as a symbol. It challenged the idea that the Palestinian movement was powerless after 1967 and helped expand Fatah's recruiting and public prestige. In 1969 Arafat became chairman of the PLO Executive Committee, marking a shift toward greater influence by guerrilla organizations within the institution.

The movement was ideologically diverse. Fatah emphasized Palestinian national liberation and often avoided a rigid program of social revolution. The Popular Front for the Liberation of Palestine, associated with George Habash, adopted Marxist-influenced revolutionary language; the Democratic Front represented a distinct leftist current. Leaders differed over relations with Arab governments, the role of armed struggle, diplomacy, and possible political settlements. Tactics that included airline hijackings and attacks on civilians generated international publicity but also harmed innocents and created major disputes within and beyond the movement. Historians should distinguish advocacy of national rights from endorsement of particular forms of violence, and distinguish the decisions of armed factions from the views of Palestinians as a whole.

Jordan became a central arena of conflict. Palestinian armed organizations established bases and armed presence in parts of the country. Some operated with considerable independence, set up checkpoints, and challenged government institutions. Israel retaliated against raids launched from Jordanian territory, exposing Jordanian civilians and security forces to consequences they had not chosen. King Hussein feared that his authority and the state's capacity to govern were being undermined, while Palestinian organizations suspected that the monarchy would limit their struggle or bargain away political claims. Social relations between Palestinians and East Bank Jordanians were far more complex than a simple division into two hostile national groups; many families, soldiers, and political activists crossed those identities.

Tension became open warfare in September 1970, known as Black September. The crisis was intensified by dramatic aircraft hijackings by the PFLP and negotiations over hostages; Jordanian forces moved against Palestinian armed positions. Syrian armored forces crossed into northern Jordan but withdrew after military and political pressure, while Egypt attempted mediation. Heavy fighting killed combatants and civilians; casualty estimates vary considerably and should be attributed when used. Nasser helped arrange a ceasefire shortly before his death on 28 September. Further fighting in 1971 drove the PLO's principal armed infrastructure out of Jordan. The organization moved much of its operational center toward Lebanon, with consequences for Lebanese politics reserved for later chapters.

The episode revealed the contradiction between Palestinian organizational autonomy and the sovereignty of host states. Palestinians asserted the right to independent political action because their homeland was occupied and their future had been decided by others. Jordanian leaders insisted that armed groups could not maintain rival authority within the kingdom. Neither argument justified indiscriminate violence or made civilian suffering incidental. Black September also demonstrated that the Arab–Israeli conflict could fuel conflict within Arab states, not merely between Israel and neighboring armies. The Palestinian movement thereafter developed a more complex combination of armed action, international diplomacy, institutional representation, and internal disagreement, preparing the political changes of the 1974 Rabat summit.

Historical Vocabulary

Vocabulary: Attrition, Deterrence, and Armed Organization

Attrition: A strategy intended to wear down an opponent through sustained costs, not necessarily through immediate conquest. The canal fighting of 1969–1970 illustrates how the costs of holding a line may become the primary objective.

Deterrence: Efforts to persuade another actor that an attack will bring unacceptable costs. Deterrence is judged by an opponent’s perceptions and choices, not simply by the size of an arsenal.

Fedayeen: A term used for self-sacrificing or irregular fighters, especially in the Palestinian context; its political use requires attention to organization and specific tactics.

PLO/Fatah: The PLO is an umbrella representative institution created in 1964; Fatah became a major constituent movement and acquired strong influence after 1967.

From Nasser to Sadat: Egyptian Strategy and Preparations for Renewed War

Gamal Abdel Nasser died on 28 September 1970, only weeks after the War of Attrition ceasefire. His successor, Anwar al-Sadat, initially appeared to some senior officials to be a compromise figure unlikely to dominate the Egyptian state. Instead, Sadat moved to consolidate authority, notably through the May 1971 struggle against powerful rivals in the political and security apparatus. His government inherited the same strategic problem: Israel controlled Sinai, the Suez Canal remained closed, and diplomatic initiatives had failed to secure withdrawal. Egyptian public expectations of recovery were intense, while another major defeat could threaten the regime. Sadat had to reconcile limited military capabilities with the political imperative to show that continued occupation was not acceptable.

The Egyptian armed forces were not identical to those routed in June 1967. Command reform, retraining, better planning, and experience under artillery fire had improved some formations. Soviet equipment remained important, especially surface-to-air missiles, antitank weapons, aircraft, and tanks. Chief of Staff Saad el-Shazly and other officers emphasized realistic operational planning. Egyptian commanders understood that their air defenses could protect forces operating relatively close to the western bank of the canal but could not readily provide equal coverage deep in Sinai. They therefore developed plans for a deliberate crossing, establishment of bridgeheads, and defense against Israeli counterattacks rather than an immediate drive to recover all the peninsula.

Sadat considered a political opening essential before a war. His proposals and communications to American and international intermediaries offered avenues to a settlement if Israel agreed to withdrawal from occupied territory. Israel's leaders were reluctant to give up strategically valuable positions without a broader settlement they considered credible, and Washington did not force an agreement. Frustration with diplomatic stalemate contributed to Sadat's judgment that a limited military operation might change the assumptions of negotiators. The purpose was not simply to win back territory by force; it was to make the prewar situation unstable enough that the United States and Israel would take Egyptian claims more seriously. Whether this was a coherent original plan or a policy refined through events is debated in memoirs and scholarship.

In July 1972 Sadat announced the departure of many Soviet military advisers and experts. The move asserted independence and signaled dissatisfaction with Moscow's hesitation to supply certain weapons. It did not mean Egypt ceased to receive Soviet arms, that Soviet-trained Egyptian officers disappeared, or that all military connections were severed. Egyptian strategy required weapons and spare parts even as Sadat searched for a different diplomatic relationship with Washington. This illustrates the difference between a public gesture of autonomy and a complete restructuring of military dependence. The Soviet government remained a major actor in Egyptian rearmament before and during the October war.

Egypt coordinated increasingly closely with Syria, whose president Hafez al-Assad sought recovery of the Golan Heights. The partners shared a need to break the stalemate but did not necessarily share the same preferred military endpoint. Egypt planned a limited operation centered on canal bridgeheads; Syria sought more extensive territorial gains on a narrower front. Their planners had to synchronize timing, logistics, and signals without giving Israel early warning. Repeated Egyptian exercises, mobilizations, and demobilizations complicated the task of intelligence analysis because an opponent might interpret genuine preparations as another practice alert. Secrecy within the Egyptian leadership also meant that not all officers were privy to the final political calculations.

For Egyptians, the objective was at once military, psychological, and diplomatic. A successful crossing could reverse the humiliation of 1967 and give negotiators a new premise: Egyptian soldiers were physically present east of the canal and the status quo could no longer be assumed permanent. But the risks were obvious. Bridgeheads dependent on fragile crossing equipment could be isolated if Israeli armor broke through; air defenses had limited range; and an attack might provoke a wider superpower crisis. The preparations culminating in October 1973 were an attempt to match attainable battlefield objectives to broader political goals. Their complexity explains why the war's political results cannot be judged solely by the final position of armies on 25 October.

Division III · Section 66.12

Syria, Israel, the Superpowers, and the Failure of Prewar Diplomacy

The Syrian leadership's objectives were anchored in the loss of the Golan Heights. President Hafez al-Assad regarded the plateau as a strategic and national priority, while his government sought to demonstrate the value of a disciplined, re-equipped Syrian army after the 1967 defeat. Soviet tanks, artillery, antiaircraft weapons, and training helped strengthen Syrian formations, but military modernization also depended on Syrian command structures and operational choices. Geography favored intense fighting: the front was relatively narrow, the terrain varied sharply in elevation, and Israeli settlements and military positions lay near strategically important roads. Syrian planners had reasons to seek a rapid breakthrough before Israeli reserves could mobilize in force.

Israeli leaders assumed that their 1967 territorial gains provided strategic depth and bargaining leverage. The Suez Canal seemed an imposing obstacle to a large Egyptian assault, and Israeli armored doctrine emphasized rapid reserve mobilization and counterattack. Israel's intelligence services monitored Egyptian and Syrian movements, but their assessment relied on an influential assumption often called the “concept”: Egypt was unlikely to initiate major war without capabilities to threaten Israel's air force or strategic rear areas. Because Egyptian planners did not intend an immediate deep offensive, the assumption created a dangerous blind spot. Even as warnings accumulated, analysts tended to interpret mobilization as political pressure or exercises rather than as evidence of a near-term attack.

Political developments encouraged complacency in parts of Israel's leadership. The defense establishment knew Egypt was dissatisfied and Syria was rearming, but Israeli officials also had confidence in the deterrent power of air superiority and the army's ability to reinforce both fronts. Mobilizing reserves too early would be economically costly and diplomatically conspicuous, particularly during a tense period. Israeli leaders also feared accusations that they had initiated another war if they struck first. The decision not to launch a preemptive air offensive in October 1973 was shaped by both military assessments and the desire to preserve international support. Such calculations show that states can possess excellent intelligence resources yet misjudge an adversary's political purpose.

Diplomacy from 1967 to 1973 produced proposals without a settlement. UN envoy Gunnar Jarring explored implementation of Resolution 242, while U.S. Secretary of State William Rogers proposed frameworks tying withdrawal to recognition and peace. Egypt and Israel offered different conditions, and Syrian positions were separately contested. The Nixon administration's internal debates also mattered: Rogers favored active pressure for progress, whereas Henry Kissinger emphasized the strategic advantages of limiting Soviet influence and cultivating Israel. U.S. policy was not fully consistent across agencies or years, and the Soviet Union likewise balanced support for Arab clients against concern about direct confrontation with Washington. The diplomatic impasse cannot be explained simply by claiming that one outside power ordered a partner to refuse peace.

Sadat argued that continued stalemate benefited Israel because occupation remained in place at limited immediate cost. Assad feared that Syria's position would weaken further if Egypt reached a separate understanding while the Golan remained occupied. Both presidents concluded that military action could alter the diplomatic calculus, although they differed on what success would mean. Egypt and Syria prepared a coordinated offensive for 6 October 1973. The timing coincided with the Jewish holy day of Yom Kippur and the Muslim month of Ramadan, conditions that influenced assessments of readiness, domestic activity, and military movements but did not determine tactical success by themselves. The operation required synchronized action across two very different theaters.

In the days before the attack, Israeli intelligence received increasingly serious indicators, including troop movements and last-minute warnings. Some officials pressed for mobilization, while others feared overreaction. The failure lay not in an absolute absence of information but in the interpretation, ranking, and transmission of what was known. In Egypt and Syria, planners weighed surprise against the need to bring large numbers of soldiers and equipment into position. Once hostilities began, Soviet and American officials faced decisions about resupply and crisis management that would internationalize the fighting. The years of failed diplomacy therefore ended in war not because negotiations were impossible in principle, but because participating governments judged the available offers and incentives insufficient to change their positions.

Division IV — The October 1973 Arab–Israeli War

Division IV · Section 66.13

October 6, 1973: Egypt Crosses the Suez Canal

On 6 October 1973 Egyptian and Syrian forces opened a coordinated offensive against Israeli positions in Sinai and the Golan. The day was both Yom Kippur, Judaism's holiest day, and part of the Muslim fasting month of Ramadan. The timing aided surprise, but the operation's effectiveness rested on careful preparation: Egypt had rehearsed canal crossings, assembled engineering units, positioned artillery and antiaircraft missiles, and planned how to move men and equipment through a narrow set of crossing points. Israel was still mobilizing reserves and had not expected hostilities on that scale. The canal, which had seemed to make Israeli positions secure, became the center of a highly organized combined operation.

Egyptian artillery opened a large preparatory bombardment while assault infantry crossed in boats. Engineers used high-pressure water jets to breach sand embankments that Israeli defenses had raised on the eastern bank, allowing vehicles and eventually tanks to pass. Pontoon and other bridges were installed amid shelling, vehicle congestion, and engineering difficulties. Egyptian infantry carried portable antitank missiles and rocket weapons designed to resist Israeli armored counterattacks before Egyptian heavy equipment could cross in strength. Units occupied bridgeheads several kilometers east of the canal, relying on a carefully layered defense rather than immediately racing into the interior of Sinai.

The Bar-Lev Line was a chain of forts and positions, not one continuous impenetrable wall. Some Israeli strongpoints resisted for days; others were isolated, bypassed, or taken. The failure of several early Israeli armored counterattacks, notably fighting on 8 October, reflected the dangers of attacking entrenched infantry supported by antitank weapons without adequate reconnaissance and coordination. The Egyptian attack did not make Israeli tanks obsolete, but it showed that armor operating without suitable infantry, artillery, and air support could suffer severe losses. The Egyptian antiaircraft network likewise limited the freedom with which Israeli aircraft could operate near the canal.

Egyptian command decisions reflected the strategy's carefully defined limits. The surface-to-air missile umbrella did not extend indefinitely eastward, and Egyptian armored units operating too far beyond it would become more vulnerable to Israeli air power. Some Egyptian and Syrian political leaders desired a larger territorial recovery than their staffs could prudently promise; tensions between a limited crossing plan and pressure for greater advance emerged as the campaign developed. The bridgeheads therefore had a dual purpose: they established an Egyptian presence in occupied Sinai and created a fortified position that Israel would have to attack, making continued occupation costly in military and diplomatic terms.

For soldiers the operation involved intense physical labor as well as combat. Crossing engineers worked under time pressure to open passages through sand and minefields; medics attempted to evacuate casualties across congested bridges; communications officers struggled to keep separate infantry and armored units synchronized. Israeli defenders experienced surprise, sometimes isolation, and uncertainty about the scale of the Egyptian advance. Civilians in Egypt followed news of the crossing with intense emotion, while Israeli families awaited mobilized relatives. Subsequent national commemorations often presented the event in triumphant terms. A textbook should preserve the real significance of the crossing without assuming that its initial success determined the result of every later engagement.

The operation transformed the diplomatic equation even before the war ended. Egyptian soldiers had crossed a barrier that Israeli planners had expected to slow or prevent a major assault, and Egypt could now point to a limited but genuine military achievement. Nevertheless, bridges, supplies, and narrow defense zones remained vulnerable to counterattack. The full war would reveal whether Egypt could preserve its gains while Syria faced an intense battle on the Golan, and whether outside powers would intervene to prevent catastrophic losses. The canal crossing was a major operational success and an important political event; it was not by itself the recovery of Sinai.

Division IV · Section 66.14

The Syrian Offensive and the Battle for the Golan Heights

Syria's offensive of 6 October opened along a much shorter front than Egypt's. Syrian units aimed to retake the occupied Golan Heights rapidly, pushing through Israeli forward defenses before reserve forces could arrive. The Syrian army committed large armored and mechanized formations supported by artillery and antiaircraft weapons. Unlike Egypt's limited canal bridgeheads, the Syrian operation sought deeper territorial gains across a plateau of roads, hills, and escarpments where control of a few junctions could determine the defense of a wide area. Israeli regular forces were thinly deployed when the attack began, and portions of the front came under severe pressure in the opening days.

Syrian units advanced through several sectors and captured important positions, including an Israeli outpost on Mount Hermon. At the same time Israeli defenders held key ground in the north and used small numbers of tanks to delay the Syrian drive. The fighting later remembered in Israel as the Battle of the Valley of Tears demonstrated how local tactics, terrain, and rapidly arriving reinforcements could disrupt a much larger attacking force. Individual stories of heroic crews are prominent in national memorial accounts, but these should be read alongside Syrian operational reports and the broader balance of firepower, communications, and supply. Syrian soldiers experienced hard fighting and heavy losses even where the advance failed to secure its objectives.

Israel mobilized reservists, reinforced the northern front, and gradually shifted from defense to counterattack. By 10 October Israeli forces had driven Syrian formations back across much of the territory seized at the start of the war. Israeli troops then advanced beyond the 1967 ceasefire line into Syrian-held territory, taking positions within artillery range of Damascus. This later phase brought the fighting closer to major civilian centers and increased Syria's need for assistance. The Soviet Union supplied equipment and ammunition, while units from Iraq and Jordan entered the northern theater; Iraqi formations in particular contributed to slowing Israeli advances. Arab governments were cooperating, but not under one fully integrated command, and their military and political priorities differed.

Air power played an important but changing role. Syrian missile defenses initially restricted Israeli aircraft, as Egyptian systems did in Sinai, yet subsequent operations and battlefield maneuver created openings for air strikes. Israel also attacked strategic targets in Syria, including infrastructure and military facilities. The war thus imposed costs on people far from frontline trenches. In Syrian towns families dealt with alarms, disruptions to transport and employment, and uncertainty over relatives serving in the armed forces. On the occupied Golan, Druze communities faced the special circumstance of fighting near their homes while the territorial dispute remained unresolved. Civilian experiences should not be subsumed under descriptions of armored formations.

The northern front was connected to Egypt's choices. Assad's government expected the coordinated attack to spread Israeli forces across two theaters; when Syria came under heavy pressure, Egyptian leaders faced appeals to advance farther into Sinai. Egyptian commanders warned that leaving air-defense coverage would expose their armor. Those conflicting priorities contributed to decisions made around the Egyptian offensive of 14 October. The Golan campaign therefore shows why cooperation between states is not the same as a common strategy. Syria hoped for comprehensive territorial recovery; Egypt sought a crossing success that could change the negotiating environment. Both needed to keep Israel from concentrating its strength against only one opponent.

The campaign ended without Syrian restoration of the Golan. Israeli forces held territory beyond the prewar line when the ceasefire took effect, and subsequent diplomacy rather than battlefield gains would establish a new separation arrangement. Syria could claim that it had demonstrated an ability to inflict serious costs and cooperate in a surprise attack; Israel could point to the reversal of the initial Syrian advance. Neither claim settles the broader political issue. The battle intensified Syria's determination to regain occupied territory, damaged communities on both sides of the front, and required sustained diplomacy to prevent renewed combat after October.

Division IV · Section 66.15

The Turning of the War: Israeli Counterattacks and Superpower Resupply

By the second week of the October war, Egypt and Israel faced a new battlefield problem. Egyptian forces had established bridgeheads east of the Suez Canal and resisted several counterattacks, but pressure mounted to relieve Syria by launching a larger advance. On 14 October Egyptian armor moved beyond the most protective parts of its air-defense network. Israeli forces, now reinforced and better organized, defeated the attack and destroyed numerous Egyptian tanks. The operation weakened Egyptian reserves available to defend the canal bridgeheads and exposed a gap near the junction of Egyptian armies. Historians debate how much this decision reflected Syrian requests, Egyptian political ambition, or misunderstandings within the Egyptian command; contemporary memoirs often assign blame to rivals.

Israeli commanders exploited the opening. During the night of 15–16 October, elements of the Israeli army crossed the canal near Deversoir, west of the Great Bitter Lake. Fighting around the area later known as the Chinese Farm was exceptionally costly and involved attempts to secure routes for bridges and supply. The first Israeli units established positions on the western bank before a large force could move across. Engineers worked to bring bridging equipment through narrow, contested corridors. The crossing marked a reversal of the campaign's opening image: Egyptian troops had first crossed from west to east, and now Israeli troops were operating west of the canal behind parts of the Egyptian front.

As additional Israeli forces crossed, they attacked Egyptian surface-to-air missile sites and communications routes and advanced along the western bank. Their movement threatened the lines supplying Egypt's Third Army, whose formations remained east of the canal. By the ceasefire period the Third Army was largely surrounded and faced a grave logistical problem. Accounts differ over the precise moment of encirclement and over whether Israeli forces could have destroyed the army without unacceptable costs or international intervention. A sound history should distinguish operational position from hypothetical later outcomes. Israeli success in crossing the canal did not undo the significance of Egypt's initial bridgeheads, but it placed Egypt in a position where diplomacy was urgent.

The military conflict increasingly depended on outside assistance. The Soviet Union sent large quantities of weapons and supplies to Egypt and Syria, while the United States launched an airlift to replenish Israel's ammunition and equipment. These efforts were politically consequential. Israel wanted to maintain battlefield effectiveness; Arab governments sought replacement losses and reassurance that their principal arms supplier would not abandon them. Both Washington and Moscow understood that the fighting could affect the international balance of influence. Neither simply controlled its allies. Local leaders made battlefield decisions with immediate consequences, even as superpower support expanded the resources available to them.

The airlifts interacted with diplomacy and escalation risks. U.S. officials worried that a clear defeat of Israel would weaken Washington's standing, while Soviet leaders feared collapse of their clients and sought an early ceasefire on acceptable terms. As the battle moved around the Third Army, Soviet warnings about possible intervention and American reactions produced a sharp Cold War crisis. The timing and meaning of the U.S. worldwide defense alert of 25 October remain topics of interpretation, but it is clear that the superpowers wished to avoid direct military collision. The crisis illustrates how a local war could become entangled in global nuclear-era calculations without being wholly dictated by them.

Military historians assess the Israeli crossing as a major operational achievement and the Egyptian attack of 14 October as an important error. Political historians also ask how the earlier Egyptian crossing changed negotiations. These are distinct questions. Egypt had disrupted the assumption that Israel could safely hold Sinai indefinitely without bargaining, while Israel had restored its operational advantage and endangered the Third Army. A settlement could emerge precisely because each government had compelling reasons to stop fighting but could tell a national audience a different story about what had been achieved. Those competing narratives shaped the subsequent disengagement talks.

Division IV · Section 66.16

Ceasefires, Resolution 338, and the Military–Political Outcome of 1973

The war did not end with one uncontested ceasefire. On 22 October 1973 the UN Security Council adopted Resolution 338, calling for fighting to cease, for Resolution 242 to be implemented in all its parts, and for negotiations toward a just and durable peace to begin. The resolution linked immediate military restraint to a longer diplomatic framework. Both sides publicly accepted ceasefire language while accusing the other of violations. Fighting continued as Israeli forces attempted to strengthen their positions west of the canal and Egypt sought to prevent the isolation of the Third Army. The military map therefore changed even after the first international call to stop.

On 23 October the Security Council adopted Resolution 339, urgently reaffirming the ceasefire and calling for forces to return to the positions they occupied when the first ceasefire should have taken effect. Continued hostilities and alarm about great-power intervention prompted Resolution 340 on 25 October, which demanded an immediate ceasefire and established the Second United Nations Emergency Force (UNEF II). This sequence—338, then 339, then 340—must be kept distinct. It shows that diplomatic resolutions do not automatically suspend combat operations and that monitoring arrangements are sometimes required to turn an agreement in principle into a more durable military reality.

The ceasefire left Israeli forces west of the canal, an Egyptian Third Army at risk of isolation, and Egyptian bridgeheads east of the waterway. Israel had reversed the opening setbacks and held a favorable operational position; Egypt had nonetheless achieved an important initial crossing and preserved a foothold on occupied territory. Syria had failed to regain the Golan and faced Israeli positions threatening additional territory. War losses were heavy, and governments published figures that did not always use comparable categories of killed, wounded, captured, and missing. Political rhetoric could convert partial gains into national myths. For the historian, a verdict about military advantage must be separated from a claim about longer-term political success.

Negotiations over humanitarian supply to the Third Army became one of the first practical tasks of postwar diplomacy. Military representatives met at kilometer 101 on the Cairo–Suez road, supervised or supported by UN officers and intermediaries. Discussions covered the movement of food and medicine, prisoners of war, observation, and arrangements that could avoid renewed fighting. The presence of Egyptian and Israeli officers in direct practical contact mattered even before any comprehensive peace agreement. The crisis exposed the limits of attempting to impose a settlement by force alone: the strongest battlefield position was difficult to exploit without risking foreign intervention, escalation, or unacceptable humanitarian consequences.

In Israel the war produced a search for accountability. Intelligence failure, delayed reserve mobilization, and the opening losses became subjects of public investigation and political controversy, including the Agranat Commission. Prime Minister Golda Meir's government was weakened, and the war contributed to longer-term changes in Israeli political life. In Egypt Sadat celebrated the crossing as a recovery of national dignity and a prelude to diplomatic efforts. In Syria Assad stressed resistance and the continuing demand for the Golan. The same campaign therefore had sharply different domestic meanings, none of which is equivalent to an objective description of the battlefield.

The war also had consequences beyond the battlefield through petroleum policy. In October 1973 several Arab oil-producing governments announced production restrictions and embargoes against selected states regarded as supporting Israel, prominently the United States. The measures were coordinated through Arab petroleum producers rather than representing one uniform decision by every member of OPEC; posted-price decisions and broader market pressures also contributed to sharply rising world oil prices. The embargo did not determine the military campaign or immediately produce a comprehensive settlement, but it raised the international economic stakes and demonstrated that some Arab governments possessed instruments of influence beyond conventional armies. The organizational distinctions between OPEC and OAPEC, and the economic effects of the oil shock, are treated in Chapter 65.

The political transformation lay in opening a new negotiation process. In December 1973 the Geneva conference convened under superpower sponsorship; in 1974 disengagement agreements began to separate armies and return some territory. That did not make the war benign. Thousands of soldiers died, civilians suffered, and the underlying Palestinian and Syrian claims remained unresolved. But it did break a diplomatic stalemate that had persisted since 1967. The chapter's central analytical distinction is visible here: the army that held the strongest final tactical position was not necessarily the only state to realize a significant political objective, and the onset of negotiation did not amount to comprehensive peace.

Division V — Diplomacy, Peace, and Reshaping Arab Politics

Division V · Section 66.17

Geneva, Disengagement Agreements, and Shuttle Diplomacy, 1973–1975

The diplomatic activity following the October war did not initially produce a general peace conference with all parties negotiating final borders. It produced a sequence of limited agreements designed to separate armies, stabilize ceasefire lines, and create conditions for further discussion. The Geneva Peace Conference opened in December 1973 under American and Soviet co-sponsorship and with United Nations involvement. Egypt, Israel, and Jordan attended, while Syria did not take part. The meeting was brief and inconclusive, but a military working group continued to explore disengagement. This arrangement demonstrated that procedural agreements could be possible even when participants disagreed over the recognition of states, Palestinian representation, and the territorial demands of a comprehensive settlement.

U.S. Secretary of State Henry Kissinger developed a method of traveling between capitals and negotiating separately with leaders who were unwilling or unable to bargain publicly at the same table. “Shuttle diplomacy” gave the United States an influential mediating position, but it also meant that the American intermediary could frame proposals, carry assurances, and decide which concerns received priority. Egypt and Israel were not passive recipients: each negotiated force levels, geographical lines, and political wording in pursuit of national interests. The Soviet Union remained important diplomatically and militarily but was less central to the day-to-day negotiations that followed.

On 18 January 1974 Egypt and Israel signed their first disengagement agreement at kilometer 101. Israel withdrew its forces from the west bank of the Suez Canal and redeployed farther east in Sinai, while Egypt maintained limited forces east of the canal under agreed restrictions. UNEF II occupied the intervening separation zone and supervised agreed arrangements. The agreement did not return all of Sinai to Egypt and did not end the state of war, but it removed the immediate danger surrounding the Third Army and reduced the prospect of another catastrophic clash. Its detailed maps and provisions for limited armaments were essential: peace diplomacy depended on verifiable physical arrangements, not solely statements of goodwill.

The Syrian front presented different problems. Assad insisted on recovery of territory taken in October as well as the broader Golan issue, while Israel sought security guarantees for its northern communities and military positions. Negotiations produced a Syrian–Israeli disengagement agreement on 31 May 1974. It established lines of separation and limited forces, provided for the return of Syrian civil administration to specified areas, including Quneitra, and led to the creation of the United Nations Disengagement Observer Force, UNDOF. This was a ceasefire-monitoring arrangement, not a peace treaty or a solution to Syria's claim to the Golan. Civilians returned to a badly damaged landscape, and the visible condition of Quneitra itself became politically charged.

A second Egyptian–Israeli agreement, usually called Sinai II, was concluded in September 1975. It provided for additional Israeli withdrawals, buffer arrangements, early-warning measures, and U.S. diplomatic assurances. The negotiation showed how incremental agreements might build confidence and shift military deployments while leaving ultimate political questions unresolved. Egypt acquired more territory and a greater role for the United States in securing its interests. Israel obtained arrangements intended to reduce the danger of surprise attack. Critics in both states asked whether partial disengagement was a path to peace or merely a more stable form of unresolved conflict.

The limits of this approach were significant. Palestinian representatives had no equivalent seat in the disengagement process; Jordan's relationship to the West Bank remained disputed; Syria did not obtain comprehensive withdrawal; and Arab governments disagreed about whether negotiating separately weakened collective leverage. Kissinger's step-by-step diplomacy could succeed where a comprehensive package failed, but it could also postpone issues that proved politically costly later. The period from 1973 to 1975 thus marked a genuine shift from large-scale battlefield confrontation toward negotiated force separation, without yet creating a common regional settlement. The subsequent Egyptian–Israeli peace process built on these precedents while taking a more controversial bilateral direction.

Historical Vocabulary

Vocabulary: Ceasefire, Disengagement, and Peace Treaty

Ceasefire: An agreement or command to halt military action; it may be violated and does not settle underlying claims.

Palestinian National Representation and the Arab Political Realignment, 1973–1977

The October war created diplomatic movement among states, but it also sharpened the question of who could legitimately represent Palestinians. Before 1967, Egyptian and Jordanian authorities had governed Gaza and the West Bank respectively, and Arab governments often claimed a central role in deciding Palestinian political options. After the war and the growing influence of Fatah, the PLO insisted that Palestinians were a people entitled to national self-determination and independent representation. The organization did not speak with one ideological voice, and Palestinians under occupation, in camps, in Israel, and across the diaspora did not necessarily share the leadership's every policy. Nonetheless, the PLO became an increasingly important institutional actor in Arab diplomacy.

The Palestinian National Council's 1974 decisions signaled debate over political strategy, including whether an independent authority might be established on territory recovered from Israeli occupation. Some factions saw this as a pragmatic intermediate objective; opponents feared it implied accepting permanent partition or abandoning claims of Palestinians displaced in 1948. Such disputes were not merely tactical disagreements. They concerned the meaning of liberation, the relation between armed struggle and diplomacy, and the possibility that a partial territorial settlement could create political institutions without resolving refugees' rights. Understanding these differences prevents a textbook from describing the PLO as a unitary organization with unchanging objectives.

At the Arab League summit in Rabat in October 1974, leaders considered the competing claims of Jordan and the PLO. Their resolution of 28 October affirmed Palestinian self-determination and recognized the PLO as the **sole legitimate representative of the Palestinian people**, including the prospective leadership of an independent national authority on liberated Palestinian territory. King Hussein accepted the summit decision, although Jordanian–Palestinian relations and the administration of West Bank institutions remained complex. The recognition did not by itself end Jordanian connections to the West Bank, settle border claims, or bring a Palestinian state into being. It did alter the diplomatic premise: Arab negotiations regarding Palestinian political rights could no longer be treated solely as bilateral questions between Israel and neighboring states.

International recognition expanded. In November 1974 Arafat addressed the UN General Assembly, and the organization received observer status. The General Assembly affirmed Palestinian rights to self-determination and national independence, although such resolutions did not create the enforcement mechanisms necessary to achieve them. Diplomatic appearances mattered symbolically because they allowed Palestinians to address a global audience in their own name. They also had institutional consequences, enabling participation in international debates. Israeli governments strongly opposed recognition of the PLO, citing its charter, armed operations, and attacks targeting civilians. These objections were part of the political conflict and should be distinguished from the broader question of Palestinian national rights.

Arab political alignments continued to change. Egypt sought territorial recovery through talks with Israel; Syria feared an Egyptian settlement that left the Golan occupied; Jordan confronted a reduced diplomatic role concerning Palestinian representation; oil-producing monarchies developed new financial influence; and Iraq and Libya promoted rival visions of Arab resistance. The PLO depended on Arab support yet feared domination by any single sponsor. Conflict in Lebanon from 1975 added another arena in which Palestinian organizational presence, Lebanese factional competition, and outside intervention interacted. A full history of Lebanon belongs elsewhere, but its relevance here is the growing cost of unresolved Palestinian claims for neighboring societies.

By 1977 the diplomatic question was no longer simply whether an Arab state could negotiate with Israel. It was whether a state could secure its own territorial objectives while a Palestinian national movement remained excluded from the process. For Sadat, separate negotiation increasingly seemed the most practical route to Sinai. For many Palestinians and other Arab governments, that route risked abandoning comprehensive withdrawal and self-determination. The disagreement helped define the political reaction to Camp David. Recognition of the PLO at Rabat was therefore not a minor conference detail; it created an enduring test for peace proposals that concerned Palestinian territory but were negotiated without Palestinian representatives.

Sadat's Jerusalem Visit, Camp David, and the Egyptian–Israeli Peace Treaty, 1977–1979

Anwar Sadat's journey to Jerusalem in November 1977 challenged a central convention of Arab–Israeli diplomacy: the expectation that official negotiations would follow a collective Arab settlement rather than a unilateral Egyptian opening. Sadat addressed Israel's Knesset and called for peace while maintaining Egypt's demand for withdrawal from occupied territory and attention to Palestinian rights. For many Israelis the visit suggested that the region's most powerful Arab state might recognize Israel and end repeated war. For many Egyptians it promised recovery of Sinai and an end to the economic and human costs of military confrontation. For critics elsewhere in the Arab world it threatened to remove Egypt's military weight from a collective negotiating position before the Palestinian and Syrian issues were resolved.

Israeli Prime Minister Menachem Begin brought his own political commitments. His Likud government strongly opposed giving up the West Bank, which he referred to by the biblical names Judea and Samaria, while recognizing that a settlement over Sinai might be possible. His government needed assurances about security, diplomatic recognition, and the future of Israeli settlements in Sinai. Sadat sought a clear timetable for withdrawal and hoped to translate a bilateral opening into a broader Middle East settlement. President Jimmy Carter favored a more comprehensive peace framework and devoted considerable personal attention to bringing these positions closer. The negotiations therefore involved overlapping but distinct agendas, not simply two leaders moving inexorably toward agreement.

Carter convened Sadat and Begin at Camp David in Maryland from 5 to 17 September 1978. Negotiations were frequently difficult and nearly collapsed. They produced two documents dated 17 September, which must not be confused with the treaty signed the following year. One was a framework for peace in the Middle East, containing proposals for a transitional self-governing authority in the West Bank and Gaza and further talks involving Egypt, Israel, Jordan, and representatives of the inhabitants. The second was a framework for concluding a peace treaty between Egypt and Israel, including withdrawal from Sinai and normalization of relations. The Palestinian framework left important matters unsettled, including sovereignty, settlements, the precise meaning of autonomy, and the position of Jerusalem.

The Egyptian–Israeli peace treaty was signed in Washington on 26 March 1979. It established peace and diplomatic relations and committed Israel to phased withdrawal from Sinai under security arrangements. It provided for demilitarized or limited-force zones and international monitoring arrangements as part of a settlement that returned Egyptian territory while addressing Israeli security concerns. The withdrawal was to be implemented over a defined period, with completion occurring after the chapter's principal endpoint. Israel's government had to confront domestic opposition to removing settlements in Sinai; Egypt's government had to explain why a treaty could be signed despite opposition to its handling of the Palestinian question. The treaty was an important bilateral achievement but not the fulfillment of the broader Camp David framework.

Palestinian leaders rejected the autonomy proposal because they did not regard it as recognizing their right to an independent state or adequate political representation. Many Arab governments condemned Egypt's separate treaty, and Egypt was suspended from the Arab League; the League's headquarters was subsequently moved from Cairo to Tunis. Syria and other states continued to insist on Israeli withdrawal from occupied territories and comprehensive settlement. Within Egypt, some welcomed recovery of Sinai and peace, while others criticized the political, economic, or regional consequences of Sadat's policy. Public opinion was never uniform, and national government decisions did not extinguish the competing claims of individuals and movements.

The lasting consequence was a reconfiguration of diplomacy. Egypt and Israel showed that two former enemies could negotiate binding borders and security arrangements after successive wars. At the same time, the treaty removed Egypt from the immediate Arab military front without resolving Palestinian national aspirations, the status of Jerusalem, the Golan Heights, or the interests of other neighboring states. Camp David should therefore be assessed in two registers: success as an Egyptian–Israeli territorial and security agreement, and limitation as a proposed framework for wider peace. Both assessments are necessary to understand why 1979 was a landmark but not an endpoint to the regional conflict.

1967 and 1973 in Historical Perspective: War, Territory, Diplomacy, and Arab Political Change

The wars of 1967 and 1973 were linked but not identical. In 1967 Israel initiated a surprise air attack amid a spiraling regional crisis and quickly gained control of extensive territories. Egypt, Jordan, and Syria experienced defeat and sought ways to reverse its consequences. In 1973 Egypt and Syria initiated a coordinated offensive, achieved early successes, and then faced powerful Israeli counterattacks. The ceasefire left a complex battlefield map and encouraged renewed negotiations. Comparing the opening moves alone would obscure the difference between the wars' strategic purposes; comparing only the political outcomes would conceal the human cost and tactical decisions that shaped them.

Territory was the central continuity. Sinai, the Golan, Gaza, the West Bank, and East Jerusalem had different legal histories but were linked through Israel's post-1967 military control and the unresolved claims of neighboring states and Palestinians. Egypt eventually secured a path to Sinai's return through a bilateral treaty. Syria did not secure recovery of the Golan, while Palestinian claims to self-determination remained unsettled. The unequal outcomes demonstrate that a state-centered diplomatic process may resolve one territorial dispute yet leave others active. They also show that strategic geography—canals, plateaus, access roads, religious sites, and towns—cannot be treated merely as backdrop to government speeches.

Political leadership was transformed. Nasser's pan-Arab prestige was shaken in 1967, though he remained influential until his death. Sadat used the 1973 crossing and subsequent negotiations to redefine Egypt's foreign policy. Assad's Syria remained committed to recovering the Golan and became a major opponent of separate Egyptian peace. Hussein's Jordan faced the growing independent political role of the PLO, especially after Rabat in 1974. Israeli leaders moved from the confident aftermath of 1967 through the shock of 1973 toward a peace treaty with Egypt amid domestic controversy. None of these trajectories was uniform: parties, military officers, displaced populations, and civic movements contested their governments' decisions.

Palestinian experience shows why a history limited to state wars is incomplete. The 1967 occupation altered daily life and produced new displacement; the PLO's rise changed diplomatic representation; and international attention increasingly addressed national self-determination rather than refugees alone. Yet Palestinian participation remained marginal to the negotiations that produced the 1979 Egyptian–Israeli treaty. The unresolved claims over land, political authority, citizenship, and refugees continued to shape regional relations. The history of Palestinians in this period cannot be reduced either to the policies of Arab governments or to the armed operations of particular organizations.

The United States and the Soviet Union played roles that also changed. Each provided weapons, intelligence, and diplomatic support, but neither consistently determined its partners' choices. Soviet military assistance helped Egypt and Syria rebuild, while American diplomacy became increasingly central after the 1973 war. The possibility of superpower confrontation intensified the urgency of a ceasefire, but local military developments gave diplomacy its immediate subject matter. The role of international petroleum exporters in 1973 added an economic dimension covered in Chapter 65; here its significance is that regional conflict affected global political calculations far beyond the battlefield.

Finally, these wars reveal the limits of judging historical success by simple victory and defeat. Israel's 1967 battlefield victory brought territory and security advantages along with the long-term burdens of occupation. Egypt's 1973 offensive eventually encountered a grave operational setback but helped create diplomatic leverage. A treaty in 1979 ended war between Egypt and Israel while leaving other conflicts unresolved. The historical lesson is not that military force inevitably leads to negotiation or that diplomacy always follows defeat; it is that political objectives, civilian experience, institutions, and agreements must be studied together. The post-1979 Middle East inherited both the possibilities of negotiated peace and the consequences of questions that the treaties had not answered.

Chapter Summary

The May–June 1967 crisis emerged from cross-border violence, conflicting diplomatic commitments, military mobilization, withdrawal of UN peacekeepers from Egyptian territory, and closure of the Straits of Tiran. Israel initiated a surprise air offensive on 5 June, defeated Egyptian forces in Sinai, occupied Gaza and the West Bank including East Jerusalem, and captured the Syrian Golan Heights. The result transformed regional military geography and created a long-term problem of occupation and competing territorial claims. The war displaced civilians, including Palestinians already uprooted in 1948, and challenged confidence in Nasserism and other Arab state-centered strategies.

The Khartoum summit expressed collective Arab opposition to accepting the results of the war, while Resolution 242 established a diplomatic framework linking withdrawal, recognized boundaries, peace, navigation, and the refugee problem. From 1967 to 1973, Egypt fought the War of Attrition, rebuilt its military capabilities, and searched for a way to alter the stalemate. Palestinian movements gained greater independent authority; the PLO's conflict with the Jordanian monarchy during Black September showed the tensions between guerrilla autonomy and state sovereignty. Sadat and Assad planned a coordinated offensive whose political aims overlapped but were not identical.

On 6 October 1973 Egyptian forces crossed the canal and Syrian troops advanced on the Golan, initially surprising Israel. Israel recovered militarily, reversed the Syrian offensive, and crossed west of the canal on 15–16 October; the Egyptian Third Army was threatened by encirclement. Security Council Resolutions 338, 339, and 340 sought an end to fighting and established UNEF II. The war's tactical outcome cannot simply substitute for the political result: Egypt's crossing helped create diplomatic leverage that led to negotiations.

The 1974 disengagement agreements, the 1975 Sinai II arrangements, Rabat's recognition of the PLO, and Sadat's 1977 Jerusalem visit changed the diplomatic landscape. Camp David produced two frameworks in September 1978, and Egypt and Israel signed a bilateral peace treaty in March 1979. The treaty established a route to recovery of Sinai and formal peace between two states, but it did not settle Palestinian national self-determination, the status of Jerusalem, or Syria's claims to the Golan. Those unresolved issues shaped the decades that followed.

Chapter Reflection

The wars of 1967 and 1973 were not episodes in which military events ended and diplomacy began on a clean slate. The territorial order created in June 1967 shaped how later states measured defeat and recovery. Palestinian civilians experienced occupation and displacement in ways that could not be captured by official communiqués. Palestinian political organizations gained authority through years of organization and conflict, including struggles with Arab governments as well as Israel.

Egypt’s 1973 crossing changed political expectations even though Israeli forces later reached the west bank of the canal and threatened the Egyptian Third Army. Diplomatic agreements in 1974 and 1975 translated an unstable battlefield into monitored military zones; the 1979 treaty ended the state of war between Egypt and Israel. That achievement did not settle every national claim. The essential historical question is how military force, civilian rights, state legitimacy, and negotiated institutions interact—sometimes reinforcing one another, often pulling in different directions. Students should evaluate the documentary evidence before adopting a national victory narrative or treating a bilateral agreement as a comprehensive regional solution.
