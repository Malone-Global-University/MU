Part XI — Independence, Revolution, and the Arab Cold War

The End of European Colonial Rule

A History of the Arab World: From Ancient Crossroads to the Modern Arab World

Chapter Opening

Between December 1951 and November 1967, flags changed in Tripoli, Khartoum, Rabat, Tunis, Algiers, and Aden. Each ceremony marked the end of European sovereignty, yet no two had the same history. One followed a United Nations vote, another a diplomatic agreement among imperial partners, two came through negotiation backed by mass mobilization, one came after a war of nearly eight years, and one came amid urban insurgency and a hurried British departure.

Libya became independent on December 24, 1951, after the General Assembly of the United Nations had set a deadline for the end of its postwar administration. Sudan followed on January 1, 1956, under an agreement that Britain and Egypt had reached three years earlier. In March 1956 France recognized the independence of Morocco and then of Tunisia, ending protectorates that had existed since 1912 and 1881. Algeria, which France had governed since 1830 as part of its national territory, became independent in July 1962 after a war whose victims were counted in the hundreds of thousands. The British left Aden, and with it the territory that became South Yemen, on November 30, 1967.

This chapter studies those transfers of authority and the shorter, less dramatic withdrawals that surrounded them. It asks why European rule ended at different moments and by different means, and how those differences shaped the states that emerged. It does not treat decolonization as a single movement that swept across the region, nor as a story in which European governments simply chose to leave. Britain, France, Italy, and Spain made decisions under pressure from nationalist movements, from the United Nations, from the United States and the Soviet Union, and from their own domestic politics. Nationalists, for their part, disagreed with one another about goals, methods, and the form independence should take.

The chapter is organized comparatively. Division I places European withdrawal in its postwar setting and examines Libya, the first case. Division II turns to Sudan, Morocco, and Tunisia, where independence was negotiated but left difficult questions unresolved. Division III gives Algeria sustained treatment because its settler society, its war, and its human costs set it apart. Division IV considers Mauritania, Aden, the Gulf, the Horn of Africa, and Western Sahara, and Division V compares the outcomes. Egypt's independence and revolution belong to Chapter 62, the Palestinian catastrophe to Chapter 61, and the later politics of the new republics to Chapter 64 and following.

The evidence for this history is unusually varied. Treaties, constitutions, United Nations records, and parliamentary debates allow us to follow negotiations. Nationalist manifestos, party newspapers, and the memoirs of political leaders show what movements claimed to want. Colonial archives record how officials understood and tried to manage opposition, though they reflect the viewpoint of the governing power. For Algeria, military records, court cases, the testimony of soldiers and conscripts, journalism, novels and films, and a growing body of oral history all contribute, and they often conflict. Statistics on casualties and displacement are frequently contested, and this chapter identifies them as estimates wherever that is the case.

Three larger lessons run through the chapter. First, European colonial power did not end in the same way everywhere, and the route taken to independence affected the political institutions that followed. Second, independence resulted from local movements acting within an international environment that changed rapidly after 1945. Third, formal sovereignty did not automatically resolve the borders, economic relationships, regional inequalities, and political divisions that colonial rule had left behind. Students should hold these lessons together rather than treating independence as either a simple triumph or a hollow gesture.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why Britain, France, Italy, and Spain withdrew from Arab territories at different times and in different ways after 1945.
  2. Distinguish protectorates, mandates and trusteeships, condominiums, and settler colonies as legal and political forms of European rule.
  3. Trace the path from Italian defeat to Libyan independence under United Nations supervision, 1943–1951.
  4. Compare the negotiated transitions of Sudan, Morocco, and Tunisia, including the roles of political parties, monarchs, labor organizations, and external powers.
  5. Analyze why Sudanese independence did not resolve the dispute between northern and southern regions.
  6. Explain why Algeria's colonial status, settler population, and relationship to metropolitan France made its path different from that of Morocco and Tunisia.
  7. Assess the military, political, and social dimensions of the Algerian War, including the use of torture and the displacement of rural populations.
  8. Interpret primary documents from the era of decolonization, identifying their purposes, perspectives, and limits.
  9. Evaluate competing explanations of why France negotiated Algerian independence.
  10. Describe the British withdrawal from Aden and the Gulf and the unresolved sovereignty questions in the Horn of Africa and Western Sahara.
  11. Distinguish formal sovereignty from substantive political and economic autonomy in the postcolonial states.

Historical Context

By 1945 European control of the Arab world rested on a patchwork of arrangements assembled over more than a century. France had conquered Algeria in 1830 and organized it as departments of France, imposed protectorates on Tunisia in 1881 and Morocco in 1912, and held Syria and Lebanon as League of Nations mandates until their independence in 1946 (Chapter 58). Britain governed Sudan jointly with Egypt under a condominium established in 1899, controlled Aden, and maintained treaty relations with the rulers of the Gulf. Italy had conquered Libya in a war that began in 1911, and Spain held a northern protectorate in Morocco and small territories along the Atlantic coast and in the Sahara.

Chapter 60 traced how nationalist politics developed in these territories between the wars, through parties such as the Destour in Tunisia, the Wafd in Egypt, the Étoile Nord-Africaine and later movements in Algeria, and the Moroccan nationalist groups of the 1930s. Those movements grew in cities and among educated and commercial classes, but also among workers, students, and religious reformers. World War II then altered the setting. France was defeated and occupied in 1940, and North Africa became a theater of war. Italy lost its African empire. Britain won the war but emerged financially strained and dependent on American support. The Atlantic Charter of 1941, the founding of the United Nations in 1945, and the Arab League established that same year all gave nationalists new arguments and new forums.

Two cautions apply. The first is to avoid the assumption that independence was inevitable from 1945. French governments in particular fought to keep their empire, and decisions that now look decisive were contested at the time. The second is to avoid treating "the colonial powers" as a single actor. Britain's relationship to Sudan, Aden, and the Gulf was strategic and commercial. France's relationship to Algeria involved a large settler population and an official claim that the territory was part of France itself. Italy's colonial regime in Libya had ended in military defeat. Each of these shaped what was possible.

Division I

The Postwar Colonial Crisis and Libya's Independence

Section 63.1

European Colonial Power after World War II

The war of 1939–1945 weakened the three European powers that held most of the Arab territories discussed in this chapter. France had been defeated, occupied, and divided between Vichy and Free French authorities, and the humiliation weakened its claim to be an unquestioned protector of its subjects. Italy lost the war and with it all its African colonies. Britain was a victor but a depleted one, burdened by debt and dependent on American finance, and it was beginning a withdrawal from South Asia that changed assumptions about the empire's future. None of these governments lost the capacity to use force, as the Algerian War and the Suez operation of 1956 (Chapter 62) would show. What they lost was the confidence, the finances, and, increasingly, the international legitimacy to rule without consent.

Decolonization was also a contest over the meaning of victory in the Second World War. North African soldiers had fought in French formations, and colonial ports and farms had supplied the Allied war effort. Veterans and families could therefore ask why service against fascism did not bring political equality at home. Governments offered incremental concessions, but the expectations produced by wartime mobilization could not easily be contained within the prewar colonial order. These expectations were not identical across communities: veterans, rural tenants, merchants, women organizers, and urban workers faced different grievances. A persuasive explanation of independence must connect the metropolitan crisis of empire to the new political organization inside colonial societies.

New institutions and principles reinforced that shift. The Atlantic Charter of 1941 affirmed the right of peoples to choose their form of government, and although Britain's leaders insisted it did not apply to the empire, nationalists quoted it repeatedly. The United Nations Charter of 1945 spoke of self-determination and, in its provisions on non-self-governing territories and the trusteeship system, made colonial administration a matter of international concern and eventually of international scrutiny. The Arab League, founded in Cairo in 1945, committed its members to support Arab independence movements, and Cairo became a refuge and base for North African nationalists, including exiled leaders from Morocco, Tunisia, and Algeria. The Bandung Conference of Asian and African states in 1955 gave the anticolonial movement a further stage.

International pressure operated through arguments, votes, committees, and publicity more often than through direct enforcement. Petitions could reach the United Nations, yet the powers holding territory still commanded soldiers, police, budgets, and diplomatic influence. The growth of Asian and African membership shifted the balance in the General Assembly; it did not automatically transfer authority on the ground. For nationalists this meant that publicity and alliance-building might strengthen a domestic campaign without replacing it. For colonial governments it created a growing reputational cost whenever security measures contradicted their stated commitments to self-determination.

The Cold War cut in several directions. The United States and the Soviet Union both proclaimed opposition to old-style imperialism, and the Soviet Union offered diplomatic support to anticolonial causes. Yet Washington also valued its allies in Western Europe and its military facilities in North Africa and the Middle East, and it was reluctant to push France or Britain so far that their governments or their commitment to NATO weakened. For nationalist leaders, this meant that external pressure was available but uncertain. The same is true of the United Nations, where the Asian and African members who joined in growing numbers after 1955 steadily increased the weight of anticolonial opinion.

European withdrawal was therefore neither uniform nor inevitable. In 1946 Syria and Lebanon secured the departure of French forces, and Jordan was recognized as independent that year, but in North Africa the French government treated the protectorates and Algeria as essential to its position as a great power. Colonial settlers, military officers, and business interests had strong influence on policy. The sections that follow show that the timing and form of each independence depended on the interaction of these structural changes with particular political decisions, many of which might have gone otherwise.

Section 63.2

The Different Paths to Independence

The legal category under which a territory was governed shaped what nationalists could demand and what Europeans could concede. A protectorate, such as Tunisia and Morocco, retained a native ruler and a nominal sovereign identity, even though the protecting power controlled foreign affairs, defense, and much of the administration. This created a legal route to independence: negotiators could argue that France need only restore powers that treaties had temporarily transferred. A condominium, such as Sudan, was governed jointly by two powers whose disagreement was itself a political opportunity. Libya was a defeated enemy colony whose fate was assigned by the peace treaty to the victors and, if they failed to agree, to the United Nations. Aden was a British colony with a surrounding group of protected rulers. Algeria was formally a set of French departments, a status that gave it both representation in the French parliament and, for its European residents, legal privileges that most Algerians did not share.

The difference between a colony and a protectorate also affected the language of law. Tunisian and Moroccan negotiators could point to established dynastic institutions and earlier treaties whose alteration might be presented as the restoration of existing sovereignty. Algerian nationalists confronted a French constitutional claim that Algeria was inseparable from France, while the great inequalities of citizenship and political representation made that claim increasingly difficult to defend. In Sudan, by contrast, two governments asserted authority and each could use Sudanese preferences in a diplomatic contest with the other. These differences shaped not only how independence was won but which political institutions could claim legitimacy on the day colonial rule ended.

Four broad paths can be distinguished. In the first, independence came through international supervision, as in Libya. In the second, negotiation between a colonial government and a nationalist movement, often under mass pressure and sometimes accompanied by limited violence, produced a transfer of sovereignty, as in Sudan, Morocco, and Tunisia. The third path was a prolonged armed struggle, as in Algeria. In the fourth, a European power determined that its strategic and financial position no longer justified remaining and withdrew on its own schedule, as Britain did from the Gulf in 1971. Real cases often combined these elements. Tunisian independence followed an insurgency as well as negotiation, and Aden's British withdrawal came after both an insurgency and a policy decision in London.

The categories should not become rigid boxes. Libya's internationally supervised settlement was shaped by the Sanusi monarchy, local parties, and British strategic calculations; it was not a purely external gift. Tunisia's negotiating table was backed by strikes and armed resistance; Algeria's revolutionary movement also relied heavily on diplomacy. In Aden an impending British withdrawal coincided with increasingly destructive competition between nationalist groups. The relevant comparison is therefore not peaceful versus violent independence, but the changing mixture of organized coercion, political consent, legal authority, international recognition, and negotiation in each territory.

Each path left characteristic problems. Where a territory was assembled from regions with different administrative histories, as in Libya and Sudan, federalism and regional representation were immediate issues. Where colonial economies were built around export agriculture, ports, or settler-owned land, as in Algeria and Tunisia, independence raised questions about property and employment. Where European minorities had settled, their rights and futures had to be addressed, and in Algeria they were not resolved by agreement. Finally, the borders of the new states usually followed colonial boundaries, which nationalists mostly accepted but which sometimes ran across the claims of neighbors, a theme that returns in Sections 63.8, 63.17, and 63.19.

Historical Vocabulary
Protectorate

A protectorate was an arrangement, usually set out in a treaty, under which a European power took responsibility for the defense and foreign relations of another state while allowing its ruler and certain institutions to continue. Tunisia (1881) and Morocco (1912) were French protectorates; the treaties left the Bey and the Sultan on their thrones.

In practice, protecting powers governed through resident-generals and administrators who held real authority, and large European communities settled in both countries. The term mattered historically because it distinguished the status of Tunisia and Morocco from that of Algeria, which France annexed as departments. Nationalists in the protectorates could demand the restoration of sovereignty they argued had never been legally surrendered, while Algerian nationalists faced a government that denied they had a separate state to restore. The modern English word "protection" can mislead: the relationship was one of control, not of mutual defense among equals.

Section 63.3

Libya: From Italian Colonialism to United Nations-Supported Independence

Italy conquered Libya after 1911 and subdued its interior only with great violence, including the confinement of much of Cyrenaica's rural population in camps in the early 1930s, where mortality was very high, and the execution of the resistance leader Omar al-Mukhtar in 1931. Italian settlement, concentrated in Tripolitania and Cyrenaica, further reshaped the colony. This history left Libyans with strong memories of foreign rule and with a leading institution of resistance, the Sanusi religious order, centered in Cyrenaica.

For many families, the post-1943 transition did not mean an immediate recovery of confiscated land or restitution for wartime damage. Local economies had been disrupted by military campaigns and years of harsh Italian administration. Roads, ports, and administration were unevenly developed, and many trained officials were foreign. Memories of anticolonial resistance could unite groups against Italian rule but did not settle which Libyan region would lead an independent state. This distinction helps explain why constitutional discussions after 1949 revolved around the relationship of the provinces as much as around independence itself.

Allied forces drove the Axis from Libya in 1943. Britain then administered Tripolitania and Cyrenaica, and France administered the Fezzan in the southwest. Both operated as military administrations, which meant that Libyans had limited political rights but some room for organization. The Sanusi leader, Muhammad Idris al-Sanusi, who had spent the war in Egypt and who had supported the British, returned to Cyrenaica and became its principal political figure. In Tripolitania, a more fragmented nationalist scene produced several parties, including the National Congress Party led by Bashir al-Sa'dawi, which favored unity under an independent state. In the Fezzan, where French influence was strongest, local leaders were divided over their future.

The peace treaty with Italy, signed in 1947, required Italy to renounce its claims to Libya and provided that the Four Powers, Britain, France, the Soviet Union, and the United States, would decide its fate within a year, failing which the question would go to the General Assembly. The powers could not agree. They disagreed over whether to grant early independence or a period of trusteeship, whether Italy should return as an administrator, and how much influence each power should retain. A British and Italian compromise proposal in 1949, the Bevin–Sforza plan, would have separated the three territories into trusteeships. It failed in the General Assembly in May 1949 by a narrow margin, in part because it appeared to restore Italian influence and divide Libya.

The Bevin–Sforza proposal is a useful example of how an international formula could fail through local and transnational opposition. Its proposed division of responsibility among administrators offended Libyans who feared partition, while newly independent states opposed the apparent rehabilitation of Italian colonial authority. The defeat of that proposal did not erase foreign interests; instead, it shifted the form of outside involvement from trusteeship proposals to an accelerated constitutional timetable. The United Nations decision made a deadline politically consequential, but every essential question—head of state, distribution of seats, federal authority, and control of revenue—still required bargaining among Libyan representatives.

The General Assembly then adopted Resolution 289 (IV) on November 21, 1949, which declared that Libya, comprising Cyrenaica, Tripolitania, and the Fezzan, should become independent no later than January 1, 1952. It created a United Nations Commissioner, the Dutch official Adrian Pelt, and a council to assist him. Libyans, with Pelt's guidance, formed a National Constituent Assembly with equal numbers of delegates from each of the three territories, a formula that overrepresented the thinly populated Fezzan and Cyrenaica relative to Tripolitania, which was more populous. The assembly met in Tripoli in late 1950 and the question of federal versus unitary structure dominated its work.

Section 63.4

The Independent Kingdom of Libya, 1951

The assembly adopted a constitution on October 7, 1951, creating a federal monarchy, and Idris was proclaimed king. Independence was declared on December 24, 1951. The federal structure reflected the preferences of Cyrenaican and Fezzani leaders, who feared domination by Tripolitania, and the constitution established provincial governments and federal institutions with two capitals, Tripoli and Benghazi. Tripolitanian nationalists had pressed for a unitary state. The compromise gave Libya a constitutional form but also an unwieldy system that proved expensive to operate and politically unstable.

The constitutional choice was a response to profound differences in regional population, economic resources, and political networks. Tripolitanian leaders feared that equal territorial representation would weaken the most populous province, while Cyrenaican and Fezzani representatives feared being absorbed by it. Federal institutions promised autonomy, but Libya lacked the fiscal resources and trained personnel needed to administer several tiers of government efficiently. The king's authority could bridge regions symbolically while leaving many decisions dependent on court advisers, tribal intermediaries, and foreign technical assistance. Students should distinguish the existence of a written constitution from the actual political distribution of power.

King Idris was a respected but remote figure whose authority rested on the Sanusi tradition and his wartime record. The early parliamentary elections of February 1952 in Tripolitania were marked by allegations of government interference. After disturbances, the National Congress Party was suppressed and its leader expelled, and political parties were soon banned altogether. The monarchy governed largely through the king, his advisers, and tribal and regional notables. Opposition did not disappear, and new currents of Arab nationalism, influenced by Egypt under Nasser (Chapter 62), found support among students, officers, and urban workers. These tensions explain why the monarchy would later fall to a military coup (Chapter 64).

The opposition between monarchy and party politics was not simply a disagreement over personalities. Parties sought to articulate urban and cross-regional constituencies; the palace and regional notables claimed to protect provincial independence and order. Restrictions on opposition weakened the representative channels that the constitution seemed to envisage. Once political organization outside the court became difficult, the appeal of military and Arab nationalist alternatives grew among parts of the younger generation. This political development belongs in the story of independence itself because the arrangements used to construct the new state immediately affected whose voices could participate in it.

Libya began independence as an extremely poor country, with few trained administrators, limited agriculture, and little industry. It depended on foreign aid and on payments for foreign military facilities. Britain concluded a treaty in 1953 that allowed it to retain bases, and in 1954 the United States agreed to maintain Wheelus Air Base near Tripoli, while a 1955 agreement with France settled the Fezzan's remaining French presence. These arrangements gave Libya revenue and security, but they also exposed the monarchy to the charge that independence was incomplete. Libya joined the Arab League in 1953 and the United Nations in 1955. The discovery of commercially significant oil in 1959, and exports from 1961, transformed the state's finances and its politics, a development treated in Chapter 65 and later. The federation was replaced by a unitary system in 1963.

Foreign bases reveal a second meaning of independence. Britain and the United States treated Libyan facilities as valuable in Mediterranean and Cold War planning, and the monarchy treated rental payments and aid as resources for an otherwise impoverished treasury. Neither position automatically cancelled sovereignty in law; the agreements were negotiated by a recognized government. But reliance on external funding could narrow practical policy choices and create domestic doubts about the independence settlement. The subsequent discovery of oil altered the financial equation without resolving the underlying question of who would control the state's new wealth.

Division II

Sudan, Morocco, and Tunisia: Negotiated Independence and Political Contestation

These three cases share a general pattern: nationalist parties gained mass support, colonial authorities tried and failed to contain them, and a negotiated transfer of sovereignty followed. Yet the negotiations did not create political consensus, and each state faced different internal challenges once its external master had departed.

Section 63.5

Sudan: Nationalism, Self-Government, and the Anglo-Egyptian Settlement

Sudan had been governed since 1899 as an Anglo-Egyptian condominium in which Britain held actual power. British policy in the southern provinces, which differed from that in the north, restricted movement between regions and encouraged missionary education and local languages in the south. Sudanese nationalism emerged first among northern educated Sudanese, organized in the Graduates' General Congress of 1938, and was then shaped by two religious and political networks. The Ansar, the followers of the Mahdist tradition under Sayyid Abd al-Rahman al-Mahdi, supported the Umma Party, which advocated a Sudan independent of both Britain and Egypt. The Khatmiyya order under Sayyid Ali al-Mirghani was associated with the Ashiqqa and later the National Unionist Party (NUP), which sought some form of unity with Egypt.

Sudan's electoral competition was also an argument over the meaning of national self-determination. The Umma movement regarded union with Egypt as a threat to genuine independence; many unionists believed proximity to Egypt could help end British rule and support an independent Nile Valley political community. Religious networks were politically important, but they should not be mistaken for identical theological parties. Their influence rested on patronage, local organization, family connections, and rival conceptions of the nation's future. Parties that appeared unified during negotiations often contained regional and personal disputes that would become more visible when actual governing responsibility arrived.

Egypt had its own claims. Egyptian governments argued that Sudan formed part of a Nile Valley unity under the Egyptian crown, and in 1951 Cairo unilaterally abrogated the 1899 and 1936 agreements and proclaimed King Farouk king of Egypt and Sudan. Britain refused to recognize this. The Egyptian revolution of 1952 (Chapter 62) changed the picture. The new officers, led initially by Muhammad Naguib, who had Sudanese connections, accepted that Sudanese opinion had to be consulted and were willing to compromise, partly in hope that the pro-Egyptian NUP would prevail in a vote.

The Anglo-Egyptian Agreement signed on February 12, 1953 set a transitional period of self-government of no more than three years, the progressive Sudanization of the administration and army, and a Governor-General acting with an international commission. At its end, Sudanese would choose between independence and a link with Egypt through an elected assembly. Elections in late 1953 gave the NUP a majority, and Ismail al-Azhari became the first Sudanese prime minister in January 1954. The election result surprised many observers who expected Umma strength, and it showed the value of Khatmiyya networks and of the Egyptian-supported campaign.

The 1953 settlement could establish a legal path to self-government without deciding every constitutional question. Sudanization of the civil service and security forces presented an immediate distributional challenge: officials needed to be trained and replaced quickly, but colonial recruitment had heavily favored northern institutions. The transition also required choices about finance, schooling, the role of governors, and the standing of southern representatives. These matters were as consequential as the final independence vote, because they determined who would possess administrative authority when British personnel departed.

Once in office, however, al-Azhari and his party shifted. Unity with Egypt had little appeal to many Sudanese, including the NUP's own supporters, who now saw independence as a surer way to secure power, and relations with Cairo cooled. By 1955 the major northern parties favored independence. Evidence for this period includes the 1953 agreement and parliamentary records, British Sudan Government reports, party papers, and memoirs, but few sources preserve the views of southern Sudanese, who were not part of the dominant negotiating process.

Section 63.6

Sudanese Independence and the North–South Political Crisis, 1955–1956

Sudanization turned a long-standing southern grievance into an acute one. As British officials left, northern Sudanese filled almost all the administrative posts, including in the south, and southerners received only a tiny handful of senior positions. Southern leaders had agreed at the Juba Conference of 1947 to participate in a unified Sudan, but many came to feel that independence would replace British rule with northern rule. Language, religion, economic disparity, and the legacy of the colonial "Southern Policy" all contributed to this perception, and they overlapped with a history of slave raiding in the nineteenth century that shaped how many southerners viewed northern merchants and officials.

The colonial state's separate educational and administrative policies made the transition especially uneven. Mission schools, English-language administration, and limited opportunities for southern officers meant that the pool of candidates for senior posts differed sharply between north and south. Southern political figures expressed fears that rapidly transferring posts to northern Sudanese without federal guarantees would reproduce domination under a new national label. Those fears did not arise from an inevitable religious conflict. They were rooted in contested employment, regional development, systems of representation, and mistrust of security forces as well as cultural and religious difference.

In August 1955, soldiers of the Equatoria Corps, a southern military unit, mutinied at Torit, and violence spread through parts of the south. Northern officials, merchants, and civilians were killed, and the army and government responded with force. Many mutineers were executed or fled, and southern grievances hardened. The total death toll is uncertain and was reported differently by different parties, and the events remain sensitive in Sudanese memory. They are the starting point of a conflict that continued, with a lull after the 1972 Addis Ababa Agreement, for decades.

The Torit mutiny should be treated as a complex eruption of anxiety and violence, not as an uncomplicated declaration of a coherent southern independence movement. News, rumor, and fears of reassignment circulated through military units; the violence killed soldiers and civilians, and retaliation deepened insecurity. Subsequently, organizers of southern resistance drew on memories of 1955, but the later civil war developed through additional political decisions and changes in leadership. Reading 1955 as the predetermined start of all subsequent conflict would erase the choices that Sudanese politicians and soldiers still faced.

Parliament declared Sudan independent in December 1955, and the British and Egyptian governments recognized the decision and completed their withdrawals. Southern members supported the declaration on the understanding that the northern majority would give serious consideration to a federal arrangement for the south. That commitment was not fulfilled. Independence came on January 1, 1956 under a transitional constitution, with political competition among the Umma, the NUP, and the splinter groups that quickly formed. Al-Azhari's government fell in mid-1956, and the parliamentary system proved unstable; a military takeover in 1958 followed (Chapter 64).

The promise that a federal arrangement would receive serious consideration was politically significant precisely because it was not written into a binding settlement acceptable to all sides. The distinction between a parliamentary assurance and an enforceable constitutional guarantee mattered after independence, when numerical majorities could dominate central institutions. The first years therefore demonstrate how decolonization could provide international recognition before building a durable internal agreement on citizenship, territory, and the use of force. This pattern is not unique to Sudan, but its consequences there were especially severe.

Sudan's independence thus resolved the question of external authority without resolving the internal question of who would govern and on what terms. This is the critical lesson of the case. A settlement negotiated by the leading nationalist parties and two imperial governments did not incorporate the demands of regions and communities that had not been at the table, and the cost of that omission was a civil war.

Section 63.7

Morocco: Nationalist Mobilization and the Return of Muhammad V

Under the 1912 Treaty of Fez, Morocco remained a sultanate under French protection, while Spain controlled a northern zone and Tangier had an international status. Moroccan nationalism developed in the 1930s among urban reformers and Salafi-influenced scholars. In December 1943 the Istiqlal (Independence) Party was founded, and on January 11, 1944 its leaders and others presented a manifesto to the French and Allied authorities that demanded independence under Sultan Muhammad V and a constitutional regime. French authorities responded with arrests and repression, but the manifesto became the foundation text of the movement.

The manifesto's demand for independence under the sultan reflected a strategic partnership rather than complete agreement about Morocco's eventual constitution. Urban nationalists hoped that the throne would provide legal continuity and popular legitimacy; the palace sought to retain its own authority against both French residents and nationalist parties. Rural resistance to French occupation, regional identities, religious institutions, and labor activism all mattered alongside Istiqlal's leadership. A history centered only on negotiations between French officials and urban politicians would miss the breadth—and the internal tensions—of the movement that made a settlement possible.

Muhammad V, Sidi Muhammad ben Yusuf, had succeeded to the throne in 1927 and was initially seen by the French as a pliable ruler. He grew closer to the nationalists, supported the principle of Moroccan sovereignty in the late 1940s, and refused to sign decrees the French resident-general wanted. This made the sultan the focus of conflict. Colonial officials, backed by European settler interests and by some Moroccan notables, including Thami el-Glaoui, the Pasha of Marrakesh, campaigned for his removal. In December 1952, after the assassination of the Tunisian labor leader Farhat Hached, riots in Casablanca were met with lethal force, and the party was banned.

In August 1953 the French authorities deposed Muhammad V and exiled him, first to Corsica and then to Madagascar, and installed an elderly relative, Muhammad ben Arafa, in his place. The result was the opposite of what the French intended. Many Moroccans refused to accept the new sultan as legitimate, and the exiled ruler became a symbol of national unity. Urban terrorism, strikes, and rural resistance grew, and in 1955 an armed movement, the Army of Liberation, became active in the north. Violence against Europeans, notably around Oued Zem in August 1955, and heavy French reprisals deepened the crisis.

The removal of Muhammad V transformed the conflict because it seemed to attack an institution French rule had claimed to preserve. The replacement sultan struggled to command religious and political legitimacy. Nationalists could now frame resistance not only as opposition to foreign control but also as defense of the lawful ruler. The ensuing violence, including lethal attacks on civilians and harsh French reprisals, intensified pressure for a settlement while sharpening divisions over tactics. Accounts of incidents such as Oued Zem require particular care: participants, victims, and later official chroniclers supplied sharply different interpretations and sometimes different totals.

French governments, already engaged in Algeria and chastened by defeat in Indochina in 1954, concluded that the policy could not be sustained. After talks at Aix-les-Bains in August 1955, ben Arafa stepped aside and a regency council was established. Muhammad V returned to Morocco on November 16, 1955, to enormous crowds. Colonial archives, party papers, and the French and Moroccan press document the crisis, though both sides' sources are partisan.

French leaders also had to decide where limited military and political resources should be deployed. The Algerian insurgency was expanding, France had recently lost its position in Indochina, and severe repression in Morocco threatened further international criticism. The return of the sultan became a way to re-establish an interlocutor who could negotiate and to reduce the danger of another protracted war. It was not a unilateral French gift: the policy shift took place after organized Moroccan resistance made the protectorate increasingly difficult to sustain.

Section 63.8

Moroccan Independence, Monarchy, and Territorial Integration, 1956

A Franco-Moroccan declaration of March 2, 1956 recognized Morocco's independence and ended the protectorate regime. Spain followed in April, agreeing to end its protectorate in the northern zone, and in October an agreement ended the international status of Tangier. The restoration of sovereignty did not happen overnight; negotiations continued on the transfer of administration, the army, the police, and economic relations, and French troops and bases remained for some years. Muhammad V took the title of king in 1957.

The 1956 settlements must be read as a series of arrangements rather than a single instant of complete administrative autonomy. The French and Spanish zones, Tangier's distinctive status, foreign military installations, and unresolved enclaves had different legal histories. Newly independent Morocco needed ministries, customs services, diplomatic missions, and security institutions capable of exercising authority across territories governed under separate systems. The monarchy gained prestige from the restoration of the sultan, but the very process of creating national institutions also produced competition over appointments, provincial autonomy, and the incorporation of armed resistance groups.

The relationship between the monarchy and the Istiqlal Party defined the first decade. The party had the best claim to lead the new state, but the palace, with the prestige earned by exile, had its own ambitions. Muhammad V and his son, Crown Prince Hassan, built the Royal Armed Forces, partly from French-trained Moroccan soldiers and partly by integrating Army of Liberation fighters, and used them to suppress a revolt in the Rif in 1958–1959. The Istiqlal split in 1959, when its left wing under Mehdi Ben Barka formed a new party. When Muhammad V died in 1961, Hassan II became king, and the monarchy retained its dominance over parties.

The Rif crisis illustrates the limits of national unity after a successful independence campaign. Fighters who had resisted colonial rule did not necessarily accept the new state's command structure, and local grievances over development, representation, and military incorporation persisted. The central government's use of force revealed that the dispute over legitimate coercion did not end when European authority receded. At the same time, Istiqlal and its dissidents differed over how rapidly monarchy and party should share responsibility for economic and political reform. Those debates shaped Morocco's postcolonial constitutional evolution without belonging exclusively to later chapters.

Territorial questions remained open. Morocco's nationalists, particularly Allal al-Fassi, had advanced a vision of a "Greater Morocco" that included Mauritania, parts of the Algerian Sahara, and the Spanish territories. In 1957–1958, Moroccan irregulars attacked Spanish positions at Ifni, and in April 1958 Spain ceded the Tarfaya strip to Morocco, though it kept Ifni until 1969. The cities of Ceuta and Melilla, and the Saharan territories that became Spanish Sahara, were not included. Their status is revisited in Sections 63.17 and 63.19. Moroccan independence thus resulted in a monarchy-centered state with inherited territorial disputes.

Section 63.9

Tunisia: Neo-Destour, Labor Mobilization, and the Struggle for Sovereignty

The Tunisian protectorate dated from the Treaty of Bardo in 1881. Habib Bourguiba and his colleagues founded the Neo-Destour Party in 1934, breaking with the older Destour. Its leaders, many of them lawyers and professionals from outside the old elite, built a mass organization with branches across the country. Bourguiba spent much of the Second World War in detention, then in 1945 left for Cairo, where he worked with other Maghribi nationalists, returning to Tunisia in 1949. Alongside the party, a labor organization became a major force: the Tunisian General Labor Union (UGTT), founded in 1946 by Farhat Hached, whose leadership linked the nationalist movement to workers.

Neo-Destour's organization gave Tunisian nationalism a different political infrastructure from a movement dependent solely on royal leadership or rural military bands. Lawyers, teachers, merchants, municipal organizers, and neighborhood activists maintained contacts between towns and countryside. Trade unionists made workplaces sites of nationalist mobilization, while rural fighters could exert pressure beyond the cities. Their objectives were not always identical: workers sought wage and organizational rights, and villagers faced questions of land and policing as well as national sovereignty. The strength of the independence campaign came partly from its capacity to join these demands without permanently resolving their tensions.

In 1951 France and Tunisian leaders engaged in negotiations, but a French note in December rejected Tunisian demands for self-government. In January 1952 Bourguiba and other leaders were arrested, and French forces conducted violent sweeps. On December 5, 1952, Hached was assassinated, in an attack attributed to the clandestine French group known as the Red Hand (La Main Rouge). His death provoked strikes and riots across Tunisia and reverberated in Morocco. By 1952–1954 an insurgency of rural fighters called fellagha had spread, and French forces responded with counterinsurgency operations that included repression of villages.

Farhat Hached's assassination mattered outside Tunisia because labor federations and nationalist organizations treated it as evidence of the dangers facing political activists under colonial rule. French investigations, press accounts, and later testimony have been interpreted differently in assessing particular perpetrators and official responsibility. The essential historical point is more securely established: the assassination radicalized opposition and made cooperation with the protectorate authorities harder to defend. It also shows why diplomatic negotiations alone cannot explain the Tunisian transition; coercion, strikes, and international solidarity shaped the bargaining conditions.

The new French premier, Pierre Mendès France, traveled to Carthage in July 1954 and promised internal autonomy. After negotiations, Conventions on internal autonomy were signed in June 1955, and Bourguiba returned to a triumphant welcome. The agreements left France in charge of defense and foreign affairs for the time being. Salah Ben Youssef, the Neo-Destour's secretary-general, denounced them as a step backward, one that maintained French control and neglected the Maghrib-wide struggle, particularly Algeria. He drew on Pan-Arab ideas and Egyptian encouragement. The dispute split the movement.

Bourguiba won it. A party congress at Sfax in November 1955 backed his strategy, and Ben Youssef left for exile and supported an armed opposition. This was a quarrel over method, but also over Tunisia's identity and alignments, between a gradualist, Western-oriented nationalism and a more radical Arabist one. Ben Youssef was assassinated in 1961 in Frankfurt, an event whose responsibility is debated.

The dispute between Bourguiba and Ben Youssef also concerned scale. Should the movement take achievable Tunisian concessions while an Algerian war was still expanding, or insist on a broader regional transformation before accepting a settlement? Bourguiba argued that institutional gains could be used to pursue further sovereignty. His opponents feared they would freeze a dependent relationship with France and sacrifice solidarity with Algeria. By choosing the first path, the party increased its negotiating leverage but also deepened an internal division that would later encourage exclusion and repression.

Section 63.10

Tunisian Independence and the Creation of a Republic, 1956–1959

On March 20, 1956, France and Tunisia signed a protocol recognizing Tunisian independence and stating that the Treaty of Bardo, the foundation of the protectorate, no longer applied. Tunisia then gained control of its diplomacy, and negotiations on defense and other subjects continued. In the elections for a Constituent Assembly, the Neo-Destour won overwhelmingly, and Bourguiba became prime minister. The Bey, Muhammad VIII al-Amin, remained head of state for the moment.

The protocol's wording matters. By declaring that the 1881 treaty could no longer regulate relations between the two states, France and Tunisia acknowledged a new relationship between sovereign governments, while arranging to negotiate practical cooperation afterward. Independence therefore did not instantaneously remove every French soldier or terminate every economic dependency. Tunisian leaders had to create diplomatic and defense institutions and bargain over military facilities while consolidating control over domestic administration. The contrast between legal sovereignty and material leverage provides an important way to compare Tunisia with Libya and Morocco.

The change from monarchy to republic was a separate step. The Constituent Assembly abolished the monarchy on July 25, 1957 and proclaimed a republic with Bourguiba as president. The Bey's dynasty, which had ruled since 1705, was seen by Neo-Destour leaders as compromised by its accommodation of the French. A constitution followed on June 1, 1959, establishing a strong presidency. The consolidation of a party-state was rapid: other parties were gradually excluded, and the Neo-Destour, renamed the Socialist Destourian Party in 1964, dominated political life for decades.

The Code of Personal Status issued in August 1956 prohibited polygamy and established new rules for marriage and divorce. It became a prominent symbol of the republic's reformist image, yet it did not emerge from a universally shared consensus. Women's organizations, religious scholars, legal officials, and the party-state differed in their roles and opportunities to shape reform. The government expanded education and administrative participation while limiting autonomous political opposition. Students should evaluate who benefited from particular policies without assuming that modernization and democratization were the same process.

The new government undertook social reforms. The Personal Status Code of August 1956 abolished polygamy, replaced unilateral repudiation with divorce through the courts, and required the consent of both partners to marriage. It was one of the most far-reaching family-law reforms in the Arab world, justified by its supporters through reinterpretation of Islamic law, and criticized by some religious scholars. The government also abolished the Islamic courts and the system of religious endowments in their old form, expanded education, and used the state to promote development.

Control of Bizerte became an especially visible test of the new state's authority. France viewed the naval base as strategically important, particularly during the Algerian War, whereas Tunisian nationalists viewed its continued occupation as an obstacle to effective sovereignty. In July 1961, a dispute over the base led to severe fighting and substantial civilian casualties; United Nations debate did not immediately settle the question. The eventual French departure in October 1963 underscored that the transfer of legal independence in 1956 and the removal of foreign military infrastructure occurred on different timetables.

Relations with France remained complicated. French forces still held the naval base of Bizerte, and French aircraft bombed the Tunisian border village of Sakiet Sidi Youssef in February 1958 in pursuit of Algerian fighters, killing many civilians. The Bizerte question exploded in July 1961, when Tunisia attempted to pressure France to evacuate the base and fighting caused heavy Tunisian casualties, estimates of which vary. France withdrew from the base in 1963. Tunisia, moreover, offered sanctuary and supply routes to Algeria's FLN, which tied its independence to the neighboring war.

Algeria: Settler Colonialism, Revolution, and the War for Independence

Algeria was fundamentally different from Morocco and Tunisia because France treated much of its territory as an integral part of the republic. This division gives the Algerian War six sections. Throughout, readers should distinguish resistance to colonial domination from the several forms of violence that civilians experienced, while attending to differences in responsibility and scale, and should treat casualty totals as contested estimates.

Section 63.11

French Algeria, Settler Colonialism, and the Crisis of Reform, 1945–1954

France conquered Algeria between 1830 and the 1870s through a long and destructive war, and from 1848 the northern territory was organized as French departments. European settlers, many of them from southern France, Spain, Italy, and Malta, took over farmland and dominated commerce and administration. By the 1950s the roughly one million Europeans, called pieds-noirs, lived among about nine million Muslim Algerians. Jewish Algerians had received French citizenship collectively in 1870. Many Muslim Algerians had long been treated as French nationals without full civic rights; post-1944 and 1946 reforms extended formal French citizenship while generally preserving Muslim personal status. The 1947 statute nonetheless maintained profoundly unequal electoral colleges and discriminatory administrative practice. The settler minority held a disproportionate share of the best farmland and the better jobs, while many Algerian peasants worked small plots or as laborers.

The legal distinctions changed over time and must not be flattened into a single unchanging category. In the nineteenth century many Muslim Algerians were French nationals without the full citizenship rights enjoyed by settlers; the path to citizenship under ordinary French civil law frequently required relinquishing Muslim personal status. In the postwar period citizenship was extended more broadly while local personal status remained, but institutions still separated Europeans and Muslims into unequal electoral structures. Thus a Muslim Algerian could formally be a French citizen and still face sharply reduced political influence, discriminatory administration, and disadvantages in access to land, schools, and employment. This contradiction undercut claims that Algeria's departmental status ensured equality.

Algerian political life in the interwar period included reformers such as Ferhat Abbas, who sought equality within a French framework, the Association of Algerian Muslim Ulama of Abd al-Hamid Ben Badis, which promoted Arabic and Islamic identity, and the more radical movement of Messali Hadj, whose parties demanded independence. After 1945, Messali's followers formed the Movement for the Triumph of Democratic Liberties (MTLD). Meanwhile France offered limited reform. The 1947 Statute created an Algerian Assembly with two colleges of equal size, one representing about a million Europeans and the other about eight million Muslims. Officials interfered in the elections of 1948, which discredited the reform in Algerian eyes.

Moderate nationalist demands also changed as experience narrowed the available options. Reformers such as Ferhat Abbas tested the possibility of equal political participation, while religious and cultural activists argued that Arabic and Islam could not be subordinated to assimilation. Messali Hadj's movements placed more direct emphasis on independent nationhood. Colonial authorities sometimes presented these currents as a single conspiracy, but the distinctions between them were politically consequential. They help explain why the FLN had to establish its leadership against competitors as well as against France after 1954.

The crisis that shaped postwar nationalism occurred in May 1945. On May 8, as Europe celebrated victory, nationalist demonstrations in Sétif turned violent after police intervened, and Algerians attacked Europeans in the surrounding area, killing roughly a hundred. French forces, aided by settler militias, responded with air and naval bombardment and mass killings in Sétif, Guelma, and the Kherrata region. Estimates of Algerian deaths range from a few thousand to tens of thousands, and the numbers remain a subject of political dispute. For many Algerians, May 1945 showed that France would respond to demands for equality with force.

The events of May 1945 cannot be reduced to an unprovoked massacre by one side. Demonstrations, armed clashes, killings of European civilians, and extensive French military and militia reprisals took place over several areas and days. French army, police, and settler records often counted victims differently from nationalist testimony, and later commemorations sometimes selected one figure as a statement of national identity. The fact of large-scale and disproportionate repression is well established; the exact number of Algerians killed is not. For young militants the episode undermined confidence that peaceful petitions would secure meaningful equality.

By the early 1950s, Algerian nationalism was divided and legal politics seemed to offer no outlet. A secret paramilitary group, the Special Organization, had been discovered and dismantled in 1950. In 1954 the MTLD split between supporters of Messali and of its central committee, and a small group of younger militants, convinced that only armed action would break the deadlock, formed a Revolutionary Committee of Unity and Action. This was the immediate background to the uprising of November 1954.

Electoral manipulation further discredited constitutional reform. The Algerian Assembly's two colleges offered formally equivalent representation to populations of radically unequal size, and officials interfered in electoral contests. In practice, European settlers could block major reforms while colonial administrators retained security authority. It was possible for individual Algerians to seek careers and concessions within these institutions, but the system did not provide a credible mechanism to replace settler control. The emerging revolutionary current exploited this gap between the universal language of French republican citizenship and the lived experience of colonial political inequality.

Section 63.12

The National Liberation Front and the Outbreak of War, 1954–1956

In the early hours of November 1, 1954, small groups attacked police posts, military installations, and other targets across Algeria. The attacks, planned by leaders inside Algeria and in Cairo, were modest in scale, and some deaths were of civilians. A proclamation that day announced the creation of the National Liberation Front (FLN) and its military arm, the National Liberation Army (ALN). It called for independence and a sovereign, democratic, and social Algerian state, and invited Algerians to join. French authorities initially saw the attacks as a limited disturbance.

The opening attacks were designed to announce a political organization rather than to defeat France militarily in a single night. The initial insurgents operated with limited weapons, uncertain communications, and uneven local support. Their proclamation sought international recognition by presenting independence as a national political demand and offering a vision of a sovereign Algerian state. At the same time, attempts to impose the FLN's authority brought the movement into conflict with nationalist rivals. An effective account must therefore distinguish the legitimacy of anticolonial aspirations from any assertion that all Algerians accepted the same revolutionary leadership or methods.

The FLN initially organized regional operational zones, reorganized into a more formal system of six wilayas at the August 1956 Soummam Congress, each under its own commander, and sought to win support in the countryside, especially in mountainous regions such as the Aurès and Kabylia. The movement had to compete with the rival organization of Messali Hadj, the Algerian National Movement (MNA), and the struggle between them was violent, in Algeria and among Algerian migrants in France. In May 1957 FLN fighters killed several hundred villagers at Melouza who were considered MNA sympathizers, an episode that demonstrated the movement's willingness to use terror against Algerians who did not follow it.

Early FLN commanders operated under uneven geographic and political conditions, with better networks in some districts than others. The effort to centralize their activities addressed major practical problems: collecting supplies, maintaining cross-regional communication, supervising political representatives, and reconciling armed commanders with leaders based abroad. They did not eliminate rivalry. The continuing conflict with the MNA, including violence among migrant workers in metropolitan France, meant that the struggle over who represented the Algerian people ran alongside the war against colonial rule.

The war escalated in 1955. In August, FLN forces in the Constantine region attacked European and Muslim civilians around Philippeville, killing, in a frequently cited breakdown, 71 European civilians, 21 Muslim civilians, and 31 security personnel. French forces and settler groups subsequently carried out mass reprisals; historians dispute their toll, with estimates ranging from the official French figure of approximately 1,273 to several thousand and higher Algerian claims. The government of Guy Mollet, elected in early 1956, expanded the military effort, obtained special powers from parliament, and sent conscripts and reservists, so that French forces eventually numbered several hundred thousand. In October 1956 the French military intercepted an aircraft carrying FLN leaders, including Ahmed Ben Bella, and arrested them.

The attacks and reprisals of August 1955 radically escalated the conflict. Arrests and military sweeps expanded well beyond the original attack sites; communal fear deepened, and French reliance on coercion increased. Some advocates of compromise concluded that the space for negotiation was disappearing. Nevertheless, the violence did not mean that all Algerians had accepted the FLN as their representative or that the war had acquired an inevitable outcome. Its effects must be reconstructed through changes in local authority, the activities of rival nationalist organizations, and the decisions of French commanders as well as casualty figures.

The FLN held a congress in the Soummam valley in August 1956 to organize and unify the movement. Its platform established a political leadership, the National Council of the Algerian Revolution and a Coordination and Execution Committee, and gave priority to the political over the military and to the interior over the exterior. These principles were contested by officers and by leaders based in Cairo, and the movement's divisions, including the killing of the organizer Abane Ramdane by rivals in 1957, remained a feature of its history.

Soummam's assertion that political direction should prevail over military command and that leaders inside Algeria should prevail over external representatives was both a declaration of principle and an attempted settlement of immediate disputes. The congress established institutions through which civilian and armed components might act coherently. Yet the rejection of these arrangements by some external leaders and the later killing of Abane Ramdane show how fragile that project remained. Students should resist projecting the army-dominated structures of the post-1962 state backward as though the FLN's internal hierarchy had never been contested.

Section 63.13

The Rural War: Counterinsurgency, Displacement, and Social Transformation

Most Algerians lived in the countryside, and the war was fought there. The ALN depended on villagers for food, shelter, and information, and French forces tried to cut that connection. The French army built border barriers, notably the Morice Line along the Tunisian frontier, an electrified fence with minefields and patrols, to prevent ALN units based in Tunisia and Morocco from entering. It also created forbidden zones, from which populations were expelled, and used bombardment, including napalm, in mountain areas. From 1959, General Maurice Challe's operations used mobile units to hunt ALN forces, and these severely damaged the interior wilayas.

The Algerian countryside was the site of a struggle over population and supply as much as territory. Insurgent units depended on families for food, shelter, couriers, local knowledge, and recruits, but those relationships could involve persuasion, kinship, taxation, or coercion. French authorities sought to separate armed fighters from civilians through patrols, population registers, passes, and controlled movement. Because the same rural communities were approached by both sides, residents often faced impossible decisions about cooperation and the consequences of refusal. The war's geography varied from mountainous Kabylia to the Aurès and border regions; an account based solely on battles would miss the daily administrative and social conflict.

A central instrument was regroupement, the forced relocation of rural people into camps or resettled villages under military control. By 1960 the French had moved well over two million people, according to most estimates, many of them from forbidden zones, and conditions in the camps were often very poor, with hunger and disease. French officials presented some camps as part of a program of modernization, and the army's psychological-action specialists attempted to win the allegiance of the population, while critics, including some French officials, exposed their human costs. Many displaced people also moved to cities and shantytowns.

Fortified border systems created tactical advantages for the French army without ending the movement's political existence. Barriers along the Tunisian and Moroccan frontiers, especially the Morice Line, impeded arms shipments and made crossings dangerous. French mobile units could isolate and destroy insurgent formations inside Algeria, notably during the Challe operations. But tactical superiority did not itself resolve the competing claims to sovereignty. As fighting continued, international pressure, settler resistance to reform, and the costs of indefinite deployment meant that military success in one region could coexist with political stalemate across the territory.

Algerians experienced the conflict in different ways. The FLN taxed villages, demanded recruits and silence, and punished those it regarded as informers or collaborators, killing many civilians. French authorities recruited local auxiliaries, known as harkis, who numbered in the tens of thousands, for reasons that included economic need, local feuds, coercion, and loyalty. Women served as couriers, nurses, fighters, and bomb carriers, and many were also victims of violence. After independence, many wartime experiences were remembered through the national narrative of the "martyrs," which tended to simplify the diversity of Algerian choices.

Recent scholarship documents the scale of forced rural relocation: more than two million Algerians were moved into approximately two thousand regroupment centers during the war. Families lost access to fields, livestock, and seasonal labor, while overcrowding, poor water supplies, and insecurity damaged health and household economies. Officials sometimes justified the centers as protection or modernization, yet their immediate military purpose was to limit support for insurgents and render populations observable. The resulting dislocation changed settlement patterns long after the sites themselves were closed. Refugees who crossed into Tunisia or Morocco had different legal and humanitarian experiences from people displaced within Algeria, and those categories should not be conflated.

Figures for the dead are contested. Algerian official sources have spoken of a million and a half martyrs, while many historians working from demographic and military evidence suggest lower figures, often in the range of several hundred thousand, and French military deaths are far fewer. Every figure should be treated as an estimate, since many deaths were unrecorded.

Local auxiliaries and civilian women complicate any binary account of French soldiers and FLN fighters. Some Algerians joined French forces for pay or protection, some through existing local loyalties, and others under pressure; none of these motives can be assigned to all harkis. Women acted as political organizers, messengers, nurses, and sometimes armed participants, while also carrying the main burden of caring for displaced families. Archives of military commands often illuminate operations but reveal little about those tasks. Oral histories and humanitarian reports help restore civilian perspectives, although memories recorded decades afterward require the same critical scrutiny as memoirs of commanders.

Historical Vocabulary
Regroupement and Harkis

Regroupement (regroupment) was the French military policy of moving rural civilians into designated camps or new villages, usually to separate them from the ALN. The term, a euphemism, concealed that many people were moved by force and lost homes and livelihoods. Harkis were Muslim Algerians who served alongside the French army in auxiliary units, a category that included volunteers, conscripts, and men recruited under pressure. The word later came to be used in Algeria as a general term of abuse for collaborators, which obscures the range of motives involved. Students should treat both terms as historical categories that require examination, not as simple labels.

Everyday Life and Social History

Living through Decolonization

For most people, decolonization meant disruption as much as political change. In Algeria, families in forbidden zones lost fields and herds, children grew up in camps, and villagers had to calculate daily whether to feed fighters or to inform on them. In cities, curfews, identity checks, and searches governed ordinary routines. In Tunisia and Morocco, the removal of the Sultan or the arrest of a leader could halt shops and schools through strikes and demonstrations, and the labor movement gave workers a direct stake in national politics.

Women participated in the nationalist movements as organizers, teachers, and fighters, and their expectations of independence, including access to education and legal status, differed widely. Tunisia's 1956 Personal Status Code responded to such expectations, while Algeria's independent government turned away from some of them. Evidence on the lives of ordinary people comes from oral history, memoirs, court records, newspapers, and fiction, and historians must weigh the limits of each.

Section 63.14

The Battle of Algiers, Urban Warfare, and Torture, 1956–1958

In Algiers, the capital, the war took the form of urban terrorism and counterterrorism. In the fall of 1956 FLN cells, led in the city by Saadi Yacef, carried out shootings and bombings, including attacks on cafés and other civilian gathering places in September 1956, in which women activists placed bombs. The aim was to demonstrate the movement's reach and to force France to confront the conflict, but the attacks killed and maimed civilians and hardened European opinion.

Urban violence developed within neighborhoods whose streets, workplaces, cafés, and transport systems mixed political organization with civilian life. FLN militants used clandestine networks and spectacular bombings to demonstrate that French authority could not guarantee security. These attacks injured and killed noncombatants and generated fear in European districts, while French police and military raids subjected many Algerian residents to collective suspicion. The resulting sequence of attacks and reprisals cannot be understood by counting armed participants alone: merchants, domestic workers, students, children, and families encountered the war in spaces previously associated with ordinary urban routines.

In January 1957 the government gave the 10th Parachute Division under General Jacques Massu police powers in Algiers. The paratroopers broke the FLN network through mass arrests, searches, informants, and the systematic use of torture to obtain information. The FLN's call for a general strike in January–February 1957 was crushed. By October 1957 the leading FLN figure still at large, Ali la Pointe, was killed, and the organization in the city was effectively dismantled. Thousands of Algerians were detained, and many disappeared, among them the mathematician Maurice Audin, a French communist, who vanished in June 1957 after his arrest.

Counterinsurgency in Algiers relied on information as much as combat. Torture, secret detention, coercive interrogations, informants, and the disruption of communications helped French forces dismantle the FLN's urban organization. These practices also produced wrongful arrests and disappearances and created lasting disputes about legal accountability. The case of Maurice Audin matters because it connects one individual's disappearance to a wider system of detention and secrecy. Historians reconstruct that system by comparing court files, official statements, veterans' testimony, journalists, and the records of families who spent years seeking news of detained relatives.

The use of torture became a major political issue in France. Henri Alleg, a journalist, published an account of his own torture in 1958, which was banned but widely circulated, and intellectuals, clergy, and some officers protested. Officers who defended the practice argued that it was necessary against terrorism, while critics argued that it violated French law and values. Decades later, General Paul Aussaresses publicly acknowledged his role, and the French government in 2018 recognized the state's responsibility for Audin's death. The Battle of Algiers also shaped the way the war is remembered through works such as Gillo Pontecorvo's 1966 film.

Winning the immediate urban operation did not settle the larger political conflict. French officers argued that superior organization and intelligence could secure the population, while critics at home and abroad increasingly saw the methods as evidence of colonial domination incompatible with republican law. The international political cost was magnified by episodes beyond Algiers, including the bombing of Sakiet Sidi Youssef in neighboring Tunisia in February 1958. That incident exposed how cross-border military action could transform a French-Algerian war into a dispute with another sovereign state, prompting United Nations consideration and Anglo-American diplomatic involvement.

The battle was a French military victory in the city but a political defeat. The methods used discredited the French cause abroad, and the war was increasingly debated at the United Nations. The French air raid on the Tunisian village of Sakiet Sidi Youssef in February 1958 drew further international criticism. Meanwhile, the army's belief that it had won in Algiers and could win the war, if not hampered by civilian politicians, contributed to the crisis of 1958.

Section 63.15

The French Political Crisis, De Gaulle, and the Road to Negotiations, 1958–1962

The Fourth Republic's governments were unstable, and Algeria deepened their paralysis. On May 13, 1958, settler demonstrators in Algiers seized government buildings and, with army support, formed a Committee of Public Safety demanding that General Charles de Gaulle return to power. Fearing a coup, the French parliament invested de Gaulle as prime minister on June 1. A new constitution for a Fifth Republic with a strong presidency was approved by referendum in September, and de Gaulle became president. Both the settlers and many army officers expected him to keep Algeria French. In September 1958, the FLN established a Provisional Government of the Algerian Republic (GPRA), based in Cairo and then Tunis, headed by Ferhat Abbas.

The events of May 1958 connected army command in Algeria, European settlers, and political crisis in metropolitan France. The Committee of Public Safety did not represent a straightforward military coup that automatically installed de Gaulle; it created a threat of military intervention that shaped civilian parliamentary decisions. De Gaulle's return and the constitution of the Fifth Republic were contested political responses to that emergency. Some supporters assumed that a stronger executive would preserve French Algeria, but presidential power later made it possible for him to pursue policies that disappointed those same supporters.

De Gaulle first tried a combination of military pressure and economic and social programs, including the Constantine Plan of 1958, and offered the rebels a "peace of the brave," which they rejected. In September 1959 he declared that Algerians would have the right to determine their own future, and thereafter his policy moved by stages toward negotiation. Settler extremists and some officers resisted: in January 1960, settlers erected barricades in Algiers, and in April 1961 four retired and serving generals, including Maurice Challe, attempted a coup in Algiers. It failed within days, as most conscripts and officers refused to follow, and the French public rallied to the government.

The shift toward self-determination was gradual and uneven. De Gaulle attempted military pacification and socioeconomic programs while testing whether a reformed relationship might keep Algeria within a French framework. FLN representatives insisted that political recognition must include the right of Algerians to decide sovereignty. Negotiators also disagreed over the Sahara, strategic military facilities, oil, French settlers, and the position of the armed forces. These disputes show why agreement required more than an abstract endorsement of independence: it needed provisions for territory, personnel, resources, and the transition of administrative power.

After the failed putsch, some of its leaders and settler militants formed the Organisation of the Secret Army (OAS), which used bombings and assassinations against Algerians, French officials, and opponents in an attempt to prevent independence. In France, police violence against Algerian demonstrators in Paris on October 17, 1961 killed a number of people, whose total remains disputed, and in February 1962 police killed nine demonstrators at the Charonne metro station during a protest against the OAS.

Events in metropolitan France were part of the war, not a separate domestic epilogue. Algerian migrant workers sent money and political support to rival organizations and were also subjected to intensive police surveillance. On October 17, 1961, a demonstration in Paris against a discriminatory curfew was violently repressed; Algerians were killed, injured, and detained in large numbers, although historians continue to disagree on the death toll and the period to which particular estimates apply. Comparing official police figures with later archival work illustrates how state record-keeping, secrecy, and political memory can shape the evidence available about civilian violence.

Negotiations with the GPRA proceeded in stages, at Évian, Lugrin, and Les Rousses, and encountered obstacles over the Sahara, with its oil and the nuclear test site, over the status of the European minority, and over the unity of the Algerian state. Meanwhile, French public opinion had shifted. In the January 1961 referendum, a large majority of voters in France endorsed self-determination for Algeria.

The failed generals' putsch and the emergence of the OAS revealed that political agreement about self-determination did not command assent from all elements of the security forces or settler population. OAS bombings and intimidation targeted not only Algerian nationalists but also French officials and others regarded as betraying French Algeria. The French government faced simultaneous pressure to prevent further violence and reach a settlement. This helps explain why the final negotiations concerned guarantees and procedures for a transition as much as the declaration of a principle.

Section 63.16

The Évian Accords, Algerian Independence, and the Human Costs of War

The Évian agreements were concluded on March 18, 1962, and a ceasefire took effect on March 19. They provided for a transitional executive, a referendum on self-determination, guarantees for the rights and property of Europeans who stayed, a three-year period in which they could choose Algerian nationality, and cooperation in areas including Saharan oil and French use of the naval base at Mers-el-Kébir. French voters approved the accords in a referendum on April 8. A referendum in Algeria on July 1 produced an overwhelming vote for independence. France formally recognized independence on July 3, and Algeria celebrated it on July 5, the anniversary of the French capture of Algiers in 1830. Students should keep these dates distinct.

The Évian texts joined the ceasefire to an elaborate transitional design. An interim executive would exercise specified responsibilities pending the referendum, and provisions addressed public order, the rights and property of European residents, language, economic cooperation, and continued French access to certain facilities. Such arrangements were important to the negotiators but could not guarantee that every armed actor would obey them. The difference between an international legal document and conditions on the ground is central to interpreting the months between the March ceasefire and July recognition.

Violence did not end with the ceasefire. The OAS stepped up terrorism in the spring, and as independence approached, most Europeans left Algeria. Roughly 800,000 departed in 1962, in what became one of the largest migrations in modern Europe. They went in fear, many believing the guarantees of Évian to be worthless, and arrived in France where they were often poorly received. On July 5, in Oran, a number of Europeans were killed or disappeared, and estimates range from a few hundred to higher figures. Historians still debate whether the violence was spontaneous or organized and who bore responsibility.

The hurried European departure transformed Algeria's urban economy and public administration. Many settlers departed because they feared violence, retribution, or an uncertain future; others were affected by OAS threats and the collapse of confidence in political guarantees. Their departure left homes, shops, farms, schools, and professional positions that an independent government needed to manage. Some Algerians welcomed the recovery of land and institutions, while others confronted urgent shortages of experienced personnel. The movement of people across the Mediterranean thus changed both Algerian society and the communities in France that received the repatriates.

The harkis suffered reprisals, in which tens of thousands are estimated to have been killed, though the numbers are disputed. French policy was to limit their transfer, and although many thousands eventually reached France, they were placed in camps and isolated settlements. In both countries their treatment became a subject of recrimination.

The fate of Algerians who had served the French administration or military illustrates the limits of a simple victor-and-defeated narrative. Auxiliary service had diverse origins, and the risks individuals faced after the ceasefire varied by locality and by their ability to seek refuge. Many were killed or persecuted; others fled to France and entered camps or marginal settlements. No single casualty estimate captures the full experience, and historians distinguish the dead from those imprisoned, threatened, displaced, or stigmatized. Accounts of the harkis must preserve these distinctions rather than use every victim category interchangeably.

Politically, the FLN split. The GPRA, the political leadership, clashed with the army of the frontiers under Houari Boumédiène, which was allied with Ben Bella, who had been released from prison in March. In summer 1962, fighting broke out among FLN forces, and the army entered Algiers in September. Ben Bella became prime minister, and in 1963 he became president under a constitution establishing a single-party system. Algeria joined the United Nations in October 1962. The new state faced the task of rebuilding a country with massive displacement, the departure of most skilled Europeans, and a divided leadership, matters examined in Chapter 64.

Independence also exposed divisions inside the victorious movement. The political leaders who had negotiated abroad faced commanders controlling armed formations, and rival factions disagreed over who had authority to establish the new state. Conflicts during the summer of 1962 showed that wartime unity did not produce an uncontested political succession. The departure of colonial administrators and a large settler population made reconstruction urgent, but the concentration of organized force helped determine which faction prevailed. This explains why a chapter about the end of European rule must end by identifying institutional problems without turning into the longer political history reserved for Chapter 64.

Division IV

Decolonization Beyond the Central North African Cases

Section 63.17

Mauritania, French West Africa, and the Question of Saharan Borders

Mauritania was part of French West Africa, governed from Dakar, with a thin administration and a population of nomadic and settled communities. Its society includes Arabic-speaking Bidhan, often called "Moors," and their former dependents, the Haratin, along with Black African groups in the south speaking Pulaar, Soninke, and Wolof. In 1958 it voted to join the French Community as a self-governing republic, and Moktar Ould Daddah led the government. It became fully independent on November 28, 1960, while Ould Daddah headed the government and a new capital was being built at Nouakchott. He was elected president in August 1961 and assumed that constitutional office in September.

The concentration of administrative leadership around Ould Daddah was consequential beyond the transition's formal dates. Limited bureaucratic infrastructure made the new government unusually reliant on people who could negotiate with France, build regional coalitions, and represent Mauritania internationally. His consolidation of authority illustrates how state formation could strengthen the politicians who managed independence while leaving difficult questions about opposition organization, regional representation, and distribution of public employment unresolved.

Mauritania's route through the French Community differed from the sharper break sought by some neighboring movements. Its leaders needed to create national institutions across a vast territory with limited roads, widely dispersed settlements, and substantial distances between political centers. Nouakchott, developed as the new capital, symbolized the creation of a governmental center that could link coastal, Saharan, and Senegal River communities. Administrative independence required new ministries, schools, fiscal systems, and foreign representation even where the inherited colonial infrastructure was thin. These tasks help explain why continuity of French advisers and technical cooperation survived the formal transfer of sovereignty.

Independence raised an immediate border problem. Moroccan nationalists, particularly Allal al-Fassi, regarded Mauritania as historically part of Morocco, and Morocco refused to recognize it, opposing its admission to the United Nations in 1960–1961. Mauritania sought support from France, from African states, and eventually from parts of the Arab world, and Morocco recognized it only in 1969. The case illustrates how colonial administrative boundaries, drawn for French convenience, became the frameworks of new states, and how nationalist claims could contest them. It also shows the difficulty of defining a "national" identity in a state whose peoples belong both to the Arab and the West African worlds.

The country also faced the problem of translating a constitutional declaration of rights into practical administration. Patterns of dependency and inherited status, including the position of Haratin communities, did not disappear with the colonial flag. Policies concerning education, land, citizenship, and the public service became sites of negotiation among groups with different historical relations to authority. Treating Mauritania simply as another Arab nationalist case would obscure its place in West African political networks and its distinctive social geography. Its experience demonstrates that an independence timeline must be accompanied by an explanation of the society the new state was expected to govern.

The border dispute with Morocco was not merely a diplomatic quarrel over a map. Moroccan advocates of a larger national territory drew on older claims of allegiance and trans-Saharan connections, whereas Mauritanian leaders insisted that the territorial community emerging from French West Africa possessed the right to determine its own future. The competing claims affected regional diplomacy, international recognition, and the search for partners in Africa and the Arab world. At the same time, the new state's Arabic-speaking and other West African populations did not share identical cultural or economic priorities. Independence therefore forced decisions about language, political integration, and the treatment of unequal social hierarchies.

Section 63.18

The British Withdrawal from Aden and South Yemen, 1963–1967

Britain had held Aden since 1839 and valued it as a coaling station, a naval and air base, and a commercial port, especially after the opening of the Suez Canal. By the mid-twentieth century Aden was a growing city with a diverse population, a labor movement, and a large oil refinery, while the hinterland comprised protected sultanates and emirates. To block nationalist pressure, and Yemeni and Egyptian influence, Britain promoted federation of the hinterland states, and in 1963 Aden was merged into the Federation of South Arabia, a move opposed by many Adenis.

British rule in Aden combined direct colonial administration in the port with treaties linking surrounding rulers to imperial security. The port's workforce, merchants, dockers, migrants, and skilled technicians had social interests quite different from those of the sultans and emirs of the hinterland. Strikes, anti-colonial student activism, and labor disputes helped politicize the city before the emergency began. Federation was therefore controversial not only because it was promoted from London but because it might give conservative protected rulers influence over a politically mobilized urban population.

The National Liberation Front (NLF), founded in 1963, launched an armed campaign in the Radfan mountains and then in Aden itself, beginning with a grenade attack in December 1963 on the British High Commissioner, which led to an emergency. A rival group, the Front for the Liberation of Occupied South Yemen (FLOSY), formed in 1966 and received Egyptian support, which was linked to Egypt's intervention in North Yemen after the 1962 revolution there (Chapter 64). The two groups fought each other as well as the British.

The war cannot be described as two national movements fighting solely against an external power. The NLF and FLOSY also contested leadership of the future state, and local armed groups, police, and federal forces faced rapidly shifting loyalties. Egypt's involvement in North Yemen and support for regional revolutionary causes affected the available resources and diplomatic possibilities, but neither of the rival organizations was simply a foreign puppet. Their conflict intensified as the British withdrawal timetable became credible: the principal question was no longer just whether Britain would depart, but which force would inherit authority when its soldiers left.

A 1966 British defense review announced that Britain would leave Aden, abandoning plans to retain a base. The federation's institutions then collapsed, as its rulers lost support, and the British faced insurgent attacks in the Crater district and elsewhere. Egypt's withdrawal from Yemen after its defeat in 1967 weakened FLOSY, and the NLF prevailed. The British High Commissioner left in late November, and the People's Republic of South Yemen became independent on November 30, 1967, with Qahtan al-Shaabi as president. It later adopted a radical Marxist orientation and was renamed in 1970 the People's Democratic Republic of Yemen. The withdrawal was a case in which a colonial government, after a costly insurgency and a strategic reassessment, left without a negotiated settlement with a single successor.

The final months show how an imperial departure can coincide with the collapse of a sponsored political framework. The Federation of South Arabia had been designed as a successor structure, yet federation leaders had limited popular legitimacy and little capacity to survive withdrawal without British protection. Fighting in Aden, including the Crater district, weakened confidence in the colonial administration and its local allies. The eventual NLF victory created a new state, but did not produce consensus among all nationalist groups. South Yemen's subsequent political radicalization belongs to later chapters; here it demonstrates how the balance of armed forces at the moment of withdrawal influenced the institutional outcome.

Section 63.19

The Remaining Colonial Settlements: The Gulf, the Horn of Africa, and Western Sahara

In the Gulf, Britain's relations with rulers rested on protection treaties. Kuwait ended its 1899 agreement with Britain and became independent on June 19, 1961. Iraq's leader, Abd al-Karim Qasim, then asserted a claim to Kuwait, Britain sent troops, and an Arab League force replaced them; Iraq later accepted Kuwait's independence. In January 1968 Britain announced it would withdraw its forces from east of Suez by the end of 1971, and Bahrain became independent on August 15, 1971, Qatar on September 3, and six emirates formed the United Arab Emirates on December 2, with a seventh joining in 1972. Iran, which had claimed Bahrain, accepted a UN finding of Bahraini preference for independence and seized three islands claimed by emirates in November 1971. Gulf independence is treated in Chapter 65.

Gulf independence rested on political bargains different from those in North Africa. British agreements had regulated external security and foreign relations while local ruling families generally controlled important domestic affairs. When Britain decided to reduce commitments east of Suez, the rulers had to determine whether to form a federation, negotiate separate independence, or preserve new defense relationships with outside powers. Disagreements about representation, revenue, borders, and the distribution of power contributed to Bahrain's and Qatar's separate paths and to the eventual federation of the emirates. The political work of establishing ministries, citizenship regimes, and international representation occurred alongside the continuing importance of oil revenues and foreign expertise.

In the Horn of Africa, British Somaliland gained independence on June 26, 1960, and the former Italian Somaliland, administered by Italy under UN trusteeship since 1950, on July 1; they merged as the Somali Republic. French Somaliland, renamed the Territory of the Afars and Issas, became independent as Djibouti in 1977, after referendums and pressure from Somalia and Ethiopia. Both Somalia and Djibouti later joined the Arab League, which illustrates how Arab membership could include states at the Arab world's African margins.

Somali unification demonstrates a different problem: two distinct colonial legal systems could be joined rapidly under a common national ideal. The former British and Italian territories had different administrative practices, educational institutions, and elite networks. Although their union fulfilled a major nationalist aspiration, state-building required coordination of laws, civil services, and language policy. Other Somali-inhabited regions remained under neighboring administrations, creating claims that exceeded the borders of the new republic. Djibouti's later independence followed another trajectory, shaped by French rule, local Afar and Somali political competition, and the interests of Ethiopia and Somalia.

In the northwest, Spain's withdrawal from Western Sahara in 1975–1976 left sovereignty unsettled. After an advisory opinion of the International Court of Justice and Morocco's "Green March," Spain, Morocco, and Mauritania signed the Madrid Accords of November 1975, and Spain withdrew in early 1976. The Polisario Front proclaimed the Sahrawi Arab Democratic Republic and fought Morocco and Mauritania, which abandoned its claim in 1979. The United Nations continues to list Western Sahara as a Non-Self-Governing Territory, and the question has not been settled by an accepted act of self-determination. European withdrawal, therefore, did not settle every question of sovereignty.

Western Sahara illustrates the crucial distinction between a colonial power leaving and a population exercising a generally accepted right to self-determination. The International Court of Justice acknowledged historical ties to neighboring rulers but did not find sovereignty bonds capable of extinguishing the territory's right to self-determination. Spain's departure was followed by competing claims, armed struggle, and displacement, not by a widely accepted final sovereignty settlement. Students should distinguish administration, recognition, physical control, and legal sovereignty rather than treating them as interchangeable. The case qualifies any claim that decolonization was complete once European flags came down.

Historical Vocabulary
Non-Self-Governing Territory

Under Chapter XI of the UN Charter, states administering territories whose peoples have not yet attained full self-government accept an obligation to promote their well-being and to report on them. The UN maintains a list of such territories; Western Sahara remains on it. The term shows that decolonization was a legal process as well as a political one, and that a territory's status can remain open after its colonizer has left.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From Colonial to Postcolonial States

What changed: Foreign sovereignty ended, flags and constitutions changed, and new governments controlled diplomacy, armies, and national budgets.

What remained continuous: Colonial borders, administrative institutions, export-oriented economies, French as an administrative language in the Maghrib, and in several cases foreign military agreements.

Who experienced change most directly: Nationalist elites who took office, and Europeans who lost privileges or left. Who experienced greater continuity: Many peasants and laborers whose land, wages, and local conditions altered little in the short run.

Why it matters: It cautions against treating independence as a clean break.

Section 63.20

The Achievements, Contradictions, and Legacies of Arab Decolonization

Why did similar anticolonial aspirations produce such different post-independence systems? Part of the answer lies in the route to independence. Libya's UN-assisted process created a federal monarchy with little national political culture. Sudan's negotiated settlement produced a parliamentary system that excluded the south. Morocco's monarchy used the prestige of exile to dominate the parties, while Tunisia's Neo-Destour, led by a leader who had won an internal contest, built a party-state. Algeria's war created an army-dominated movement with an unquestioned claim to legitimacy and a society heavily damaged by displacement and loss. South Yemen's insurgent victors established a radical state. These outcomes were not determined by the paths themselves, but the paths affected who held organized force, who had legitimacy, and what social tensions were unresolved.

A comparative approach should also distinguish constitutional design from the distribution of organized coercion. Libya's federal charter did not ensure open party competition, and Sudan's parliamentary institutions did not include a broadly accepted settlement of southern demands. Tunisia's nationalist party was highly effective at mobilizing support but later marginalized rivals; Morocco's restored monarchy had its own networks of power independent of Istiqlal. In Algeria, the military organization necessary to sustain a prolonged war influenced post-independence succession. These differences arose through political choices as well as structural legacies, and none made authoritarianism an inevitable consequence of formal independence.

The achievements were real. Peoples gained control of their governments and international voices, and several states carried out notable social reforms, such as Tunisia's family law and education expansion. International institutions, the UN above all, became arenas in which new states had standing. The contradictions were also real. Borders were drawn by colonial administrators and mostly preserved, with disputes such as those over Western Sahara. Armies and single parties often took over the role of the colonial state. Economic structures, including export agriculture, hydrocarbons, and dependence on foreign capital, changed slowly, and foreign bases and treaties limited the freedom of action of some states. Sovereignty in law was not always autonomy in practice.

The independence settlements also created new opportunities for historical action. Governments could join international organizations, establish foreign policies, finance schools and health services, and bargain over bases or natural resources in their own names. Yet economic systems built around export commodities and colonial infrastructure did not change overnight. A new flag could coincide with dependence on foreign technical expertise, credit, security guarantees, or export markets. The relevant question for historians is not whether sovereignty was genuine or false in the abstract, but which powers the new government could exercise, which it delegated by agreement, and which remained constrained by inherited inequalities.

The chapter's three lessons can now be stated together. European colonial power ended differently in different places because its legal forms, settler presence, and strategic value differed. Independence came from the interaction of local movements and international power relations, not from either alone. And formal sovereignty did not automatically resolve inherited inequalities: it did not guarantee representative government, economic equality, or stability, as the cases of Sudan, Libya, and Algeria showed. The later histories in Chapters 64, 65, and 66 take up these themes.

Finally, the chapter's unresolved disputes should be understood as questions left open by specific settlements, not evidence that independence had no value. Algeria faced demobilization, displacement, and fierce political rivalry; Mauritania struggled for recognition and internal integration; Sudan lacked agreement on regional autonomy; Western Sahara did not obtain an accepted final status. Later chapters will show how new leaders approached these problems, sometimes reforming inherited structures and sometimes reproducing coercive practices. The first-edition historical purpose is to establish the choices, constraints, and human consequences of decolonization without pretending that the histories of the new states ended at the moment of independence.

The experiences of ordinary people complicate assessments focused only on constitutional dates. The Algerian farmer forced into a regroupment camp, the Tunisian worker who joined a nationalist strike, the southern Sudanese official excluded from promotion, and the Libyan household facing shortages of schools encountered different paths to independence. These are illustrative social positions, not interchangeable eyewitness testimonies. Their experiences remind us to compare access to land, education, physical safety, and political voice as well as formal diplomatic recognition. Decolonization was both a change in international standing and an uneven transformation in the everyday relationships through which states governed populations.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how European colonial rule ended across much of the Arab world between 1951 and 1967, with later withdrawals in the Gulf, the Horn of Africa, and Western Sahara. Its central argument is that the end of empire took several forms, and that those forms shaped the states that followed.

After 1945 Britain, France, and Italy were weakened, and new principles and institutions, from the Atlantic Charter and the United Nations to the Arab League and the Cold War rivalry, enlarged the arena for nationalists. Withdrawal was nevertheless contested, and France in particular resisted.

Libya's independence in 1951 resulted from a deadlock among the great powers and a United Nations resolution. The constitution created a federal monarchy of three territories under King Idris, a state that was poor, dependent on foreign bases and aid, and politically fragile.

Sudan's transition was negotiated between Britain and Egypt in 1953 and carried out under the leadership of northern parties. Independence on January 1, 1956 settled the question of external authority but not the position of the south, and the mutiny at Torit in 1955 foreshadowed a long civil war.

In Morocco and Tunisia, mass nationalist parties, a monarch turned national symbol, a powerful labor movement, and insurgency pushed France to negotiate in 1956. Morocco's monarchy retained dominance over the parties, while Tunisia's Neo-Destour, after defeating Ben Youssef, abolished the monarchy and built a party-state with a notable program of social reform.

Algeria's colonial status, large settler population, and the violence of 1945 set it apart. The FLN's uprising in 1954 led to a war that affected the countryside through displacement and regroupment and the cities through terrorism, repression, and torture, with civilians suffering from several forms of violence. Casualty figures remain contested.

The war transformed French politics, bringing down the Fourth Republic and de Gaulle to power, and he moved, against settler and army resistance, to negotiation. The Évian Accords of March 1962, the referenda, French recognition on July 3, and the celebration on July 5 ended French rule, but the exodus of the Europeans, violence against harkis and in Oran, and a split in the FLN showed how much remained unresolved.

Elsewhere, Mauritania's independence in 1960 provoked a Moroccan territorial claim, Britain left Aden in 1967 after an insurgency, the Gulf states became independent in 1961 and 1971, and Western Sahara's status remains unsettled under the UN's Non-Self-Governing Territory framework.

The comparison shows that routes to independence influenced institutions: monarchies, party-states, army-dominated movements, and radical regimes emerged from different histories. Decolonization brought real achievements and real contradictions, and sovereignty in law did not automatically yield autonomy, equality, or stability. These themes continue in Chapters 64 through 66.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because the end of European rule created the states in which most Arabs live today, and it did so by different routes whose consequences persist. Understanding why Libya, Sudan, Morocco, Tunisia, and Algeria diverged helps explain later differences in their political institutions and in their relationships with Europe.

The chapter also reveals processes that cut across regions. Nationalist movements, the Arab League, and the UN created networks of support, and events in one country, such as Hached's assassination or the Algerian War, affected others. Cairo and Tunis became centers of exile and organization. At the same time, local conditions such as settlement, religion, and regional inequality shaped outcomes.

Many of the chapter's questions did not end with independence. Sudan's civil war, Western Sahara, the status of Gulf islands, the memory of Algeria's war in both Algeria and France, and debates over borders and sovereignty continue. Later chapters on republics and coups, oil and the Gulf, and the wars of 1967 and 1973 show how the new states acted within a world still shaped by the colonial past.

Finally, the chapter contributes to the textbook's larger argument that the Arab world was shaped by interaction among local societies, regional networks, and external powers, in which Arab peoples were actors and not only objects of imperial policy. Independence was a beginning for new states as much as the end of an old order.
