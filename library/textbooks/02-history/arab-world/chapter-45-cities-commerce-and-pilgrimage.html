Cities, Commerce, and Pilgrimage

A History of the Arab World: From Ancient Crossroads to the Modern Arab World

Chapter Opening

Chapter 44 explained how the Ottoman state governed its Arab provinces. This chapter asks a different question: how did those provinces actually live and work? The answer lies in cities, markets, caravan roads, harbors, and pilgrimage routes, a commercial world that the Ottomans joined together and taxed but did not invent, and never made uniform.

Around the year 1600, a Cairo merchant named Isma'il Abu Taqiyya conducted business on a scale that makes the integration of the Ottoman Arab world visible in a single career. Historian Nelly Hanna, working from court records and other documents, has reconstructed how Abu Taqiyya traded across the Red Sea and the Mediterranean, dealt with officials as well as merchants, and accumulated great wealth in a city that was one of the largest in the Mediterranean world. His story matters less as the story of an exceptional man than as a window onto the institutions that made such a career possible: markets, warehouses, credit, courts, ports, caravans, and the demand of millions of consumers.

Now picture a different scene from the same century. Far to the north, in Aleppo, caravans arrived with Iranian silk, goods from Anatolia, and merchandise from Baghdad and Basra, while European consuls and merchants lodged in great stone khans and arranged shipments through the Mediterranean port of Alexandretta. In Damascus, a caravan prepared each year for the pilgrimage to Mecca, drawing on the markets of the city, the grain of the surrounding countryside, and the cooperation, or the hostility, of Bedouin communities along the desert road. At Mocha on the Yemeni coast, coffee grown in the highlands was loaded onto ships bound for Jeddah, Suez, and India. At Algiers, ships, crafts, captives, ransom, and the provisioning of a large port city created an economy unlike that of any inland capital.

This chapter treats these places as parts of interconnected urban and commercial systems rather than as isolated stories. Its central argument is that Ottoman rule helped link existing urban and trading networks, by providing relatively wide political security, widely accepted legal and fiscal institutions, and large markets such as Istanbul and the pilgrimage cities, without making those networks economically uniform or exclusively dependent on the imperial capital. Cairo, Aleppo, Damascus, Baghdad, Basra, Tunis, Algiers, and Mecca performed different functions, were tied to different hinterlands, and responded differently to changes in global trade.

The chapter also treats pilgrimage as an economic and logistical system as well as a devotional act. The annual hajj moved tens of thousands of travelers across deserts, required money, animals, water, food, and armed protection, and gave towns and nomadic communities along the way strong commercial interests in its success. The religious meaning of the pilgrimage and the institutions of religious life belong primarily to Chapter 46; here the emphasis falls on movement, organization, markets, and urban economies.

The chapter is built on a particular kind of evidence. Ottoman court registers record sales, partnerships, loans, inheritances, and disputes; customs and tax records track goods and revenues; European consular and company correspondence describes trade from the viewpoint of foreign merchants; travelers' narratives describe markets and caravans; and the surviving buildings, khans, markets, and ports themselves are sources, studied by architectural historians such as Nancy Um. Each source type has strengths and blind spots, and the chapter calls attention to them throughout, especially because commercial history written from European archives can accidentally make European merchants look more central than they were.

The chapter's chronological focus is the sixteenth through eighteenth centuries. It deliberately stops before the transformations of the early nineteenth century, treated in Chapters 49 and 50, and it reserves the political rise of provincial households for Chapter 47 and Arabia's eighteenth-century political transformation for Chapter 48. It also challenges a persistent simplification: the idea that Ottoman Arab commerce simply declined after Europeans reached the Indian Ocean by sea. The evidence reveals a more complicated story of adaptation, regional specialization, new commodities, and shifting centers of activity.

By the end of the chapter, students should be able to describe how major cities worked, how goods and people moved among them, and why historians increasingly regard the Ottoman Arab world as a dynamic commercial zone rather than as a passive victim of changing global routes.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how Ottoman incorporation linked existing urban and commercial networks across the Arab provinces without creating economic uniformity.
  2. Describe the physical and institutional structure of an Ottoman Arab city, including markets, khans, workshops, neighborhoods, and water infrastructure.
  3. Analyze how crafts, guilds, merchants, credit, and courts organized production and exchange, with attention to regional and chronological variation.
  4. Compare the commercial roles of Cairo, Damascus, Aleppo, Baghdad, Basra, Mosul, Algiers, Tunis, and Tripoli.
  5. Trace the Mediterranean, Nile, Red Sea, Gulf, Indian Ocean, and overland caravan routes that connected these cities.
  6. Distinguish the commercial participation of Muslim, Jewish, Christian, Armenian, and European merchants, and of women as property owners and investors.
  7. Explain how the Cairo and Damascus hajj caravans were organized, financed, and protected, and why they mattered economically.
  8. Interpret court-registered and other documentary evidence of commercial activity, recognizing its limits and whose perspectives it omits.
  9. Assess historical arguments about whether changing global trade routes weakened or transformed Ottoman Arab commerce.
  10. Evaluate continuity and change in Ottoman urban commerce between the sixteenth and eighteenth centuries.
  11. Contextualize the chapter within the governance of Chapter 44, the religious and communal life of Chapter 46, and the provincial politics of Chapter 47.

  2. Why did cities as close in culture and law as Cairo, Aleppo, and Damascus develop such different commercial specializations?

Historical Context

The commercial world of the Ottoman Arab lands was inherited. By the early sixteenth century, Cairo and Damascus were among the great centers of the Mamluk Sultanate (Chapters 37 and 38); Aleppo was an important meeting point of Syrian, Anatolian, and Iranian trade; Baghdad, though diminished since the Abbasid age, remained a regional hub; Tunis and Tripoli belonged to Mediterranean and Saharan networks (Chapter 39); and Yemen and the Hijaz were linked to the Indian Ocean (Chapter 40). The Ottoman conquests of Syria and Egypt in 1516–1517, of Baghdad in 1534, and of the North African coast over the following decades (Chapter 43) placed these networks inside a single imperial framework.

That framework mattered in several ways. It created large zones of relatively predictable law, taxation, and coinage, put the great pilgrimage sites under the authority of a sultan who claimed the duty of protecting them, and linked Arab cities to a very large consumer market in Istanbul and the Balkans. The institutions that allowed the empire to govern its provinces, such as tax farming, courts, and military garrisons, are examined in Chapter 44 and are treated here only when they directly shaped trade. The religious endowments (waqf) that financed many commercial buildings are discussed here as economic infrastructure, while their religious and charitable organization belongs to Chapter 46.

Students should also place these developments within the global economy. The Portuguese entry into the Indian Ocean after 1498, the arrival of Dutch, English, and French trading companies in the seventeenth century, the flow of American silver into the Mediterranean, and the later expansion of Atlantic plantation commodities all affected Arab commerce. Historians continue to argue about how much, and in which places. This chapter avoids two extremes: the assumption that the Ottoman Arab economy was a stagnant periphery waiting for Europe's arrival, and the opposite assumption that it was wholly untouched by global change.

Section 45.1

The Ottoman Arab World as an Urban and Commercial Network

The Ottoman Arab provinces were organized around a hierarchy of cities. At the top stood a handful of great centers: Cairo above all, followed by Aleppo, Damascus, Baghdad, Algiers, and Tunis. Beneath them lay provincial cities and ports such as Nablus, Gaza, Mosul, Basra, Tripoli, Rosetta, Damietta, and Jeddah; beneath those, market towns that collected the produce of villages and sold manufactured goods in return; and at the base, thousands of villages and pastoral encampments. Every city depended on a hinterland. Cairo drew on the Nile Valley and Delta, Damascus on the grain lands of the Hawran and the orchards of its own oasis, Aleppo on the cotton and grain of northern Syria and on caravans from the east. To understand a city's economy is therefore to understand the countryside that fed it and the roads that connected it to other cities.

The Ottomans did not invent these networks. Cairo and Damascus had been the principal cities of the Mamluk Sultanate (Chapters 37 and 38); Aleppo had long been a meeting point of Syrian, Anatolian, and Iranian routes; Baghdad, though far smaller than in the Abbasid age (Chapter 26), remained a regional hub; Tunis, Tripoli, and Algiers belonged to Mediterranean and Saharan systems already described in Chapter 39; and the ports of Yemen and the Hijaz were tied to the Indian Ocean (Chapter 40). What the Ottoman conquests of 1516–1517 (Syria and Egypt), 1534 (Baghdad), and the following decades (the North African coast) did was place these networks under a single imperial authority. That authority secured a great deal of road and river traffic, accepted the legal and commercial customs it found, and tapped the resulting wealth through customs, tolls, and taxes (the fiscal mechanisms are the subject of Chapter 44).

Imperial incorporation mattered in three ways. First, it created very large, relatively open markets. Merchants could carry cloth, rice, coffee, soap, or metalwork from Egypt or Syria to Istanbul, Anatolia, and the Balkans under one legal regime, and the capital's immense consumption drew Arab goods northward. Second, the sultans took on the protection of the pilgrimage and the Holy Cities, which gave Cairo and Damascus an annual imperial function and generated flows of money, grain, and people toward the Hijaz. Third, the empire's frontier with Safavid Iran and its Mediterranean seaboard shaped where trade could flow freely and where it was taxed, delayed, or interrupted by war.

At the same time, the Ottoman Arab world was never economically uniform. Egypt's commerce was oriented to the Nile, the Red Sea, and the Mediterranean; Syria's to caravans, silk, and cotton; Iraq's to rivers, the Gulf, and the Iranian frontier; the Maghreb's to the Mediterranean and the Sahara; Yemen's, for much of the period, to a single commodity whose markets lay largely beyond Ottoman borders. Imperial coinage and law offered common reference points, but merchants worked with local weights and measures, local market customs, and local officials. Provincial governors, tax farmers, and, increasingly, entrenched local elites influenced the costs and risks of trade (Chapters 44 and 47). The chapter that follows therefore proceeds city by city and route by route, because the differences among them are as important as the similarities.

One further caution applies. Ottoman Arab cities were deeply interconnected, but they were not simply nodes in a European-centered trading system. Their merchants traded with one another, with Anatolia and Iran, with East Africa and India, and with Europeans. Historians such as Bruce Masters, who studied Aleppo's commerce between 1600 and 1750, have emphasized that European merchants were important participants in that trade but operated within a market whose main structures, institutions, and consumers were Ottoman and Middle Eastern. This textbook adopts that perspective: European merchants appear, but they do not stand for the whole.

Historical Evidence
Reading the Ottoman Urban Record

Court registers (sijills) are the foundation of much recent urban history. Judges recorded sales of property, partnerships, loans, inheritances, marriage settlements, and disputes, often naming men and women of different religions. They reveal ordinary economic transactions, but they record only matters that people brought before the court, and they use formulaic language that can conceal motives and negotiations.

Merchant correspondence and account books survive in smaller numbers, especially for individual families, and show commercial reasoning directly. Customs and tax records provide quantities and revenues, but their coverage varies and they reflect what officials could assess, not everything that moved. European consular and company correspondence, kept in well-preserved archives, describes trade in detail but from the viewpoint of foreign traders and often exaggerates their share of the whole. Travelers' narratives convey impressions of markets and caravans, though travelers saw selectively and sometimes repeated stereotypes. Buildings, including khans, markets, and ports, preserve physical evidence of investment and use that texts may omit. Historians treat these sources as complementary: each fills gaps in the others, and none is neutral.

Section 45.2

The Physical City: Markets, Neighborhoods, Khans, and Infrastructure

The commercial heart of an Ottoman Arab city was usually a dense zone of market streets, workshops, and warehouses, often covered or lined with shops, in which trades were grouped by type. Sellers of cloth, spices, leather, metalwork, perfumes, or books stood close together, which made it easy for buyers to compare goods and for officials to supervise weights, prices, and quality. Around and within these markets stood larger commercial buildings: khans and caravanserais (the Syrian and Iraqi terms), wakalas (the usual Egyptian term), and covered market halls (qaysariyyas, bedestens in the Ottoman world more widely). These combined storage on the ground floor with lodging for merchants above, and they concentrated wholesale trade in secure, supervised spaces. Beyond the commercial core lay residential quarters, each with its own streets, gates, mosques, bakeries, small shops, and fountains, so that a merchant's household was rarely far from his place of business, and neighborhood life and commercial life overlapped.

Infrastructure was a commercial matter. Aqueducts, cisterns, public fountains, and bathhouses supplied cities with water and employed many people; city walls and gates controlled movement and customs collection; bridges, quays, and customs houses shaped where goods could pass; and caravanserais along roads extended the urban system into the countryside. Much of this infrastructure was financed by endowments (waqf), through which sultans, governors, military officers, and wealthy merchants dedicated income-producing property, often a khan or a row of shops, to support a mosque, school, fountain, or soup kitchen. The religious and charitable organization of waqf belongs to Chapter 46. Here the important point is economic: the rents of a commercial building paid for public services, and wealthy patrons invested in markets because markets generated predictable income and social prestige.

Specific buildings show how such investment worked. Aleppo's Khan al-Gumruk, the customs khan, is dated to 1574 and became a center of the city's international trade, housing merchants, warehouses, and the establishments of foreign traders. Cairo's merchants and artisans used an array of wakalas, some inherited from the Mamluk period and some built under Ottoman rule, which served as wholesale trading houses for specific goods such as coffee, textiles, or grain, and the city's river port at Bulaq connected the markets to Nile shipping. In Damascus, the Sulaymaniyya complex, built in the mid-sixteenth century with a hostel and soup kitchen for travelers, served as a way station for the pilgrimage, while in the eighteenth century As'ad Pasha al-Azm, the governor of Damascus, built a large khan, begun about 1751–1752, combining trade and lodging. These examples illustrate that sultans, governors, and local elites all participated in shaping the commercial landscape.

Architectural historians read these buildings as evidence. A khan's size and plan reveal what goods it was designed to handle; its location shows which route or market it served; its endowment deed, when it survives, tells who financed it; and its repairs and rebuilding show how long it stayed in use. Changes in building patterns, such as the growth of commercial construction in Aleppo and Mocha, can indicate periods when trade was expanding. Because many of these buildings survive in part, they provide physical evidence that complements, and sometimes corrects, the written record.

Historical Vocabulary
Khan, Wakala, and Caravanserai

A khan is a walled commercial building, usually arranged around a courtyard, with ground-floor storage and workspaces and upper-floor rooms for merchants. The word caravanserai commonly describes a similar building placed along a road to shelter travelers and animals, while wakala (also wikala) is the usual term in Egypt for an urban wholesale and lodging building. Usage varied by region and period, so the labels are not perfectly interchangeable.

These buildings mattered because they organized trade: they stored goods, housed merchants, concentrated wholesale transactions under supervision, and generated rental income. In cities with many foreign merchants, a khan could also serve as a recognized base for a foreign community. Modern equivalents such as "hotel" or "warehouse" capture only part of the function.

Section 45.3

Crafts, Workshops, Guilds, and Urban Production

Ottoman Arab cities were major centers of manufacture, and production was as important to their commercial standing as trade itself. Cairo produced textiles, metalwork, leather goods, glass, and processed foods, as well as a notable book and paper trade. Damascus was known for silk and mixed-fiber cloths, metalwork, and food products, and its fabrics were so widely traded that the city's name entered European languages in the word "damask." Aleppo produced textiles and processed silk and cotton. Mosul's cotton cloth became widely known in Europe, giving the English language the word "muslin." Tunis made woolen caps (shashiyyas) that were sold across the Mediterranean world, an industry to which Andalusi refugees who settled in Tunisia after the Spanish expulsions of the early seventeenth century contributed. Nablus, in central Palestine, produced olive-oil soap in quantities large enough to be sold in Egypt and Syria. Each city's specialization reflected its raw materials, labor skills, and markets.

Production usually took place in small workshops, often attached to the master's home or shop, employing a master, a few journeymen, and apprentices, sometimes with additional labor from family members. Women were important participants in textile preparation, especially spinning and finishing, even though many of their activities took place in homes and appear only indirectly in records. Skilled artisans could be prosperous: research on Cairo by Nelly Hanna and André Raymond has shown artisans who owned property, participated in credit networks, and in some cases invested in trade. This finding challenges the picture of artisans as a uniformly poor and passive group.

Most trades were organized into occupational associations commonly called guilds, though the terms for them varied (ta'ifa was a common one). Guild leaders, often called shaykhs, represented their trades before the authorities, settled disputes among members, supervised apprenticeship, and helped the state collect taxes and organize labor and supplies. Market inspectors (muhtasibs) checked weights, measures, and quality (see §45.5). The extent of guild control over prices, entry into trades, and production methods varied substantially across cities and centuries. Scholars once emphasized the guilds' role as instruments of state control; more recent work stresses that they also served members' interests, that membership and rules were negotiated, and that guild organization changed over time. There was no single "Ottoman guild system."

Artisans and merchants were closely linked. Some artisans sold directly to consumers; others supplied wholesale merchants who exported finished goods; and some merchants financed production by advancing raw materials or credit. In some trades, commercial capital increasingly shaped production; in others, workshops remained independent. Competition from imported textiles affected some crafts, particularly in later centuries, while others, such as trades connected with coffee and luxury goods, expanded. The balance between expansion and strain differed by trade and city, a point developed in §45.21. Students should note that the histories of crafts are closely tied to those of the cities that hosted them: Aleppo's textile and silk industries depended on the same caravans and merchants that supplied the export trade.

Section 45.4

Merchants, Partnerships, Credit, and Commercial Law

Ottoman merchants worked chiefly through partnerships, family networks, and agents, rather than through large permanent companies. A typical enterprise might combine capital from one or more investors with the labor and expertise of a traveling or managing partner, with profits divided by prior agreement. Islamic commercial law provided several recognized partnership forms, among them the mudaraba (an arrangement in which capital and management are combined, usually between an investor and a working partner) and various forms of shared investment among partners. These were flexible enough to finance a single caravan journey or a long-term business. Brokers (dallals and simsars) connected buyers and sellers; relatives, apprentices, and trusted agents represented merchants in distant cities; and letters carried by caravan or ship maintained contact. Because lending at fixed interest was legally controversial under Islamic law, merchants and jurists relied on credit sales, partnership contracts, and various legal devices, and the actual practice of lending and borrowing varied by place and period. Masters's study of Aleppo devotes substantial attention to money, credit, and investment, showing how merchants used these tools and how widely they circulated.

Courts were central to commercial life. A transaction or partnership recorded before a judge could be proven later, and the registers (sijills) that survive for many cities contain thousands of entries on sales of property, loans, partnerships, wills, and disputes. These registers show that Muslims, Christians, and Jews alike used Ottoman courts, even though communities also resolved some matters internally (Chapter 46). They also show women as owners of houses, shops, and orchards, as creditors, and as investors in endowments. Women's economic activity was shaped by social expectations and legal constraints, and the records mostly reveal women of property, but they contradict any assumption that women were absent from the market. A trained reader of a court register can see not only who did business but how: who stood as witness, who guaranteed a debt, and what happened when a debtor failed to pay.

Merchants themselves belonged to many communities. Muslim merchant families dominated much of the trade of Cairo, Damascus, and Aleppo; Jewish merchants were active in Cairo, Aleppo, Tunis, and Mediterranean ports; Syrian and Greek Christians took part in local and maritime trade; Armenian merchants maintained networks that stretched from Iran to the Mediterranean; and Europeans, under capitulatory privileges, traded in the major ports and in Aleppo. Merchants from North Africa and West Africa appeared in Cairo and the Hijaz on pilgrimage routes, and Indian merchants were present in Red Sea ports and Mocha. Masters's chapters on merchant diasporas and on the commercial institutions of a caravan city show how such communities were integrated into the same markets.

The merchant Isma'il Abu Taqiyya (c. 1580–1625?) shows how these institutions could work at the top of the system. Nelly Hanna's study, Making Big Money in 1600 (1998), reconstructs his career from court documents and other records. Abu Taqiyya built a large business in Cairo around 1600, with interests that extended across the Red Sea and the Mediterranean and with connections to the Ottoman administration in Egypt. His story illustrates that large fortunes were possible in Ottoman Egypt, that merchants, officials, and military households were linked, and that business depended on credit, agents, and courts. It also demonstrates the limits of any one case: Abu Taqiyya was exceptional, and his documentation survives precisely because he was wealthy. Most merchants worked on a far smaller scale, and their dealings left correspondingly fainter traces.

Primary Source Window

A Baghdad–Aleppo–Bursa Caravan Partnership, 1614

Creator: A judge's court, recording an agreement between two merchants · Date: 1614 · Type: Entry in the register of the judge (kadi) of Bursa, as discussed by Halil İnalcık in "Capital Formation in the Ottoman Empire," Journal of Economic History 29, no. 1 (1969), note 93

Context: Ottoman judges recorded private commercial agreements so that they could be proved and enforced later. İnalcık, an economic historian of the Ottoman Empire, cited one such entry in a discussion of how capital was raised and used in the early seventeenth century. The text below is a scholarly summary of that discussion, not a quotation from the register, and students should consult İnalcık's article for his exact wording and for the register itself.

Summary (after İnalcık): Two merchants of Irbil (Erbil), near Baghdad, named Osman and Allahkulu, formed a mudaraba partnership. Each contributed 1,540 riyals. Allahkulu undertook to manage the business and to take part in the caravan trade linking Baghdad, Aleppo, and Bursa. The partners agreed to divide the profits equally. The agreement was entered in the Bursa judge's register.

Analysis: The record shows several things historians value. It documents a concrete financial structure: a partnership in which both men supplied capital while one also took responsibility for management and travel, with profits shared equally. It shows that a Bursa court recorded a contract concerning a route running from Iraq through Aleppo to western Anatolia, which illustrates how Ottoman commercial law operated across provincial boundaries and how Iraqi merchants took part in a Syrian and Anatolian market. It also shows that merchants from a smaller Iraqi town could be active in long-distance trade. Students should note that the classic mudaraba usually assigns capital to one party and management to the other, so the equal capital contributions in this case suggest how flexibly merchants adapted legal forms to their needs.

The source also has limits. It records an agreement, not its outcome, so it cannot tell us whether the venture earned a profit or suffered a loss. It tells us nothing about the goods carried, the routes taken in detail, or the camel owners, caravan guides, and Bedouin protectors on whom the journey depended. It records only men who could afford the court and the capital, and it reflects the formulas of the court rather than the partners' own words. A single register entry cannot support generalizations about all Ottoman commerce, but, set beside other registers, it helps show how partnerships worked.

  1. What does this summary say about how Osman and Allahkulu organized their partnership?
  2. Who created the record, where was it kept, and why might a judge in Bursa have recorded it?
  3. What can historians learn about Baghdad–Aleppo–Bursa commerce from such a record?
  4. What can historians not establish from this entry alone, and what other sources might help?
Section 45.5

Feeding the City: Food Supply, Prices, Consumption, and Everyday Life

A large city could not survive without secure provisioning, and grain was the foundation of urban life. Cairo drew wheat and other grain from Upper Egypt and the Delta, Damascus from the Hawran plain south of the city, Aleppo from the surrounding agricultural districts, and Baghdad and Mosul from the river valleys. Meat came from pastoral suppliers, whose herds supplied markets and whose relations with the city were commercial and political; fruit, vegetables, and olive oil came from nearby gardens and orchards. Bread was the staple of most urban diets, and its price and weight were closely watched. The market inspector (muhtasib) and other officials checked weights and quality, authorities sometimes intervened to control prices or release stored grain during shortages, and failures of supply could lead to protest and unrest. Egypt carried obligations beyond its own population, since substantial quantities of Egyptian grain and other produce were dispatched to the Holy Cities of Arabia and, in various forms, to Istanbul (the fiscal framework is explained in Chapter 44), which could tighten local supply.

Consumption differed greatly by social rank. Wealthy households bought imported spices, fine textiles, porcelain, and coffee, and decorated their houses with furnishings from across the empire and beyond. Ordinary families depended on bread, legumes, seasonal vegetables, and, when they could afford it, meat, and the poor were vulnerable to price spikes, epidemic disease, and fluctuations in employment (for the earlier demographic background of plague, see Chapter 41). Water carriers, bakers, butchers, porters, and street sellers formed a large labor force whose work seldom appears in elite sources but is visible in court records, market regulations, and later descriptions. Provisioning thus connects the history of the market to the history of poverty, and historians use price series from court and institutional records to trace the pressures on urban households.

Coffee became an important commodity and social institution. Coffee drinking spread from Yemen to the Hijaz and Cairo in the late fifteenth and early sixteenth centuries, and then to Syria and Istanbul. Coffeehouses became places where merchants exchanged news, townspeople met for conversation and entertainment, and craftsmen and soldiers gathered. They also drew periodic scrutiny from officials and jurists, a subject that belongs to Chapter 46. Economically, coffee created a large new trade: Yemeni growers, port merchants at Mocha, shippers across the Red Sea, wholesalers in Jeddah, Suez, and Cairo, and retailers across the empire all participated in its commerce (§§45.6 and 45.16). The history of coffee therefore illustrates how a new commodity could restructure trading networks without requiring a new political order.

Everyday Life and Social History

Working in an Aleppo Market or a Cairo Workshop

A workshop in Cairo or Aleppo was usually small. A master craftsman owned or rented the shop, taught apprentices, and sold directly or through merchants; family members often assisted, and women could work in spinning, embroidery, and trade from the home even where they did not appear in the public market. Porters, carriers, and water sellers had no workshop at all, but depended on market demand and on the movement of caravans, whose arrival could raise or collapse their income. Court registers record purchases and rental of shops, wages, and disputes between masters and apprentices, but they seldom describe daily routine, so historians infer such detail by combining records, buildings, and later descriptions, and should not invent scenes that the evidence cannot support.

Religious and ethnic communities lived side by side in many markets, while residential quarters sometimes grouped by community. Their economic interdependence did not erase inequality: access to credit, protection from officials, and capital varied sharply by wealth, status, and gender.

Section 45.6

Cairo: Metropolis of Egypt and the Eastern Mediterranean

Cairo was the largest city of the Ottoman Arab provinces and one of the great cities of the Mediterranean world. Population estimates are uncertain and must be treated with caution: figures around 200,000 are commonly cited for the early sixteenth century, and estimates for the later seventeenth and eighteenth centuries run higher, with some scholars proposing totals of roughly a quarter of a million by the end of the eighteenth century. Plague and famine caused sharp fluctuations, and no single number can stand for the whole period. What the evidence does establish is the city's scale and its concentration of merchants, artisans, soldiers, scholars, officials, and laborers, as well as its position at the point where the Nile Valley opens into the Delta, within reach of the Mediterranean, the Red Sea, and the roads to Syria, the Hijaz, and Africa.

Cairo's markets drew on the productive agriculture of the Nile. Grain, rice, sugar, flax, legumes, and livestock reached the city from Upper Egypt and the Delta by river and road; the port of Bulaq on the Nile served as a principal landing for river traffic; and wakalas and markets throughout the city handled specialized commodities, textiles, grain, and coffee among them. The city's industries made cloth, metalwork, leather goods, glass, and paper, and Hanna's work on artisans, merchants, and middle-class households shows an urban society in which wealth was spread more widely than the great military households and merchant princes alone would suggest. Large merchants and ordinary shopkeepers worked in overlapping circuits of credit and exchange, and the city's courts recorded their dealings.

Cairo's regional role depended heavily on the Red Sea. Spices and Indian textiles had long come through Red Sea routes to Egypt, and in the sixteenth and early seventeenth centuries this traffic was still substantial. In the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries coffee from Yemen became the dominant import, and Cairo's merchants distributed it to Syria, Anatolia, North Africa, and elsewhere in the empire; some also re-exported it to the Mediterranean. Merchants such as Abu Taqiyya, discussed in §45.4, built fortunes in the Red Sea trade; others, whose dealings are documented in the court registers, invested in partnerships, shipping, and urban property. African trade routes brought commodities such as gold and ivory in smaller quantities, and enslaved people, whose forced labor and sale formed part of the city's commerce and domestic economy, came from the Sudanese, Ethiopian, and Saharan frontiers and, in some periods, from the Black Sea and Caucasus. These forced migrations belong to the history of the market as much as the commodities do.

Cairo's merchants and artisans also operated in an environment shaped by powerful military households, whose members invested in trade, urban property, and tax farming. The political rise of these households is the subject of Chapter 47; here the point is economic. Their wealth supported building, crafts, and consumption, while their rivalries and demands sometimes disrupted markets and burdened merchants and artisans. Cairo's prosperity was uneven: the wealth of leading merchants and officials stood alongside the poverty of many laborers, and shortages of bread, epidemic disease, and soldiers' disorders periodically hurt ordinary residents. The city's resilience over three centuries, and its capacity to adapt its trade to new commodities, is the main reason historians now doubt simple narratives of Ottoman commercial decline.

Section 45.7

Alexandria, Rosetta, Damietta, and Egypt's Maritime Trade

Egypt's Mediterranean ports linked Cairo to Anatolia, the Balkans, Italy, France, and the wider Ottoman Mediterranean. Alexandria, the old port, had lost much of its ancient prominence and had a limited population for much of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, but it remained the principal customs and shipping harbor for Mediterranean cargoes and revived, in part with growing French trade, in the eighteenth century. Rosetta (Rashid) and Damietta (Dumyat), at the mouths of the two main branches of the Nile, were river-mouth ports where sea-going vessels met river craft; they handled substantial coastal and river traffic, and their relative importance shifted over time with navigation conditions and commercial preferences. Goods moved between Cairo and the ports by river, a journey that required boatmen, pilots, warehouse keepers, and customs officials at each stage. Because sea-going ships had to navigate sandbars at the Nile mouths and were sometimes transferred to smaller craft, the ports' hinterland connections mattered as much as their harbors.

Customs were an important source of revenue and a crucial feature of the port economy. Ottoman officials, often under tax-farming arrangements discussed in Chapter 44, assessed duties on imports and exports; merchants and their agents negotiated valuations, paid brokers, and kept goods in bonded warehouses; and Europeans operated under the privileges negotiated by their governments (§45.14), which usually set specific customs rates. Egyptian exports to the Mediterranean included rice, flax, sugar, hides, and, increasingly, coffee re-exports, along with grain at various times, while imports included European cloth, metals, paper, and other manufactured goods, together with products from Anatolia and the Balkans. In the eighteenth century, French merchants, particularly those linked to Marseille, became especially prominent in Egyptian trade, and French consuls and traders were established in Cairo and the ports.

Much of the carrying trade, however, was conducted by Ottoman subjects. Greek, Syrian Christian, and Muslim shipowners and captains sailed between Egyptian ports, the Syrian coast, Anatolia, and Istanbul, and local merchants arranged freight and insurance, including through partnerships. Students should therefore avoid describing Egypt's maritime economy as simply a market for European merchants. The evidence from European archives, which is abundant, naturally emphasizes European activity; evidence from Ottoman customs records and court registers shows a larger and more varied commercial community. The ports also had distinctive social worlds: sailors, porters, brokers, customs clerks, and boatmen worked alongside foreign merchants in the khans and wakalas, and port towns sometimes supported small, multilingual communities of foreign residents.

The administration of customs shows how closely port commerce and fiscal arrangements were intertwined. Customs revenues in Egypt were commonly collected through tax-farming contracts (iltizam), in which a contractor advanced or guaranteed payment to the treasury and kept the surplus; the practice, whose general logic is explained in Chapter 44, gave contractors an interest in maximizing trade through the ports and also made them powerful figures in the merchant community. Merchants disputed valuations, sought exemptions, and used the courts and their consuls to challenge charges they considered excessive. The practical consequence was that a cargo's journey from ship to warehouse involved not only freight and brokerage but also negotiation with a chain of officials, and the cost of those negotiations affected which goods were profitable to ship.

The ports' fortunes shifted over the three centuries covered here. Rosetta and Damietta benefited from their position on the Nile branches and from the movement of rice and other Delta products; Alexandria, with deeper anchorage and better access for larger ships, regained importance as European shipping, especially French shipping, increased. Foreign merchants resident in these ports lived in designated khans and wakalas, with consuls who represented them before Ottoman officials and settled disputes among their nationals under the terms of the privileges they held. The evidence for these communities, drawn from consular correspondence, is detailed but reflects the concerns of foreign residents; local officials, brokers, boatmen, and porters appear mainly through Ottoman registers and travelers' descriptions, and combining the two kinds of source gives a more balanced picture of port life.

Section 45.8

Damascus: Markets, Caravans, and the Syrian Interior

Damascus combined agriculture, manufacture, regional trade, and pilgrimage in a way that made it one of the most important cities of the Arab provinces. Its oasis, watered by the Barada River and a system of channels, supported gardens and orchards that supplied fruit and vegetables; the Hawran plain to the south produced grain for the city and, under favorable conditions, for the pilgrimage caravan; and the surrounding villages and pastoral groups provided cotton, livestock, and labor. Within the city, workshops produced textiles, including silk and mixed-fiber cloth, metalwork, leather goods, and food products, and the markets served as a meeting place for merchants from Aleppo, Palestine, Egypt, Anatolia, and Iraq. Roads from Damascus ran north to Aleppo, southwest to Palestine and Egypt, west to the coastal ports of Beirut, Sidon, and Tripoli, east across the desert to Baghdad, and south toward the Hijaz.

Damascus's commercial identity was inseparable from the hajj caravan, treated in §45.18, which assembled in and near the city each year. Preparing the caravan generated demand for camels, mules, grain, water skins, cloth, and equipment; provisioning required cooperation between officials, merchants, and rural suppliers; and the pilgrims themselves, who came from Anatolia, Syria, Iraq, and farther afield, spent money in the city's markets and lodgings. The mid-sixteenth-century Sulaymaniyya complex, with its hostel and kitchen, was one of the institutions through which the Ottoman state and wealthy patrons supported this movement. The caravan returned with goods from the Hijaz, including coffee, spices, and Indian textiles, and with pilgrims who also traded, so that the pilgrimage worked as a seasonal fair as well as a religious journey.

Damascus's elites invested in this economy. Provincial governors, among them members of the Azm family who dominated the governorship of Damascus and the leadership of the pilgrimage caravan for much of the period from 1725 to 1757, built markets and khans and controlled supplies, while local merchants and tax farmers took part in the caravan's financing. The eighteenth-century Khan As'ad Pasha, built about 1751–1752 under As'ad Pasha al-Azm, is a monumental example, combining commercial and lodging functions on a grand scale. The political history of these governing households is the subject of Chapter 47. For this chapter, the key point is that Damascus's economy rested on a combination of agricultural supply, local manufacture, regional trade, and the annual pilgrimage, and that its fortunes could rise or fall with the security of the roads and the health of the caravan.

Damascus's connections to the coast mattered as much as its desert and Syrian links. Merchants sent goods to Mediterranean outlets such as Beirut, Sidon, and Tripoli, where European merchants, French merchants prominent among them, purchased cotton, silk, and other Syrian products and sold cloth and other imports. The relative importance of these ports changed with local politics and the preferences of European traders, a subject taken up again in Chapter 47. In the other direction, Damascus received coffee, spices, and Indian textiles from Egypt and the Hijaz, and merchants who specialized in these goods played a large role in the city's wholesale markets. Abdul-Karim Rafeq's studies of the province of Damascus and Karl Barbir's study of Ottoman rule in the city in the early eighteenth century, both drawing on court and administrative records, provide the principal scholarly foundations for the interplay between governors, merchants, and artisans.

Damascus's artisans and merchants also took part in financing the pilgrimage. Merchants and notables lent money or supplied goods on credit to the governor and the caravan's administration, and contracts for the supply of camels and provisions went to local contractors; the sums involved gave the pilgrimage an importance in the city's credit system as well as its markets. Arrears and disputes over the caravan's accounts sometimes became political, as the charge of embezzlement against a governor in 1730, which a modern historian has judged dubious, suggests. Such episodes show that the caravan's finances were a source of both opportunity and risk for the city's elite.

Section 45.9

Aleppo: Silk, Textiles, and International Commerce

Aleppo was the principal commercial city of northern Syria and one of the great caravan cities of the Ottoman Empire. Its location, near the Euphrates and the routes from Anatolia, Iraq, and Iran, and within reach of Mediterranean ports, made it the terminus of caravans from the east and a collection point for goods destined for the Mediterranean. Ottoman tax records suggest that the city's population in the 1520s was in the range of roughly 57,000 to 67,000; later estimates for the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries are higher, on the order of a hundred thousand, though they vary. The city's growth in the Ottoman period is visible in its commercial buildings: Khan al-Gumruk, dated to 1574, and other khans and markets that expanded the city's capacity to house and store goods. Bruce Masters's research, especially his study of Aleppo between 1600 and 1750, remains the foundation for understanding this commercial system, including its caravan trade, merchant diasporas, commercial institutions, and credit.

Silk from Iran was among Aleppo's most valuable commodities. Armenian merchants, whose network linked Iran, Anatolia, and the Mediterranean, played a major role in carrying it, and the relocation of Armenians to New Julfa near Isfahan under Shah Abbas I in the early seventeenth century strengthened a trading diaspora that reached from Iran to Aleppo and beyond. Silk reached Aleppo by caravan, was sold to local and European buyers, and went on to Mediterranean ports and Europe; the trade's volume and route changed with the fortunes of war between the Ottomans and Safavids, the policies of the Safavid state, and competition from other routes. Aleppo also produced and exported textiles and drew on Syrian cotton, gall nuts, and other regional products, and it imported European cloth, metals, and other goods. The city's merchants, Muslim, Christian, Jewish, and Armenian, dealt with caravans from Baghdad, Basra, and Persia, and with Mediterranean ports through the nearby harbors on the Gulf of Alexandretta.

European merchants, English, French, Dutch, and Venetian, operated in Aleppo through established trading houses and consuls, working with interpreters (dragomans) and local brokers. The English Levant Company, chartered in 1581, maintained an Aleppo establishment, and consuls represented their nations' merchants before Ottoman authorities. Capitulatory privileges set the terms of their taxation and legal protection. Their numbers were small relative to Aleppo's population, and local merchants, brokers, and artisans remained central to commerce. One of Masters's main arguments is that Aleppo's trade was shaped by an Islamic and Ottoman commercial economy within which Europeans found a place, even as European mercantilist policies and competing routes put pressure on that economy in later centuries.

Aleppo's fortunes were sensitive to politics and conflict. Ottoman–Safavid warfare disrupted routes; changes in Safavid trade policy affected the silk supply; and European demand fluctuated. The city's merchants responded by shifting commodities, diversifying routes, and drawing on regional markets. The great khans that survive in Aleppo's old city preserve the memory of this commerce, and the city's history helps counter the view that Ottoman Arab trade simply faded after the Portuguese entered the Indian Ocean. Aleppo's wealth in the seventeenth century rested on overland trade that sea routes never fully replaced.

Section 45.10

Jerusalem, Nablus, Gaza, Safed, and the Palestinian Urban Network

Palestine's towns formed a network of modest but economically distinct cities, tied to Damascus, Egypt, and the Mediterranean rather than dominated by any single metropolis. Their commercial roles differed. Nablus, in the central hills, became a center for the production of olive-oil soap, which was sold in Syria and Egypt, and for cotton and cloth; Adel Manna, Beshara Doumani, and other historians of Palestine have used its court records to show how merchants, soap makers, and peasants were linked through credit and trade, especially in the eighteenth century. Gaza, on the road between Egypt and Syria, benefited from caravan traffic and from grain and cotton production in its hinterland. Hebron was known for glass and leather goods. Safed, in Galilee, had a woolen textile industry and a significant Jewish community in the sixteenth century, though its textile production declined later. Coastal towns such as Jaffa and, in the eighteenth century, Acre, served as outlets for pilgrims, grain, and cotton, and the rise of Acre's cotton trade with French merchants foreshadows the provincial politics of Chapter 47.

These towns depended on the countryside around them. Villages produced wheat, barley, olives, cotton, and livestock; merchants advanced credit to farmers against future harvests; and market towns sold imported goods to rural customers. Roads connected the towns to Damascus and Cairo, and merchants moved between them on routes whose security varied with local politics and Bedouin relations. Because Palestine lay on the road between Egypt and Syria and on the pilgrimage routes, its towns also benefited from, and were burdened by, the movement of armies, officials, and pilgrims.

Jerusalem was above all a religious city, but pilgrimage made it an economic one as well. Muslim, Christian, and Jewish visitors arrived in large numbers, especially around major festivals, and they needed lodging, food, guides, and transport. The city's artisans and merchants supplied devotional objects and other goods, hospices housed visitors, and institutions connected to the pilgrimage employed local residents. Hebron and other holy sites attracted visitors as well. The religious institutions and communal organization of these cities belong to Chapter 46; the point here is that pilgrimage traffic supported local economies and connected Palestine to the wider Ottoman world, even though none of its cities rivalled Cairo, Damascus, or Aleppo in size.

The history of Nablus's soap industry illustrates how provincial commerce worked. Soap was made from olive oil and alkali, produced in specialized soap factories (masbana) that required substantial capital, and sold to markets in Syria, Egypt, and beyond, with merchants arranging transport by caravan and by sea from coastal ports. Beshara Doumani's study of Nablus, which draws on the town's court records, shows how local merchants, soap makers, and rural producers were connected through credit, and how Nablus's commercial ties extended to Damascus, Egypt, and the coast. In the eighteenth century cotton, grown in the hills and sold to European buyers through Mediterranean ports, was another major export. The Nablus case also demonstrates how much can be learned from a regional court archive and why historians of the Ottoman Arab world have turned to such sources.

The Palestinian towns' relations with the pilgrimage and with Egypt shaped their trade as well. Gaza, Ramla, and other towns on the Egypt–Syria road supplied caravans and travelers, and Palestinian produce, such as grain, olive oil, and soap, flowed to Egypt, while Egyptian goods, rice and coffee among them, flowed north. Local officials, notables, and Bedouin groups collected tolls and offered protection along the roads, so that trade routes in Palestine were also political arrangements. The towns of Palestine thus formed a network in which each place had a distinct economic specialty and all were linked by roads, credit, and shared markets.

Section 45.11

Baghdad, Basra, and Mosul: Commerce in Ottoman Iraq

Ottoman Iraq's three principal cities occupied different commercial positions. Baghdad, on the Tigris, was the administrative and commercial center of central and southern Iraq, linked by caravan routes to Aleppo and Damascus, by river to Basra, and by overland roads to western Iran. Merchants there handled Iranian and Indian goods, textiles, dates, and Syrian and Anatolian products, and the city was a gathering point for caravans. The 1614 partnership discussed in the Primary Source Window shows merchants linked to Baghdad, Aleppo, and Bursa within a single credit arrangement, indicating how far Iraqi commerce reached into Syria and Anatolia.

Basra, near the head of the Gulf, faced the sea. Ottoman control there dates from the mid-sixteenth century, and the city served as the main outlet for Iraq's Gulf trade: dates from the surrounding groves, Indian textiles and spices, and goods from the Gulf and the Arabian coast passed through its markets. Dutch and English trading companies established agencies there in the seventeenth century, and the port's relations with the Arab tribal groups of the lower Tigris-Euphrates region and the Gulf coast shaped its security and tolls. Regional shipping connected Basra to ports in the Gulf, western India, and Oman. Mosul, on the upper Tigris, linked Iraq to Anatolia and the north; it was known for cotton textiles and for the export of gall nuts and other regional products, and merchants sent goods down the Tigris on rafts supported by inflated skins to Baghdad, a traditional river technology that carried cargo downstream.

The Ottoman–Safavid frontier was both a constraint and an opportunity for Iraqi commerce. Warfare could interrupt trade directly: the Safavid occupation of Baghdad from 1623 to 1638, and the Ottoman recovery confirmed by the Treaty of Zuhab in 1639, disrupted caravans, damaged cities, and shifted trade to alternative routes. Yet the frontier also generated commerce. Pilgrims and merchants crossed it, Iranian goods entered Iraqi markets, and Iraqi cities profited from the transit of travelers to the Shi'a shrine cities (§45.20). Customs, tolls, and arrangements with tribal groups along rivers and desert routes affected costs, and the political arrangements of Ottoman Iraq (Chapters 44 and 47) shaped how securely trade could move. The commercial history of Iraq in this period is best understood as a story of three cities facing three directions, each responding differently to the same imperial and frontier conditions.

River transport and local shipbuilding supported the commerce of Iraq. Boats of various kinds carried goods on the Tigris and Euphrates and on the canals of the lower country, and ferry and boat operators were an important occupational group. The Gulf trade also involved the Ottoman-Portuguese rivalry of the sixteenth century: Portuguese naval power centered on Hormuz challenged Ottoman efforts to control Gulf commerce, and the Portuguese were expelled from Hormuz in 1622 by an Anglo-Persian force, changing the strategic setting for Basra's trade. In the seventeenth century a local family, the Afrasiyab dynasty, held authority at Basra for several decades under nominal Ottoman suzerainty, a subject treated in Chapter 47. Under such rulers the city's customs and relations with merchants and tribes were shaped by local rather than imperial priorities.

Baghdad's merchants dealt in a wide range of goods, and the city's markets and caravanserais served as a hub for trade between Iran, Iraq, and Syria. The city's society included Muslim, Jewish, and Christian merchants, as well as Persian and Indian traders, and its guilds and market officials regulated crafts and commerce much as in other cities. Because Baghdad lay on the main routes for pilgrims to Najaf and Karbala and for Iranian travelers to the Hijaz and Syria, its markets benefited from this traffic, and the effects of the Safavid-Ottoman rivalry on movement through the city were felt by merchants and residents alike.

Section 45.12

Algiers: Maritime Commerce, Corsair Enterprise, and Urban Society

Algiers became the capital of an Ottoman-linked regency whose economy combined Mediterranean trade, urban crafts, agricultural exports, and corsair activity. The city's port imported timber, metals, cloth, and other manufactured goods, and exported hides, wax, grain, and other products to European markets; French and other European commercial establishments on the Algerian coast, including those engaged in coral fishing and the export of grain, linked the regency to Marseille and other ports. Corsairs, privateering captains who operated with the regency's authorization and shared proceeds with its rulers, seized ships, cargo, and people from European states with which Algiers was at war. Peace treaties, tribute, and ransom arrangements formed part of the regency's diplomacy. Corsair enterprise was thus both a form of organized maritime warfare and a branch of the local economy, and it connected the city to shipbuilders, merchants, brokers, and suppliers.

Corsairing should be understood without romanticism or reduction. It involved violence, the seizure of cargo, and the enslavement of captives, whose labor was used in the city and its surroundings, in households, in public works, and in the corsair fleet itself. Historians' estimates of the number of Europeans held captive in North Africa between the sixteenth and eighteenth centuries are controversial; Robert Davis has proposed that roughly 1 million to 1.25 million European Christians were enslaved in North Africa between 1530 and 1780, but other historians question the reliability of the underlying evidence and consider the estimate too high, so it should be treated as a disputed figure and not as established fact. Captives could be ransomed through intermediaries, including Catholic religious orders such as the Trinitarians and Mercedarians that raised funds for redemption, and some captives converted or remained in North Africa. Corsairing was also not one-directional: Christian powers captured and enslaved Muslims in the Mediterranean, and ports such as Malta and Livorno hosted slave markets. The Mediterranean was a zone of mutual raiding and captivity.

Algiers was also a diverse and complex city. Its population included Ottoman soldiers and officials, local Arabs and Berbers, Andalusi refugees, Jews, merchants, artisans, and captives and former captives of many origins. Its crafts, markets, and commercial institutions supplied the city and its fleet, and its merchants, including Jewish brokers, mediated between the regency and European states. The regency's relations with Istanbul and its internal politics are discussed in Chapters 44 and 47. For the purposes of this chapter, the city should be understood as a commercial society whose maritime enterprise, including its violent side, rested on ordinary economic institutions: shipbuilding, provisioning, brokerage, taxation, and markets.

Section 45.13

Tunis, Tripoli, and the Markets of the Maghreb

Tunis was a large manufacturing and trading city, with markets that served both the Mediterranean and the interior. Its woolen cap industry was among the most famous of the Ottoman world: caps made in Tunis were exported widely, and the trade employed spinners, knitters, dyers, finishers, and merchants. The city's merchant communities included Muslims, Jews (among them, from the seventeenth century, Livornese Jews connected to Italian trade), Andalusi families, and Europeans, and its exports included olive oil, grain, wool, and manufactured goods, with imports of European cloth, metals, and other products. Tunis's status as a provincial capital under Ottoman suzerainty, and the evolving political arrangements that governed it, are treated in Chapters 44 and 47.

Tripoli, a smaller port, drew its importance from its position as the northern terminus of Saharan routes through Fezzan to Bornu and the central Sahel. Caravans carried gold, slaves, ivory, natron, ostrich feathers, and other goods northward and returned with textiles, metalwork, glass, and other manufactured items. Oasis towns such as Murzuq in Fezzan and Ghadames served as stations, and the pastoral communities of the Sahara, including Tuareg and Tubu groups, supplied animals, guides, and protection. The trans-Saharan trade also involved the sale of enslaved Africans, whose forced migration to North Africa and the Mediterranean forms part of the region's commercial history and cannot be separated from its social history. Tripoli's own corsairing resembled that of Algiers and Tunis.

North African economic life cannot be reduced to European maritime exchange. Regional markets linked coastal cities with the interior; caravans moved between the Maghreb and West and Central Africa; and merchants from North Africa traded in Cairo, the Hijaz, and the Sahel. Earlier phases of this trans-Saharan system are discussed in Chapters 32 and 39. The Ottoman regencies of Algiers, Tunis, and Tripoli differed in their relations with Istanbul, in their internal politics, and in their commercial orientations, and the commercial opportunities of each port depended on a combination of Mediterranean and Saharan conditions. Comparing them shows that the Ottoman Maghreb was a region of several distinct trading systems, not a single economy.

Tunis's artisans illustrate how a manufacturing industry could organize a city's commerce. The makers of woolen caps (shashiyyas), whose products were sold across North Africa, the Ottoman Mediterranean, and beyond, worked in a specialized market district with its own guild, and the industry employed workers in spinning, knitting, fulling, dyeing, and finishing. Andalusi immigrants, who settled in Tunisia in large numbers after the Spanish expulsions of 1609–1614, brought skills in agriculture and crafts and strengthened the city's textile industry. The city's Jewish community included both long-established local families and, from the seventeenth century, merchants of Livornese origin who maintained commercial links with Italian ports, and these groups played a major role in Mediterranean trade. European merchants, including French and Italian traders, resided in the city under privileges and exported olive oil, grain, wool, and other goods.

Tripoli's hinterland connections, by contrast, extended south. Merchants in the city organized caravans to Fezzan and Bornu, dealing with the rulers of oasis towns and with pastoral groups who guided the caravans through the desert, and they sold European cloth, metalwork, and other goods in exchange for the commodities of the Sahel. The scale of these Saharan routes varied with political conditions in the desert, the strength of local rulers, and the demand for gold and slaves. The movement of enslaved Africans across the Sahara and their sale in North African and Mediterranean markets forms a painful part of the region's commercial history, and students should treat it as integral to, not separate from, the economic life of the period.

Section 45.14

Mediterranean Commerce: Shipping, Merchants, and European Connections

The Ottoman Mediterranean was a zone of trade and war at the same time. Venetians were the principal European traders with the Levant in the early sixteenth century, buying spices, cotton, silk, and other goods in Alexandria, Beirut, and Aleppo. French merchants, helped by diplomatic ties with the Ottoman state, expanded their presence from the sixteenth century. English merchants, organized in the Levant Company from 1581, and Dutch merchants established themselves in the late sixteenth and seventeenth centuries. The legal framework for their presence consisted of privileges, usually called capitulations (ahdnames), granted by the sultan. The 1569 grant to France is firmly documented, whereas the agreement traditionally dated to 1536 has a disputed documentary status, and historians treat it with caution; English privileges date from 1580. These grants defined customs rates, the legal status of merchants, and the role of consuls, and the sultan granted them partly for diplomatic reasons and partly because European merchants brought revenue and supplied goods, particularly metals, cloth, and, in some periods, silver.

The mechanics of Mediterranean trade deserve explanation. A European merchant house established a factor or consul in a port such as Alexandria or Aleppo, who purchased goods through local brokers and loaded them on ships; the goods traveled in convoys or individually, subject to weather, piracy, and the risk of wars; insurance and credit were arranged in European financial centers; and customs duties were paid at ports of entry and exit under the privileges in force. Ships were relatively small by later standards, voyages were seasonal, and cargoes were typically a mixture of bulk goods and high-value items. Free ports such as Livorno, established in the 1590s as a trading hub, and Marseille, which gained prominence in the seventeenth century, became major nodes in these exchanges, and Ottoman Jewish, Greek, and Armenian merchants also used them.

Commerce coexisted with naval conflict and corsair activity, and trade often continued in the intervals between hostilities. European merchants relied on Ottoman subjects as brokers, interpreters, ship captains, and suppliers, and Ottoman merchants sailed and traded on their own account. European archives, notably consular and company records, preserve rich evidence of this trade, but historians caution against inferring that Europeans controlled the Levant trade, since those archives naturally emphasize European activity. For Arab cities, European demand for silk, cotton, and other goods created opportunities, while imports of European textiles and the pressure of European mercantilist policies created competition in some sectors, a subject Masters analyzes for Aleppo. The overall effect varied by city, commodity, and period, and the evidence does not support a single account of Mediterranean commerce as either wholly beneficial or wholly damaging to Arab economies.

Section 45.15

The Red Sea: Suez, Jeddah, Suakin, and Maritime Exchange

The Red Sea was a difficult body of water to navigate. Its northern reaches have contrary winds, reefs, and few good harbors; larger cargoes were often transferred between vessels at intermediate ports; and seasonal wind patterns determined sailing schedules. Suez, at the northern end, connected the sea to Cairo by a desert road that caravans crossed in a few days, and Ottoman Egypt maintained an arsenal and shipping base at Suez, importing timber, iron, and other materials from elsewhere to build and repair vessels. Jeddah, on the Hijaz coast, was the port of Mecca and the central market of the Red Sea; Yanbu served Medina; Suakin and Massawa served the African coast; and Yemeni ports, Mocha chief among them, connected the Red Sea to the Indian Ocean.

Egypt, the Hijaz, Yemen, and the African coast depended on one another for grain, coffee, textiles, metals, and other goods. Egypt supplied grain and other provisions to the Holy Cities, an important part of the pilgrimage economy discussed in §45.19; Yemen supplied coffee; India supplied textiles, spices, and other commodities carried by Indian and Arab ships; and Egypt sent cloth, metals, and manufactured goods in return. Jeddah's importance came from its dual role as a pilgrimage port and a commercial entrepôt, where Indian, Yemeni, Egyptian, and other merchants met each year; customs revenues at Jeddah were substantial and were divided among local and imperial authorities. Ottoman naval and administrative activity in the Red Sea in the sixteenth century, discussed in Chapter 43, responded to the Portuguese challenge, and the region stayed commercially active in later centuries.

Historians have shown that the Red Sea spice trade revived by the middle of the sixteenth century, after the initial disruption caused by Portuguese action in the Indian Ocean; Frederic Lane's research on the Venetian spice trade was an early contribution to this revision. The revival did not last indefinitely, and by the seventeenth century coffee had become the dominant Red Sea commodity, but the sequence shows that the Red Sea route adapted to changing conditions rather than collapsing. African connections, including the trade in enslaved people, ivory, and gold through Suakin and Massawa, formed another strand of Red Sea commerce, and these ports linked the Sudanese and Ethiopian interior to Egypt and Arabia.

The practicalities of Red Sea shipping help explain the sea's commercial organization. Vessels of the region, including those known as jalbas and sambuqs, were built with sewn or nailed planking, sailed according to the seasonal winds, and typically hugged the coasts, anchoring at night in sheltered harbors. Large vessels from India reached Jeddah or Mocha, where goods were transferred to smaller craft or to the camels of the pilgrimage roads. Timber for shipbuilding was scarce, so Egypt imported wood and iron for the Suez fleet, linking the arsenal to Mediterranean and Anatolian sources of timber. The Ottomans also established a presence at Suakin and Massawa on the African coast in the sixteenth century, which tied the Red Sea's western shore to Egypt and to the commerce in ivory, gold, and enslaved people.

Jeddah's commercial society was cosmopolitan. Indian merchants, including Gujarati traders, maintained agents there, Egyptian and Yemeni merchants bought and sold coffee and textiles, and the town's customs administration, shared among local and imperial authorities, collected duties from ships and from caravans. The arrival of pilgrim ships and caravans created a seasonal peak in trade, with coffee, spices, textiles, and other goods circulating among the merchants who gathered there. Because Jeddah served both pilgrimage and commerce, its fortunes depended on the same regional security and shipping conditions as the Red Sea as a whole.

Section 45.16

Yemen, Mocha, Coffee, and Indian Ocean Trade

For roughly two centuries, Yemen was the principal source of the world's coffee. Coffee was grown on highland terraces, picked and dried by cultivators, carried by caravan to coastal ports, and exported through Mocha, which, in Nancy Um's account, gained prominence as a seaport under the Ottomans in the mid-sixteenth century. The Ottomans held parts of Yemen from 1538, but the Qasimi imams ousted them in 1635, after which, as Um shows, Mocha's trade turned eastward toward the Indian Ocean and western India. Mocha's coffee boom of the seventeenth and early eighteenth centuries therefore took place largely under local rule, not Ottoman administration, even though coffee continued to flow to Ottoman markets through the Red Sea.

Mocha was a hub in a broad trading system. Merchants and shipowners from Asian, African, and European shores gathered there to trade coffee and aromatics, Indian textiles, Asian spices, and New World silver. The records of the Dutch and English East India Companies document their purchases, and Um's study of the merchant houses of Mocha uses these sources together with Arabic records, travel accounts, and surviving architecture to explore how merchants, ship captains (nakhudhas), brokers, and officials worked together. Her later book, Shipped but Not Sold, examines goods such as horses, textiles, and ceremonial gifts that were part of the social protocols of trade at Mocha. These studies show that trade involved relationships, gifts, and ceremony as well as price bargaining, and that Indian merchants, notably those from Gujarat, were prominent in the port.

Mocha was part of a wider world. Coffee shipped north to Jeddah and Suez reached Cairo, where merchants distributed it across the empire; coffee shipped east reached India and the Persian Gulf. Oman, outside Ottoman rule, developed its own maritime networks; after the expulsion of the Portuguese from Muscat in 1650, Omani ships became active in the Indian Ocean and along the East African coast. The Gulf's pearl fisheries and Basra's date and textile trade linked Iraq and Arabia to India. These connections show that Arab commerce was not confined to Ottoman territory, and students should distinguish areas under Ottoman rule from independent or autonomous trading states such as Qasimi Yemen and Oman. From the early eighteenth century, competition from coffee grown elsewhere, including Java, where commercial harvesting was documented by 1719, and later the Caribbean, gradually reduced Yemen's dominance. The change was a gradual erosion, not a sudden collapse, and Yemeni coffee remained important well into the century.

Historical Debate

Did Changing Global Trade Routes Weaken Ottoman Arab Commerce, or Encourage Adaptation?

The question: After the Portuguese reached India by sea in 1498, and as Dutch and English companies expanded, did Ottoman Arab cities lose their commercial position?

Interpretation A — Diversion and decline: Older accounts argued that the Cape route redirected the spice trade away from the Red Sea and Levant, impoverishing Cairo, Aleppo, and other entrepôts, and that Ottoman commerce declined as European maritime power rose.
Interpretation B — Adaptation and new opportunities: More recent scholarship stresses that Red Sea and overland routes stayed active, that spices continued to reach Cairo and Aleppo for long periods, and that new commodities, notably coffee and Iranian silk, created fresh fortunes. It also points to a growing regional trade and to the vitality of cities such as Aleppo and Cairo.

Evidence: Customs accounts, court registers, merchant papers, and European company records all bear on the question, but each has gaps, and quantifying trade volumes is difficult. European archives may exaggerate European roles, while local records may under-record unregistered trade.

Why it matters: If decline was early and inevitable, Ottoman Arab economies appear passive. If adaptation was common, the later nineteenth-century transformations (Chapters 49 and 50) must be explained by different, later causes. This chapter follows the view that the evidence supports adaptation, while recognizing that some trades, regions, and periods did suffer.

Section 45.17

Overland Caravans, Transport, and the Geography of Trade

Caravans moved most goods between the inland cities discussed above, and understanding their mechanics explains how commerce actually worked. In desert regions the camel was the principal pack animal, carrying loads that mules or donkeys could not manage over waterless stretches, while mules and donkeys served in hilly or settled areas. Large caravans traveled together for protection and efficiency, and travel was scheduled according to seasons, water availability, and pilgrimage dates. A caravan had a leader, guides, animal handlers, and merchants who owned or shipped goods; some merchants traveled with their goods, while others sent agents or shipped consignments through carriers. Caravans stopped at khans and caravanserais, paid tolls and customs, and relied on desert wells and, in some regions, fortified water stations.

Caravan trade was a partnership between settled and pastoral peoples. Bedouin and other pastoral communities supplied camels, guides, and escorts, and some profited by selling animals and services; others demanded payments for safe passage, sometimes described by terms such as khuwwa or khafara, whose precise meaning and form varied by region. Merchants and officials negotiated with tribal leaders, and when agreements broke down, caravans could be raided. Security on the roads thus depended on a combination of imperial authority, local arrangements, and the interests of the pastoral groups who lived along the routes. Chapter 44 treats the government of tribal groups, and Chapter 47 treats their political role in the eighteenth century; here the key fact is that these relationships were commercial as well as political.

Principal routes included the road from Aleppo across the Syrian desert and along the Euphrates to Baghdad, the Damascus–Baghdad route, the roads between Syria and Egypt through Palestine, the pilgrimage roads across northern Arabia, and the Saharan routes from Tripoli, Tunis, and Algiers to the Sahel. Transport costs, tolls, and the risk of delay or loss entered into merchants' calculations, and merchants financed long-distance caravans through partnerships like the 1614 contract discussed in the Primary Source Window. The movement of goods between cities thus depended on a layered system of animals, laborers, protectors, brokers, financiers, and legal arrangements, and the performance of that system was a major determinant of urban prosperity.

The financing and management of caravans reveal the layers of the system. A caravan leader (in Ottoman usage, a kafile basi or equivalent) organized the journey, hired guides and handlers, negotiated payments to protectors, and kept order. Merchants shipping goods paid freight to camel owners or contractors, and they bore the risk of loss, which they could reduce by traveling in large groups, by buying protection, or by spreading consignments across several caravans. Credit played an important role: partnerships such as the 1614 contract financed the purchase of goods, and sales on credit at the destination allowed merchants to turn over capital. Journeys between Aleppo and Baghdad could take several weeks, with stops at wells and caravanserais, and delays or losses affected prices in the cities at either end.

Different regions had distinct transport patterns. In the Fertile Crescent, caravans moved along routes connected to rivers and oases, with Bedouin groups such as the Anaza living along the desert margin and supplying camels. In Arabia, the pilgrimage routes carried commercial traffic as well as pilgrims, and Hijaz and Najd merchants moved goods along them. In North Africa and the Sahara, camel caravans organized by merchants and pastoral groups connected the coast to oasis towns and to the Sahel, while in Egypt the Nile carried most heavy freight, with caravans linking Cairo to Suez, Sinai, and the Red Sea coast. Across all these routes, the commercial system depended on relationships between merchants and mobile pastoral groups, a point that explains why historians increasingly treat nomads as participants in the economy and not as outsiders to it (see Chapter 3 for the earlier discussion of this historiographical issue).

Section 45.18

Organizing the Hajj: The Cairo and Damascus Caravans

The two great pilgrimage caravans, from Cairo and from Damascus, were among the largest organized movements of people in the early modern Middle East. Each gathered pilgrims from across the Ottoman world and beyond: Anatolians, Syrians, and Iraqis and Iranians joined the Damascus caravan, while Egyptians, North Africans, and West Africans, who often traveled through Cairo, joined the Egyptian one. The caravans departed at fixed times in the pilgrimage calendar, moved at a pace set by water and pasture, and returned months later. Suraiya Faroqhi's study of the Ottoman hajj and Karl Barbir's work on Damascus are among the principal scholarly foundations for understanding their organization, and the Damascus route is particularly well documented in archaeological research on the forts along the road.

Each caravan was led by an amir al-hajj, appointed by imperial authorities. In Damascus the office was often held by the governor or by an official closely connected to him; between 1725 and 1757, members of the Azm family nearly monopolized both the governorship and the leadership of the caravan, a fact that tied the pilgrimage to provincial politics (Chapter 47). The amir al-hajj commanded the escort, oversaw provisioning, and distributed payments to Bedouin leaders. Ottoman sultans, beginning with Suleyman I, relied on three measures to protect the Damascus caravan: an armed, mounted escort, payments to tribes (surre), and the construction of forts. The first Ottoman fort on the road was built in 1531, followed in 1559 by fortresses at Qatrana, Ma'an, and Dhat al-Hajj, and in the 1570s by forts at 'Unayza and Hadiya; in all, some fifteen forts were built or refurbished between Damascus and Aqaba, with their primary function the protection of water supplies. Three more were added in the eighteenth century, by which time Bedouin groups had acquired firearms, making them more dangerous to the slow-moving caravan.

Financing and provisioning required elaborate coordination. The funds for the caravan's protection and supply came in large part from taxes collected in the province, including during an annual tax-collecting tour by the governor, earmarked for the hajj. These funds paid for escorts, supplies, and payments to Bedouin chiefs, who were also given gifts and sometimes appointed to roles in the caravan's protection. The first major stopping place at Muzayrib, southwest of Damascus, held a large open market during the pilgrimage and had a fort where officials collected fees and the amir al-hajj distributed money to tribal leaders. Pilgrims bought animals, water containers, food, and other supplies in Damascus and Muzayrib, and merchants traveled with the caravan to trade along the road and in the Hijaz. The Cairo caravan, with its own amir al-hajj, followed routes through Sinai and the Red Sea coast to the Hijaz, carried the ceremonial litter (mahmal) that symbolized the sultan's patronage, and was likewise financed by imperial funds and endowments, with Cairo traditionally producing the cloth covering of the Ka'ba.

The system was always vulnerable to breakdown. When payments to tribal groups were delayed, when governors embezzled or mismanaged funds, when drought or epidemic struck, or when Bedouin groups saw greater advantage in plunder than in payment, caravans could be attacked or suffer from shortages. In 1757 the Damascus caravan was attacked by Bedouin forces on its return journey, and large numbers of pilgrims were killed and the caravan plundered. The disaster shook confidence in the pilgrimage's organization and had political repercussions in Damascus. Chapter 46 treats the religious meaning of the hajj; for present purposes, the caravans should be understood as complex logistical operations whose success depended on money, supplies, protection, and negotiation.

Historical Vocabulary
Amir al-Hajj and Surra

The amir al-hajj ("commander of the pilgrimage") was the official responsible for leading a caravan and securing its protection, supplies, and discipline. The surra refers to the annual gifts and funds sent to the Holy Cities, and in the pilgrimage economy the term sits alongside separate payments to tribes along the way. These arrangements mattered because they converted a religious journey into a state-supported logistical operation. Modern "tour leader" or "tax" only partly captures their meaning.

Section 45.19

Mecca and Medina: Pilgrimage Cities and Urban Economies

Mecca and Medina depended heavily on the arrival of pilgrims and on the imports that sustained the pilgrimage. Mecca had very limited agricultural resources, while Medina possessed an oasis economy with date gardens and cultivated land. Nevertheless, neither city's local grain supply was sufficient to sustain its population and the seasonal influx of pilgrims, and both imported much of their grain and other supplies, much of it from Egypt through the Red Sea ports of Jeddah and Yanbu and by caravan. Imperial and charitable arrangements supported this supply: grain shipments from Egypt and the funds known as surra, sent annually with the caravans, helped provision the Holy Cities and support their inhabitants. Charitable distributions of food and money, funded by endowments, also supported residents and visitors, and the city's large number of scholars, students, and religious functionaries received stipends.

During the pilgrimage season, the cities' economies expanded dramatically. Pilgrims required lodging, food, water, transport animals, and guides; local households rented rooms; brokers and guides (mutawwifs, who also assisted pilgrims with ritual arrangements) organized services; and merchants sold goods brought by caravans and ships. Pilgrims were also traders, and the pilgrimage period functioned as a fair at which coffee, spices, textiles, and other goods changed hands. Prices rose when pilgrims arrived and fell when they left, and residents' incomes depended on that cycle. Pilgrims came from a vast area: Anatolia, Syria, Iraq, Iran, Egypt, North and West Africa, Yemen, India, and Southeast Asia, so Mecca and Medina were among the most cosmopolitan cities of the Muslim world.

Relations among the various groups were shaped by power and interest. The sharifs of Mecca governed the Hijaz under Ottoman suzerainty and received a share of customs and other revenue; Ottoman officials, including judges and the commanders of caravan escorts, represented the sultan; merchants, scholars, and long-term residents from across the Muslim world formed communities in the cities; and nomadic groups of the Hijaz supplied animals and services. The religious institutions of the Holy Cities are discussed in Chapter 46, and Arabia's eighteenth-century political transformation in Chapter 48. The point for this chapter is that the Holy Cities were urban economies dependent on a complex web of imperial support, long-distance trade, and annual pilgrim spending.

Estimates of how many pilgrims reached the Holy Cities each year are uncertain, and figures in sources vary widely; the caravans from Cairo and Damascus are usually described as comprising thousands to tens of thousands of people, but historians treat such numbers with caution because contemporary observers often estimated roughly and pilgrim numbers fluctuated with security, plague, and politics. What is clear is that the pilgrimage was a very large seasonal movement relative to the resident population of Mecca and Medina, and that its timing set the rhythm of life. When caravans were delayed, plundered, or reduced, the effects reached markets, rents, and food supplies. When they arrived safely, the cities profited, and returning pilgrims carried goods and news to their home regions.

The local economy included many specialized roles. Guides (mutawwifs) and those who supplied water from the Zamzam well and other sources served pilgrims; innkeepers and landlords rented rooms; merchants sold coffee, food, textiles, and other goods; and porters and camel owners provided transport. Enslaved people worked in households and in some trades, and the Holy Cities received enslaved persons from African and other regions through the Red Sea and overland routes. Wealthy residents and scholars received stipends and gifts, while poorer residents depended on charitable distributions. The effect was an economy highly sensitive to the pilgrimage cycle and to imperial and charitable support.

Medina's economy had a somewhat different character from Mecca's. The oasis supported date gardens and agriculture, which supplied part of the city's food, and the city's relations with Bedouin groups of the surrounding region influenced the security of supplies and roads. Pilgrims visiting the Prophet's mosque, whether before or after the pilgrimage, also supported local lodging and services. Both cities relied on the Red Sea ports, especially Jeddah for Mecca and Yanbu for Medina, for imported goods, and the commercial links among the Holy Cities, their ports, and the caravan routes were the foundation of the Hijaz economy. The imperial commitment to this system, including the provision of grain and funds, is part of the larger story of Ottoman legitimacy examined in Chapters 44 and 46.

Section 45.20

Other Pilgrimage Routes and Sacred Urban Centers

Pilgrimage in the Ottoman Arab world was not limited to Mecca and Medina. Jerusalem attracted Muslim, Christian, and Jewish pilgrims, with Christian pilgrims arriving especially for Easter and traveling by sea to Jaffa or overland by Damascus; Hebron's tomb of Abraham drew Muslim and Jewish visitors; and the shrines of saints and holy men across Syria, Egypt, and North Africa drew regional visitors. In Egypt, the annual festival of a saint such as Sayyid Ahmad al-Badawi at Tanta combined devotion with a large commercial fair, with traders, entertainers, and craftsmen gathering from the Delta and beyond, and similar festivals took place elsewhere. These pilgrimages generated demand for lodging, food, transport, and goods, and they encouraged markets, hospices, and services in the towns concerned.

The Shi'a shrine cities of Najaf and Karbala in Iraq formed another major pilgrimage network. Pilgrims from Iran, Iraq, and elsewhere visited the tombs of Ali and Husayn, traveling through Ottoman territory, often across the Ottoman–Safavid frontier. The flows of travelers brought income to the shrine towns and to Baghdad and the river routes, supported lodging, guides, and transport, and also gave rise to the practice of transporting the dead for burial in sacred ground, which created its own economy of carriers and burial services. The political relations between the empires affected travel: wars and tensions could close routes or raise tolls, while periods of peace allowed larger flows. The doctrinal and communal aspects of Shi'a religious life are discussed in Chapter 46. For the purposes of this chapter, the important point is that pilgrimage was a diverse set of movements, each with its own logistics, patrons, and commercial effects, all contributing to the urban economies of the Ottoman Arab world.

Jerusalem's pilgrimage economy was organized through institutions and customs that connected visitors, local residents, and officials. Christian pilgrims from Europe and the Orthodox world generally arrived at Jaffa, traveled inland under escort, and lodged in hospices run by religious orders, notably the Franciscans, and in the quarters of other Christian communities; Ottoman officials collected fees and imposed taxes, and local Bedouin groups sometimes demanded payment for protection on the road from the coast. Jewish pilgrims visited Jerusalem, Hebron, Safed, and other holy sites, and the cities' Jewish communities were in part sustained by donations from abroad. The pilgrims' spending supported innkeepers, guides, artisans producing devotional objects, and suppliers of food and transport. The political and communal arrangements governing the holy places are examined in Chapter 46.

The Iraqi shrine cities attracted pilgrims at specific times of year, and the movements of pilgrims across the frontier were affected by Ottoman and Safavid policies, tolls, and the state of relations between the two empires. Funds from Iranian rulers and elites supported shrines and local institutions, and the shrine towns developed markets, caravanserais, and services for visitors. The movement of corpses for burial near the shrines created a specialized trade in transport and burial services. In Egypt and the Maghreb, shrine festivals and the tombs of saints formed regional pilgrimage circuits: the saint festivals of the Nile Delta, such as that at Tanta, attracted large crowds and brought together merchants, performers, and craftsmen in what were also major fairs. These examples show that pilgrimage in the Ottoman Arab world was not a single network but many overlapping ones, from the long-distance hajj to local shrine visits, each supporting its own economy of transport, lodging, and exchange.

Section 45.21

Continuity, Adaptation, and Change in Ottoman Urban Commerce

Looking across three centuries, the sixteenth century was a period of incorporation and consolidation. Ottoman rule joined Egypt, Syria, and the Hijaz, built forts and caravanserais along the main roads, and established the framework of customs and privileges that structured trade. The Red Sea spice trade, though challenged by the Portuguese, revived by mid-century; Aleppo's khans and markets grew; and Cairo remained a great commercial city. The sixteenth century also saw the foundation of Ottoman rule in Iraq and North Africa, with new frontiers and new ports.

The seventeenth century was shaped by new commodities and new politics. Coffee became a major Red Sea commodity; the English, Dutch, and French trading companies expanded their activity in the Indian Ocean and the Mediterranean; Mocha and the Qasimi state rose in Yemen after the Ottoman withdrawal of 1635; and the Ottoman–Safavid conflict, including the occupation of Baghdad in 1623–1638, disrupted Iraqi and Iranian trade. Cairo merchants such as Abu Taqiyya built great fortunes, Aleppo's silk trade continued despite the disruptions of war, and merchants such as those of the 1614 partnership connected Iraq, Syria, and Anatolia.

The eighteenth century saw greater regional autonomy and shifting patterns. In Cairo, coffee-based fortunes and military households shaped the market; in Damascus, the Azm governors invested in commerce and controlled the pilgrimage; in Palestine, towns such as Nablus and Acre developed export trades in soap and cotton; Yemeni coffee lost part of its market to Java and the Caribbean; French merchants became more prominent in Egyptian and Syrian trade; and the 1757 attack on the Damascus caravan revealed the fragility of the pilgrimage system. Wars, plague, and famine continued to cause hardship for ordinary people.

The evidence therefore does not support a simple narrative of continuous decline following the opening of the Cape route. Some trades contracted, some crafts struggled against imports, and some regions suffered from insecurity. But new commodities, new merchant communities, and regional specializations created growth elsewhere, and merchants adapted by shifting routes and goods. The urban and commercial institutions described here, including markets, khans, guilds, partnerships, courts, caravans, and pilgrimage logistics, formed the economic setting for the religious and communal life examined in Chapter 46 and the provincial political transformations of Chapter 47, and they provide the baseline against which the changes of the nineteenth century (Chapters 49 and 50) must be measured.

Comparing specific cities shows how differently commerce developed. Cairo's fortunes rested on its position between the Nile, the Red Sea, and the Mediterranean, and on the coffee trade, which sustained merchant fortunes in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. Aleppo's rested on caravans and Iranian silk, and its commerce was exposed to the fortunes of war between the Ottoman and Safavid states and to the shifting demands of European buyers. Mocha's rose with a single commodity and was vulnerable to the appearance of competing producers. Algiers combined maritime enterprise, including corsair activity, with ordinary Mediterranean commerce and was shaped by the diplomacy of its European partners and adversaries. Damascus's economy was bound up with the pilgrimage and its fragile security arrangements. None of these experiences can stand for the whole.

The comparison also highlights shared features. Everywhere, merchants relied on partnerships, brokers, and courts; artisans on guilds and local markets; cities on hinterland supply and on the cooperation of pastoral groups; and states on customs and the protection of routes. Everywhere, too, commerce involved people whose experience was far from prosperous: porters, caravan workers, captives, and enslaved laborers. Recognizing both the diversity of regional experience and the common institutional foundation is the central task of the historian of Ottoman Arab commerce, and it is the reason this chapter has moved from city to city before drawing general conclusions.

Continuity and Change Over Time

How Ottoman Commerce Changed Between the Sixteenth and Eighteenth Centuries

What Changed

  • Coffee became a dominant commodity and coffeehouses a new urban institution
  • European companies and consuls increased their presence
  • Regional elites invested more heavily in commercial property

What Stayed the Same

  • Cities depended on hinterlands, caravans, and ports
  • Courts, partnerships, and khans structured trade
  • Pilgrimage remained a major annual economic event

Who experienced change most directly? Merchants in coffee and silk, and artisans exposed to imported goods. Who experienced continuity? Many porters, small shopkeepers, and farmers. Why it matters: It shows commerce as adaptive, with uneven effects across social groups.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that Ottoman rule linked existing urban and commercial networks across the Arab provinces without making them economically uniform or wholly dependent on Istanbul. Cairo, Damascus, Aleppo, Baghdad, Basra, Mosul, Algiers, Tunis, Tripoli, Jeddah, and Mocha each occupied a distinct place in a system of rivers, seas, deserts, and roads.

Cities were organized around markets, khans, workshops, neighborhoods, and water infrastructure, much of it financed by endowments and rental income. Their economies combined craft production, guild or occupational organization, merchant partnerships, credit, and courts, and they depended on hinterlands that supplied grain, cotton, wool, and labor. Provisioning and price supervision were matters of public concern, and coffee created both a major commodity and a new social space.

Cairo was the great metropolis, tied to the Nile, the Red Sea, and the Mediterranean, and enriched in later centuries by the coffee trade. Damascus joined manufacture, caravan commerce, and the pilgrimage; Aleppo became a major entrepôt for Iranian silk, with Armenian, local, and European merchants. Palestinian towns formed a network of specialized economies, while Iraq's cities faced inland routes, the Gulf, and the Iranian frontier. North African ports such as Algiers, Tunis, and Tripoli combined Mediterranean commerce, corsair activity, crafts, and Saharan connections.

International routes were not simply conduits for European influence. Venetian, French, English, and Dutch merchants operated under Ottoman privileges, but they relied on local brokers, interpreters, and suppliers, and Ottoman subjects also sailed and traded. The Red Sea and Indian Ocean connected Egypt, the Hijaz, Yemen, India, Oman, and East Africa, and Mocha shows how an Indian Ocean port outside direct Ottoman rule could become central to Arab commerce.

Caravans gave overland trade its structure, and their dependence on Bedouin guides, animals, and agreements showed the economic partnership between settled and pastoral peoples. The Cairo and Damascus hajj caravans were great logistical and fiscal operations, sustaining routes, markets, and the economies of Mecca and Medina. Other pilgrimage centers, including Jerusalem, Najaf, and Karbala, similarly generated trade and services.

The evidence for this history comes from court registers, merchant papers, customs accounts, consular archives, travelers' narratives, and buildings. Each source reveals some groups and hides others, which is why historians combine them and remain cautious about quantitative claims.

The chapter challenged the claim that Ottoman Arab commerce simply declined after the Cape route opened. Some trades suffered and some regions faced hardship, but new commodities, merchant communities, and regional specializations created growth in other places, and the debate shows how interpretations depend on evidence and sources.

Women, minorities, artisans, porters, caravan workers, pilgrims, and captives all experienced this commercial world differently. Prosperity for merchants did not mean equal benefit for all, and forced labor and captivity were part of Mediterranean and African commerce.

Finally, these urban and commercial institutions formed the setting for the religious and communal life of Chapter 46 and the provincial political transformations of Chapter 47, and they provide the baseline against which the changes of Chapters 49 and 50 should be measured.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it reveals the Ottoman Arab world as a working economy rather than only a political structure. Cities, markets, caravans, ports, and pilgrimage routes show how provincial societies produced, traded, and traveled, and how imperial authority interacted with long-established local networks.

It also reveals processes that shaped later developments. The wealth and investments of merchants and military households help explain the provincial power of Chapter 47. The institutions of urban commerce and pilgrimage formed the setting for the communal life of Chapter 46. The adaptation of merchants to new commodities and routes provides the baseline for understanding the transformations of the nineteenth century in Chapters 49 and 50.

Finally, the chapter connects regions: Egypt to Yemen and India, Syria to Iran and Anatolia, Iraq to the Gulf, the Maghreb to the Sahara and the Mediterranean. These links show that the history of the Arab world cannot be told from one capital or one frontier, and that it was always part of wider worlds in which Arab merchants, artisans, pilgrims, and rulers were active participants.
