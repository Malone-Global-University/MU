Part VIII — The Ottoman Arab World

The Ottoman Conquest of the Arab Lands

A History of the Arab World: From Ancient Crossroads to the Modern Arab World

Chapter Opening

Chapter 42 ended on the eve of a transformation: a Mamluk Sultanate that still controlled Egypt, Syria, and the guardianship of the Hijaz, an Ottoman state growing in Anatolia and the Balkans, a Safavid empire rising in Iran, and Portuguese ships reaching the Indian Ocean. This chapter explains what happened next. Within about sixty years, most of the central and eastern Arab lands, and a substantial part of the Arab Mediterranean coast, came under Ottoman authority. They did not come under it in one way, at one moment, or by one method.

Among the people who lived through the fall of Cairo in January 1517 was Muhammad ibn Ahmad ibn Iyas, a Cairene chronicler whose long, day-by-day history of Egypt is among the most valuable surviving records of the Mamluk Sultanate's last decades. Ibn Iyas was not a soldier or a courtier. He recorded prices, rumors, proclamations, fires, killings, and the anxieties of a city that had been ruled by the same military system for more than two and a half centuries. His account matters for this chapter because it reminds students that the Ottoman conquest was experienced by living communities, and that the grand narratives later told about 1517 were assembled from many such moments.

The central problem of the chapter is how to describe expansion that was both rapid and uneven. Between 1514 and 1517, Sultan Selim I defeated the Safavids at Chaldiran, destroyed a Mamluk army at Marj Dabiq, occupied Aleppo and Damascus, and took Cairo after a hard battle at Ridaniya. These campaigns are often compressed into the phrase "the conquest of 1517," as though the whole Arab world changed hands in a single stroke. The historical record is more complicated. The Hijaz entered the Ottoman orbit through negotiated recognition of suzerainty by the sharif of Mecca. Baghdad was taken only in 1534, after a long contest with the Safavids, and Basra was brought under firmer control in 1546. Yemen, Aden, and the Red Sea were contested against the Portuguese and local rulers across decades. In the Maghreb, the story began with Muslim corsair brothers from the Aegean who were invited by the people of Algiers to confront Spain, and the final Ottoman conquest of Tunis came as late as 1574.

A second problem concerns agency. Ottoman victory is sometimes explained as the triumph of a gunpowder empire over an archaic cavalry aristocracy. That explanation contains some truth but is incomplete, and this chapter examines it critically. Mamluk emirs, Syrian notables, Egyptian merchants and scholars, Arab tribal leaders, Hijazi sharifs, Kurdish chiefs, Maghrebi rulers, Algiers townsmen, Zaydi imams, and Portuguese commanders all shaped what happened. Local actors were not only victims or bystanders. Many negotiated, defected, resisted, or sought advantage, and their choices determined how Ottoman authority was established in each region.

A third problem concerns legitimacy. The sultans who now governed Cairo, Damascus, and, in a different way, Mecca and Medina presented themselves as defenders of Sunni Islam against the Shi'i Safavids and as protectors of the pilgrimage and the holy cities. This chapter distinguishes what Ottoman rulers claimed at the time from later tradition, in particular the popular story that the last Abbasid caliph in Cairo formally transferred the caliphate to Selim I in 1517. The evidence for that ceremony is much later than the events it describes, and historians therefore treat it as a tradition requiring analysis, not as an established fact.

The chapter relies on several kinds of evidence. Arabic chronicles such as Ibn Iyas's account of Egypt and the Damascene chronicles of Ibn Tulun and others give local perspectives. Ottoman court chronicles, campaign diaries, and imperial registers give the viewpoint of the conquering state. European, Venetian, Portuguese, and Spanish reports and diplomatic correspondence illuminate the Mediterranean and Indian Ocean contests. Coins, inscriptions, fortifications, and surviving architecture add material evidence. Each source type has its own purpose and bias, and students are encouraged to ask who wrote it, for whom, and what it leaves out.

The chapter's boundaries are deliberate. It explains how Ottoman authority was acquired, region by region. It does not describe in detail how that authority worked once established, for that belongs to Chapter 44. Nor does it fully treat Ottoman-era cities, commerce, and pilgrimage (Chapter 45), religious and communal life (Chapter 46), or the rise of provincial households and local dynasties (Chapter 47). It builds directly on Chapter 42's portrait of the Arab world around 1500, and it ends by explaining why the political order created in these decades set the terms for the imperial-provincial world of the next three centuries.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain the strategic, political, and religious circumstances that led the Ottoman state to attack the Mamluk Sultanate after Chaldiran.
  2. Trace the campaigns of 1516–1517 through Marj Dabiq, Aleppo, Damascus, Gaza, Ridaniya, and Cairo.
  3. Analyze the competing explanations for the Mamluk defeat, including military organization, factional division, economic strain, and strategic decisions.
  4. Distinguish military conquest, negotiated allegiance, and nominal suzerainty as different ways in which Ottoman authority was established.
  5. Explain why the sharifs of Mecca recognized Ottoman suzerainty and why the Hijaz was not taken as Cairo was.
  6. Evaluate the tradition that the last Abbasid caliph transferred the caliphate to Selim I, and explain why historians treat it as a later tradition.
  7. Trace the separate Ottoman–Safavid contest for Iraq from 1508 through the capture of Baghdad in 1534, the consolidation at Basra in 1546, and the settlement of 1555.
  8. Describe Ottoman–Portuguese rivalry in the Red Sea and Indian Ocean, including the expedition of 1538 and its limits.
  9. Compare the incorporation of Algiers, Tripoli, and Tunis, identifying the roles of local rulers, corsair leaders, Spain, and the Ottoman navy.
  10. Explain why Morocco remained outside Ottoman rule and why other regions lay beyond effective Ottoman control.
  11. Assess the continuities and changes that Ottoman expansion produced in the Arab political order.
  12. Interpret contemporary chronicles and later traditions as distinct kinds of historical evidence.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did an Ottoman state that had been focused on Europe and the Safavid frontier turn against the Mamluk Sultanate in 1516?
  2. How much of the Ottoman victory should be attributed to weapons and organization, and how much to Mamluk divisions and strategic misjudgments?
  3. Why did the incorporation of Syria, Egypt, the Hijaz, Iraq, Yemen, and North Africa follow such different patterns?
  4. What role did local actors, such as Mamluk defectors, Hijazi sharifs, Kurdish emirs, Algiers townsmen, and Zaydi imams, play in determining the outcome of Ottoman expansion?
  5. How did religious legitimacy, especially defense of Sunni Islam and protection of the holy cities, support Ottoman claims, and how should historians assess the later story of a transferred caliphate?
  6. In what ways did the Ottoman, Safavid, Spanish, and Portuguese empires compete for the Arab lands, and why were Arab regions central to those contests?
  7. What is the difference between claiming a territory, defeating its ruler, and exercising practical control over it?
  8. How does the evidence available to historians, Arabic chronicles, Ottoman histories, European reports, shape the story of the conquests?

Historical Context

By 1500, the Arab world was politically divided among several powers. The Mamluk Sultanate, ruled from Cairo by a military elite drawn largely from purchased soldiers of Circassian and Turkic background, governed Egypt, Syria, and the Hijaz, and claimed leadership of the Sunni world as protector of the pilgrimage cities. The Abbasid line survived in Cairo as a ceremonial caliphate under Mamluk patronage (Chapter 36). Yemen was divided between the Tahirid sultans of the lowlands, whose main sultanate a Mamluk expedition overthrew in 1517, and the Zaydi imams of the highlands. Oman, the Gulf, and the Arabian interior contained local dynasties and tribal confederations. In the Maghreb, the Hafsids of Tunis, the Zayyanids of Tlemcen, and the Wattasids of Morocco held weakened but long-established states (Chapters 39 and 42), while Spain and Portugal, having completed the conquest of Granada in 1492, were pushing onto the North African coast.

The Ottoman state had by then grown from a frontier principality in northwestern Anatolia into a major empire. It had captured Constantinople in 1453, absorbed much of the Balkans and Anatolia, and fought an indecisive war with the Mamluks between 1485 and 1491 over borderlands in southern Anatolia and northern Syria. That war showed that the two states were neighbors with overlapping ambitions but did not end their rivalry. Chapter 42 described the political, economic, religious, and strategic landscape around 1500 and explained why Ottoman expansion would transform it. This chapter takes up the story where that analysis ends.

Three developments created the conditions for expansion. First, the rise of the Safavid state in Iran under Shah Ismail I after 1501 created a militant Shi'i power on the Ottoman and Mamluk frontiers and drew the attention of both Sunni states. Second, Portuguese entry into the Indian Ocean after 1498 threatened the Red Sea routes that supplied Mamluk revenue and Mediterranean commerce. Third, within the Mamluk state, fiscal strain, factional rivalry, and the decline of trade income reduced the capacity of the sultan to meet these threats. None of these developments made the Ottoman conquest inevitable. They created opportunities and pressures that Ottoman rulers chose to exploit.

Students should keep two cautions in mind. First, the term "conquest" in the chapter title covers very different processes, from the destruction of an army and a sultanate in Egypt to the recognition of suzerainty at Mecca and the incorporation of North African port cities through alliance with corsair leaders. Second, an Ottoman claim to a territory was not the same as practical control. Large parts of Arabia, the Sahara, Mount Lebanon, and Yemen's highlands remained effectively beyond direct Ottoman rule for long periods. Chapter 44 will examine how the empire governed the regions it did hold.

Section 43.1

From Frontier Rivalry to Imperial Conquest

The Ottoman–Mamluk relationship before 1516 was not simple hostility. For much of the fifteenth century the two Sunni sultanates exchanged embassies, competed for prestige, and quarreled over a shared borderland in southern Anatolia and northern Syria where small principalities, notably Dulkadir, shifted allegiance between them. The inconclusive war of 1485–1491 left both sides aware of the other's strength. The Mamluks had held the line, but they had not defeated the Ottomans, and the Ottomans had learned that Syria could not be won cheaply. The rivalry was also symbolic. The Mamluk sultan's title as protector of Mecca and Medina gave him a claim to leadership of the Sunni world, one that a rising Ottoman dynasty, newly master of Constantinople, increasingly wished to share or to claim for itself.

Strategic change made the old rivalry more dangerous. The Portuguese arrival in the Indian Ocean after 1498 threatened the spice and commercial routes that ran through the Red Sea and Egypt, an important source of Mamluk customs revenue. The Mamluk sultan Qansuh al-Ghawri responded by building up a Red Sea fleet and fortifying Jeddah. He recruited Mediterranean and Anatolian craftsmen, and he needed timber, metal, and skilled labor that were available largely through Ottoman territory. The Ottomans, who controlled the forests and arsenals of Anatolia, could therefore either aid or obstruct Mamluk defense. An empire that had been a distant neighbor was now a necessary supplier and a possible rival in the defense of the same holy places.

The second change was the Safavid emergence. Shah Ismail I, a charismatic Shi'i leader supported by Turkmen Qizilbash followers, established his rule in Tabriz in 1501 and by 1510 controlled all of Iran. His appeal reached across the Ottoman frontier into Anatolia, where many Turkmen communities admired him, and in 1508 he took Baghdad. For the Ottomans, the Safavid state posed an internal as well as external danger. For the Mamluks, it created a fear of a new power on the Euphrates. Both sultanates had reason to oppose it, but they could not agree on how, and each suspected the other of seeking advantage from the crisis.

Internal politics in the Ottoman state also mattered. Selim I came to the throne in 1512 after a succession struggle that ended with the removal of his father Bayezid II. His reign was characterized by determined, aggressive campaigning, and his military base, especially the janissaries, expected activity and reward. Historians caution against attributing the conquest to a single motive. Earlier interpretations emphasized religious zeal or the lure of Egyptian wealth. More recent work stresses a combination: the need to secure the southeastern frontier, the opportunity created by Mamluk weakness, the prestige of the holy cities, and the logic of an empire whose army had just won a great victory and could be redirected.

For students, the key point is that Ottoman conquest of Arab lands in 1516 was a decision, not a destiny. A different sequence of events, for example a prolonged Safavid war or a Mamluk–Safavid alliance, might have produced a different outcome. Contemporary sources reflect these uncertainties, and the reasons they give for the war, from Mamluk support for Ottoman enemies to the sultan's religious duty, partly reflect the efforts of later chroniclers to justify the decision. The following sections therefore treat the road to war as a series of linked choices.

Section 43.2

Selim I, the Safavid Challenge, and the Road to War

On 23 August 1514, Selim I's army met Shah Ismail's forces at Chaldiran in northwestern Iran. The Ottomans, who had marched far from their bases, relied on organized infantry with firearms and on field artillery, deployed behind defensive arrangements. The Safavid army, dominated by mounted Qizilbash warriors, charged with courage but could not break the Ottoman line. The Safavid defeat was serious but not decisive. Selim entered Tabriz briefly, then withdrew, partly because supply problems and unrest in his army made a longer occupation impractical. The Safavid state survived, and Shah Ismail remained a rival for decades.

Chaldiran did, however, change the strategic map. The battle stopped Safavid expansion westward and strengthened the Ottoman position in eastern Anatolia. In the following year, Ottoman commanders, assisted by a number of Kurdish emirs who were won over, in part through the diplomacy of the scholar Idris Bitlisi (Kurdish leaders did not act as a bloc, and their terms differed), brought much of the Diyarbakir region under Ottoman authority, and the Ottomans eliminated the Dulkadir principality, a long-time buffer between the empires that had leaned toward the Mamluks. These steps brought Ottoman power to the northern edge of Syria and the Jazira. They also demonstrated a pattern that would recur: the Ottomans advanced by combining battlefield pressure with alliances among local rulers, who were often given recognition and autonomy in exchange for loyalty.

The final decision to attack the Mamluks came in 1516. Ottoman sources claim that Sultan Qansuh al-Ghawri had been negotiating with Shah Ismail, offering diplomatic support against a common enemy and in effect taking the Safavid side. Mamluk sources and later scholarship suggest a more ambiguous picture. Al-Ghawri did correspond with Shah Ismail and with others, a normal feature of the diplomacy of the day, and he moved his army north to Aleppo in the summer of 1516, ostensibly to mediate between the sultans or to defend northern Syria. Whether the Mamluk move was defensive or a prelude to joining the Safavids remains disputed. What is clear is that an Ottoman army, preparing for a campaign that could plausibly be directed against either Safavids or Mamluks, found a justification for turning south.

That justification had a religious dimension. Ottoman legal scholars and court writers portrayed Selim's campaign as a defense of Sunni Islam against a Shi'i threat, and the Mamluk sultan's contacts with the Safavids as a betrayal of that cause. This argument was politically useful because it allowed the Ottomans to present war against a fellow Sunni sultanate as consistent with orthodox duty. It should not be mistaken for a simple account of motives. Selim's decision also reflected the military opportunity, the state of the army, and the strategic goal of eliminating a possible Safavid partner and securing the Syrian frontier.

Selim's army moved south through the Taurus passes in the summer of 1516, supported by a logistical system that drew on Anatolian grain and transport. Estimates of its size in the sources are inconsistent and often exaggerated, and historians prefer to speak in general terms. What matters is that the Ottoman force combined experienced janissary infantry, a powerful artillery train, provincial cavalry, and allies from the frontier, and that it was led by a sultan who had recently won a major victory. Against it, the Mamluks assembled a large and prestigious but differently organized army, which met the Ottomans north of Aleppo.

Section 43.3

The Battle of Marj Dabiq, 1516

The battle took place on 24 August 1516 on the plain of Marj Dabiq, north of Aleppo. The Mamluk army was commanded by Sultan Qansuh al-Ghawri, then about seventy-five years old, and included the sultan's own mamluks, other Mamluk households, Syrian contingents under the governors of Aleppo, Damascus, and other provinces, and auxiliaries including Arab tribal forces. Its core was a cavalry elite trained in horsemanship and archery, with considerable prestige and a tradition of victories against Mongols and Crusaders. The Ottoman army, by contrast, was a combined-arms force in which musketeers, artillery, and disciplined infantry were integrated with cavalry under tight central command.

Accounts of the battle differ in detail, but the general outline is clear. The armies deployed with the Mamluk left wing, under Khayr Bey, the governor of Aleppo, facing Ottoman troops on the Ottoman right, while Janbirdi al-Ghazali, governor of Damascus, held the opposing flank. Ottoman artillery and firearms inflicted heavy losses on the cavalry formations, and Ottoman commanders held their positions while the Mamluk charges lost momentum. At a critical stage, part of the Mamluk line gave way. Later accounts, both Ottoman and Arabic, say that Khayr Bey withdrew his forces from the fighting, and that he had been in secret contact with the Ottomans beforehand. The sultan himself died during or immediately after the battle, according to the sources from a stroke or exhaustion as the defeat became clear, though other versions exist.

The reliability of the reports of defection varies. Khayr Bey's later career as the first Ottoman governor of Egypt made the story of earlier collusion attractive to Ottoman writers, who could present it as a sign of divine favor or of the Mamluk elite's disloyalty. Mamluk and later critics used the same story to explain a defeat that was otherwise difficult to accept. Modern historians treat the allegations with caution. Some argue that contacts between Mamluk emirs and the Ottomans were plausible, given the long-standing uncertainty of Mamluk politics, while others stress that the story may have been shaped by later events. Regardless of the degree of prior arrangement, the collapse of the Mamluk line at a crucial moment was real.

Marj Dabiq mattered for several reasons. It destroyed the main Mamluk field army and killed the sultan, leaving the Mamluk state without its established leader. It opened Aleppo, and then Syria, to Ottoman occupation. And it raised a question that would recur throughout the campaign: how would the Mamluk elite, split among factions and households with different interests, respond to a conqueror who offered continued positions to those who submitted? In the weeks that followed, many Syrian commanders and officials accepted Ottoman authority. The defeat was thus both a military event and the beginning of a political realignment.

The battle is also used in debates about the "gunpowder empire" thesis, the idea that Ottoman victory resulted from superiority in firearms. Mamluk forces did possess firearms and artillery, and earlier historians, who emphasized Mamluk contempt for gunpowder weapons, may have overstated the gap. The more careful view is that Mamluk armies lacked the integrated organization of infantry, artillery, and cavalry that the Ottomans had developed, and that the contrast was as much institutional as technological. This issue is examined further in the Historical Debate feature later in this chapter.

Section 43.4

The Ottoman Conquest of Syria and Palestine

After Marj Dabiq, Selim I entered Aleppo in late August 1516, largely without further fighting. Aleppo had been the Mamluk northern base, and its capture gave the Ottomans a large, productive city at the junction of routes to Anatolia, Iraq, and the Mediterranean. The sultan remained there for some weeks, receiving the submission of local officials and settling the immediate administration. This pattern of occupation, in which the conqueror confirmed many local notables, scholars, and officials who accepted his authority, was repeated in the other Syrian cities and was central to the stability of the new order.

From Aleppo the army moved south. Damascus, the principal Mamluk city in Syria, was entered in September 1516. The city's population and officials had learned of the defeat, and the Mamluk governor had left. Accounts, including those of the Damascene chronicler Ibn Tulun, record the sultan's arrival and the Friday prayer in which Selim's name replaced that of the Mamluk ruler, a standard sign that a ruler had changed. Selim wintered in the city. During this stay, according to tradition and local sources, he arranged for a complex to be built around the tomb of the mystic Ibn al-Arabi (d. 1240) at the foot of Mount Qasiyun, a gesture that connected Ottoman rule to a revered Sufi saint and that also signaled attention to Damascene religious sensibilities.

Palestine followed. Jerusalem submitted without a battle, and Ottoman sources, together with later tradition, report that Selim visited the city and its holy places. The visit carried symbolic weight, since the sultan was becoming ruler of cities sacred to Muslims, Christians, and Jews. In later decades, Ottoman patronage in Jerusalem, notably the rebuilding of the city walls under Süleyman I, would build on this beginning, a subject for Chapter 44. Gaza was the last significant Syrian position before Sinai. In late December 1516, an Ottoman force defeated a Mamluk detachment near Khan Yunis in the region of Gaza, closing the southern Syrian frontier and clearing the way into Egypt.

Local responses varied. Many Syrian notables, officials, and religious scholars accepted the new regime, often with a mixture of relief at ending the war and anxiety about what would follow. Some Mamluk emirs joined the Ottoman side, and Janbirdi al-Ghazali, who had commanded the Damascus contingent at Marj Dabiq, was confirmed as governor of Damascus, a position that gave him a stake in the new order. Mountain districts such as Mount Lebanon, which had enjoyed practical autonomy under the Mamluks, were reached only indirectly. Later tradition names particular Mount Lebanon chiefs as confirmed by Selim in 1516–1517, but dependable contemporary evidence for specific confirmations is lacking. The familiar story about Fakhr al-Din I of the Ma'n family, for example, is undermined by Kamal Salibi's research, which places that figure's death in 1506, and historians do not rely on it. What can be said is that Ottoman authorities worked through existing local intermediaries in such districts in the following years, a subject for Chapter 44. The conquest of Syria was thus also a process of negotiation with local power-holders, in the cities as well as the mountains.

The Ottoman settlement of Syria was not secure at once. Selim's death in 1520 prompted Janbirdi al-Ghazali to revolt, declare himself sultan, and attempt to restore an independent Mamluk-style state. An Ottoman army defeated him in early 1521. The episode shows both the limits of the initial settlement and the fact that Mamluk loyalties were not simply transferred to the new sultan. It also indicates why the Ottomans, in the years after the conquest, put considerable effort into placing Syria under a more regular provincial administration, as described in Chapter 44.

Everyday Life and Social History

Living through Conquest

For most inhabitants of Syria and Egypt, the conquest was less a political transfer than a disruption of daily life. Armies needed food, fodder, animals, lodgings, and transport, and the presence of tens of thousands of soldiers affected markets and households. Chroniclers, including Ibn Iyas, record rising prices, fear of looting, and the closing of shops during moments of danger. Cairo experienced serious disorder when Ottoman troops entered. The accounts also record that Ottoman commanders and the sultan issued proclamations to restore order, and that soldiers were punished for violence in some instances, which suggests that conquerors recognized the importance of stability.

Experiences differed by group. Mamluk soldiers and officials faced the greatest risk and the greatest uncertainty, as they might be killed, dismissed, absorbed into Ottoman service, or forced to flee. Merchants and artisans feared disruption of markets but often also benefited from restored security and from the continuity of trade. Religious scholars, who staffed the courts, schools, and endowments, depended on the new rulers to confirm their positions and endowment revenues. Peasants, whose labor sustained the tax system, were affected by military requisitions and by changes in the collection of taxes, though the surviving records give less direct information about them. Because the main written sources were produced by urban, literate, often elite authors, the voices of rural populations, women, and enslaved people are largely missing, and historians must infer their experiences from indirect evidence.

Section 43.5

The Campaign into Egypt and the Battle of Ridaniya, 1517

When news of Marj Dabiq reached Cairo, the Mamluk elite faced the need to choose a successor to Qansuh al-Ghawri. After some hesitation and negotiation among rival factions, they selected Tuman Bay, a former senior commander and the sultan's nephew, in the autumn of 1516. Tuman Bay was a reluctant and capable ruler who understood that Egypt's survival depended on mobilizing a defense quickly. He gathered troops, reorganized the artillery, and fortified a position outside Cairo, while also sending envoys to the Ottomans in an effort to negotiate. Selim rejected terms that would have left the Mamluks in control of Egypt, and the Ottoman army moved south toward the Sinai.

The Sinai crossing was a significant logistical feat. The army, accompanied by its artillery, moved through Gaza and across the desert, using water supplies and local guides, including Bedouin groups who supplied intelligence and transport. Some Arab tribal leaders, notably in the Sinai and the Egyptian delta, aligned with the Ottomans, while others supported the Mamluks, and the loyalty of tribal leaders shifted with the fortunes of war. Tuman Bay had first considered meeting the Ottomans on the eastern frontier but instead chose to defend the approaches to Cairo at Ridaniya, a position northeast of the city, where he built entrenchments and positioned artillery. Critics at the time, and many historians later, questioned this decision, since it left much of the country open.

The battle took place on 22 January 1517. Tuman Bay's defensive works were designed to withstand a frontal assault. The Ottomans, however, avoided the strongest fortifications. According to the accounts, Ottoman forces outflanked the defensive line and attacked from the side, where the Mamluk artillery, positioned to face the expected direction, could not easily respond. Heavy fighting followed, and the Ottoman commander Sinan Pasha was killed. Tuman Bay and his troops fought hard, and the Ottoman victory came at considerable cost. The Mamluk army, however, was broken and withdrew into the city.

Ridaniya differed from Marj Dabiq. At Marj Dabiq, much of the story concerns the collapse of a divided army and the death of an elderly sultan. At Ridaniya, a determined and well-prepared Mamluk force fought for survival, and the Ottomans won through maneuver and numbers. Historians often point to this contrast to avoid a single explanation of Mamluk defeat. At the same time, Ridaniya shows the vulnerability of fixed defensive systems against a mobile, coordinated attacker, and it underlines the importance of leadership and intelligence.

The battle opened Cairo. The Ottoman army entered the city in late January, and for several days there was fighting in the streets and quarters, as Mamluk fighters, supported by some of the population, resisted. Ibn Iyas describes disorder, killing, and looting, as well as the efforts of the new authorities to stop them. The city's size, its dense urban fabric, and its large population made it a difficult place to hold, and the Ottoman leadership soon realized that governing Egypt would require cooperation from local officials, merchants, and scholars. The next section examines how the Mamluk state ended and how its personnel were absorbed.

Section 43.6

Cairo, Tuman Bay, and the End of the Mamluk Sultanate

Tuman Bay did not surrender after Ridaniya. He escaped from Cairo, regrouped, and at one point recaptured parts of the city and fought Ottoman troops in its streets. When this attempt failed, he withdrew across the Nile to the Giza area and into the delta, seeking support from Bedouin leaders and rural supporters. For some weeks the Ottomans did not control the country outside Cairo. Selim negotiated with Tuman Bay and his emissaries, but according to the sources the Ottoman leadership became suspicious and the discussions broke down. The final defeat came after Tuman Bay was betrayed by a Bedouin leader, Hasan ibn Mar'i, and brought to Selim. In April 1517, he was hanged at Bab Zuwayla, one of the city gates, a public execution witnessed by the population and recorded in the chronicles.

The execution marks the end of the Mamluk Sultanate as a sovereign state. It did not mean the end of the Mamluks as a social and military group. The Ottomans recognized that the Mamluk households, with their military skills, economic holdings, and networks, represented both a danger and a resource. Some Mamluks were executed or exiled, and some were sent to Istanbul. Others were integrated into Ottoman structures, retaining positions in the Egyptian military and administration. Khayr Bey was appointed Ottoman governor of Egypt (1517–1522). Over the following decades Mamluk-style military households continued to flourish in Egypt, a process that Chapters 44 and 47 examine.

Selim remained in Egypt until the late summer of 1517. During this time he dealt with a range of problems. He sought to restore order and to arrange the collection of revenues. He took the ceremonial Abbasid caliph, al-Mutawakkil III, and other notables to Istanbul, along with craftsmen, artisans, and valuable items, a transfer that has been remembered as a drain of Egypt's wealth and talent, though the sources do not permit precise accounting. Al-Mutawakkil later returned to Cairo, according to the sources, and died there in 1543. Selim also left a garrison, including janissaries, and arranged for the initial structures of Ottoman administration. The reorganization of Egyptian government under Süleyman's grand vizier Ibrahim Pasha in the 1520s belongs to Chapter 44.

The Mamluk collapse had broad consequences beyond Egypt. The sultanate had been the principal Muslim power in the eastern Mediterranean and Red Sea, and its fall transferred control of the Egyptian grain supply, the Red Sea trade routes, and the guardianship of the holy cities. It also removed the only Sunni state that had resisted Ottoman expansion in the Arab East. The Ottoman Empire, which had been primarily a Balkan and Anatolian state, now ruled many Arabic-speaking regions, and its future would depend significantly on how it governed them.

Historians have approached the end of the Mamluk Sultanate from different angles. Some emphasize the disappearance of an established regime and its cultural, artistic, and religious institutions. Others stress the continuity of social and economic structures, since many officials, scholars, merchants, and estates survived the transfer. The sources reflect both views. Ibn Iyas, for example, laments the end of the old order and criticizes the disorder of the conquest, while Ottoman accounts present the event as a triumph. Students should read both kinds of source as perspectives shaped by position and purpose.

Historical Debate

Why Did the Mamluks Lose?

The question: Was the Ottoman conquest of Syria and Egypt in 1516–1517 primarily the result of Ottoman military superiority, particularly firearms and organization, or of Mamluk weaknesses, such as internal divisions, economic strain, and poor strategic choices?

Interpretation A — Military and technological superiority: An influential older view, associated in particular with the work of David Ayalon on Mamluk attitudes to firearms, held that the Mamluk military elite remained committed to cavalry and was reluctant to adopt firearms and artillery on a large scale, while the Ottomans had integrated gunpowder weapons into a disciplined army. Ottoman victory, in this view, reflected a military gap that was itself a consequence of social and institutional conservatism.
Interpretation B — Political, fiscal, and strategic factors: More recent scholarship points out that the Mamluks did use firearms and artillery, and that the difference lay more in organization, logistics, and command than in technology alone. This view also emphasizes the effects of factional rivalry within the Mamluk elite, financial pressure on the state, the decline of customs revenues under Portuguese competition, and the conduct of particular commanders and political leaders. In this view, Ottoman success depended on exploiting these weaknesses as much as on superior weapons.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on Arabic chronicles, Ottoman campaign accounts, European reports, and administrative records. They differ in how much weight to place on sources that report Mamluk contempt for firearms, which may reflect polemic, and on accounts of defection and division, which may reflect hindsight. Archaeological and technical evidence for Mamluk artillery is limited.

Why it matters: The question shapes how historians understand early modern Middle Eastern states, whether as backward victims of technological change or as adaptable systems that were defeated by a particular combination of circumstances. Many historians now regard the two interpretations as overlapping and emphasize the interaction of military organization, politics, and finance.

Student task: Using the evidence and descriptions of Marj Dabiq and Ridaniya in this chapter, explain which factors you consider most important and identify what additional evidence might help resolve the question.

Section 43.7

The Hijaz and the Submission of the Holy Cities

The Hijaz, with Mecca and Medina, held a special place in the Ottoman conquest. The two cities were the destination of the annual pilgrimage and the foundation of Islamic religious identity. They lay at a distance from the Syrian and Egyptian centers of Mamluk power, in a region governed by the sharifs of Mecca, descendants of the Prophet who exercised local authority under Mamluk overlordship. The Mamluk sultans had provided financial support, supplied grain, sponsored building projects, and protected the pilgrimage caravans from Cairo and Damascus. The sharifs, for their part, recognized Mamluk sovereignty in the Friday sermon and the coinage while keeping effective control of their territory.

When the Mamluk Sultanate fell, the sharif of Mecca, Barakat, and his advisers had to decide how to respond. They had no reason to resist an army that had just destroyed the state that protected them, and they had considerable reason to maintain relations with the power that controlled Egypt's grain and the pilgrimage routes. According to the accounts, Barakat sent his son Abu Numayy to Cairo to meet the Ottoman sultan, bringing gifts and a formal acknowledgment of Ottoman authority. Selim confirmed Barakat in his position. The Ottoman sultan's name was thereafter mentioned in the Friday sermon at Mecca, and the sharif remained the local ruler, subject to Ottoman suzerainty.

This was a different process from the conquest of Cairo. No Ottoman army marched into Mecca and Medina, and no battle was fought for them. The arrangement was negotiated and relatively peaceful, and it left local sharifian authority in place. Over the following decades the Ottomans established a closer presence, including a governor and garrison at Jeddah, the port of Mecca, and fortifications and naval forces in the Red Sea, because the Portuguese threat gave them reason to defend the coast. Even so, the sharifs of Mecca retained a significant degree of autonomy and a distinct dynastic identity. The Hijaz was thus an example of Ottoman rule through partnership with a local dynasty.

The importance of this arrangement lay in its symbolic and practical value. For the Ottoman sultans, protection of the holy cities and the pilgrimage was a central source of prestige among Muslims. It allowed them to present themselves as defenders of the faith and to distinguish themselves from rivals such as the Safavids. For the sharifs, Ottoman recognition meant security, financial support, and the preservation of their position. For pilgrims from across the Muslim world, the Ottoman guarantee of safe passage and supply was a major change from the sometimes uncertain conditions of the late Mamluk period, though the details of the pilgrimage administration belong to Chapter 45.

The submission of the Hijaz also illustrates the difference between claiming and controlling. Ottoman authority in the Hijaz was real, but it was limited by distance, by the political strength of the sharifs, and by the character of the region's tribal society. The Ottomans could provide support and exercise influence, but they could not easily impose direct rule on the Arabian interior. This limitation would shape the empire's relationship with Arabia for centuries, a theme returned to in Chapter 48.

Historical Vocabulary
Suzerainty and Negotiated Allegiance

Suzerainty is a relationship in which a dominant power is recognized as overlord by a ruler who keeps substantial authority within his own territory. Recognition typically took visible forms: mention of the overlord's name in the Friday sermon, use of his name on coinage, payment of tribute or gifts, and sometimes the presence of a representative or garrison. In the Ottoman context, negotiated allegiance describes cases, such as the Hijaz, in which a local ruler accepted Ottoman overlordship without being defeated in battle.

The concept matters because Ottoman expansion cannot be described only as conquest. Modern territorial categories, in which a state either rules a place or does not, do not fit early modern practice. A region could be formally Ottoman, with Ottoman officials, garrisons, and taxes, or Ottoman in name, with a local ruler governing largely on his own. The two situations could shift over time, and historians must examine the actual practice in each place.

Section 43.8

Conquest, Legitimacy, and the Ottoman Sultanate

Ottoman rulers did not rely on force alone. They needed a framework within which their rule over the Arab provinces could be understood as legitimate by their new subjects, by the Sunni scholarly community, and by the wider Muslim world. Several elements of this framework emerged in the years after 1517. The first was the defense of Sunni Islam. Ottoman writers contrasted their dynasty with the Safavids and presented war against Shi'i Iran as a religious duty. The second was guardianship of Mecca and Medina. The third was the maintenance of justice and order, a core theme in Islamic political thought, through the application of law and the protection of the population.

The title commonly associated with the second element is "Servant of the Two Noble Sanctuaries" (Khadim al-Haramayn al-Sharifayn). Selim adopted the title after the conquest, and it appears in Ottoman inscriptions and documents thereafter. Earlier Mamluk rulers had used similar language, so the title represented continuity with an established tradition of Islamic kingship as well as a new assertion of Ottoman responsibility. Its significance lay in the combination of religious service and political claim.

A different and more controversial question concerns the caliphate. A widely repeated story says that the last Abbasid caliph in Cairo, al-Mutawakkil III, formally transferred the caliphate and the relics of the Prophet to Selim I in a ceremony in 1517, making the Ottoman sultan the legitimate caliph of all Muslims. The story acquired great force in later periods, especially in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, when it was used to support Ottoman claims to religious leadership. Historians, however, have not found contemporary documentation of such a ceremony. The earliest surviving Ottoman and Arabic accounts of the conquest do not describe it, and the story appears in sources written considerably later.

The better-supported picture is more modest. Selim and his successors claimed the title of caliph in a general sense, as rulers of Muslims, and in some contexts used the term, as other Muslim rulers did. The sultans' principal titles in the sixteenth century, however, emphasized sultanic authority, custody of the holy places, and defense of the faith. A more systematic claim to the caliphate as a formal office, with the Abbasid transfer as its foundation, developed later and served the political needs of later Ottoman rulers. Historians therefore treat the transfer as a tradition to be analyzed, a story told for specific purposes, and not as an event established by evidence.

This distinction does not make legitimacy unimportant. It shows how political authority was built in practice: through titles, ceremonies, patronage of shrines and scholars, and the maintenance of the pilgrimage, as well as through the memory of events as later generations told them. The conquest thus created not only a new political order but also a new set of claims about the meaning of that order, claims that Chapter 46 and later chapters will examine in the context of religious and communal life.

Historical Vocabulary
Khadim al-Haramayn al-Sharifayn (Servant of the Two Noble Sanctuaries)

This title identifies a ruler as the servant, in effect the protector and provider, of the sanctuaries of Mecca and Medina. Mamluk sultans had used comparable formulations because they funded and guarded the pilgrimage. Ottoman sultans adopted the title after 1517 and made it a regular part of their imperial self-presentation.

The title should not be confused with the caliphate. It expressed a responsibility toward the holy places, not a claim to a particular office of succession to the Prophet. It also should not be read as evidence that the Ottomans ruled Mecca and Medina in the way they ruled Cairo. The title coexisted with continued sharifian government in the Hijaz. Its importance lay in giving the Ottoman dynasty a religious prestige that its rivals could not easily match.

Section 43.9

The Ottoman–Safavid Struggle for Iraq

Iraq lay between the two empires and was contested as much for its religious significance as for its resources. It contained Baghdad, the former Abbasid capital and a major city of Sunni learning, with the tombs of Abu Hanifa and the mystic Abd al-Qadir al-Jilani. It also contained the Shi'i shrine cities of Najaf and Karbala, and Samarra and Kazimayn, where Imams are buried. Its agricultural plains, rivers, and trade routes connected Iran, the Gulf, Syria, and Anatolia. Control of Iraq therefore had political, religious, and economic importance for both the Sunni Ottomans and the Shi'i Safavids.

Before the sixteenth century, Iraq had been governed by a succession of regional powers following the Mongol and Timurid periods (Chapter 42), most recently by the Turkmen confederation of the Aq Qoyunlu. After the Safavids defeated the Aq Qoyunlu, Shah Ismail I occupied Baghdad in 1508. Sunni and Ottoman sources report that the Safavid occupation promoted Shi'ism and damaged important Sunni sites, including the tomb of Abu Hanifa. These accounts vary in detail, the extent of the damage is hard to verify independently, and they were shaped by confessional hostility. Baghdad's population was religiously and socially mixed, and the evidence does not support one statement about how all of its communities experienced Safavid rule: the reports of persecution come largely from writers with reason to portray the Safavids as oppressors, and Shi'i, Jewish, Christian, and other residents may have seen matters differently. The memory of the period nonetheless shaped Ottoman claims to be restoring Sunni order.

The Ottomans did not immediately challenge the Safavid hold on Iraq. After Chaldiran in 1514 and the conquests in the Jazira, Mosul and surrounding areas moved into the Ottoman sphere in the later 1510s and 1520s, largely through local arrangements with Kurdish and Arab leaders and Ottoman commanders. Baghdad itself remained Safavid. The Ottomans were focused on Syria and Egypt, then on the European campaigns of Süleyman I, including Belgrade in 1521 and Rhodes in 1522, and on the Hungarian front. Safavid Iran, for its part, faced pressure from the Uzbeks to its east.

Local politics complicated the contest. Iraq's population included Arab tribal confederations, Kurdish emirates, Turkmen military groups, urban Sunni and Shi'i communities, and Jewish and Christian minorities. Tribal leaders in the south and along the Euphrates controlled much of the countryside regardless of who held Baghdad. They allied with whichever empire offered the best terms and shifted loyalty when circumstances changed. A Safavid governor in Baghdad might be unable to control the surrounding territory, and an Ottoman commander who took the city would face the same limits. These conditions made Iraq an area where imperial claims and local power coexisted.

The struggle for Iraq thus belongs to a broader contest between the Ottoman and Safavid empires for Anatolia, the Caucasus, Azerbaijan, and Mesopotamia. In the Arab lands, its principal effect was to bring one of the region's great historic cities, and the revered shrines of both Sunni and Shi'i Islam, into the political calculations of two empires, a situation that would persist for centuries. The next section examines how the Ottomans took Baghdad and what that conquest did and did not achieve.

Section 43.10

Baghdad, Basra, and the Mesopotamian Frontier

In 1533, after a settlement with the Habsburgs in Hungary freed his hands, Süleyman I launched a campaign against the Safavids known in Ottoman sources as the "Campaign of the Two Iraqs," meaning Iraq proper and western Iran. His grand vizier Ibrahim Pasha advanced first, entering Tabriz in 1534, while the sultan followed with the main army. The Safavid shah, Tahmasp I, avoided pitched battle, preferring to withdraw and wear down the Ottoman force. The Ottomans, finding that they could not hold Tabriz, turned south to Iraq.

Baghdad fell in late 1534. The Safavid governor, according to Ottoman and other accounts, abandoned the city or was unable to defend it, and local notables, many of them Sunni, accepted Ottoman authority. Süleyman entered the city and, in a gesture consistent with Ottoman claims to protect Sunni Islam, visited and patronized the tomb of Abu Hanifa and that of Abd al-Qadir al-Jilani. He also visited the Shi'i shrines at Najaf and Karbala, and later Ottoman policy in these cities, including provision of water and support for shrine staff, reflected the recognition that these sites were important to a large part of the population and to pilgrims from Iran. The mixture of Sunni patronage and practical accommodation of Shi'i holy places would remain a feature of Ottoman Iraq.

The conquest did not end the contest. Safavid forces and diplomacy continued to challenge the Ottoman position, and the two empires fought several further wars. The Peace of Amasya in 1555 confirmed Ottoman possession of Baghdad and much of Iraq while leaving Tabriz and Azerbaijan with the Safavids and guaranteeing that Iranian pilgrims could visit the holy sites in Iraq. The agreement did not permanently settle the frontier. The Safavids under Shah Abbas I took Baghdad again in 1623, and the Ottomans under Murad IV retook it in 1638, an episode foreshadowed here and discussed in Chapter 44.

In southern Iraq, Ottoman control developed more slowly and was more dependent on local alliances. Basra, at the head of the Gulf, was a significant commercial and military location, linked to Gulf trade and to the Portuguese-controlled approaches to the Indian Ocean. In the 1530s and 1540s the city's local rulers, associated with Arab tribal confederations such as the Muntafiq, managed relations with the Ottomans and with others. The Ottoman position at Basra was consolidated in 1546, when the Ottomans obtained recognition from the local ruler and established a presence in the city. Even then, effective control of the southern marshes, the desert margins, and the Gulf coast remained partial. The Ottomans also established a military and administrative presence on the eastern Arabian coast in the middle of the century, in the al-Hasa region, with garrisons and officials; by 1559 Ottoman officials were mounting regional operations from al-Hasa. The evidence for the earliest years is thinner than for Baghdad, and the reach of this presence was uneven. Beyond its bases it depended on cooperation, rivalry, and negotiation with local tribal leaders, and authority outside the fortified centers was repeatedly contested.

The Mesopotamian frontier thus shows a recurring Ottoman pattern. The capture of a major city, accomplished by a large army, was followed by years of negotiation with tribes, local governors, and neighbors to make that capture effective. Ottoman rule in Iraq depended on the cooperation of tribal and urban elites and was always subject to the pressure of the Safavid frontier. The administrative consequences of this situation are examined in Chapter 44.

Section 43.11

The Ottoman Advance into the Red Sea World

The conquest of Egypt transformed the Ottoman relationship with the Red Sea. Cairo's ruler had long been concerned with the sea route linking the Indian Ocean to the Mediterranean via Suez and the Egyptian ports, and with the safety of Jeddah, the main port for pilgrims and for the Hijaz. When the Ottomans took Egypt, they inherited these concerns and the infrastructure, including shipyards, officials, and naval personnel, that the Mamluks had built. They also inherited an enemy: the Portuguese Estado da Índia.

Portuguese naval power in the Indian Ocean was based on fortified ports such as Goa, Hormuz, and Malacca, and on ships armed with heavy artillery, which allowed them to dominate sea lanes and attempt to control the spice trade. In 1509, a Mamluk-supported fleet had been defeated at Diu, off western India, by a Portuguese force. In 1513, a Portuguese attack on Aden failed. The Portuguese sporadically entered the Red Sea and raided its coasts, and they had considered attacks on Jeddah and even the holy cities. Muslim rulers in the region, from Gujarat to the Hijaz, saw the Portuguese as a serious threat, both commercially and religiously.

For the Ottomans, the Red Sea had strategic, economic, and religious importance. Controlling it protected the pilgrimage routes, secured Egypt's customs revenue, and offered a way to challenge Portuguese commercial dominance. The Ottomans began constructing and repairing ships at Suez, using timber imported from Anatolia and the skills of Egyptian and Mediterranean shipbuilders. They fortified Jeddah and other Red Sea ports. These preparations led to the expedition of 1538 described below, and also to a longer-term effort to control the coasts and to establish a sustained naval presence.

Scholars have debated how to interpret these efforts. Some, such as Salih Özbaran, emphasize the Ottomans' systematic effort to respond to Portuguese expansion, arguing that the Red Sea and Indian Ocean were a major arena of Ottoman policy. Others, including Giancarlo Casale, stress the Ottoman state's sustained exploratory and commercial ambitions in the Indian Ocean, which included diplomatic contacts with Muslim rulers in India, Southeast Asia, and East Africa. Still others note that Ottoman resources were stretched between several fronts and that the Indian Ocean was never the empire's main priority. All agree that Ottoman Red Sea policy was both ambitious and limited by distance and competing demands.

The Red Sea campaigns also involved Arab and African actors. Local rulers in Yemen, the Hijaz, and the Red Sea ports, such as the Zaydi imams, the surviving Tahirid authority at Aden, and the sharifs, shaped Ottoman strategy through their alliances and rivalries. Ethiopian and Muslim Horn of Africa states were drawn into conflict, and the Ottomans turned to the African coast as well. In July 1555 the sultan established the Habesh (Abyssinia) province, with Özdemir Pasha, the conqueror of highland Yemen, as its first governor, although the territory was not yet conquered. Massawa fell to Ottoman forces on 2 April 1557, followed by Arkiko, and Suakin and nearby islands came into the same Ottoman system. This was control of coastal ports and islands, not rule over the Ethiopian highlands, where Christian Ethiopian kings retained their authority and Ottoman forces struggled to hold even the coast. The Red Sea world was thus a regional system in which Ottoman power was one element.

Section 43.12

Yemen, Aden, and the Indian Ocean Campaigns

Yemen's politics were already fragmented before the Ottomans arrived, and the Tahirid dynasty did not rule the lowlands in 1538. The Tahirid sultans of the lowlands had declined to supply resources for the Mamluk Red Sea effort. A Mamluk fleet under Salman Reis landed at Kamaran in 1515, failed to take Aden, and in the following campaigns defeated the Tahirid sultan al-Zafir Amir II, whose reign ended in 1517, the year Mamluk rule itself fell in Egypt. The Mamluk force left a tributary regime at Zabid and withdrew, so that the Mamluk presence faded with the state that had sent it. Aden alone held out under Tahirid governors, first Amir Murjan and later Amir b. Da'ud, while the highlands were controlled by the Zaydi imam al-Mutawakkil Yahya Sharaf al-Din, who pressed the remaining Tahirid positions. Details of this period vary among sources. By 1538 Yemen was thus a patchwork: a Zaydi highland state, a fragile coastal regime at Zabid, and an isolated Aden. Ottoman officials in Egypt saw Aden as an important base for challenging the Portuguese in the Indian Ocean.

In 1538, Hadım Süleyman Pasha, who had just governed Egypt and now commanded the fleet at Suez, sailed with a large force. At Aden, Amir b. Da'ud, the last Tahirid ruler there, was seized and hanged with leading associates (on 3 August 1538 in one modern account). The circumstances are told in conflicting ways. One narrative says Amir had appealed to the Ottoman commander for help against the Zaydi imam and was lured aboard; an Ottoman-side summary says instead that he had made an agreement with the Portuguese. These versions come from later narratives and should not be treated as established; what is secure is that the last Tahirid authority at Aden ended by Ottoman action and that the city received an Ottoman governor and garrison. The fleet then sailed to Diu on the coast of Gujarat. Diu was a Gujarati port in which the Portuguese had been permitted to build a fortress beginning in 1535, and the 1538 siege was a joint operation of Ottoman and Gujarati forces against that fortress. Their interests differed: Gujarat's rulers wished to remove a Portuguese stronghold from their own port, while the Ottomans sought a base and a strategic and commercial advantage in the Indian Ocean. The Ottoman commander did not receive the local support he expected, and when word came that a large Portuguese fleet was approaching he withdrew in November 1538, after a siege begun in September. The expedition showed that the Ottomans could project naval power across the Arabian Sea, but also that they could not sustain operations far from their bases.

Despite the failure at Diu, the expedition had lasting effects. The Ottomans occupied coastal Yemen, including Zabid and the Tihama plain, and established a presence at Aden. In the following years Ottoman governors expanded inland, and in 1547 Özdemir Pasha took Sana'a, the highland capital. Here they met sustained resistance from the Zaydi imams, who retained their political and religious authority in the mountains and mobilized tribal support. The imam al-Mutahhar ibn Sharaf al-Din and his successors fought the Ottomans for decades, and in the 1560s Zaydi forces made major gains. The Ottomans reconquered much of the country around 1570–1571, but the cost was great.

Ottoman operations in the wider Indian Ocean continued, though on a smaller scale. Ottoman captains such as Piri Reis and, after him, Murad Reis and Seydi Ali Reis led expeditions to the Gulf and Arabian Sea in the early 1550s. In August 1552 Piri Reis seized Muscat from the Portuguese, but the Ottomans did not hold it, and the capture was a raid, not a permanent annexation. His siege of Hormuz in autumn 1552 failed, and he withdrew to Basra, leaving most of his fleet there and returning to Egypt with only two galleys. In Cairo he was received coldly by the governor of Egypt and was accused of abandoning his command and of lifting the siege in return for a bribe. These charges, reported by the Ottoman governor of Basra, are disputed, since a Portuguese fleet did arrive at Hormuz soon afterward, which suggests the retreat had a military rationale. Piri Reis was put to death in Cairo in late 1553, probably by decision of the Egyptian divan, while Sultan Süleyman was in Aleppo. The available account leaves uncertain whether the sentence was ordered by the sultan or decided by the authorities in Egypt. Later operations in the Strait of Hormuz in 1553 also ended in Portuguese success at sea. Seydi Ali Reis, returning from the Gulf, was driven by storms and Portuguese pressure to the coast of India and later made his way back overland to Istanbul. These episodes show the Ottoman state's continuing interest in the Indian Ocean, and also the persistence of Portuguese naval strength.

Yemen illustrates the contrast between occupying a region and governing it. The Ottomans held the coastal plain and some cities but found the highlands difficult to control, partly because of the terrain and the strength of Zaydi leadership, partly because of the distance from other Ottoman provinces. Yemen would remain a difficult province, with a record of revolts, withdrawal, and renewed conquest, a history developed in later chapters. For the sixteenth century, it demonstrates that Ottoman power in the Arab lands had boundaries that were set by geography, local resistance, and competing European power.

Section 43.13

Algiers and the Beginnings of Ottoman North Africa

North Africa's incorporation began from a different starting point. The Maghreb in 1500 was divided among the Hafsid, Zayyanid, and Wattasid states, none strong enough to resist outside pressure, and Spanish and Portuguese forces were seizing coastal positions. Spain captured Oran in 1509, Bejaia in 1510, and Tripoli in 1510, and built a fortress on a small island, the Peñón, off the harbor of Algiers in 1510, which dominated the city. Coastal towns often accepted payment of tribute to Spain to avoid attack. The population saw the Spanish advance as a serious danger, and many looked for support from other Muslim powers.

The Barbarossa brothers, Aruj and Hayreddin, were corsairs of Aegean origin who had operated in the central Mediterranean and had a base at Jerba and later Jijel. They attracted followers and prestige by attacking Spanish shipping and ports. In about 1516, the people of Algiers, led by a local ruler, Salim al-Tumi, invited Aruj to help against the Spanish at the Peñón. Aruj took control of the city, and according to the sources eliminated Salim al-Tumi, so that an invitation became a seizure of power. The details are reported mainly in later narratives, including Ottoman sources and the memoir attributed to Hayreddin, and historians treat them with caution.

Aruj died in 1518 while fighting Spanish and local forces near Tlemcen. His brother Hayreddin took over, and, faced with the combined opposition of Spain and North African rivals, made a decisive move: he offered Algiers to the Ottoman sultan. Selim I accepted in 1519, conferred the rank of beylerbeyi on Hayreddin, and sent troops, including janissaries, and artillery. Algiers thus became the first Ottoman province in the central Maghreb. In 1529, Hayreddin captured the Peñón and connected it to the mainland, giving Algiers a protected harbor, and the city's naval power grew.

The role of Ottoman backing was real but limited. Algiers depended on the Ottoman Empire for legitimacy, men, and weapons, but it retained substantial autonomy, with its own military and corsair interests. Local agency was important. Algiers' townsmen, scholars, and tribal leaders had their own motives, in part defensive and in part opportunistic. The nearby kingdom of Kuku in the Kabylia and the Zayyanid state at Tlemcen opposed Algiers at different times, and their relations with it shifted. Algiers' prosperity came to rely on a system of naval raiding and maritime commerce that gave the regency a distinctive character, as Chapter 44 will explain.

In 1533, Süleyman summoned Hayreddin to Istanbul and appointed him kapudan pasha, commander of the Ottoman navy, and the corsair leader became an Ottoman admiral. In 1534 he conquered Tunis, which Spain retook in 1535 under Charles V, restoring a Hafsid ruler as a client. In 1541 an expedition by Charles V against Algiers itself failed in autumn storms and fierce resistance. The failure strengthened the Ottoman position and the reputation of Algiers as a bastion against Spain. The North African conquest was thus one of alliance, mutual need, and local power, not of overwhelming imperial force.

Section 43.14

Tripoli, Tunis, and the Western Mediterranean Struggle

Tripoli had been taken by Spain in 1510 and in 1530 was granted by Charles V to the Knights of St John, who also held Malta. The Knights' control of the city was contested by Muslim naval leaders and by the surrounding population. Turgut Reis (known in European sources as Dragut), a corsair who had served under Hayreddin and had become one of the leading Muslim naval commanders, worked with the Ottoman admiral Sinan Pasha to besiege Tripoli. In August 1551 the Ottoman fleet and army compelled the Knights to surrender. Hadım Murad Agha, an Ottoman official who had worked for years from Tajura, east of Tripoli, and who supported the siege, was appointed the first Ottoman governor. Dragut, who had expected the post, was instead given a district command in western Greece (Karlı-ili). He became beylerbeyi of Tripoli only in March 1556, after Murad Agha's death, and held the post until 1565. Tripoli became the center of Ottoman authority in Libya.

The capture was followed by a period of struggle with Spain and its allies. A large Christian fleet seized the island of Jerba in 1560, but an Ottoman fleet under Piyale Pasha destroyed it in a major battle that year, a victory that strengthened Ottoman control of the central Mediterranean. In 1565, Dragut, by then governor of Tripoli, was killed during the Ottoman siege of Malta, which failed. Tripoli's role as a base for naval operations and for the control of overland routes into the Sahara increased over time, though Ottoman authority in Libya remained concentrated on the coast, and the interior, including Fezzan and Cyrenaica, was reached only gradually and partially.

Tunis was the most contested of the three. As the capital of the Hafsid dynasty, it was a major city with a long tradition of scholarship and trade. Hayreddin's conquest in 1534 was brief, and Spain's reconquest in 1535 left a Hafsid ruler, Mulay Hasan, as a dependent of Spain, with a Spanish garrison at the fortress of La Goletta. The Spanish presence was unpopular in many quarters, and Tunis changed hands more than once as Ottoman and Spanish forces competed. In 1569, the Ottoman commander Uluç Ali took the city, then lost it in 1573 when Don John of Austria captured it, shortly after the Christian victory at Lepanto in 1571 had shown that Ottoman naval power could be checked but not destroyed.

The final Ottoman conquest occurred in 1574. A large fleet and army under Sinan Pasha and Uluç Ali besieged La Goletta and the new Spanish fort built beside Tunis, and in September the Ottomans took both. The Hafsid dynasty, which had ruled Ifriqiya since the thirteenth century, came to an end, and Tunis became an Ottoman province. The victory, though costly, completed the Ottoman position across the central Maghreb coast from Algiers to Tripoli. It ended Spanish control of Tunis and strengthened Ottoman predominance in the central Maghreb, although Spain kept other North African positions, such as Oran and Melilla.

The history of Tripoli and Tunis shows several features of Ottoman expansion in North Africa: reliance on naval forces and corsair leaders, competition with Spain, dependence on local support, and the role of the central Mediterranean as a contested sea. The Arab and Amazigh populations of the region, whose loyalties and resources were crucial, were not passive. Their responses to Spanish occupation, to Ottoman arrival, and to the demands of both determined how the new provinces developed.

Historical Vocabulary
Reis and Beylerbeyi: Corsair Commanders in Ottoman Service

Reis means captain or chief. In the Ottoman Mediterranean it was applied to commanders of ships and fleets such as Hayreddin Barbarossa, Turgut Reis, and Piri Reis. Many were corsairs, that is, maritime raiders who operated under the authority of a state or with its recognition, and whose activities blended warfare, commerce, and plunder. The word should be distinguished from "pirate," a term that European sources often applied to them but that obscures their relationship with states. Beylerbeyi ("lord of lords") was the rank of an Ottoman provincial governor-general. When Hayreddin was named beylerbeyi of Algiers, a corsair leader entered the formal structure of the empire.

This vocabulary matters because it shows how Ottoman expansion in North Africa used flexible partnerships between the central state and maritime entrepreneurs. It also warns students against two simple narratives, one that treats the corsairs as mere outlaws and another that treats them as obedient imperial agents. They acted for their own interests and for the Ottoman cause, and the relationship changed over time.

Section 43.15

Regional Differences and the Limits of Ottoman Expansion

The preceding sections describe several different paths into Ottoman authority. In Syria and Egypt, the empire destroyed the previous state in battle and occupied the major cities, though it confirmed many local elites. In the Hijaz, it accepted the recognition of a local dynasty and exercised influence through patronage, garrisons at ports, and control of the pilgrimage supply. In Iraq, it took Baghdad from a rival empire after a long contest and then worked to extend control to the countryside and the south. In Yemen, it combined naval expeditions with inland campaigns against a determined local opposition. In the Maghreb, it responded to invitations and the initiatives of corsair leaders and then integrated them into the imperial system. In each case, the nature of the sources and of the participants shaped the outcome.

The limits were as significant as the successes. Much of the Arabian interior, including Najd and the eastern desert, lay outside effective Ottoman control, and local rulers governed with little interference. Oman and much of the southern Arabian coast were not incorporated, and Portuguese power was present in the Gulf and the Arabian Sea. The Sahara and its tribal populations were reached only indirectly through the coastal provinces. The highlands of Yemen, the mountain districts of Lebanon, and many tribal areas remained largely autonomous. Even in provinces formally Ottoman, central authority depended on negotiation with local power-holders.

Morocco is the clearest case of non-incorporation. The Saadian dynasty, which rose in southern Morocco in the early sixteenth century, took Marrakesh in 1525 and later displaced the Wattasids at Fez. Ottoman authorities in Algiers made efforts to extend influence westward, and in 1554 forces from Algiers helped to take Fez briefly before the Saadians reasserted control. In 1576, Ottoman support helped Abd al-Malik take the throne at Fez, and in 1578 he and his Ottoman-backed forces defeated and killed King Sebastian of Portugal at Alcácer Quibir (the Battle of the Three Kings), though Abd al-Malik himself died during the battle. His successor Ahmad al-Mansur, while acknowledging the Ottoman sultan in some formal ways, governed independently. Morocco's position was based on a strong local dynasty, distance, a military system that could resist Ottoman pressure, and the Ottoman decision not to commit large forces to the far western Maghreb.

These differences matter because they warn against the assumption that the Arab world was uniformly Ottoman. Historians sometimes speak of an "Ottoman Arab world," and in a political sense the phrase is useful for much of the region, but it describes a pattern of varying intensity of control and varied forms of relationship. Some regions were governed directly, some indirectly, and some not at all. Subsequent chapters will show that this variety was one of the principal characteristics of Ottoman rule, as provincial elites, tribal leaders, and local dynasties continued to shape political life.

Section 43.16

The Transformation of the Arab Political Order

By the end of the sixteenth century, the Arab world's political map differed greatly from that of 1500. The Mamluk Sultanate had disappeared, as had the Hafsid dynasty. In their place stood Ottoman provinces centered on Cairo, Damascus, Aleppo, Baghdad, Basra, Tripoli, Tunis, Algiers, and, with less security, Yemen. The Hijaz was within the Ottoman sphere under its sharifs. The Ottoman sultan, from Istanbul, claimed leadership of the Sunni world, and the empire became the dominant power in the eastern and central Mediterranean, the Red Sea, and much of western Asia. For many Arabic-speaking people, the Ottoman state became the framework within which political, legal, and commercial life took place for the next several centuries.

At the same time, the conquest did not produce a single, centralized Arab province of the empire. The regions it incorporated differed in their histories, economies, and social structures, and they were joined to the empire in different ways. The Ottoman Empire's strength lay partly in its ability to adapt to this variety, allowing local elites, customary law, and regional institutions to continue while establishing its own officials, taxation, and military presence. The balance between central authority and local autonomy varied from place to place and changed over time, a theme that runs through Part VIII.

The conquests also altered the Empire. By absorbing Egypt, Syria, and Iraq, the Ottoman state gained its wealthiest and most populous provinces, its principal religious centers, and a large Arabic-speaking population. Its center of gravity shifted from the Balkans and Anatolia toward a larger Mediterranean and Middle Eastern empire, with consequences for its finances, religious claims, and diplomacy. The Ottoman sultans increasingly presented themselves as leaders of the Muslim world, and the empire's relationship with the holy cities became central to its identity.

Evidence for these developments includes Arabic and Ottoman chronicles, administrative records, European reports, inscriptions, and surviving buildings. Historians continue to debate the character of the conquests, the causes of Mamluk defeat, the nature of Ottoman legitimacy, and the extent of Ottoman control. The caliphate transfer story is a reminder that later periods shaped how this history was told, and historians must separate what contemporaries said from what later generations believed.

This chapter has traced how Ottoman authority was acquired. Chapter 44 turns to the question of how it was exercised: how provinces were organized and governed, how taxes were collected, how courts and military institutions operated, and how local elites, tribes, and villages negotiated with imperial authority. The conquests explained here created the conditions for that system, and the variety of their origins helps explain why Ottoman government in the Arab lands was never uniform.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how Ottoman power spread across the Arab lands between about 1514 and 1574. Its central argument is that the expansion was regionally differentiated. It combined battlefield conquest, diplomacy, negotiated allegiance, local participation, religious legitimacy, and maritime competition, and it did not occur as a single event in 1517.

The background was a changing strategic environment. The Mamluk and Ottoman states had long competed along their shared frontier. The Safavid rise created a new Shi'i power that threatened both, and the Portuguese entry into the Indian Ocean undermined the Red Sea routes on which Mamluk revenue depended. Within the Mamluk state, fiscal strain and factionalism weakened the response. Selim I's victory over the Safavids at Chaldiran in 1514 secured the Ottoman eastern frontier, and the decision to turn south in 1516 was made on the basis of both opportunity and justification.

The campaigns of 1516–1517 destroyed the Mamluk Sultanate. At Marj Dabiq, in August 1516, a divided Mamluk army was defeated and Sultan Qansuh al-Ghawri died. Aleppo, Damascus, and Jerusalem submitted or were occupied, a Mamluk force was beaten near Gaza, and in January 1517 the Ottomans defeated Tuman Bay at Ridaniya and took Cairo after street fighting. Tuman Bay's capture and execution in April ended Mamluk sovereignty. Many Mamluk officials and military households, however, entered Ottoman service, and the survival of these elites shaped later Egyptian and Syrian history.

The Hijaz followed a different path. The sharif of Mecca recognized Ottoman suzerainty and was confirmed in his position, so that the holy cities came under Ottoman guardianship by negotiation. The Ottoman sultans adopted the title Servant of the Two Noble Sanctuaries and built a close relationship with the pilgrimage, while the sharifs retained considerable autonomy. The chapter also examined the tradition that al-Mutawakkil III transferred the caliphate to Selim in 1517. Because no contemporary evidence for such a ceremony survives, historians treat it as a later tradition, and the real foundations of Ottoman legitimacy lay in defense of Sunni Islam, protection of the holy places, and justice.

In Iraq, the Ottoman advance followed a prolonged struggle with the Safavids, who had taken Baghdad in 1508. Süleyman I captured Baghdad in 1534, and the Peace of Amasya in 1555 confirmed Ottoman control of Iraq while safeguarding access to the shrine cities for Iranian pilgrims. Basra's incorporation in 1546 was only a partial extension of control over the south, where tribal and local power remained important.

The Red Sea and Indian Ocean campaigns showed Ottoman ambition and its limits. The Suez-based fleet of 1538 took Aden but failed to take Portuguese Diu. Ottoman forces occupied coastal Yemen and later the highland capital Sana'a, but the Zaydi imams and highland tribes resisted for decades, and control of the interior was never secure. Ottoman captains also ventured into the Gulf and Arabian Sea, with mixed results, while the Portuguese retained naval strength.

North Africa's incorporation relied on corsair leaders and local invitation. The Barbarossa brothers took Algiers around 1516 in the context of Spanish pressure, and Hayreddin offered the city to Selim in 1519. Tripoli was taken from the Knights of St John in 1551, and Tunis, after repeated contests with Spain, was conquered in 1574, ending the Hafsid dynasty. In each case, local agency, naval power, and Spanish–Ottoman competition shaped events. Morocco, ruled by the Saadian dynasty, remained independent.

The chapter emphasized the difference between conquest, claim, and control. Parts of Arabia, the Sahara, Mount Lebanon, and Yemen's highlands remained largely autonomous, and even in the provinces the empire relied on local elites. The Ottoman Empire was transformed by its new Arab provinces, which brought wealth, population, and religious prestige, and which shifted the empire's center of gravity toward the Middle East and the Mediterranean.

The chapter also stressed the use of evidence. Arabic chronicles such as Ibn Iyas's offer a view from inside the conquered cities, Ottoman accounts reflect the viewpoint of the conquerors, and European reports illuminate the Mediterranean and Indian Ocean contests. Each type of source has biases, and later traditions, such as the caliphate transfer, can distort understanding of events. The Historical Debate showed that the Mamluk defeat is best understood as the result of interacting military, political, and financial factors.

Finally, the chapter prepared the transition to Chapter 44. The conquests created a set of provinces with varied origins, and the empire's ability to govern them depended on adapting to this variety. Chapter 44 turns from how Ottoman authority was acquired to how it operated in practice.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it created the political framework within which most of the Arab world lived for several centuries. The conquests did not simply replace one ruler with another. They linked Cairo, Damascus, Baghdad, Algiers, Tunis, and the holy cities of the Hijaz into a single imperial system whose institutions, claims, and relationships shaped later history, as the following chapters will show.

The chapter also reveals how empires expand. Ottoman success depended on military organization, but also on political division among the Mamluks, alliances with local rulers, religious legitimacy, and the readiness of corsair leaders, sharifs, and city notables to deal with the new power. Where those conditions were absent, as in Morocco, the Arabian interior, or the highlands of Yemen, expansion stalled. The history of conquest is therefore also a history of local choice.

Several continuities extended well beyond the sixteenth century: the survival of Mamluk households in Egypt, the autonomy of the sharifs, the importance of tribal and mountain communities, and the competition for the Red Sea, the Gulf, and the Mediterranean. The memory of the conquest, including the story of a transferred caliphate, also shaped political claims in later periods.

For the larger history of the Arab world, the chapter marks a shift from a medieval order of competing dynasties, centered on Cairo, Tunis, Baghdad, and others, to an early modern imperial order governed from Istanbul. Understanding how that order was established, and how uneven it was, prepares students to examine how it worked in practice.
