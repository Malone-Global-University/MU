Part X — World War and the Making of the Modern Arab States

Iraq, Transjordan, and the Arabian Peninsula

A History of the Arab World: From Ancient Crossroads to the Modern Arab World

Chapter Opening

In the summer of 1920 the tribes of the Middle Euphrates rose against British administration in Iraq; in March 1921 a British minister met an Arabian prince in Jerusalem and agreed to a provisional arrangement east of the Jordan; in 1924 and 1925 an army from the Najdi interior took Mecca, Medina, and Jeddah; and in September 1932 a kingdom bearing a family's name was proclaimed across most of the Arabian Peninsula. These episodes had no single author and followed no single model. Together they show that the political order of the Arab East after the First World War was built by many hands, through revolt, negotiation, conquest, and treaty.

Chapter 56 examined the international decisions that followed the Ottoman collapse: the mandate system, the San Remo arrangements, and the drawing of new borders. This chapter turns from the conference rooms of Europe to the societies that had to live inside those arrangements and, in much of Arabia, outside them. It asks how Iraq, Transjordan, Saudi Arabia, Yemen, Oman, and the Gulf sheikhdoms developed different systems of political authority between 1918 and 1939, and how local movements, dynastic ambition, British influence, religious institutions, and the first petroleum economies shaped each outcome.

The central argument is that these states did not emerge from one process. Iraq and Transjordan were created within the mandate framework and governed by Hashemite monarchs, but Iraqis forced Britain to rethink direct rule through a large revolt, and Iraq later negotiated treaty-based independence. Saudi Arabia was largely the product of indigenous military and religious mobilization, though it operated within a world in which Britain was the dominant external power. Yemen emerged as an independent imamate with its own religious and tribal foundations. Oman and the Gulf sheikhdoms were governed through older maritime treaty relationships that were neither mandates nor colonies in the ordinary sense. A student who treats these as variations of a single "mandate" story will misunderstand all of them.

The chapter also resists two simplifications. One treats the new states as artificial British inventions with no local roots; the other treats them as the natural expression of pre-existing nations. Hashemite monarchs, Najdi imams, Zaydi scholars, Gulf merchants, Shi'i clerics, Kurdish chiefs, Assyrian leaders, Bedouin shaykhs, and British officials all had interests, and the outcomes reflect bargaining among them. Violence, including the aerial bombing of Iraqi villages, the Saudi conquest of the Hijaz, and the killings at Simele, was part of that bargaining and is treated here directly.

The evidence is uneven. For Iraq and Jordan the record is rich in British administrative reports, League of Nations files, parliamentary debates, newspapers, memoirs, and treaties. For Saudi Arabia, Yemen, and Oman, historians combine chronicles written under dynastic patronage, British political-agent correspondence, treaty texts, and oral tradition, and must always ask whose voice the archive preserves. For the Gulf, the records of the British Political Residency, now digitized in part, are indispensable but reflect imperial priorities. Ordinary cultivators, divers, women, and nomads appear mainly through the eyes of officials, a limitation this chapter tries to make visible.

The chapter covers roughly 1918 to 1939 and stops before the Second World War, the 1941 Iraqi crisis, postwar independence in Transjordan, and the mature petroleum economies of the Gulf. Chapter 58 turns to Syria and Lebanon under the French Mandate, Chapter 59 to Palestine, and Chapter 65 to the large-scale transformation of the oil monarchies.

  4. Analyze how religion, tribe, land, and ethnicity shaped Iraqi politics, including Shi'i, Kurdish, and Assyrian experiences of the new state.

  8. Assess the importance of British treaty relationships in Oman, Aden, Kuwait, Bahrain, Qatar, and the Trucial Coast.

Historical Context

By 1918 the Ottoman Empire, which had been the nominal sovereign of nearly all these lands, had lost them. British forces held Basra, Baghdad, and, by the armistice, positions toward Mosul. The Hijaz had been ruled by Sharif Hussein ibn Ali since the Arab Revolt (Chapter 55). In Najd, Abd al-Aziz ibn Abd al-Rahman Al Saud, known as Ibn Saud, ruled a recovering emirate descended from the Saudi states of Chapter 48. Imam Yahya controlled the Yemeni highlands, the Sultan of Muscat and the Ibadi imams of the interior divided Oman, and a string of Gulf rulers were bound to Britain by older treaties.

Chapter 56 explained that the peace settlements placed Iraq and Palestine, including the territory east of the Jordan, under British mandate, and Syria and Lebanon under French mandate. Mandates were international instruments requiring the mandatory power to prepare peoples for self-government under League supervision. Arabia proper was not mandated. The British government also had commitments to Hussein (Chapter 55), to Ibn Saud, to Gulf rulers, and to the Government of India, whose interests in the Gulf and Aden were long-standing. These overlapping obligations help explain why British policy often appeared inconsistent.

The region's societies were diverse. Iraq's population included Shi'i and Sunni Arabs, Kurds, Assyrians and other Christians, Jews, Turkmen, and Yazidis; its economy mixed riverine agriculture, tribal confederations, and commerce in Baghdad and Basra (compare Chapter 3 on water and settlement). Arabia combined oasis towns, pastoral tribes, pilgrimage economies, and maritime trade. The Gulf's wealth came overwhelmingly from pearl fishing and entrepôt commerce. Oil was known to exist in Iran and was suspected elsewhere, but it was not yet the foundation of any Arab state.

Finally, the political vocabulary of the age was in flux. "Arab" nationalism, Hashemite dynastic claims, Islamic reform, Zaydi and Ibadi imamate traditions, and tribal custom all offered ways of justifying authority. This chapter treats them as competing languages of legitimacy rather than as stages on a single path to the nation-state.

Division I — The Iraqi Revolt and the Establishment of the Hashemite Monarchy
Section 57.1

Iraq after Ottoman Rule: British Occupation and Competing Political Futures

The modern Iraqi state, with its later borders and composite population, did not yet exist in 1918, although al-'Iraq had long been used in Arabic as a geographical term for the lower Tigris–Euphrates lands and the Ottoman provinces had long administrative histories. The Ottoman provinces of Basra, Baghdad, and Mosul had distinct economies and orientations: Basra faced the Gulf and India, Baghdad was a river-and-caravan city with a large Sunni and Shi'i population and a sizeable Jewish community, and Mosul looked toward Syria, Anatolia, and the Kurdish highlands. British forces occupied Basra in 1914 and Baghdad in 1917 and were pushing north when the war ended. Under Civil Commissioner Arnold Wilson and the Indian-trained administrators who served him, Britain retained many Ottoman laws and officials while introducing a more direct, district-level system of British political officers.

Iraqis held competing visions. Some Sunni and Shi'i notables, and former Ottoman officers, favored an Arab state; many Shi'i religious leaders in Najaf and Karbala distrusted any arrangement that might subordinate their communities to Sunni officials or to a foreign power; Kurdish chiefs in the north held varied views, some seeking autonomy; Basra merchants often favored continued British protection. Wilson's consultations in 1918–1919, which asked leading Iraqis about an Arab state under British guidance, produced answers that officials read as support for British tutelage, but critics then and since have noted how narrow and managed the questioning was. The point for this chapter is that the Iraqi state would be shaped as much by these internal forces as by London's plans.

British administration in these years was shaped by the Indian model on which many officers had been trained. Political officers ran districts, Indian troops and officials supplied much of the manpower, and the rupee circulated as currency. Ottoman land law and registration practices were largely kept, and the Tribal Criminal and Civil Disputes Regulation of 1918 gave tribal shaykhs recognized judicial roles under customary law, an arrangement that would outlast the occupation. Critics inside the British government and among Iraqis argued that the system was too direct, too costly, and too inconsistent with wartime promises of Arab self-government.

The shrine cities of Najaf and Karbala occupied a distinct position. They drew pilgrims and endowments from across the Shi'i world and housed senior clerics who had long enjoyed autonomy from Ottoman authority. British taxation and administrative reach threatened both that autonomy and the revenues of the shrine towns, and Sunni and Shi'i townsmen in Baghdad, including a large Jewish community of merchants and officials, each assessed the new rulers in light of their own prospects. The evidence for these years comes mainly from British administration reports, Wilson's own writings, press and memoirs, and a smaller body of Iraqi sources; the first of these is especially rich on British intentions and poor on the views of cultivators and of women.

Section 57.2

The Origins of the Iraqi Revolt of 1920

Several grievances converged in 1920. The announcement at San Remo in April that Britain would hold the mandate for Iraq seemed to many to confirm that wartime promises of Arab self-rule would not be honored. Taxation had risen, and officials collected it more efficiently than Ottoman governors had, while land and grain requisitions strained cultivators. In the cities, nationalist associations such as al-Jam'iyya al-Wataniyya al-Islamiyya and the secret Haras al-Istiqlal (Guard of Independence) held meetings, particularly in Baghdad and the shrine cities, and Sunni and Shi'i notables jointly demonstrated during Ramadan in the summer of 1920. In Syria, Faisal's kingdom was under French pressure, and its fall in July intensified the sense that Arab claims were being overridden.

Religious leadership was crucial. Ayatollah Muhammad Taqi al-Shirazi, resident in Karbala and widely respected among Shi'is, issued rulings, reported in the memoirs and press of the time, that Muslims should demand their rights and, if refused, could resort to force. His son and other clerics carried the message to the tribes. The revolt was therefore not purely "tribal," nor purely religious, nor purely nationalist; it was a coalition of urban nationalists, clerics, and tribal shaykhs whose motives overlapped but were not identical. Some tribal leaders resented British limits on their authority over land and water; others were pursuing disputes with rivals.

The grievances were not evenly distributed. In the Middle Euphrates, tribal leaders objected to the extension of direct taxation and to officials who interfered with disputes among their followers, while cultivators resented the collection of revenue in a season of poor returns. In the cities, officials and notables who had expected Arab self-government under Faisal were angered by British consultation procedures that seemed to narrow the options. Educated young men in Baghdad, Najaf, and Karbala, many connected through newspapers and clubs, gave the movement a vocabulary of independence drawn from Arab nationalism and from the language of self-determination then circulating at the peace conference.

Historians therefore distinguish between triggers and causes. The arrest of a shaykh at Rumaitha started the fighting, but years of administrative friction, the disappointment of San Remo, and the prestige of religious leaders explain why local anger became regional rebellion. Later Iraqi nationalists would stress unity of Sunni and Shi'i participants; British officials, writing at the time, often stressed tribal restlessness and agitators from outside. Neither framing captures the combination of motives, and students should read both as interested accounts.

Historical Vocabulary
Mandate, Constitutional Monarchy, and Treaty Sovereignty

A mandate was a League of Nations arrangement under which a power governed a territory until it was judged ready for independence. A constitutional monarchy is a system in which a king's powers are defined and limited by a written constitution and elected or appointed bodies. Treaty sovereignty describes a state whose independence is recognized internationally but limited by treaties granting another state military, financial, or diplomatic privileges.

These terms help distinguish Iraq, where a mandate gave way to treaty sovereignty under a constitutional monarchy; Transjordan, where an emirate existed under the mandate; and Saudi Arabia, where independence was asserted by conquest and recognized by treaty. Modern readers should not assume "independent" meant the same thing in each case.

Section 57.3

The Revolt's Campaigns, Suppression, and Political Consequences

The immediate spark came on 30 June 1920 at Rumaitha on the Middle Euphrates, when authorities arrested a tribal shaykh over a dispute involving taxes and his followers freed him. Within weeks resistance spread through the Euphrates towns and tribes: Kifl, Kufa, Najaf, Karbala, Hilla, and Diwaniyya, and parts of the Diyala region. Rebels besieged garrisons, cut the railway, and captured some British posts; British and Indian columns were ambushed. Insurgents also governed some districts for a time through local committees, and in places leaders attempted to institute local order and claimed to be acting for an Arab government.

Britain responded with troop reinforcements from India and with the Royal Air Force, which bombed villages and tribal concentrations. Contemporary officials treated air power as a cheap tool of control, and historians now regard these operations as an important early case of colonial air policing. Major fighting subsided by the autumn, though punitive operations and suppression continued in some districts for longer. Estimates of casualties vary: British and Indian dead are usually put in the hundreds, while Iraqi deaths are estimated in the thousands; a commonly used benchmark is roughly 500 British and Indian dead and about 6,000 Iraqis, but other totals exist and all are approximate. The cost to the British treasury, often cited as tens of millions of pounds, shocked a government under pressure to reduce spending.

The revolt's meaning remains contested. Iraqi nationalist narratives, especially after the 1930s, portrayed it as the foundational national uprising of Sunnis and Shi'is together; later scholars emphasize its local origins and the divergent aims of participants, noting that urban Sunni elites and many tribes did not join, while others joined for tribal reasons. Both interpretations agree on its political effect: Britain concluded that direct rule was too costly and began planning an Arab government that would protect British strategic interests more cheaply. Al-Shirazi's death in August 1920 removed a major religious figure from the scene.

The military story has a regional shape. The rising spread first along the Euphrates, where tribes cut the railway and invested the small garrison towns, then reached the holy cities, where armed committees took over local affairs for a time. British columns relieved besieged posts, but some were ambushed and suffered serious losses, and the army's dependence on the railway and on river transport exposed it. By autumn reinforcements had reached the area, and a combination of ground columns, aerial attacks, and negotiation with tribal leaders restored control. Many tribes made separate terms, a reminder that the rebel coalition lacked a unified command.

The social consequences were heavy. Villages were burned or bombed, livestock was seized, fines were imposed on tribes, and families were displaced. Taxes that had helped cause the revolt continued to be collected after it. At the same time, the experience gave Iraqis a shared event around which to organize memory. Memoirs and later histories treat it as formative, while British parliamentary debates over its cost helped drive the policy shift traced in the next section. As with other colonial wars, the sources are asymmetric: British reports detail operations and cost, while Iraqi narratives survive chiefly in later memoirs, poetry, and oral tradition.

The revolt also had a northern and eastern dimension that is often overlooked. Unrest at Tal Afar in June 1920 preceded the Euphrates rising, and in the late summer fighting spread to districts of the Diyala region, where Arab and Kurdish tribes rose against local garrisons. In the Middle Euphrates a British column suffered heavy losses near Kifl and Hilla in late July, a defeat that shocked officials. Reinforcements from India and Iran, together with aircraft, eventually restored control, but the cost to the British treasury, often cited as tens of millions of pounds, became a major theme in Parliament and the press.

Section 57.4

The Cairo Conference of 1921 and the Selection of Faisal

In March 1921 Winston Churchill, newly Colonial Secretary, convened British officials in Cairo to settle policy in the Middle East. Participants included Percy Cox, the High Commissioner for Iraq, Gertrude Bell, the Oriental Secretary, and T. E. Lawrence, who advised Churchill. The conference favored reducing costs by relying on air power and on Arab governments friendly to Britain. For Iraq it endorsed Faisal ibn Hussein, who had been expelled from Damascus by the French and who had credibility with Sunni nationalists and former Ottoman officers.

Faisal reached Basra in June 1921 and was proclaimed king in Baghdad on 23 August. A referendum reported overwhelming approval, but it was conducted under British supervision and with limited choice; it should be described as a managed endorsement rather than a free election. Even so, there was real Iraqi support among Sunni officials, Sharifian officers, and some Shi'i and tribal leaders, and the monarchy was not simply imposed on a passive population.

The Cairo Conference was an exercise in imperial economy. The British government wanted to cut military spending, secure the air and imperial communications routes to India, and protect oil prospects, while avoiding a repetition of 1920. Officials discussed whether to rely on a local ruler, on a council of notables, or on continued direct administration, and they concluded that a Hashemite king combined acceptability among Arab nationalists with dependence on Britain. Wartime ties between Britain and the Sharifian family made Faisal an obvious candidate, and the arrangement could also be presented as compensation for his loss of Syria.

Iraqi reactions were mixed. Faisal's supporters among former officers and Sunni notables welcomed him; some Shi'i leaders and tribal chiefs accepted him as preferable to direct British rule; a section of nationalists regarded him as imposed. Kurdish leaders in the north were not fully integrated into the process, and the Kurdish districts were left with an ambiguous status. The managed referendum should be understood as a way of giving the outcome a popular face rather than as a measure of opinion. Chapter 56 discussed the international side of this choice; here the emphasis is on how Iraqi politics absorbed it.

Section 57.5

Faisal I and the Building of the Iraqi Government, 1921–1924

Faisal had to create legitimacy with few resources. He drew on Sharifian officers who had served in the Arab Revolt, such as Nuri al-Sa'id and Ja'far al-Askari, and on Ottoman-trained civil servants. Ministries were organized, a small army was formed in 1921 from these officers and Ottoman veterans, and schools expanded under the Education ministry, though British advisers remained embedded in key ministries and Britain controlled the Royal Air Force and the Iraq Levies. Finance depended heavily on customs, taxes on land, and British subsidies.

Faisal also negotiated among communities. He sought Shi'i and tribal support while relying on a largely Sunni officer class and bureaucracy, and he attempted to present the monarchy as above sect. His authority was never secure: nationalists pressed him to resist Britain, while Britain pressed him to accept treaty obligations. Historians see in his pragmatism an attempt to widen Iraq's freedom of action step by step.

Faisal had to create a state with little autonomous force of its own. The army, at first small, was formed from Sharifian officers and Ottoman veterans and became a symbol of sovereignty, though British air and ground forces remained the real guarantors of security. The civil service combined former Ottoman officials, graduates of Ottoman schools, and young recruits, and the education ministry, under figures who emphasized Arab identity, expanded schooling and shaped a generation. Finance was constrained by the dependence on customs and land revenue and by British control over some expenditures.

Legitimacy was the harder task. Faisal had no Iraqi base of his own and was a Sunni ruling a country with a Shi'i majority among its Arabs. He cultivated tribal shaykhs, visited the south, and sought to present himself as protector of all communities, while negotiating with British High Commissioners about the pace of change. The monarchy's later reputation as a mere British instrument obscures both his skill at bargaining and the genuine Iraqi networks that sustained the government. Historians accordingly study the state as the outcome of interaction between a Hashemite court, a Sharifian officer corps, local notables, and a British administration that never ceased to claim the last word.

Fiscal arrangements show how limited the state's room for maneuver was. The Iraqi budget rested on customs, land revenue, irrigation charges, and railways, with British officials embedded in finance and other key ministries, and Britain's willingness to meet part of the military cost depended on keeping that cost low through air power and local forces. Faisal and his ministers pressed for control over the army and revenue, and the pace of Iraqi control rose in step with their ability to show order and solvency.

Section 57.6

The Anglo-Iraqi Treaty of 1922 and the Organic Law of 1925

The Anglo-Iraqi Treaty, signed on 10 October 1922, gave British advisers influence over finance and administration and obliged Iraq to consult Britain on foreign policy; as signed it contemplated a period of up to twenty years. Nationalists attacked it as a mandate in disguise. A protocol of 30 April 1923 sharply limited its term, and ratification was delayed until the Constituent Assembly voted in June 1924 under heavy pressure. The treaty's duration was revised again in January 1926, when an agreement arising from the Mosul settlement tied it to the longer mandate framework unless Iraq entered the League sooner. Resistance to the elections had already led to the deportation, in June 1923, of the senior Shi'i cleric Mahdi al-Khalisi and some relatives, and other clerics left Iraq in protest; these were separate episodes, but together they showed the limits of consent.

The Organic Law (Iraqi Constitution) of 1925 created a constitutional monarchy with a bicameral parliament, a cabinet responsible to the Chamber of Deputies, an appointed Senate, and a king with substantial powers, including the ability to dissolve parliament, appoint prime ministers, and approve laws. It set the formal framework for Iraqi politics until 1958. Yet Britain's rights under the treaty and its retained High Commissioner meant that constitutional government coexisted with significant external influence, and historians treat the constitution as an instrument of state-building rather than proof of full sovereignty.

The constitutional documents show how sovereignty was negotiated. The Organic Law set out the monarchy's hereditary succession, the responsibilities of ministers, and the structure of parliament, but the treaty gave Britain rights of consultation, the High Commissioner retained a recognized role, and the mandate itself remained in force. Elections for the first Chamber were conducted under conditions that favored government candidates, and some opposition groups boycotted or were excluded. Thus constitutional forms were real in that they structured politics, yet they did not by themselves make Iraq sovereign. Students should compare this with Transjordan's later Basic Law (57.18), where British financial control was tighter.

Division II — Iraq: Political Consolidation, Independence, and Crisis
Section 57.7

Parliamentary Politics, Political Parties, and the Iraqi Governing Elite

Iraqi parliamentary life was dominated by a small governing elite. Nuri al-Sa'id, repeatedly prime minister, favored a gradualist path of cooperation with Britain; Ja'far al-Askari, a soldier and diplomat, served as minister and prime minister; Yasin al-Hashimi, a former Ottoman officer, led a more nationalist faction; and Shi'i politicians such as Muhammad Rida al-Shabibi and Ja'far Abu al-Timman voiced opposition and communal concerns. Parties like the Progress Party (pro-government) and the National Party (nationalist) were loose networks of notables rather than mass parties.

Elections were managed by officials, tribal leaders, and the king's preferences; no cabinet in the period was brought down purely by popular vote. The king and the High Commissioner mattered more than parliament in selecting governments, and cabinets changed frequently. This system produced experience in negotiation but also frustration among younger educated Iraqis, teachers, officers, and lawyers who found paths to power blocked.

The elite was cohesive in important ways. Many leading politicians were former Ottoman or Sharifian officers or officials, they intermarried and shared networks, and they relied on the monarchy and on British goodwill. Cabinets were often reshuffled as members traded portfolios, and rivalries centered less on policy than on patronage and personal followings. Opposition came from nationalist parties in Baghdad and from Shi'i politicians who argued that southern Iraq was under-represented, as well as from young officers and educated townsmen who saw the older generation as too accommodating to Britain.

Elections also tell a story about rural power. Candidates needed tribal and landowner support, so shaykhs and notables entered parliament in large numbers and shaped legislation on land and tribal law. At the same time, newspapers and political clubs in Baghdad, Basra, and Mosul created a public sphere that increasingly criticized the government. The relationship between the narrow parliamentary elite and an expanding educated public explains much of the instability of the 1930s.

The mechanics of elections favored the governing elite. Under the electoral law of 1924, voting was indirect: citizens chose secondary electors, who then chose deputies, an arrangement that magnified the influence of notables, shaykhs, and officials. The Senate was appointed by the king, and the king could dissolve the Chamber and appoint prime ministers. Consequently ministers often owed their positions to the palace, the British High Commissioner or ambassador, and personal networks rather than parliamentary majorities, and opposition parties, such as the National Party and later reformist groups, contested elections with limited success or boycotted them. Newspapers and political clubs in Baghdad, Basra, and Mosul carried the criticism that parliament could not.

Section 57.8

Land, Agriculture, Tribes, and the Social Foundations of the Iraqi State

Most Iraqis lived in the countryside, and rural society shaped the state. The government relied on tribal shaykhs to maintain order and collect taxes, and the Tribal Criminal and Civil Disputes Regulation, inherited from British administration, gave shaykhs judicial powers under customary law. Land policy, including registration of title and grants or leases of state land, favored shaykhs and urban notables, and many cultivators became tenants or sharecroppers. Irrigation, whether the Euphrates canals of the Middle Euphrates or pump-fed farms along the Tigris, determined where production grew (see Chapter 3 on river irrigation).

This arrangement stabilized the state but at a social cost: shaykhs gained power relative to their tribesmen, and cultivators' debts and obligations increased. The government's dependence on rural elites also made it vulnerable when tribes resisted conscription or taxes, as in the revolts of the mid-1930s (see 57.14). Evidence comes mainly from British and Iraqi administrative reports, which show elite perspectives far better than those of cultivators.

The agrarian system had deep roots in Ottoman land law and in the tribal order. Registration of title, especially for large estates in the south, often favored shaykhs who obtained legal ownership of lands their tribesmen had cultivated under customary rights. The growth of pump irrigation in the 1920s and 1930s, financed by merchants and landowners, enlarged cultivated area along the rivers, but it also concentrated control of water and land. A 1933 law on the rights and duties of cultivators sought to regulate relations between landlords and peasants and in practice strengthened landlords' claims over cultivators' debts and movement.

For cultivators and tribesmen, the state's growth meant conscription debates, tax collection, and the increasing reach of courts and police. Some tribal leaders became wealthy landowners and political figures, while others lost standing, and the gap between shaykh and tribesman widened. Women and children carried much of the labor of planting, harvesting, and date and livestock work, though the sources describe their experience mainly indirectly. The result was a state resting on a negotiated partnership with rural elites, which gave stability but also made later reform politically difficult.

Law structured the relationship between landlords, cultivators, and the state. The Tribal Criminal and Civil Disputes Regulation, introduced by the British in 1918 and amended by the Iraqi government in 1924, kept customary tribal law operating for rural populations and recognized shaykhly authority, creating a legal dualism between tribal and urban courts. The 1933 Law Governing the Rights and Duties of Cultivators regulated tenancy and debt and in practice tied cultivators more firmly to landowners. Conscription, introduced by law in 1934 with the aim of building a national army, provoked tribal and Shi'i resistance in the Middle Euphrates in 1935, which the army suppressed.

Section 57.9

Shi'i Religious Leadership, Sunni Political Elites, and Representation

Iraq's Shi'i Arabs formed a plurality or majority of the population, but Sunni Arabs, a smaller group with Ottoman administrative experience, dominated the army and bureaucracy. Shi'i leaders were divided: some clerics boycotted the state, Mahdi al-Khalisi was deported in 1923, and others left Iraq in protest; Shi'i politicians sought representation in cabinets and parliament; and many tribal shaykhs of the south worked with the government. Communal identity was important but did not dictate a single program: Sunni nationalists criticized British influence, and Shi'i landowners and merchants sometimes allied with the king. Historians caution against reading later sectarian conflict back into these years.

The question of representation was both numerical and institutional. Shi'i Arabs formed a large part of the Arab population, but their access to senior army and civil-service posts was limited by the legacy of Ottoman preference for Sunni officials, differences in education, and the early boycotts by some clerics. Shi'i politicians pressed for more places in cabinet, for schools and for investment in the south, and the government made some concessions, for example by including Shi'i ministers and by appointing Shi'i deputies. Clerical authorities in Najaf and Karbala retained religious and social influence, though their relationship to the state oscillated between distance and engagement.

Not all Shi'is thought alike. Landowning shaykhs of the south often cooperated with the government, merchants and urban professionals pursued their own interests, and younger radicals, including some inspired by emerging leftist and nationalist ideas, criticized both the elite and the clergy. Sunni communities were likewise divided among officials, tribal leaders, and townsmen. Historians therefore resist treating the period as a simple Sunni–Shi'i contest, while recognizing that questions of sect, access, and recognition were real and would recur.

Section 57.10

Kurdish Regions, Mosul, and the Struggle over Northern Iraq

The status of the Mosul vilayet, whose population included Kurds, Arabs, Turkmen, Assyrians, and Yazidis, was disputed between Britain (acting for Iraq) and Turkey after the Treaty of Lausanne (1923) left it unresolved. Kurdish leaders pursued varied goals. Shaykh Mahmud Barzinji, who had been appointed governor in Sulaymaniyya by the British, declared himself king of Kurdistan in 1922 and resisted British and Iraqi forces until his defeat in 1924. This was not the end of Kurdish resistance: Mahmud continued to contest control in the mountains for some years and reappeared in the Sulaymaniyya unrest and renewed revolt of 1930–1931. A League of Nations commission visited the region in 1925, and the Council in December 1925 awarded Mosul to Iraq under conditions that included continuing the mandate for twenty-five years; the Anglo-Turkish-Iraqi Treaty of Ankara in June 1926 confirmed the frontier and gave Turkey a share of oil royalties for a period. Kurdish expectations of autonomy were largely unmet, and the unresolved Kurdish question would recur in Iraqi politics.

The Mosul dispute had economic and strategic dimensions. Turkey claimed the vilayet as part of the Ottoman heritage and as home to many Kurds; Britain argued that Iraq needed the northern region for economic viability and defense; and oil prospects made the territory more valuable. The League commission, which traveled through the region in 1925, reported a population composed largely of Kurds, with large Arab, Turkmen, and Christian minorities, and recommended that Mosul remain in Iraq on condition that Kurdish interests be protected and that the mandate be extended. The Council accepted this, Turkey ultimately agreed in 1926, and the frontier was thereby fixed.

The Kurdish question remained unresolved. British officials had at times spoken of Kurdish autonomy, and Iraqi governments gave assurances about Kurdish language and administration, but implementation was partial. Kurdish leaders differed among themselves, with chiefs, religious families such as the Barzanjis and Barzanis, and urban intellectuals pursuing varied aims. The sources are heavily British, and Kurdish voices come through petitions, memoirs, and later histories.

Kurdish politics in these years moved between negotiation and revolt. In December 1922 the British and Iraqi governments issued a joint declaration recognizing the right of Kurds within Iraq to form a government, but implementation was limited, and Shaykh Mahmud Barzinji's attempt to rule Sulaymaniyya independently ended in his defeat in 1924. Iraqi legislation in the early 1930s permitted the use of Kurdish in schools and administration in Kurdish districts, but practice varied. Unrest in Sulaymaniyya in 1930 and the revolt of Shaykh Ahmad Barzani in 1931–1932, put down with the help of the RAF, showed that Kurdish questions of language, tax, and autonomy remained open. Kurdish voices appear in petitions, memoirs, and later histories, while the official record is mostly British and Iraqi.

Section 57.11

Petroleum, the Iraq Petroleum Company, and Economic Development

Before the war, the Turkish Petroleum Company, a consortium of British, German, and Ottoman interests, held rights in the Mosul and Baghdad provinces. After the war, its shares were redistributed among British, French, American, and Dutch interests, and it was renamed the Iraq Petroleum Company (IPC) in 1929. A 1925 concession gave IPC wide rights. In October 1927 the Baba Gurgur well near Kirkuk struck a major gusher, confirming that Iraq had large oil reserves. The 1928 "Red Line Agreement" among the IPC partners restricted competition across much of the former Ottoman world.

Revenue to the Iraqi state remained modest through the 1930s: royalties were fixed by concession terms, and exports began only after the pipelines from Kirkuk to Haifa and Tripoli opened in 1934. Oil promised future independence from British subsidies, but the foreign ownership of IPC kept control over production outside Iraq's hands. Students should avoid assuming that the later scale of oil wealth was visible in the 1920s; contemporaries saw potential rather than certainty.

Oil finance and diplomacy were entangled. The Iraqi government had little power to bargain with the company, and the 1925 concession was negotiated in a setting where British influence was strong. Revenue came as fixed royalties, and Iraq depended on them to fund the army and public works. The Red Line Agreement among the company's partners limited competition within the former Ottoman area, with consequences for Saudi and Gulf concessions (57.29, 57.40). The construction of the pipelines in the early 1930s employed local workers, introduced new technologies, and created a small modern labor force, though conditions and wages were a source of later grievance.

The concession history is complex. The Turkish Petroleum Company, a consortium of British, Dutch, French, and American interests formed after the war, obtained a seventy-five-year concession from the Iraqi government in 1925; after the Baba Gurgur strike of October 1927 the partners signed the Red Line Agreement of 1928 to avoid competing within a defined area of the former Ottoman Empire, and in 1929 the company was renamed the Iraq Petroleum Company. The Iraqi government received a fixed royalty per ton and had little say in production decisions. The pipelines to Haifa and Tripoli, completed in 1934, made export possible, and exports provided the Iraqi budget with a growing but still modest source of income in the late 1930s.

Section 57.12

The Anglo-Iraqi Treaty of 1930 and Independence in 1932

The League's Permanent Mandates Commission and Iraqi demands pressed Britain to define a path to independence. Nuri al-Sa'id negotiated the treaty signed in June 1930, an alliance for twenty-five years in which Britain and Iraq agreed to consult on foreign affairs, Britain would have the use of air bases at Habbaniyya and Shu'ayba, British forces could move through Iraq in wartime, and Britain would support Iraq's admission to the League. Iraq entered the League in October 1932 and the mandate formally ended.

Nationalists criticized the treaty for perpetuating British military rights, while its defenders argued it achieved the maximum attainable. Iraq's independence was real in international law and in the conduct of domestic government, but the strategic position of Britain remained powerful. This tension is explored in the following feature.

Negotiation of the treaty involved long argument about the status of British military rights. Britain wanted guaranteed access to bases and communications; Iraqi nationalists wanted a short treaty and a clear end to mandatory control; and the League's Mandates Commission examined whether Iraq met standards of stability, justice, and protection of minorities. British officials certified Iraq's readiness, a judgment criticized later given the events of 1933. After 1932 the High Commissioner was replaced by an ambassador, British officers remained as advisers in some branches, and the Iraqi army and government assumed fuller control of domestic affairs. The treaty of 1930 took effect on the end of the mandate and Iraq's League admission, rather than as a continuation of the earlier shortened treaties.

Section 57.13

Minority Protection, the Assyrians, and the Simele Massacre of 1933

The Assyrians of Iraq included people who had fled the Hakkari highlands and Urmia during the First World War, many of whom served in the British-organized Iraq Levies. Their leaders, particularly Patriarch Mar Shimun XXI Eshai, sought autonomy or guaranteed security as British protection ended, and tension rose in 1933 after armed Assyrians crossed to and from Syria. In August 1933 the Iraqi army, led in the north by General Bakr Sidqi, attacked Assyrian villages around Dohuk and Simele, and many civilians were killed. The mass killing of civilians by Iraqi forces is firmly established. Careful modern studies put the deaths at no fewer than about 600 Assyrians in the wider 1933 atrocities, while higher totals depend on the geographic and temporal scope adopted; Iraqi and Assyrian accounts differ in framing, and League investigation was limited. Disagreement over totals and over the degree of central planning is not disagreement over whether massacres occurred. The episode brought wide popular acclaim to the army inside Iraq and showed that the League's minority guarantees had limited effect.

The immediate background lay in uncertainty after the mandate. Assyrian leaders feared that without British protection they would be vulnerable, while Iraqi officials and press suspected them of disloyalty because many had served in the Levies and had sought autonomy. In July 1933 a group of Assyrian fighters crossed into Syria and returned, and the Iraqi government mobilized the army and tribal volunteers. Fighting and killings followed in several places, and the events at Simele, where soldiers killed men in the village, became the symbol. Thousands of Assyrians left Iraq in the following years, and the patriarch was deprived of citizenship and exiled.

Why does the episode matter? It showed that minority protection under the League was weak once the mandate ended, that the army could command popular approval for violence, and that Iraqi nationalism could be defined in ways that excluded minorities. Historians use Iraqi and British official records, League papers, eyewitness reports by missionaries and relief workers, and Assyrian testimony, and they differ over the degree of central planning and over numbers, while agreeing on the central facts.

The sequence of events in 1933 illustrates how quickly tension became violence. In July, several hundred armed Assyrians, led by supporters of the patriarch, crossed into Syria seeking resettlement and returned when the French refused to accept them; in early August, clashes occurred between them and Iraqi forces in the Dohuk district, including at Dairabun, where the Assyrians scored a local success. The Iraqi army, joined by Kurdish and Arab irregulars, then advanced, and during the first half of August villages in the area were attacked and civilians killed, culminating in the massacre at Simele on 11 August. The mass killing of unarmed civilians by Iraqi forces is firmly established, and the patriarch was detained and then exiled. Iraqi public opinion celebrated the army's success, and General Bakr Sidqi emerged as a popular hero, a development that shaped his political role in 1936.

Section 57.14

Iraq after Faisal I: Ghazi, the Army, and Political Instability, 1933–1939

Faisal I died in Switzerland in September 1933. His son Ghazi (1933–1939) lacked his father's political skills and nationalist credentials, and he was drawn toward the army and radical nationalists. Cabinets changed rapidly, and tribal revolts in the Middle Euphrates in 1935–1936 over conscription, taxation, and politics were put down by the army. In October 1936 General Bakr Sidqi, with Hikmat Sulayman, carried out a coup that overthrew the government of Yasin al-Hashimi, in the first military coup in the modern Arab world. Ja'far al-Askari was killed during the coup. Sidqi was assassinated in 1937, and later governments depended on officers' factions. Ghazi died in a car accident in April 1939, leaving a young heir and a regency. The chapter closes before the crisis of 1941.

Instability in the later 1930s reflected structural problems. The king and prime ministers competed for influence over the army, tribal revolts demonstrated the limits of rural control, and educated youths, including those drawn to new reformist groups, criticized the older elite. The 1936 coup was launched by army units but supported by reform-minded civilians who hoped for social change, and it opened a period in which officers intervened repeatedly. Sidqi's government pursued some reforms and an authoritarian line, antagonized rivals, and fell after his assassination in 1937. Later cabinets were shaped by competition among officers' groups and civilian leaders, including some more openly anti-British. The state therefore entered 1939 with formal independence, growing oil prospects, and a politicized army.

Division III — Transjordan and the Hashemite Emirate
Section 57.15

Transjordan after the Fall of the Syrian Kingdom

After the French destroyed Faisal's Syrian kingdom at Maysalun in July 1920, the lands east of the Jordan were left in political uncertainty. They had been loosely tied to Damascus, and no mandatory power had decided how to govern them. They were not empty: the towns of Salt, Karak, Irbid, and Ma'an had notable families; Circassian and Chechen settlers, brought by the Ottomans from 1878, farmed around Amman and in other villages; and Bedouin tribes such as the Bani Sakhr, Huwaytat, and Adwan moved across the steppe and held claims to pasture and to the allowances once paid for protecting the pilgrimage and the Hijaz railway. Local councils formed in the districts of Ajlun, the Balqa, and Karak under a handful of British political officers sent from Jerusalem, and authority was fragmented.

For Britain the territory mattered as a land bridge between Palestine, Iraq, and the Gulf and as a buffer to Palestine and the Suez route, but London was unwilling to commit resources. Abdullah ibn Hussein reached Ma'an in November 1920 at the head of followers and announced his intention of marching on the French in Syria. His arrival gave the region a leader who could keep order cheaply, and local notables, some of whom hoped for a share in an Arab government, accepted him, though they soon resented the Syrian and Hijazi followers who filled posts. The emirate was therefore the result of a convergence between a Hashemite prince with prestige but no base, local leaders seeking order and patronage, and a British government seeking economy.

The Ottoman legacy east of the Jordan was administrative as well as geographic. The region had been divided among districts of the province of Syria, with Karak and Ma'an organized as a separate district in the 1890s, and Ottoman officials had introduced land registration, tax collection, schools in a few towns, and military garrisons. The Hijaz railway, completed to Medina in 1908, ran through Amman, Ma'an, and Tabuk and created stations that became settlements. During the war the area had been a theater of the Arab Revolt, including the capture of Aqaba in 1917, and veterans of that campaign, as well as families who had served the Ottomans, formed part of the local landscape. The towns' merchants and landowning families, especially those of Salt, had commercial ties to Nablus, Damascus, and Jerusalem, and many of their sons had been educated in Ottoman or Syrian schools, which made them a natural pool for the new administration.

The sources for this moment are asymmetric. British political officers' reports, memoirs of Hashemite associates, and later Jordanian histories describe the emergence of the emirate, but the views of villagers, Bedouin women, and the many families outside the notable class are visible mainly through petitions, tax records, and oral history. Historians therefore reconstruct the period by comparing British files with Arabic memoirs, newspapers, and tribal genealogies, and by noticing where each account assigns agency: British accounts credit imperial planning, Hashemite accounts credit the emir's initiative, and local accounts stress the choices of tribal and town leaders.

Section 57.16

Abdullah, Britain, and the Formation of the Emirate

In March 1921 Churchill met Abdullah in Jerusalem and agreed that he would administer Transjordan provisionally, under British supervision and as part of the mandate for Palestine, and would prevent attacks on the French. The arrangement was open to revision, but it lasted. Abdullah set up a government in Amman, then a small town, and British officials created the supporting framework: a British Resident, financial control, and a small administration. In 1922 the British government obtained League approval to exclude Transjordan from the clauses of the mandate concerning the Jewish national home, and in May 1923 Britain announced its recognition of an "independent" government under Abdullah, subject to the mandate and to British financial and military control. This recognition should not be confused with the full independence of 1946.

Abdullah's authority combined prestige, patronage, and constant negotiation. He used his descent from the Prophet's family, gifts, and marriage ties to build a following among shaykhs and townsmen, and he dreamed of a larger Hashemite realm, but he lacked an independent income and depended on British subsidy. The British resident and the Foreign and Colonial Offices watched his expenditure and his contacts with Syrian and Palestinian nationalists, and local notables, especially in Karak and Salt, objected to rule by outsiders and demanded a constitution. The emirate emerged from interaction among these actors, not only from British design.

British personnel shaped the early emirate more than the formal arrangements suggest. Harry St John Philby served as Chief British Representative in Amman from 1921 to 1924 and clashed with Abdullah over spending, appointments, and the emir's ambitions in Syria, while Syrian and Palestinian advisers around the emir, notably Rashid Tali'a, ran much of the administration. The friction between British supervisors, Hashemite courtiers, and local notables was a recurring feature of the period and explains why the emir's government was repeatedly reorganized. Britain's priorities were economy, security on the Palestine frontier, and the avoidance of French complaints; Abdullah's were status, a larger realm, and a secure income.

Section 57.17

The Arab Legion, Tribal Security, and State Administration

The state's first priority was security, and security forces consumed much of the budget. A reserve force organized in 1920–1921 under Frederick Peake became the Arab Legion, which, together with the Transjordan Frontier Force raised by Britain in 1926, policed the territory. Two episodes show the early challenges. The Kura disturbances of 1921–1922, in the northern highlands, were a revolt against the new administration and its taxation, and in September 1923 a more serious revolt led by Sultan al-Adwan, a prominent tribal leader, challenged the emirate and was put down with British help, including aircraft. A series of Ikhwan attacks in the early 1920s, culminating in the attack of 1924 that was driven off by British forces, showed the vulnerability of the eastern frontier.

After 1930 John Bagot Glubb organized the Desert Patrol, recruiting Bedouin to police the desert and the frontier with Saudi Arabia, an effective way of drawing nomads into the state's security system. The government also negotiated with shaykhs: allowances, recognized tribal courts for customary disputes, and understandings about pasture and routes. The system therefore combined coercion and negotiation. British officers and funds underpinned it, so Britain held the means of force. The surviving documents are mostly British, and local perspectives must be reconstructed from later memoirs, tribal oral tradition, and Arabic sources.

The Arab Legion's early composition shows how the state drew on varied groups. It recruited villagers and townsmen from the settled districts, Bedouin from tribal territories, and former officers and soldiers of the Ottoman and Sharifian armies, many from Syria and the Hijaz, and it was paid, armed, and largely officered by Britain. Its roles were police as much as military: guarding roads and the railway, collecting taxes in the face of resistance, escorting officials, and responding to raids. The Frontier Force, raised in 1926 as a British-controlled formation drawn partly from the Palestine Gendarmerie, served the larger imperial purpose of defending the frontier with Palestine and the desert approaches. The existence of two forces under British control meant that the emir commanded neither.

The Adwan revolt of 1923, led by Sultan al-Adwan and supported by tribal and political discontent, and the Kura disturbances of 1921–1922 reflected resistance to taxation, to the presence of outsiders in government, and to the loss of local autonomy. The Ikhwan raid of August 1924, repelled with the aid of British aircraft and armored cars at Umm el-Amad south of Amman, showed that external threats could strike the heart of the emirate. These events strengthened the argument, made by British officials and by Abdullah, that a regular force and British air power were necessary. They also taught the government the usefulness of negotiated settlements with tribal leaders, such as the Bani Sakhr under Mithqal al-Fayez, who held land and influence in the Amman area and became pillars of the emirate.

The Desert Patrol under Glubb, formed in the early 1930s, institutionalized this approach by recruiting Bedouin to police their own territory under British officers and by using trucks and wireless to extend control. It replaced the older system of reliance on subsidy-driven tribal alliances with a more direct form of state authority, but it also reduced the raids that had been a part of Bedouin economy and politics, and its effect on tribal society was therefore double-edged: it provided salaries and status for some, and removed customary sources of income for others.

Section 57.18

The 1928 Treaty, Basic Law, and Legislative Institutions

The 1928 constitutional arrangements reflected a balance of forces. Britain wanted the emirate to remain solvent and manageable, Abdullah wanted greater authority and a firm constitutional base, and a small group of nationalists, many of them educated townsmen, demanded real representation. The Anglo-Transjordan Agreement of 20 February 1928 defined British rights over finance, defense, and foreign relations while recognizing Abdullah's government; the Basic Law of 16 April 1928 set out the emir's powers, an executive council, and a Legislative Council; an electoral law was issued in June 1928; voting took place in some districts in February 1929; and the council's first session met on 2 April 1929. The emir held wide powers, including appointing ministers and dissolving the council, and the council's authority over budgets and legislation was limited, with British officials holding a veto in practice.

The agreement provoked protest. A Transjordan National Congress that met in 1928 rejected it and demanded a government responsible to an elected body, and the government restricted its activities. The framework nonetheless created institutions, an electoral practice, and a small political class that would persist. It is best described as constitutional development without sovereignty: forms of representation existed, but the reserved powers meant that key decisions were made in Jerusalem and London.

Politics around the 1928 documents reveal the limits of the arrangement. The Transjordan National Congress, meeting in July 1928 with delegates from towns and tribal areas, demanded a government responsible to an elected assembly, rejected the Anglo-Transjordan Agreement as incompatible with real independence, and called for a boycott of its implementation. The government responded by arresting some leaders and by banning the Congress's activities. In the 1929 elections, many voters were tribal and rural, and notables who accepted the emir's authority were returned; the first Legislative Council contained both elected members and officials, and the emir kept the ability to dissolve it. The council's debates, partly preserved, show members raising complaints about taxation, the cost of British officials, and the distribution of public works, even though they could not control policy.

The constitutional episode matters because it created expectations. Educated Transjordanians, many trained in Syrian or Palestinian schools and influenced by nationalist politics, treated the Basic Law as a base on which to build, and in later years they pressed for revisions. British officials treated it as a means of channeling criticism without conceding control. Both views help explain why the emirate's politics would revolve around the question of when and how the reserved powers would be reduced, a question answered only in the 1940s.

Section 57.19

Transjordanian Society: Towns, Tribes, Agriculture, and Education

Transjordan's population, perhaps a few hundred thousand, was mostly rural and mixed. In the northern highlands and around Irbid and Ajlun, villagers farmed grain, olives, and vines; on the Balqa and the Karak plateau, cultivation combined with herding; and Bedouin groups moved with their herds to the desert margins. Land registration and the sale of tribal and state lands to merchants and officials, together with taxation, debt, and the unpredictability of rainfall, shaped rural life, and land settlement and tax reform in the 1930s changed relations between cultivators and the state. Amman, with its Circassian, Arab, and Syrian residents, grew from a village into an administrative and commercial center with government buildings, schools, and markets, and the Hijaz railway, though damaged, still carried goods and pilgrims.

Schooling expanded slowly through government and mission schools, and a small educated class of teachers, clerks, and officers appeared; girls' education lagged. Women's work in farming, herding, and household production was central, though the sources describe it mainly indirectly through officials' and travelers' reports. Households combined agriculture, herding, labor, and remittances, and many Transjordanians found work in Palestine, in Syria, or in the security forces. The government's tax base was thin, so British subsidy remained essential.

The economic framework limited development. Transjordan shared a currency and customs arrangements with Palestine, so that revenue from customs and the movement of goods depended on decisions made in Jerusalem, and the emirate's own taxes fell heavily on the agricultural produce of villagers, notably the grain tithe, until reforms in the 1930s associated with land settlement and the registration of holdings. Rainfall variation produced hard years, and the sale of land by indebted cultivators to merchants and officials increased inequality in some districts. Bedouin communities faced pressure on pasture from settlement and border controls, even as the Desert Patrol brought some of them into state service. A small number of migrants, including Palestinians and Syrians, settled in Amman and the towns, adding to the urban population and its political ideas.

Section 57.20

Transjordan's Frontiers and the Limits of Hashemite Autonomy

Transjordan's frontiers were set largely by British decisions and negotiations rather than by local agreement. In 1925, as British policy shifted, Ma'an and Aqaba, which had been held by the Hijaz, were attached to Transjordan, and agreements with Ibn Saud, the Hadda and Bahra agreements of 1925, defined arrangements for the desert frontier and the Wadi Sirhan, a corridor used by tribes. Raids and the movement of tribes across the border continued to cause friction and required British air and ground action. Relations with Syria involved French authorities and refugees from the Syrian revolt of 1925, and relations with Palestine involved trade, migration, and the implications of Zionist immigration, subjects treated in Chapter 59.

Abdullah's ambition for a larger Hashemite realm, whether in Syria or Iraq, ran against French and British resistance, and he was often portrayed by his rivals as a British client. British financial and military assistance sustained the emirate but also constrained it. Independence in 1946 is outside this chapter.

The frontier with Saudi Arabia shaped Transjordan's first decade. Ikhwan raids in the early 1920s, and the dispute over Ma'an and Aqaba, which Hijazi forces had held in 1924, made the south the most sensitive border. When Ibn Saud conquered the Hijaz, Britain arranged, in 1925, for Ma'an and Aqaba to be attached to Transjordan, securing the road to the sea and the connection with the Hijaz railway line, and the Hadda and Bahra agreements of that year set out rules for the desert frontier, tribal movement, and wells. Abdullah, whose family had just lost the Hijaz, maintained an uneasy relationship with Ibn Saud, and British officials mediated between them. The Wadi Sirhan, a corridor linking Transjordan, Najd, and Iraq, remained a zone of tribal movement and a source of friction.

To the north, Syrian rebels fleeing the French after the revolt of 1925, among them Druze leaders such as Sultan al-Atrash, found refuge in Transjordan, creating a diplomatic problem as France pressed Britain to restrain them. To the west, Palestine was linked through trade, migration, and the movement of people between the two banks of the Jordan. Abdullah's contacts with Palestinian and Zionist figures, which are treated in Chapter 59, were part of his search for support, but they also fed suspicion among Arab nationalists. The emirate's position, small, poor, and surrounded by larger and better-resourced neighbors, helps explain its dependence on Britain and the care with which its rulers calculated every step.

Division IV — Ibn Saud and the Creation of Saudi Arabia
Section 57.21

Ibn Saud and the Recovery of Saudi Power in Najd

The Saudi state of the nineteenth century (Chapter 48) had collapsed under Rashidi and Ottoman pressure in the 1890s, and the family spent years in exile in Kuwait. In 1902 Abd al-Aziz ibn Abd al-Rahman, known as Ibn Saud, took Riyadh in a small night raid and began to rebuild power by combining military initiative with careful politics: marriages with leading families, the appointment of relatives and loyal followers as governors, subsidies to tribal chiefs, and a partnership with the religious scholars descended from or aligned with Muhammad ibn Abd al-Wahhab, who supplied judges, teachers, and the language of religious legitimacy. He took al-Hasa from the Ottomans in 1913 and in 1915 signed a treaty with Britain that brought recognition and a subsidy, though he did not join the Arab Revolt.

After 1918 his task was different: to transform a regional emirate dependent on personal loyalty into a durable state. That meant securing the interior against the Rashidis, finding a means of controlling the tribes that had joined him, managing a British government that was simultaneously subsidizing Hussein and listening to Ibn Saud, and finding revenue. His method was personal and flexible. He traveled constantly, held open audiences, arbitrated disputes, and bound leaders to him through kinship and gifts. For the historian the evidence is diverse: the chronicles of Najdi scholars and later court historians, British political reports and the writings of officers such as Harry St John Philby, who knew him well, and travelers' accounts. Each is partial: Saudi chronicles celebrate the dynasty, British reports view him as a diplomatic problem, and the tribes and townspeople he ruled speak mainly through later oral tradition.

Section 57.22

The Defeat of the Rashidis and the Unification of Najd

The Rashidi emirate of Ha'il had been the main rival in northern Najd, and for decades it was a tribal partner of the Ottoman Empire, which supplied it with arms and recognition, and it fought on the Ottoman side in the First World War. When the empire collapsed in 1918 the Rashidis lost their principal patron, and the dynasty was already weakened by repeated assassinations and succession disputes among its princes. British officials considered Ha'il as a possible counterweight to Ibn Saud but came to favor the Saudi side in practice, and by the early 1920s no outside power would rescue the Rashidis.

Ibn Saud's campaign combined military pressure, diplomacy, and the use of the Ikhwan. After reducing Rashidi allies in the countryside, he besieged Ha'il in 1921 and took it in November, ending the Rashidi emirate; he then incorporated Jabal Shammar, appointed governors from his family and trusted retainers, and took the title Sultan of Najd and its dependencies. Control of the north brought him to the frontiers of Iraq and Transjordan, where the problems of raiding and borders would soon demand attention. Consolidating Najd mattered for two reasons: it removed a rival who might have allied with the Hashemites, and it gave Ibn Saud a secure base from which to turn to the Hijaz.

Historians see this as the decisive stage of Saudi state-building because it combined conquest with incorporation. Defeated chiefs were often kept in place if loyal, marriages tied families to the dynasty, and religious instruction was extended through the Najdi towns and the new settlements. The pattern would be repeated, with variations, in each new region Ibn Saud acquired.

Section 57.23

The Ikhwan: Religious Mobilization, Settlement, and Military Organization

The Ikhwan (the "brothers") were the most distinctive institution of Saudi expansion. From about 1912, nomadic and semi-nomadic tribesmen of Najd, including sections of the Mutayr, Utayba, Ajman, and Harb, were encouraged to settle in agricultural colonies called hijar (singular hijra), the first of which, al-Artawiyya, became a model. The term recalled the Prophet's emigration to Medina and signified a break with the tribal life of raiding and with practices regarded as un-Islamic. In the hijar the settlers received religious instruction from scholars sympathetic to the Wahhabi tradition, lived in permanent villages with wells, fields, and mosques, and pledged allegiance to the ruler as the leader of a community of believers. The movement fused religious zeal with tribal fighting power.

Militarily the Ikhwan were the cutting edge of Ibn Saud's forces. They were mobile, disciplined by faith, and ready to fight those they regarded as polytheists or heretics; they played a major part against the Rashidis, destroyed a Hashemite force at Turaba in 1919, and led the assaults on Ta'if and Mecca in 1924. Their leaders, among them Faisal al-Duwish of the Mutayr, Sultan ibn Bijad of the Utayba, and Dhidan ibn Hithlayn of the Ajman, were powerful men with their own followings. The settlement project also served Ibn Saud: it brought nomads under supervision, created a reservoir of manpower, and expanded cultivation. But the hijar remained tribal in structure, and their members' loyalty was to a religious mission as well as to a king.

Evidence about the Ikhwan comes from Saudi chronicles, British reports, and the accounts of travelers and officials who sometimes reported on the settlements from outside. They differ in how they depict the movement's religious sincerity, the degree of coercion in settlement, and its relation to the ruler. It should not be confused with the twentieth-century Muslim Brotherhood (Chapter 67), an unrelated Egyptian movement.

Historical Vocabulary
Ikhwan, Hijra, Imamate, and Emirate

The Ikhwan were the Najdi settler-warriors described above. A hijra in this context was a planned agricultural settlement, deliberately echoing the Prophet's migration to Medina, where nomads settled and studied religion. An imamate is a political-religious leadership: in Yemen, the Zaydi imam combined religious authority and rule; in Oman, the Ibadi imam was chosen by scholars and tribal leaders. An emirate is a state ruled by an emir (prince or commander), as in Najd and Transjordan.

These terms show that legitimacy could rest on religion, descent, election by notables, or military success.

Section 57.24

The Hashemite Kingdom of the Hijaz after the Arab Revolt

The Hijaz differed sharply from Najd. Mecca and Medina were centers of pilgrimage and learning with a cosmopolitan population of scholars, merchants, and pilgrim-guides drawn from across the Muslim world; Jeddah was a busy port with merchants from India, Java, Egypt, and Yemen; and Ta'if, a summer resort and orchard district, was home to Hijazi notables. The economy rested on the pilgrimage, customs at Jeddah, and trade, with a small agricultural base. Government under Sharif Hussein ibn Ali, who had led the Arab Revolt and been recognized by Britain as king of the Hijaz, had to function on this narrow base.

After 1918 Hussein's position weakened. The British subsidy that had supported the Revolt ended in 1919, and he quarreled with Britain over the peace settlement, refusing to accept the Treaty of Versailles and later the treaty drafts offered to him. His centralized and personal style of government, his reliance on his sons and favorites, and the burden of taxes and customs alienated merchants and notables. In March 1924, after Turkey abolished the caliphate, Hussein proclaimed himself caliph, a claim that most Muslims rejected and that gave Ibn Saud and his religious allies a religious pretext, while Britain declined to support him. Hijazi notables were divided: some resented Hussein's rule and welcomed an alternative, while others feared Najdi rule and its religious demands.

Section 57.25

The Saudi Conquest of the Hijaz, 1924–1925

The campaign began in the late summer of 1924. Ikhwan forces took Ta'if in early September, in an assault in which many residents were killed; the memory of the event was bitter in the Hijaz. Hussein abdicated in October in favor of his son Ali, but Saudi forces entered Mecca that month, and Ali held Jeddah and Medina for over a year. Medina surrendered in December 1925, and Jeddah soon afterward after a siege marked by shortages and desertions. In January 1926 Ibn Saud was proclaimed king of the Hijaz, in addition to sultan of Najd. Hussein left for Aqaba and later Cyprus, and Ali withdrew to Iraq, where his brother Faisal was king. Britain declined to intervene, partly because it had lost patience with Hussein, partly because Ibn Saud's army controlled events, and partly because it wished to preserve its relations with a ruler who now controlled the holy cities.

The conquest brought changes in religious life and administration. The Saudi government enforced Wahhabi-inspired restrictions, including bans on certain forms of shrine veneration and on practices regarded as innovations, and tombs and domes in cemeteries were demolished, provoking protests from Muslims elsewhere, including Egypt and Shi'i communities. At the same time Ibn Saud sought to reassure pilgrims and the Hijazi elite: he convened a Muslim congress in Mecca in 1926, and in the Hijaz he issued basic instructions, established advisory and municipal bodies, and kept much of the existing administration, appointing his son Faisal as viceroy. These measures were intended to integrate a more cosmopolitan and urban region into the Saudi state without abruptly replacing its institutions. Hijazi merchants and scholars gained some influence, but Najdi officials and advisers held the key posts.

Evidence is divided by perspective: Saudi chronicles emphasize unification and the restoration of religious order; Hijazi memoirs and British consular reports record violence, hardship, and anxiety; and Egyptian and Indian Muslim opinion, expressed in the press, shows how widely the fate of the holy cities mattered.

Section 57.26

British Recognition, the Treaty of Jeddah, and Arabian Frontiers

The border zones of the new Saudi realm were arenas of tribal movement, and Britain acted as mediator for its clients. At the Uqair conference of December 1922, Percy Cox helped define the frontiers between Najd, Iraq, and Kuwait and established neutral zones where tribes could graze; Kuwait lost territory, Iraq gained a defined line, and the arrangement left the claims of Ibn Saud's tribes partly satisfied but also set up conflicts over wells and customs. In the mid-1920s the Hadda and Bahra agreements with Britain dealt with the Transjordan frontier. The Ikhwan regarded the Iraqi border forts, such as that at Busayya, as a violation of the arrangements and attacked, leading to British air action and a diplomatic crisis in 1927–1928.

The Treaty of Friendship and Good Understanding signed at Jeddah on 20 May 1927 gave Britain what it wanted: Ibn Saud's undertaking to respect the territories of Kuwait, Bahrain, Qatar, and the Trucial rulers and the mandated territories of Iraq, Transjordan, and Palestine, and cooperation on the pilgrimage and the suppression of the slave trade. In return Britain recognized the independence of his dominions, which were from then on styled the Kingdom of the Hijaz and Nejd and its Dependencies. The treaty therefore recognized a reality created by conquest and bound the new state to restraints on its relations with British clients. It did not settle the frontiers in detail, and many of Saudi Arabia's boundaries were defined only later.

Section 57.27

The Ikhwan Rebellion and the Battle of Sabilla, 1927–1930

Once the Hijaz was secure and Britain had recognized him, Ibn Saud wanted stability. The Ikhwan leaders wanted something else. They saw the expansion as an unfinished jihad against Iraq, Kuwait, and Transjordan, whose rulers were under British influence; they distrusted the king's treaty with Britain, his willingness to deal with non-Muslim powers, and the borders that restricted raiding. They objected also to new technologies, such as the telegraph, motor vehicles, and the wireless, which Ibn Saud was introducing, and to the taxes and customs charged on pilgrims and trade, and they criticized the king's tolerance of Hijazi customs and his dealings with Shi'is in al-Hasa. Economic grievances mattered as well: a ban on raiding removed a source of income, and the Depression of the late 1920s and early 1930s hurt the hijar.

Ibn Saud responded with a combination of religious legitimation and force. In 1926–1927 he consulted senior scholars, who issued rulings supporting the use of technology under proper conditions and confirming the ruler's authority to make treaties, and in late 1928 he held a large assembly of notables and scholars in Riyadh. Ikhwan raids on Iraq and Kuwait in 1927–1928 provoked British aerial and military reaction, and Ibn Saud faced the dilemma of restraining his own forces or risking war with Britain. In March 1929 his forces, with motor vehicles and machine guns, defeated Ikhwan contingents at Sabilla, near Zilfi, in a battle that decided the rebellion. Faisal al-Duwish escaped, surrendered to the British in Kuwait in January 1930, was handed over to Ibn Saud, and died in confinement in 1931. Accounts of the fate of Sultan ibn Bijad differ, some placing his death at or soon after Sabilla and others in later captivity, and precise biographical details should be checked against specific sources.

The rebellion was suppressed into 1930. Loyal tribal levies, some of whom came from Ikhwan settlements, continued to serve Ibn Saud and, after the period covered here, tribal forces were reorganized into institutions that later became the Saudi National Guard, but the defeated rebels of 1929 were not simply absorbed into that body. The significance of Sabilla is that consolidating the state required restraining the force that had made the conquests.

Section 57.28

The Proclamation of the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia in 1932

The decree of 17 September 1932, proclaiming that the realm would be called the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia from 23 September, marked a transition from the rule of a person over a collection of territories to a dynastic state bearing the family's name. It also expressed a political project: the unity of Hijaz and Najd under a single crown, with the extension of authority over Asir, al-Hasa, and the interior. The government was still small. The king, assisted by relatives, a few trusted advisers (including Hijazis, Syrians, Egyptians, and others), and the religious establishment, ruled through governors who combined administrative, financial, and judicial functions. Tribal leaders were bound by allowances and marriage, and scholars controlled the courts and much education.

Integration was a challenge because the regions differed. The Hijaz retained a degree of administrative distinctness, with its 1926 basic instructions, advisory councils, and municipal bodies, and had a cosmopolitan merchant class and a vibrant, if restricted, press. Najd had its own religious and tribal traditions. Al-Hasa and Qatif on the Gulf coast included Shi'i communities who faced restrictions on public religious practice. Asir's highland tribes retained autonomy. The king's solution was to combine central control over revenue, security, and religious law with flexible local arrangements. Revenue was thin and unpredictable: pilgrimage fees, customs duties at Jeddah, and zakat were the main sources, and the Depression reduced them. Fiscal weakness meant that the monarchy depended on personal ties and on subsidies and loans, which explains the king's interest in oil concessions in 1933.

Section 57.29

Saudi Arabia before the Oil Boom: Finance, Concessions, and the 1938 Discovery

Saudi finances in the early 1930s were precarious. The number of pilgrims dropped during the Depression, customs receipts fell, and the king struggled to pay officials and tribal allowances; rumor and complaint reached British and American observers. Geological interest in the eastern province, encouraged by the discovery of oil in nearby Bahrain in 1932 and by American advisers such as the mining engineer Karl Twitchell, led to negotiations with Standard Oil of California (SOCAL). On 29 May 1933 the king granted a concession covering a large area of the eastern province in return for advance payments, rents, and royalties, and the California Arabian Standard Oil Company was formed. The British Iraq Petroleum Company's rival interests were unsuccessful, partly because of the terms and partly because of the king's desire for American capital.

Exploration was expensive and disappointing. Camps, roads, and drilling rigs were established around Dammam, with American geologists and drillers and Arab laborers recruited from the region. After several dry wells, Dammam No. 7 struck commercial oil in March 1938, and the first tanker shipment of crude left in May 1939. These events opened a new era, but in the 1930s production was small and the Second World War soon interrupted expansion. The transformation of Saudi Arabia's economy, society, and state by oil belongs to Chapter 65; the point here is that oil appeared as a solution to the financial weakness of a newly unified kingdom, not yet as the foundation of a wealthy state.

Division V — Yemen, Asir, and British Aden
Section 57.30

Imam Yahya and the Establishment of Independent Yemen

When the Ottomans withdrew in 1918, Imam Yahya Muhammad Hamid al-Din was the one figure in highland Yemen with an established claim to rule. He had been recognized as imam in 1904, had fought the Ottomans for years, and had obtained their acceptance of Zaydi religious autonomy in the Treaty of Da'an of 1911. His position rested on three supports. The first was Zaydi doctrine, which holds that the imam must be a learned male descendant of the Prophet who asserts his claim by rising against unjust rule; descent and learning gave Yahya and the sada (sayyid) families that supplied his judges, governors, and secretaries a distinct legitimacy. The second was the tribal confederations of the northern highlands, above all Hashid and Bakil, whose shaykhs supplied fighters and who were managed through payments, mediation, and the practice of keeping tribal hostages in imamic strongholds. The third was a tax system, built around zakat and levies collected largely in kind, that fed the court and the army.

The state this produced, called the Mutawakkilite Kingdom after the imam's title al-Mutawakkil 'ala Allah, was personal and thinly bureaucratic. Yahya ruled from Sanaa, appointed his sons as governors in key districts (Ahmad at Ta'izz became the most prominent), and insisted on controlling access to the outside world. Foreign contact was carefully managed: he signed a treaty with Italy in 1926 that brought arms and recognition, and a treaty with Britain in 1934, but he kept foreign advisers, schools, and concessions to a minimum. This policy protected the imamate from outside pressure and also left Yemen with little modern infrastructure, few schools beyond religious ones, and a small educated elite.

Yemen's society was not uniform. Zaydis, who dominated the northern plateau, formed the ruling community, while Shafi'i Sunnis in the Tihama coastal plain and the southern districts around Ta'izz and Ibb were numerous and resented heavy taxation and Zaydi officials. Jewish communities of craftsmen in the highland towns lived under imamic rules that restricted but also protected them, and a decree of 1922 concerning Jewish orphans is remembered as a source of fear and later emigration. Evidence comes from imamic chronicles, British political reports from Aden, Italian and other European diplomatic accounts, and travelers' narratives; all of these view the imamate from the outside or from the court, and the experience of tribal cultivators and Shafi'i townspeople survives mostly through later oral history.

The imamate's justice and administration were conducted largely through Islamic law as understood in the Zaydi tradition, with judges drawn from learned families, and through customary tribal mediation, which the imam respected and used. The state's military consisted of a small regular force drawing on Ottoman military experience, alongside tribal levies called up as required; from 1935 selected Yemeni cadets also received military training in Iraq; the practice of holding sons of tribal shaykhs in imamic custody served as a guarantee of loyalty and was resented, but it was also an instrument of integration into the court. Yahya's economy relied on grain, coffee, and other agricultural products, on the trade through Hodeida, and on taxation of the highland peasantry, and the court's relative austerity was often remarked on by visitors.

Succession was a persistent concern. The imamate was not strictly hereditary in Zaydi doctrine, since the imam was to be chosen from among qualified claimants, but Yahya favored his sons, especially Ahmad, and the question of who would succeed shaped factional politics in Sanaa, with consequences visible after 1948. For the period of this chapter the imamate was stable by the standards of the region, and it deserves to be understood as a functioning political system with its own religious and tribal logic, not simply as an isolated or backward state, a characterization that comes largely from outside observers.

Section 57.31

Yemen's Political Geography, Asir, and Rival Arabian Authorities

Southwestern Arabia in 1918 contained several political worlds that no boundary treaty had defined. The highland plateau around Sanaa was Zaydi and tribal; the Tihama plain along the Red Sea, with its port of Hodeida, was hot, Shafi'i, and commercially oriented toward the sea; the rugged coast and highlands of Asir to the north looked toward Mecca and the Yemeni plateau alike; and to the south the British held Aden and a belt of treaty-bound territories. Ottoman provincial lines had been loosely administered and were interpreted differently by every claimant, and the collapse of the empire opened a contest among the Imam, the Idrisi rulers of Asir, Ibn Saud, and Britain.

The Idrisi emirate had been founded by Muhammad ibn Ali al-Idrisi, a scholar of a Sufi-reformist lineage who built a state on the Asir coast and highlands during the Ottoman decline and who cooperated with Britain during the war. After his death in 1923 the emirate was weakened by succession quarrels. Britain, which had occupied Hodeida in 1918, withdrew in 1921 and left it to the Idrisi, whose hold was precarious; in 1925 Imam Yahya's forces seized Hodeida and much of the Tihama. Meanwhile Ibn Saud, who had taken the Hijaz, pressed from the north, and in 1926 the Idrisi ruler Hasan accepted Saudi protection in a treaty concluded at Mecca. Saudi authority in Asir became direct around 1930, though the region retained its own tribal structure and remained restive. An Asiri uprising in 1932 under Hasan al-Idrisi drew Yemeni support, and Yahya's forces also moved into Najran, whose Ismaili community and strategic location made it a prize.

These events explain why there was no settlement in 1918–1919: no power could enforce one, and local tribes shifted allegiance according to payments, protection, and perceived advantage. The border between Yemen and the British protectorates was also unsettled, with Yahya claiming the Aden hinterland as part of Yemen and Britain defending its protectorate treaties, sometimes by air action. Historians therefore read the Saudi–Yemeni war of 1934 as the culmination of a decade of competing claims rather than a sudden quarrel.

The Tihama and Asir had their own social worlds. The Tihama, with towns such as Zabid and the port of Hodeida, had a Shafi'i population oriented toward the sea and the coffee and grain trade, and it was subject to alternating control by the Imam, the Idrisis, and the British. Asir's highland tribes, with centers at Abha and the Idrisi capital at Sabya, were linked to Mecca by pilgrimage and trade routes and to Yemen by kinship and commerce. Najran, a valley oasis with a population that included Ismaili Sulaymani communities and the Yam tribe, was a strategic crossing on the routes between the Yemeni plateau and central Arabia, and whoever controlled it commanded movement across the border zone.

The Saudi–Idrisi treaty of 1926 and the later consolidation of Saudi rule in Asir brought changes: taxation, the appointment of governors, and religious regulation. Tribal leaders who accepted Saudi authority retained local power, and those who resisted faced military pressure, as in the 1932 rising. The Imam's policies toward the area also combined force and patronage. For Asir's and Najran's inhabitants, the contest meant uncertainty about who would collect taxes, protect their trade, and arbitrate disputes, and repeated displacement of leaders. Historians rely on Saudi and Yemeni chronicles, British consular and political reports from Jeddah and Aden, and later local accounts; none is neutral, and the voices of tribal communities are the hardest to recover.

Section 57.32

The Saudi–Yemeni War of 1934 and the Treaty of Taif

The war of 1934 combined local and regional causes. Yemeni and Saudi forces had been in contact for years along the contested districts of Najran, Asir, and the Tihama. After the Asiri rising of 1932 failed, Yemeni support for Hasan al-Idrisi, Yemeni advances in Najran, and Saudi accusations that the imam sheltered Saudi rebels inflamed relations. In spring 1934 Ibn Saud sent two main forces: one under Prince Faisal advanced along the Red Sea coast and took Hodeida, and another under Prince Saud moved through the highlands toward Najran and beyond. Yemeni forces, short of modern arms and facing aircraft and vehicles, were defeated in the field. Britain, concerned that a Saudi occupation of Hodeida and the Tihama would alter the Red Sea balance near Aden, signaled its opposition to annexation.

Ibn Saud chose restraint. Having defeated Yahya, he did not occupy the highlands but accepted a settlement, a decision that scholars connect to concern about British reaction, to the cost and difficulty of ruling Yemen's highlands, and to the principle that Muslim rulers should not take the territory of a fellow Muslim ruler beyond what was claimed. The Treaty of Taif of 20 May 1934 ended hostilities, provided for withdrawal of forces from occupied areas including Hodeida, set out a boundary to be demarcated, established forms of cooperation and mutual obligation, and was to be renewed periodically. The result was that Saudi Arabia retained Asir, Jizan, and Najran, while Yemen retained the highlands and the Tihama. The treaty did not settle the whole frontier, and disagreements over the boundary and its renewal would recur in later decades.

For the societies involved, the war meant movements of troops, requisition, and displacement, and the resulting border divided tribes and families, including those of Najran. The chronology of the fighting and the terms of the settlement are documented in treaty texts and British reports; Yemeni and Saudi chronicles supply their own narratives, and the human cost is poorly recorded.

The war's diplomacy extended beyond the two rulers. In February 1934, before the Saudi offensive, Yahya had concluded a treaty with Britain in Sanaa recognizing his independence and freezing the status quo on the southern frontier for a period of years, a settlement that reduced one danger as he faced another. Italy's interest in the Red Sea, expressed in its earlier treaty with Yemen and later in its Ethiopian ambitions, added to British concern for the security of Aden and the Red Sea route. Ibn Saud's decision to stop at the Tihama and the highlands and to accept a treaty therefore also reflected the wider diplomatic setting.

The Taif settlement left an enduring ambiguity. The treaty provided that the boundary would apply for a fixed period and be subject to renewal, and Saudi Arabia and Yemen later interpreted that provision differently: Saudi leaders treated the settled territory as theirs, while many Yemenis regarded the arrangement as provisional and preserved claims to the lost districts. The border populations, particularly in Najran and Asir, found themselves subject to new authorities, with consequences for tribal relationships, taxation, and religious practice. The episode shows how treaties may end wars without resolving the issues behind them.

Section 57.33

Aden, the British Protectorate, and Southern Arabian Society

Aden was a British possession of a different kind from northern Yemen. Taken in 1839 and governed from India until 1937, it was a coaling station, a free port, and a strategic position on the route to India and Africa. Its population, which numbered in the tens of thousands, included Arabs from Yemen and the Hadramawt, Indians, Somalis, Jews, Parsis, and Europeans, and its economy depended on shipping services, trade with the Horn of Africa and the Yemen interior, salt, and the British garrison. On 1 April 1937 Aden became a Crown Colony under the Colonial Office, a change that reflected its strategic importance and Britain's wish to define it separately from the Indian administration.

Around the port lay a mosaic of protected territories, linked to Britain by treaties of protection signed from the late nineteenth century. In the west, the sultanates and shaykhdoms of Lahej, the Fadhli, the Audhali, the Yafa'i, and others retained internal authority; in the east, in the Hadramawt, the Qu'ayti sultanate at Mukalla and Shihr and the Kathiri sultanate at Seiyun ruled valleys whose merchant and sayyid families had long-standing ties with Java, Singapore, East Africa, and India. British policy before the mid-1930s was largely non-interventionist, offering subsidies, arms, and occasional air action to protect the frontier against the Imam and to keep the peace. In the late 1930s the British adopted a more active approach in the Hadramawt, where Harold Ingrams negotiated a truce among tribes in 1937 (the "Ingrams Peace") and advisory treaties were extended.

Emigration and remittances shaped southern Arabian society. Hadrami migrants worked and invested abroad, and Yemeni and Adeni workers circulated across the Gulf of Aden and the Indian Ocean. These links meant that the south was as much part of an Indian Ocean world as of a Yemeni one. It is essential to separate this region, governed by British treaties, from the Imam's independent kingdom to the north; the later histories of South Yemen and unification are outside this chapter.

Security in the hinterland relied on instruments that expanded in the 1920s and 1930s. The Aden Protectorate Levies, organized in the late 1920s, and the use of Royal Air Force aircraft against tribal and Yemeni incursions replaced earlier dependence on ground expeditions, and British political officers visited rulers and arbitrated disputes. The Imam's claim that the protectorate belonged to Yemen, expressed in occasional occupation of border districts, led to clashes and diplomatic exchanges, and the 1934 treaty of Sanaa postponed the question. At the same time, Italy's growing presence on the other side of the Red Sea, especially after the invasion of Ethiopia in 1935, made Aden more important to British planners.

The Hadramawt was a distinct society. Its valleys were dominated by sayyid families and Hadrami merchants whose wealth was often earned in the Indian Ocean diaspora and remitted home, and by the rival sultanates of the Kathiri at Seiyun and the Qu'ayti at Mukalla, who competed for authority. Tribal warfare and blood feuds disrupted trade and agriculture until the late 1930s, when Harold Ingrams, the British Resident Adviser at Mukalla, brokered truces among the tribes and the Qu'ayti sultan accepted an advisory treaty in 1937, extending British influence. These arrangements, often described as an effort to establish peace and administrative reform, also deepened British control, so that historians treat them both as local pacification and as imperial expansion. The experience of the region's women, laborers, and enslaved or formerly enslaved people is poorly documented, and official sources dwell on rulers and treaties.

Division VI — Oman and the Emerging Gulf States
Section 57.34

Muscat, the Omani Imamate, and the Treaty of Seeb

Oman combined a coastal sultanate at Muscat, ruled by the Al Bu Sa'id dynasty, with an Ibadi interior organized around an elected imam and tribal confederations. Ibadism, a tradition distinct from both Sunni and Shi'i Islam, holds that the imam should be chosen by scholars and tribal leaders for his learning and piety and may be removed if he fails in his duties. The interior centers of Nizwa and Rustaq, with their scholars and tribal factions (the Hinawi and Ghafiri alignments), had long resisted coastal control and taxation, while Muscat, a commercial town linked to India and East Africa and sustained by customs and by British support, depended on the sultan's ties with Britain, whose 1891 agreement bound the sultan not to alienate his territory without British consent.

In 1913 a revolt had brought the interior under an imam, and fighting between the sultan, Taimur bin Faisal (r. 1913–1932), and interior forces continued, at times threatening Muscat itself. The Treaty of Seeb of September 1920, negotiated with British mediation, ended the conflict by creating a working division of authority. The sultan would receive limited customs duty on goods passing from the interior to coastal towns, and the people of the interior would be left to govern themselves under their imam, with provisions on movement, security, and noninterference. The text did not state in so many words which party was sovereign over which territory, and that ambiguity allowed both sides to read it as recognition of their own position: the sultanate saw it as an arrangement within one state, while the imamate's supporters saw it as an agreement between two authorities.

Taimur's government was chronically short of money. The decline of the arms trade after British controls, the loss of the Zanzibar connection, and the Depression left the treasury poor, and Britain exercised financial oversight and appointed advisers. In 1932 Taimur abdicated in favor of his son Said, whose long rule belongs to later chapters. The sources for Oman are heavily British, and Omani chronicles and later histories supply the interior's perspective.

Ibadi legal and political thought gave the imamate coherence beyond tribal loyalty. Scholars in the interior regarded the imam's election as a community responsibility, and the Muhammad ibn Abdullah al-Khalili, elected imam in 1920, symbolized this tradition; his supporters regarded Muscat's rulers as sultans whose dependence on Britain compromised their legitimacy. The sultan, for his part, relied on a small force, on slaves and retainers, on tribal allies on the coast, and on British financial and military support. Muscat's connections with India and East Africa meant that its society included Baluchi, Indian, and African communities, in addition to Arab coastal families, and its economy was far more oriented toward the sea than that of the interior.

Section 57.35

Kuwait: The Al Sabah, Territorial Security, and British Influence

Kuwait was a city-state whose prosperity came from the sea. Its merchants financed the largest pearling fleet in the Gulf and trade with India, East Africa, and Iraq, and its rulers of the Al Sabah family depended on their loans and on customs revenue. The ruler's authority rested on consultation with the leading merchant families, which gave them leverage. The Exclusive Agreement of 1899, concluded under Shaykh Mubarak, committed the ruler not to deal with other powers without British consent and brought British protection.

Kuwait's security was tested in October 1920, when Ikhwan forces under Faisal al-Duwish attacked the Red Fort at Jahra and were repulsed; the town built a new wall afterward. At the Uqair conference of 1922 Britain defined a Kuwait–Najd frontier and a neutral zone, shrinking Kuwait's claims in the interior, and Ibn Saud imposed an economic blockade on Kuwait from 1923 that lasted into the 1930s, damaging trade. Ahmad al-Jabir, who became ruler in 1921, faced a threatened economy, pressure from Najd, and pressure from his own merchants. The pearl crisis and Depression deepened discontent, and in 1938 a reform movement of merchants and younger educated men elected a legislative council that demanded control over finances and a share of oil revenue. The ruler, supported by Britain, dissolved the council in 1939. Oil, for which a concession had been granted in December 1934 to a joint British-American company, appeared in commercial quantity at Burgan in 1938, but exports began only after the war.

Kuwait's merchants were a force in politics long before oil. They provided the ruler with loans, influenced decisions on taxation and trade, and sometimes opposed his policies; a short-lived consultative council in 1921 was an early expression of this role. The 1938 movement drew on the same social base, now joined by young men educated in the new schools, and demanded accountability in the management of public funds as oil prospects improved. The ruler's response, to dissolve the council with British support, illustrated the alignment between a protected dynasty and an external power. Many Kuwaiti families had commercial ties across the Gulf and to India, and the blockade imposed by Ibn Saud encouraged them to look elsewhere for trade.

Section 57.36

Bahrain: Administrative Reform, Social Conflict, and Early Oil

Bahrain's governance was shaped by deep British involvement. The Al Khalifa family had ruled since the eighteenth century, and Britain's treaties gave it control of the island's external relations. In the 1920s, citing misgovernment and disputes over justice, Britain pressed for reform. In 1923 it removed Shaykh Isa bin Ali and installed his son Hamad, and from 1926 Charles Belgrave served as adviser to the ruler and became the effective head of the administration, reorganizing customs, police, courts, municipal services, and education. The reforms improved efficiency and revenue but also increased British influence and sharpened conflict over who benefited.

The island's population included Sunni merchants and ruling-family supporters and a large Shi'i majority, the Baharna, many of them cultivators and pearl divers, as well as Persian-origin merchants and workers. Shi'i petitions in the 1920s and 1930s complained of heavy taxation, forced labor, and favoritism toward the ruling family and its allies, and disturbances in 1938 reflected tensions among communities and between rulers and subjects. In May 1932 the Bahrain Petroleum Company, a subsidiary of Standard Oil of California incorporated in Canada so that it counted as a British company, struck oil. Production and refining expanded during the 1930s and brought employment and a nascent wage-earning class, yet revenue remained modest compared with later oil states. Bahrain illustrates how British protection, local dynasty, and foreign capital interacted in a small island economy.

Section 57.37

Qatar: The Al Thani and the Development of a British Treaty Relationship

Qatar, in the 1930s, was a poor peninsula whose population lived mostly by pearling, fishing, and small-scale trade in Doha and a few other settlements. Shaykh Abdullah bin Jassim Al Thani ruled through personal relations with tribal leaders and merchants, and by a treaty with Britain of November 1916 which accepted British control over his external relations in return for protection. The boundaries and relations with neighbors were unsettled: the Al Khalifa claimed Zubara on Qatar's northwest coast, tensions with Bahrain led to a clash in 1937, and Saudi power was close by. The Depression and the collapse of pearling left the ruler's revenue meager and his dependence on British support greater.

Oil changed the calculation. In May 1935 the ruler granted an exclusive concession to the Anglo-Persian Oil Company, and a separate British political agreement with the company on 5 June linked the concession to British government interests, including British control of the company's registration and labor policy; the correspondence also records a British assurance of protection. Drilling began in 1939 and a first successful well dates to about 1939–1940, but the war soon halted work and commercial production began only afterward. For most Qataris the more immediate reality was economic hardship, and the concession mattered mainly as a promise for the future.

Section 57.38

The Trucial Coast: Rulers, Maritime Agreements, and Changing Political Authority

The Trucial Coast was a cluster of small sheikhdoms linked to Britain by the General Treaty of 1820, the Perpetual Maritime Truce of 1853, and the Exclusive Agreement of 1892. Each had its own ruling family, territory, and economic profile. Abu Dhabi, home of the Bani Yas, controlled a large but poor territory including the Liwa oases and the Buraimi area, and after a period of killings within the ruling family in the 1920s power passed to Shakhbut bin Sultan in 1928. Dubai, ruled by Sa'id bin Maktum, grew as a trading port, attracting merchants from the Persian coast who left because of Persian tariff and customs policies, and its merchants pressed for a share in decisions, as in a reform movement of 1938. Sharjah, under Sultan bin Saqr Al Qasimi, was a center of the Qawasim and in 1932 became the site of an Imperial Airways landing and rest house on the route to India. Ras al-Khaimah, Ajman, and Umm al-Quwain were small, and Fujairah was not recognized by Britain until 1952.

These rulers governed through kinship, tribal alliance, and merchant finance, with minimal bureaucracy. British officials mediated disputes, suppressed piracy and the slave trade, and by the late 1930s encouraged oil exploration; Abu Dhabi granted a concession in 1939 to a British-linked company. The United Arab Emirates, formed in 1971, did not exist, and the interwar sheikhdoms should be treated as separate polities, each with its own history.

The Trucial sheikhdoms also faced social and economic pressures. The suppression of the slave trade by British naval action and by treaty changed the labor supply for pearling and date cultivation, though slavery and its legacies persisted for years. Rulers collected customs, pearl taxes, and tribute from tribal groups, and merchants, many of them from Persian-coast and Indian origins, were important sources of credit. In Dubai, a municipal-style administration was still undeveloped, but the merchants' influence, shown in the 1938 movement, indicated a pattern of negotiation between rulers and traders. Interior tribes, based at oases such as Liwa and Buraimi, moved between the coast and the desert and linked the sheikhdoms to Oman and Saudi Arabia, and their allegiances made the delimitation of the interior frontier a continuing difficulty.

Section 57.39

The Collapse of the Pearl Economy and the Gulf's Changing Economic Foundations

For generations the Gulf economy had rested on pearls. The industry peaked in the years before 1914 and remained large into the 1920s, but several shocks struck in succession. Japanese cultured pearls, developed in the 1920s, reached world markets and undercut natural pearl prices, the Depression reduced demand for luxury goods further, and by the early 1930s the trade was in crisis. The collapse was uneven. Kuwait, with a diversified merchant economy, adapted by shifting to other trade; Bahrain gained employment from oil after 1932; Qatar and the Trucial Coast, which depended heavily on pearls, suffered acute hardship; and many families migrated to other Gulf ports, to Saudi Arabia and Iran, or to the new oil camps.

The organization of pearling shaped the social impact. Merchants advanced credit to boat captains, who in turn advanced money to divers and haulers, creating chains of debt that could continue across seasons and sometimes across generations. When prices fell, debts could not be repaid, leaving divers and their families vulnerable. Rulers lost customs income and in some places faced demands from merchants for reforms, as in Kuwait and Dubai in 1938. The crisis left Gulf rulers more reliant on external revenue, which helps explain their readiness to grant oil concessions. Historians reconstruct the story from British consular reports, merchants' papers, and oral histories collected later, each with its own bias: officials viewed the economy from above, merchants spoke for their class, and divers' voices appear mainly through later recollection.

Everyday Life and Social History

Pearling Households

The main diving season ran through the summer months. Boat captains (nakhudas) hired crews of divers, who descended by breath-hold, and rope-pullers (saibs), and merchants advanced money to captains and to crew families before the season, so that a poor season could leave debts passed to the next year. While men were away for months, women managed households, relatives, and local trade; the evidence for this comes largely from later oral histories and traveler accounts and must be treated cautiously. Enslaved and formerly enslaved people worked in pearling and other trades, though British anti-slavery pressure and local custom shaped how this was documented. The industry's decline altered family fortunes, migration, and relations with merchants.

Section 57.40

British Political Residency, Treaty Relationships, and the Beginnings of the Oil Era

Britain managed Gulf relations through the Political Resident, based at Bushire on the Persian coast and answerable to the Government of India and the Secretary of State for India; Political Agents in Kuwait, Bahrain, and Muscat advised rulers and handled relations with foreign powers, and on the Trucial Coast British interests were handled through the Bahrain agency and local representatives. The Royal Navy kept maritime order. Under the treaties, rulers accepted British control of their external relations and agreed not to alienate territory or grant concessions without British consent, in return for protection and non-interference in internal affairs, though in practice British advice reached deeply into government, as the Bahrain reforms show. The status of these rulers differed from that of the mandates in Iraq and Transjordan, since the Gulf polities were not under League supervision.

Oil sharpened British interest. London wished to keep foreign, especially American, companies from entrenching themselves, and insisted that concessions be held by British-registered firms; this is why the American-owned Bahrain operation was incorporated in Canada and why the political agreement of June 1935 required the Qatar concessionaire to remain a British company. Rulers bargained within these limits: they chose among bidders, extracted payments, and used the treaty relationship to preserve their dynasties. The discoveries and concessions of the 1930s in Bahrain, Kuwait, Qatar, and later Abu Dhabi mark the start of an era whose development, including large-scale production and the transformation of Gulf societies, belongs to Chapter 65.

Historical Vocabulary
Concession, Protectorate, Pearling, and Treaty Relationship

A concession is a grant by a ruler to a company of exclusive rights to explore for and produce oil in a defined area, usually in return for payments; it is not the same as production. A protectorate is a territory whose external affairs, and sometimes defense, are controlled by a stronger state. A treaty relationship describes the Gulf rulers' agreements with Britain, which varied from state to state. Pearling was the diving industry described above.

Division VII — Comparative State-Building in the Arab East
Section 57.41

Dynastic Legitimacy, Religion, Tribal Authority, and the Social Foundations of States

The comparison among the states of the Arab East shows that each solved the problem of authority differently. Iraq combined a constitutional monarchy with a bureaucracy, a conscript army, and a partnership with rural elites. Its king depended on officers and politicians from the former Ottoman and Sharifian elite, and the state's reach into the countryside was exercised through shaykhs and landlords. Transjordan relied on an emir with limited resources, tribal negotiation, and British subsidy and officers. Saudi Arabia built a dynastic kingdom on conquest, kinship, religious legitimacy, and the control, after Sabilla, of independent armed forces. Yemen's imamate rested on Zaydi descent and learning and tribal bargaining. In Oman authority was divided by agreement between coast and interior, and the Gulf rulers drew authority from lineage, merchant financing, and British protection.

Differences in revenue help explain these patterns. Iraq collected land and customs revenue and received oil royalties from 1934; Transjordan depended on subsidy; Saudi Arabia drew on pilgrimage and customs, and then on oil advances; Yemen relied on taxes in kind; and the Gulf sheikhdoms relied on customs and pearl dues, then concession payments. The ability to pay an army and officials, or to buy tribal loyalty, followed from revenue. Social factors also mattered: education created teachers, officers, and clerks in Baghdad, Amman, and Jeddah; urbanization and labor migration reshaped Gulf ports and oil camps; and agrarian structures determined how landlords, cultivators, and nomads related to the state. In all cases tribes and religious leaders were partners and rivals of the state, and violence, whether the bombing of rebel villages, the conquest of the Hijaz, the killings at Simele, or the suppression of the Ikhwan, played a role in consolidation.

The comparison therefore warns against one model of state formation. The mandate states were designed by external powers but were also shaped by internal negotiation; the independent states were products of conquest and religious mobilization but also depended on external recognition and subsidies; and the treaty states were protected rather than governed by Britain. None was a nation-state in the later sense, and none followed the same path.

The military institutions illustrate the range. Iraq built a conscript national army in the 1930s, officered by men trained in Ottoman and Iraqi schools; Transjordan's Arab Legion was British-funded and British-officered but increasingly recruited from local Bedouin and villagers; Saudi Arabia relied on tribal levies and the descendants of Ikhwan-style forces, supplemented by a smaller body of retainers and, after 1929, by motor vehicles and machine guns; Yemen combined a small regular army with tribal contingents; and the Gulf rulers depended on their retainers and British naval power, rather than on armies. These differences shaped each polity's vulnerability to coups, rebellion, and external pressure: officers could intervene in Baghdad in 1936, whereas in Arabia the tribal and religious structures made such an intervention unlikely.

Law and religious authority also differed. In Iraq, codes of Ottoman origin, new legislation, and tribal regulation coexisted; in Saudi Arabia, courts applied Hanbali jurisprudence in the Najdi tradition and, in the Hijaz, accommodated other schools; in Yemen, Zaydi jurisprudence was central; in Oman, Ibadi law structured the interior; and in the Gulf, rulers' councils (majlis), customary law, and British consular courts for foreigners combined. Households were affected unevenly. In rural Iraq, cultivators' debts and tenure rules shaped family life; in the Gulf, pearling debt did; in Najd and the Hijaz, settlement, pilgrimage, and religious regulation mattered; in Yemen, hostage-taking and taxes in kind did. A comparison that overlooked these household-level differences would leave out much of what the states meant to ordinary people.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From Ottoman and Tribal Structures to Modern State Institutions

What changed? Armies, ministries, constitutions, treaties, and (later) oil revenue created more centralized states; frontiers became more defined.

What remained continuous? Tribal leadership, notable families, religious authority, and rural social structures persisted.

Who experienced change most directly? Officials, officers, merchants, and settled Ikhwan, as well as divers and Assyrian and Kurdish communities affected by new borders.

Who experienced greater continuity? Many cultivators and pastoralists, whose daily work changed more slowly.

Why does the pattern matter? It shows that the modern state was built on older foundations rather than replacing them.

Section 57.42

Sovereignty and State Formation in Iraq and Arabia on the Eve of the Second World War

By 1939 three broad routes to statehood can be distinguished, and their differences were consequential. First, Iraq and Transjordan came into existence within the mandate framework. Iraq moved to treaty independence in 1932 with British rights preserved: a constitutional monarchy, growing oil revenue after 1934, and an army whose political role expanded, as the Simele killings and the 1936 coup showed. Transjordan remained an emirate under British supervision, with a Basic Law and Legislative Council from 1928–1929 but with finance, defense, and foreign affairs reserved to Britain; independence came only in 1946. Second, Saudi Arabia and Yemen were products of indigenous leadership. Ibn Saud's kingdom, proclaimed in 1932, was forged by conquest and religious mobilization and then disciplined by the defeat of the Ikhwan; Imam Yahya's Yemen was an imamate with Zaydi and tribal foundations, and its frontier with Saudi Arabia was fixed provisionally at Taif in 1934. Third, Oman, Aden's hinterland, and the Gulf sheikhdoms were governed through treaty relations with Britain that predated the First World War and left internal rule to local dynasties.

These paths were linked by British regional power, by competition among the Hashemite and Saudi dynasties, and by the first oil concessions, but they were not parts of a single plan. The outcome by 1939 was a patchwork of sovereignties whose legitimacy rested on different foundations and whose future was linked to oil, to the coming war, and to the pressures of nationalism examined in later chapters. For students the central lesson is that terms such as mandate, independence, protectorate, and kingdom describe different political realities, and that historians must ask in each case how authority was justified, financed, and enforced.

The horizon of 1939 can be summarized by contrasting what had been settled and what had not. Settled, for the most part, were the dynastic bases of rule: Hashemite monarchs in Baghdad and Amman, the Al Saud in Riyadh, the Hamid al-Din imams in Sanaa, and ruling families in the Gulf. Unsettled were frontiers (the Saudi–Yemeni line, the interior borders of the Gulf, the northern Iraqi border populations), the position of minorities (Kurds, Assyrians, Shi'is, and others), the relationship of armies to politics, and the use of oil revenue. Each unsettled question would shape later events, among them the Iraqi crisis of 1941, treated in a later chapter, and the postwar transformation of the Gulf in Chapter 65.

Historians also differ over how to interpret the period. Older accounts, often drawing on British archives, emphasized imperial design; nationalist accounts emphasized resistance and the long struggle for independence; more recent scholarship has stressed the agency of local elites, the importance of tribal and religious institutions, and the diversity of the paths to statehood. This chapter has tried to hold these interpretations together by relying on a range of documents, treaties, administrative records, chronicles, and later memory, while noting where each has limits.

International recognition added another dimension. Iraq alone among these polities became a member of the League of Nations, in 1932, a status that gave it legal equality but not an end to British rights; Britain recognized the independence of Ibn Saud's realm in 1927, and other powers, including the Soviet Union, which had been the first to recognize Ibn Saud as king of the Hijaz in February 1926, and the United States, which recognized his Kingdom of the Hijaz and Nejd and its Dependencies on 1 May 1931, before the 1932 proclamation of Saudi Arabia, followed in their own ways; Yemen concluded treaties with Italy and Britain; and the Gulf rulers' external affairs remained in British hands. Recognition did not translate directly into capacity, but it shaped bargaining: Ibn Saud's ability to negotiate with American oil companies in 1933, for example, depended on his status as an independent sovereign.

A final comparison concerns what each polity would need for the future. Iraq needed an army answerable to civilian government and a way to share power among its communities; Transjordan needed greater control over finance and defense; Saudi Arabia needed a stable revenue and a bureaucracy capable of governing the Hijaz, Najd, Hasa, and Asir as one state; Yemen needed to resolve the question of succession and its frontiers; and the Gulf rulers needed to manage the transition from pearling to oil. These needs help explain why the period from 1939 to the 1950s, outside this chapter, saw so much change, and they mark the limits of any narrative that treats the interwar settlements as final.

Chapter Summary

Between 1918 and 1939 the Arab East acquired a new political order that was built through several different processes rather than a single settlement. The mandate framework of Chapter 56 shaped Iraq and Transjordan, but it did not create Saudi Arabia or Yemen, and it did not govern Oman or the Gulf sheikhdoms, which were bound to Britain by older treaties.

In Iraq, British occupation and the mandate provoked the revolt of 1920, a coalition of nationalists, Shi'i clerics, and tribal leaders that Britain suppressed with heavy force. The revolt persuaded Britain that direct rule was too costly. At the Cairo Conference of 1921 it chose Faisal, who was proclaimed king after a managed endorsement. Iraqi politicians such as Nuri al-Sa'id and Ja'far al-Askari, and the officers and officials around Faisal, built the institutions of the state.

Constitutional development came through the treaty of 1922 and the Organic Law of 1925, but these coexisted with British control. Iraq's society presented obstacles: tribal shaykhs and landowners held rural power, Shi'i and Sunni elites competed for representation, Kurds pursued autonomy, and the Mosul dispute was resolved in 1926 to Iraq's advantage but not to the satisfaction of all communities. The discovery of oil in 1927 raised hopes of financial independence.

The treaty of 1930 and League membership in 1932 gave Iraq formal independence while preserving British military rights. The Simele killings of 1933, the death of Faisal, tribal revolts, and the 1936 coup revealed how fragile Iraqi state-building was and how powerful the army had become. Historians disagree on how substantive independence was, and the evidence supports both views in part.

In Transjordan, Abdullah's arrival in 1920 and the Jerusalem meeting of 1921 produced an emirate under the mandate, recognized in 1923. The Arab Legion, tribal bargaining, the treaty and Basic Law of 1928, and the 1929 elections created institutions, but British financial and military control limited autonomy.

In Arabia, Ibn Saud restored Saudi power, defeated the Rashidis, and used the Ikhwan to conquer the Hijaz in 1924–1925. He gained British recognition in 1927, crushed the Ikhwan rebellion at Sabilla in 1929, and proclaimed the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia in 1932. The war with Yemen in 1934 and the Treaty of Taif defined a frontier zone in the southwest. The concession of 1933 and the oil discovery of 1938 opened a new economic era whose effects lie beyond this chapter.

Yemen, under Imam Yahya, developed as a Zaydi imamate based on religious legitimacy and tribal bargaining, while Aden and its protectorates were governed by Britain. Oman remained divided between the sultanate and the interior imamate under the Treaty of Seeb.

The Gulf sheikhdoms of Kuwait, Bahrain, Qatar, and the Trucial coast were governed by ruling dynasties under British treaty protection. Their economies rested on pearling, which collapsed under Japanese competition and the Depression, leaving rulers more dependent on oil concessions and British oversight. Early oil in Bahrain (1932) and the concessions of the 1930s began a transformation developed in Chapter 65.

Social history complicates the political narrative. Rural cultivators in Iraq lived under shaykhly landlords and new taxes; Assyrians and Kurds faced the limits of minority protection; Transjordan's Bedouin, villagers, and Circassians were drawn into a state whose revenues were small; Najdi nomads were settled in Ikhwan villages and then disciplined; Gulf divers and their families bore the costs of a collapsing industry; and Hijazis and Yemenis experienced rulers with very different habits of government. Women appear in the sources mainly through household and agricultural roles, and historians must reconstruct their experience indirectly.

The wider world was always present. Britain's imperial interests, expressed through the India Office, the Colonial Office, the Royal Air Force, the Residency, and the oil companies, shaped frontiers, treaties, and finances. But Britain did not control outcomes: Iraqi clerics and tribes forced a change of policy, Ibn Saud outmaneuvered British preferences in the Hijaz, and Gulf rulers used the treaty system for their own ends. Trade and migration linked Iraq, the Gulf, Yemen, and the Hijaz to India, East Africa, Persia, and the Mediterranean.

The chapter also demonstrates how historians handle contested evidence. Casualty figures for the 1920 revolt and for Simele are estimates, treaty texts do not reveal how they were applied, and chronicles reflect dynastic viewpoints. Careful historians separate documented events from later memory and nationalist narratives, and they note where the sources are one-sided.

The Arabian polities developed institutions suited to their circumstances. Saudi government joined a dynastic household, religious scholars, governors drawn from the family, and an uneven fiscal base; in the Hijaz it kept advisory and municipal bodies that reflected the region's urban traditions. Yemen's imamate rested on Zaydi sayyid families, tribal hostages, and taxation in kind, and its isolation was both a defense and a limitation. The Saudi–Yemeni war of 1934 showed how force and restraint combined in the making of a frontier zone that the Treaty of Taif defined but did not finally settle.

In Oman and the Gulf, treaty relations with Britain, merchant finance, and the pearl economy defined political life. The Treaty of Seeb gave Oman a divided polity; Kuwait, Bahrain, Qatar, and the Trucial sheikhdoms each combined dynastic authority with British protection, and the archive of the 1935 Qatar concession shows how commercial rights were bound to British political control. The collapse of pearling, with its chains of debt, drove social change before oil revenue arrived, and early oil in Bahrain, Saudi Arabia, and Kuwait signaled a transition that Chapter 65 develops.

Across the region, the sources of legitimacy varied: constitutions and treaties, religion, dynastic descent, military success, and external protection. Tribal and religious leaders were partners as well as rivals of the state, and British power shaped every outcome without determining it. These developments set the stage for Chapter 58 on Syria and Lebanon and for the later political history of the Arab states.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows that the modern Arab East was not shaped by one blueprint. Revolt in Iraq, dynastic bargaining in Transjordan, conquest in Arabia, imamate in Yemen, and treaty protection in the Gulf each produced different institutions, and these differences continue to matter in the later political histories of the region.

It also shows that outside powers and local actors shaped one another. Britain mattered everywhere, but Iraqi clerics and tribes, Ikhwan fighters, Zaydi scholars, Gulf merchants, and Bedouin shaykhs set limits on what Britain could do.

The comparison among these states is itself a reflection on historical method. It would be tempting to tell the story as the triumph of one model, whether the constitutional monarchy of Iraq, the Saudi kingdom, or the sheikhdoms that survived into the oil age. Seen from the 1930s, however, none of these outcomes was assured. Iraq's constitutional order was fragile, Saudi unity depended on a king who had just defeated his own army of settlers, Yemen's isolation proved double-edged, and the Gulf's treaty system faced the shock of the pearl collapse. Understanding how people made choices under such uncertainty is part of what it means to study this period.

The chapter also bears on how later political memory is formed. Iraqi nationalists remembered 1920 as a founding revolt, Assyrian communities remember Simele as a catastrophe, Saudis remember the unification of 1932 as a founding moment, and Omanis, Yemenis, and Gulf peoples debate the meaning of the treaties their rulers signed. Each memory is tied to evidence but also to later political needs, and students are encouraged to read them critically.

The chapter also shows that political outcomes depended on how violence was organized and constrained. In Iraq, the air campaigns of 1920 and the army's role at Simele and in the tribal revolts of the 1930s showed how a young state used force. In Arabia, the Ikhwan were instruments of expansion and then targets of consolidation. In Transjordan, tribal negotiation and a British-officered force made order possible. In the Gulf, naval protection reduced the need for rulers to maintain armies. In each case the monopoly of force was a goal rather than a starting point.

Finally, the chapter points forward. The oil concessions of the 1930s begin a story told in Chapter 65, the army's entrance into Iraqi politics anticipates later coups, and the unresolved questions of Kurdish and Assyrian status, Saudi–Yemeni frontiers, and Omani authority recur in later chapters. Chapter 58 turns next to Syria and Lebanon under the French mandate.
