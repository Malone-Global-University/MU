North Africa: Amazigh Peoples, Carthage, and Rome

A History of the Arab World: From Ancient Crossroads to the Modern Arab World

Chapter Opening

Long before a Phoenician ship ever beached on the coast near a low hill later called Byrsa, the lands stretching from Cyrenaica in the east to the Atlantic shore of the Maghreb in the west were already home to farming villages, pastoral communities, oasis towns, and networks of exchange that connected the Mediterranean coast to the Sahara and the Sahara to lands farther south. This chapter tells the history of ancient North Africa as a history belonging first to the peoples who inhabited it, and only then as a history of the outsiders who arrived from beyond the region — Phoenician settlers and, centuries later, Roman generals and administrators — who settled, competed, conquered, and were themselves transformed by the region they entered. The Carthaginians who emerged from that Phoenician settlement, by contrast, became over the centuries a North African society in their own right, not a perpetually foreign presence upon African soil.

Consider a single stretch of coastline in what is today northern Tunisia during the eighth century BCE. Indigenous Libyan farming and pastoral communities had long worked the surrounding hills and plains, moving flocks between coastal pasture and inland grazing according to season, trading grain, wool, and livestock with neighboring communities, and maintaining their own forms of political organization under local chiefs and lineage leaders. Into this landscape came Phoenician sailors and settlers from the eastern Mediterranean, seeking not empty land but a defensible harbor, fresh water, and workable relationships with the people who already lived there. The settlement that grew from this encounter — later known to the Greeks and Romans as Carthage — would in time become one of the most powerful cities of the ancient Mediterranean world. But its power was never simply transplanted from Phoenicia. It grew from centuries of interaction with the North African environment and the North African peoples among whom Carthage's Phoenician founders had settled.

This chapter resists a narrative pattern that has shaped a great deal of older historical writing about ancient North Africa: the assumption that the region's history begins with the arrival of literate Mediterranean settlers, that it is then organized around the rise and fall of Carthage, and that it concludes with Roman conquest bringing "civilization" to a supposedly passive African landscape. That pattern erases indigenous North African societies as historical actors, reduces the vast Saharan interior to empty desert, and treats North Africa's Mediterranean coast as though it belonged more naturally to Europe than to Africa. This chapter argues instead that ancient North Africa possessed multiple centers of political and economic life — indigenous Libyan and Numidian communities, Saharan oasis societies such as the Garamantes, Greek Cyrenaica, Punic Carthage, and eventually Roman provincial Africa — that interacted with one another across many centuries, none of them simply a backdrop for the others.

The chapter follows a long arc: from the diverse indigenous societies of the region in the first millennium BCE, through Phoenician coastal settlement and the emergence of Carthage as a major Mediterranean power, through the long and consequential rivalry between Carthage and Rome, through the parallel rise of the Numidian kingdom under rulers such as Massinissa, to the destruction of Punic Carthage in 146 BCE and the gradual, uneven incorporation of North Africa into the Roman provincial world over the following centuries. Along the way, it gives sustained attention to populations that many older accounts mention only in passing — Libyan farmers and herders, Numidian political elites, Saharan Garamantian oasis-dwellers, and the ordinary craftspeople, farmers, and dependent laborers of Carthage itself.

It is essential to state clearly, as this textbook does throughout Part II, that none of the societies examined in this chapter should be described as Arab. The Arabization of North Africa lies many centuries in the future, beginning with the seventh-century conquests examined in Part IV of this textbook. The peoples discussed here — Libyans, Numidians, Mauri, Garamantes, Phoenician and Punic settlers, Greeks, and Romans — inhabited lands that would only later become part of the Arab world. Indigenous North African communities did, however, leave a lasting inheritance: the modern Amazigh peoples of Morocco, Algeria, Tunisia, Libya, and the wider Sahara trace deep linguistic and cultural roots to the ancient populations this chapter examines, and Amazigh identity and language remain a vital, continuing presence across the Maghreb today.

Historians reconstruct this history from an unusually uneven body of evidence. Much of the surviving literary testimony about Carthage was written by its enemies — Greek and Roman authors who had reason to portray Carthaginian society in an unflattering light, and whose works survive in far greater quantity than anything written by Carthaginians themselves, since the Carthaginian archives were largely destroyed along with the city in 146 BCE. Indigenous North African voices survive even less directly, mostly through archaeology, inscriptions in Punic and Libyan scripts, coinage, and the occasional favorable or unfavorable mention in outside sources. This chapter treats these limitations seriously, asking students to distinguish between what the evidence can support and what later legend, hostile propaganda, or simple absence of contrary testimony has left unchallenged for too long.

By the chapter's end, students should understand ancient North Africa not as a stage on which Mediterranean powers acted out their rivalries, but as a region whose indigenous societies, Saharan networks, and evolving Punic and Numidian states shaped their own histories — histories that Roman conquest transformed but did not erase, and that remain visible in the Amazigh-speaking communities, place names, and archaeological landscapes of the modern Maghreb.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why ancient North African history should not be understood as beginning with Phoenician settlement, and identify the indigenous societies present before and alongside Carthage.
  2. Distinguish among ancient labels such as Libyan, Numidian, Mauri, and Garamantian, and explain their relationship to the modern term Amazigh.
  3. Describe the founding and early development of Carthage as part of a wider pattern of Phoenician settlement in the western Mediterranean.
  4. Analyze the political, economic, social, and religious institutions of Carthage, including its government, agriculture, commerce, and religious practice.
  5. Explain the relationship between Carthage and its North African hinterland, including indigenous farming and pastoral communities.
  6. Trace the causes, courses, and consequences of the three Punic Wars, situating Hannibal's campaigns within a broader Mediterranean and North African context.
  7. Assess the independent political significance of the Numidian kingdom under Massinissa and his successors.
  8. Evaluate the evidence and competing interpretations surrounding the Carthaginian tophet and practices associated with it.
  9. Explain how Roman conquest of North Africa proceeded incrementally rather than as a single event, from 146 BCE through the annexation of Mauretania.
  10. Describe continuity and change in North African society, language, and religion under Roman provincial rule.
  11. Identify the Garamantes and other Saharan societies as active historical participants rather than peripheral desert populations.
  12. Contextualize ancient North African history within the textbook's broader argument that Arabization and Islamization are later, separate historical processes.

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  2. In what ways did Carthage remain connected to its Levantine Phoenician origins, and in what ways did it develop a distinctively North African and western Mediterranean character?
  3. Why should Carthage's relationship with its African hinterland be considered as important as its Mediterranean trade?
  4. What made the Numidian kingdom under Massinissa a significant independent state rather than merely an ally of Rome or a former subject of Carthage?

Historical Context

Chapter 10 examined the Levantine societies of Canaan, Phoenicia, Aram, and ancient Israel, including the maritime city-states of Tyre, Sidon, and Byblos from which Phoenician settlers eventually sailed westward across the Mediterranean. This chapter picks up that Phoenician westward movement but immediately redirects attention to the North African setting into which it arrived — a setting that, this chapter insists, was not an empty stage awaiting outside settlement.

By the early first millennium BCE, the lands stretching from Cyrenaica through the Tell of modern Tunisia and Algeria to the Atlantic coast of Morocco supported a wide range of indigenous societies: settled farming communities in well-watered coastal and highland zones, pastoral communities moving seasonally between coastal and interior pasture, and oasis-based societies deep in the Sahara whose agricultural and commercial life depended on managed groundwater rather than rainfall, a pattern of adaptation already introduced in Chapter 3. These communities possessed their own languages — ancestral to the modern Amazigh (Berber) language family — their own political structures organized around chiefs, lineages, and, over time, more centralized kingship, and their own networks of exchange connecting the Mediterranean coast to the Sahara and, through Saharan intermediaries, to societies farther south.

It is important to distinguish this chapter's subject from Chapter 9's treatment of Egypt and Nubia along the Nile. Egypt possessed a concentrated, single-river environment that supported early state formation along a narrow corridor; North Africa west of Egypt possessed a more varied environment of Mediterranean coastal plains, mountain ranges, semi-arid steppe, and true desert, producing a correspondingly more varied pattern of settlement, without a single dominant river to concentrate population and political power in one narrow zone. This chapter also anticipates Chapter 32, which will examine the medieval Maghreb and the Amazigh dynasties, including the Almoravids and Almohads, that arose many centuries later; the ancient societies examined here are the deep historical background for that later story, not a direct political continuation of it.

Section 11.1

North Africa between the Mediterranean and the Sahara

Ancient North Africa west of Egypt stretched across an unusually varied physical environment: a narrow, fertile Mediterranean coastal plain running from Cyrenaica through Tripolitania and the Tunisian Tell into Numidian and Mauri territory; the Atlas mountain ranges rising inland across modern Algeria and Morocco; a broad semi-arid steppe zone between the coastal mountains and the true desert; and, beyond it, the vast expanse of the Sahara itself, interrupted by scattered but historically significant oasis chains. No single river dominated this landscape the way the Nile dominated Egypt or the Tigris and Euphrates dominated Mesopotamia. Instead, agricultural and pastoral life depended on a patchwork of rainfall zones, seasonal wadis, mountain springs, and, in the desert interior, managed groundwater — a pattern of adaptation consistent with the broader environmental strategies introduced in Chapter 3.

It is tempting, especially from a modern vantage point that associates the Mediterranean coast with Europe, to imagine that ancient North Africa's coastal societies "faced" northward, toward Sicily, Sardinia, and the Italian peninsula, while turning their backs on the African interior. This chapter rejects that framing. The Mediterranean and the Sahara were both connectors and constraints — the sea allowed rapid, high-volume exchange along the coast and across to Sicily, Sardinia, and Iberia, while the desert, far from forming an impermeable wall, supported its own long-distance networks of trade and communication linking the Mediterranean coast to societies deep in the Sahara and, through Saharan intermediaries, to regions farther south in Africa. Coastal North African societies participated in both networks simultaneously, and this chapter's treatment of Carthage, Numidia, and the Garamantes should be read with that dual orientation constantly in view.

The region's climate varied correspondingly. Mediterranean-facing coastal zones, particularly in northern Tunisia, northern Algeria, and northern Morocco, received enough winter rainfall to support unirrigated grain agriculture, olive cultivation, and viticulture — resources that would prove central to Carthaginian and later Roman North African wealth. Interior steppe zones supported more pastoral and mixed subsistence patterns, while the Sahara itself demanded the specialized oasis technologies examined in Chapter 3 and revisited later in this chapter in connection with the Garamantes.

Section 11.2

Indigenous Peoples and the Problem of Names

Before turning to Phoenician settlement, this chapter must address a terminological problem that has distorted a great deal of older historical writing about North Africa: the tendency to treat "Libyan," "Numidian," "Mauri," and "Berber" as interchangeable labels for a single, unchanging ancient population. They are not. Each term carries a distinct history and a distinct relationship to the peoples it describes.

Libyan was, above all, a label used by outside observers — first Egyptian, later Greek and Roman — for indigenous populations living west of Egypt. It functioned as a broad geographic and ethnographic category rather than a term of self-identification shared uniformly by every community it covered. Ancient Libyan-speaking populations were themselves diverse, organized into numerous distinct groups with their own leadership, territory, and local identity, much as "Arab" would later come to cover an internally diverse range of societies discussed elsewhere in this textbook.

Numidian refers more specifically to indigenous populations of the region corresponding roughly to modern eastern Algeria and western Tunisia, who developed, over the centuries examined in this chapter, an increasingly centralized and territorially defined kingdom under rulers such as Massinissa. Mauri refers to populations farther west, in modern Morocco and western Algeria, later associated with the kingdom and Roman province of Mauretania. Gaetuli was a broader ancient designation, used by Greek and Roman writers, for populations of the interior steppe and Saharan margins to the south of Numidia and Mauretania; it should not be treated as simply another name for Numidians or Mauri, since ancient authors generally used it to distinguish more mobile, pastoral, interior populations from the more settled kingdoms of the Mediterranean-facing coast. Garamantian refers to the distinct Saharan society of the Fazzan region, examined in Section 11.3, whose material culture and political organization differed considerably from Mediterranean-facing Numidian and Mauri society.

The modern term Amazigh (plural Imazighen), meaning roughly "free people" or "noble people" in Tamazight languages, is the term increasingly preferred by North African communities themselves for the indigenous peoples of the Maghreb and their modern descendants, and for the language family — often called Tamazight collectively — that these communities speak. Amazigh identity and language provide a genuine and important continuity with the ancient populations examined in this chapter. At the same time, historians should not mechanically project the modern collective Amazigh identity backward onto every ancient Libyan, Numidian, or Mauri community as though they had all shared a single unified self-identification in antiquity; ancient political identity was typically organized around more local kingdoms, lineages, and communities rather than a single pan-regional ethnic consciousness. The older European-language term Berber, derived ultimately from the Greek and Latin word for "foreigner" or "barbarian," remains common in some older scholarship but carries an outsider's, often pejorative, origin; this textbook generally prefers Amazigh or "indigenous North African peoples," using "Berber" only when discussing the historiography of the term itself.

Section 11.3

Cyrenaica, the Sahara, and Other North African Worlds

Before Carthage dominates the remainder of this chapter, it is important to establish that ancient North Africa possessed multiple centers of political and economic life, not one. Two examples illustrate this clearly: Greek Cyrenaica in the east, and the Garamantes of the central Saharan interior.

In eastern Libya, Greek settlers traditionally founded the city of Cyrene around 631 BCE, beginning a distinct zone of outside settlement quite separate from Phoenician and later Punic North Africa. Cyrene and its surrounding communities, collectively known as Cyrenaica, developed their own relationship with neighboring indigenous Libyan populations — a relationship involving both conflict and accommodation, intermarriage among elites, and the gradual blending of Greek and Libyan cultural elements. Cyrenaica's history followed its own trajectory, entangled with the broader Greek world, and should not be collapsed into the Carthaginian story that dominates the rest of this chapter.

Farther south and west, in the Fazzan region of what is now southern Libya, the Garamantes developed one of the ancient Sahara's most significant indigenous societies. Rather than the simple image of nomadic "desert wanderers" found in some hostile Greek and Roman accounts, archaeological research has revealed a society built around extensive oasis agriculture sustained by underground foggara channels — gravity-fed irrigation systems related in principle to the qanat and falaj technologies discussed in Chapter 3 — supporting fortified settlements, a capital at Garama, and a political structure capable of organizing labor, defense, and long-distance trade across the Sahara. Garamantian society engaged in exchange with Mediterranean coastal societies, including Carthage and, more securely documented, later Rome; ancient and archaeological evidence points to trade in commodities such as ivory and enslaved captives, while claims of an extensive Carthaginian-era trans-Saharan gold trade rest on thinner and more disputed evidence and should be treated with caution. Garamantian networks also extended southward into the wider Sahara and, very likely, toward the Sahel.

Establishing Cyrenaica and the Garamantes early in this chapter matters for a specific reason: it prevents North African history from collapsing into a single narrative in which Carthage represents the whole region. Carthage was the most powerful Mediterranean-facing North African state for several centuries, and it will accordingly receive the largest share of this chapter's attention, but it was never the only North African society worth studying, and its power depended on relationships with neighboring indigenous societies rather than existing in isolation from them.

Section 11.4

Phoenician Settlement and the Founding of Carthage

As Chapter 10 explained, the Phoenician city-states of the Levantine coast — Tyre, Sidon, and Byblos foremost among them — developed extensive maritime trading networks across the Mediterranean beginning in the early first millennium BCE, driven by demand for metals, agricultural products, raw materials, and access to western trading networks not as readily available closer to the Levant, and facilitated by Phoenician skill in shipbuilding, navigation, and long-distance commerce. Phoenician sailors and settlers established coastal trading posts and settlements across Cyprus, Sicily, Sardinia, Iberia, and the North African coast, seeking defensible harbors, fresh water, and access to inland trade and resources rather than large tracts of unoccupied farmland.

Carthage, on a defensible peninsula near modern Tunis, was one such settlement. Later Greek and Roman literary tradition attributed its founding to the legendary Phoenician princess Dido, also known as Elissa, who was said to have fled political conflict in Tyre and negotiated with local inhabitants for land on which to build her new city — according to legend, only as much land as could be covered by an ox-hide cut into thin strips. This story, most fully developed centuries later in Roman literature, functions as a foundation legend explaining Carthage's origins in culturally meaningful terms rather than as a reliable historical account of the city's actual founding circumstances, and it should be presented to students as such.

The traditional foundation date for Carthage, 814 or 813 BCE, likewise derives from later ancient chronographic tradition rather than a contemporary inscription or record. Archaeological evidence from the earliest excavated layers at Carthage, including radiocarbon dating of early settlement material, broadly supports Phoenician occupation of the site beginning in the second half of the ninth century BCE, lending the traditional date a degree of credibility while not confirming it as an exact, verified historical fact. Students should understand this as a case in which archaeological evidence and inherited literary tradition point in a broadly compatible direction without archaeology providing precise confirmation of every detail the tradition reports.

Critically, the founding of Carthage did not mean the disappearance or displacement of the indigenous Libyan population already present in the region. Phoenician settlers arrived seeking a harbor and a base for trade, not a large agricultural territory, and their initial settlement necessarily depended on negotiated or at least tolerated relationships with the surrounding Libyan communities who controlled the agricultural and pastoral resources of the immediate hinterland. This relationship — sometimes cooperative, sometimes contentious, and changing considerably over the centuries that followed — forms a recurring theme throughout the rest of this chapter.

Section 11.5

From Phoenician Settlement to Punic Power

Carthage was not the only, nor initially the most important, Phoenician settlement in the western Mediterranean; other Phoenician-founded communities existed in Sicily, Sardinia, and Iberia, some predating Carthage itself. Over the sixth and fifth centuries BCE, Carthage grew into the dominant western Phoenician power through a combination of factors: its defensible and productive location, its inheritance of established western Phoenician trading networks, its access to agricultural resources in its North African hinterland, and its own developing political institutions. Increasing pressure on the eastern Phoenician homeland from Assyrian, Babylonian, and later Persian imperial expansion, examined in Chapter 8, may have been one contributing factor encouraging a shift of commercial initiative westward, though historians increasingly caution against treating this eastern pressure as the primary or sole engine of Carthaginian ascendancy; much of Carthage's growth likely developed organically from long-established western Mediterranean commerce rather than as a sudden response to a single set of distant political events.

Historians and archaeologists increasingly describe the distinct culture that developed at Carthage and among its affiliated western Mediterranean communities as Punic — a term derived from the Latin word for Phoenician, used to describe a culture that retained clear Levantine Phoenician roots in language, religion, and material culture while developing its own distinctive regional character over centuries of settlement in a North African and western Mediterranean environment. Punic should not be understood as marking a separate "race" distinct from Phoenician, but rather as identifying the specific historical trajectory of western Phoenician-derived communities, centered on Carthage, as they developed political institutions, artistic styles, and social structures shaped by their North African setting and their engagement with neighboring Numidian, Greek, Etruscan, and other Mediterranean societies.

It is equally important not to attribute every western Phoenician settlement's founding to Carthage itself. Many western Phoenician communities were established independently, sometimes before Carthage rose to prominence, and Carthage's later hegemony over parts of this network developed through a combination of trade dominance, alliance, and, in some cases, more direct political and military control exercised over subsequent centuries rather than through original colonial foundation.

Section 11.6

Government, Society, Economy, and Religion at Carthage

By the fifth and fourth centuries BCE, Carthage had developed into a large, wealthy, and institutionally sophisticated city whose government, ancient observers noted, combined elements that a Greek political theorist might have called monarchic, aristocratic, and popular. Executive authority rested chiefly with two annually elected officials known as suffetes, whose title derives from a Semitic root related to the Hebrew word for "judge," discussed further in the Historical Vocabulary feature later in this chapter. The suffetes presided over civic and religious functions and worked alongside a powerful council of elders, generally understood to have been drawn from Carthage's wealthiest and most established families, together with a broader citizen assembly whose precise powers are not fully documented but which appears to have had a role, at least under some circumstances, in ratifying major decisions.

Carthaginian society was not simply a merchant oligarchy, though maritime trade was certainly central to its wealth. Carthage's economy rested on at least three interlocking foundations: extensive maritime commerce connecting the eastern and western Mediterranean; substantial agricultural production from Carthage's own North African hinterland, including grain, olives, and livestock; and craft production — textiles, ceramics, metalwork, and purple dye derived from murex shellfish, an industry inherited from Phoenician tradition — that supplied both local consumption and export trade. Carthaginian agricultural writing, most famously the treatise attributed to the Carthaginian writer Mago, was influential enough that the Roman Senate later ordered it translated into Latin following the destruction of Carthage, a detail that itself demonstrates that Roman observers respected Carthaginian agricultural expertise even as they destroyed the city that had produced it.

Carthaginian society included citizens of Punic descent, resident populations of mixed Phoenician and indigenous North African ancestry, indigenous Libyan communities within Carthaginian-controlled territory, and enslaved and dependent laborers whose labor sustained much of the city's agricultural and craft production. Carthaginian military forces, examined further in later sections of this chapter, similarly drew on a wide range of populations — citizen soldiers, subject Libyan levies, Numidian cavalry allies, Iberian and Gallic hired soldiers, and others — depending on period and campaign; describing the Carthaginian army simply as "mercenaries" understates the significant role played by citizen and subject troops and by long-term Numidian alliance.

Religious life at Carthage centered on deities inherited from the Phoenician homeland, chief among them Baal Hammon, a major Punic deity frequently invoked as a father figure and closely associated with the tophet sanctuaries discussed later in this chapter, though his precise divine functions remain difficult to reconstruct and are debated among specialists — the traditional description of Baal Hammon as chiefly a fertility god rests partly on a later identification with the Roman god Saturn rather than on secure Punic-era evidence — and Tanit, a goddess who rose to particular prominence in Carthaginian religious life from around the fifth century BCE onward, becoming closely associated with the city's civic identity. Family and household religious practice, evidenced by votive offerings, domestic shrines, and inscribed stelae, formed an important complement to major civic and temple worship, a pattern examined further in the Everyday Life feature later in this chapter.

Section 11.7

Carthage and Its African Hinterland

No account of Carthage is complete without sustained attention to its relationship with the surrounding North African hinterland — a relationship too often reduced, in older scholarship, to a brief mention before moving on to Carthaginian maritime trade and warfare. Carthage was, geographically and economically, an African city. Its agricultural wealth depended directly on productive land in its North African hinterland, land that had been inhabited and worked by indigenous Libyan communities long before Phoenician settlement and that remained home to those communities, in varying legal and political relationships to Carthaginian authority, throughout the city's history.

As Carthaginian power expanded from the sixth century BCE onward, the city's relationship with neighboring Libyan populations grew increasingly structured and, in many periods, increasingly extractive. Carthaginian authorities imposed taxation and rents on Libyan agricultural communities within their sphere of control, recruited Libyan soldiers for Carthaginian military campaigns, and negotiated or imposed political relationships with indigenous chiefs and communities across the North African interior. Ancient sources indicate that these relationships could be a significant source of local resentment; a large-scale Libyan revolt against Carthaginian authority, driven partly by heavy taxation demands during a period of military strain, occurred in the later third century BCE, following the First Punic War, illustrating that Carthaginian dominance over its own African hinterland was neither simple nor uncontested.

At the same time, this relationship should not be reduced to a simple colonizer-versus-native binary applied uniformly across five centuries. Carthaginian and Libyan elites intermarried and interacted at various levels of society; Libyan and, later, Numidian political and military elites participated actively in Carthaginian affairs, sometimes as allies and sometimes as rivals; and Punic cultural and religious influence spread inland among some indigenous communities through trade, intermarriage, and political interaction, just as indigenous North African cultural and political forms influenced Carthage in turn. This chapter's later sections on the emerging Numidian kingdom continue this theme directly, showing how the political relationship between Carthage and indigenous North African polities shifted decisively during the era of the Punic Wars.

Section 11.8

Carthage and the Western Mediterranean

Beyond its African hinterland, Carthage built an extensive western Mediterranean network encompassing western Sicily, Sardinia, the Balearic Islands, southern Iberia, and parts of southern Gaul, securing access to metals, agricultural resources, and strategic maritime routes. Carthaginian material culture from this period reflects a genuinely cosmopolitan Mediterranean environment, incorporating artistic and craft influences from Phoenician, Egyptian, Greek, Etruscan, and North African traditions alongside distinctly Punic religious and civic forms — evidence, as major museum collections describing Carthaginian material culture have emphasized, of a major commercial and artistic center engaged with the entire Mediterranean world rather than an isolated colonial outpost.

Carthaginian interests in Sicily brought the city into recurring competition, and at times open warfare, with Sicily's Greek city-states, most notably Syracuse, across the fifth and fourth centuries BCE. These conflicts should not be understood as a predetermined, civilizational struggle between "East" and "West," a framing later Greek and Roman authors sometimes encouraged but that oversimplifies a more complicated pattern of shifting alliances, commercial competition, and periodic negotiated settlements between Carthaginian and Sicilian Greek powers.

By the third century BCE, Carthaginian interests extended significantly into Iberia, where members of the prominent Barcid family — including Hamilcar Barca and, later, his son Hannibal — built an expanding Carthaginian territorial and military presence following Carthage's losses in the First Punic War, described in the next section. Iberian silver, agricultural resources, and manpower became increasingly important to Carthaginian strength during this period, and Barcid ambitions in Iberia set the stage directly for the conflict examined in Section 11.10.

Section 11.9

Rome, Sicily, and the First Punic War

By the mid-third century BCE, Rome had completed its conquest of the Italian peninsula and had begun to develop interests in Sicily, an island long divided among Carthaginian, Greek, and various local powers. Earlier Roman-Carthaginian relations had been regulated, at various points, by treaties respecting each power's spheres of interest; the outbreak of war in 264 BCE should not be presented as an inevitable, long-predetermined clash between two rising powers, but rather as the outcome of a specific crisis at the Sicilian city of Messana, where a local political faction's appeal for outside support drew both Carthage and Rome into direct confrontation.

The resulting First Punic War, fought chiefly across Sicily and the surrounding waters from 264 to 241 BCE, tested both powers severely. Rome, possessing some earlier naval experience but lacking anything comparable to Carthage's established Mediterranean naval power, built substantial fleets over the course of the war and gradually developed the naval capacity to challenge Carthaginian dominance at sea — a development that occurred through sustained effort and heavy losses over many years rather than, as a popular simplification sometimes suggests, through Rome simply copying the design of a single captured Carthaginian vessel and instantly matching Carthaginian naval skill. The war ultimately exhausted both sides; Carthage's defeat resulted in the loss of Sicily to Rome, a substantial war indemnity, and, in the unstable aftermath, Rome's opportunistic seizure of Sardinia and Corsica as well, straining Carthaginian resources and contributing to the internal tensions, including the major Libyan and mercenary revolt mentioned in Section 11.7, that followed the war's end.

Section 11.10

Hannibal, Numidia, and the Second Punic War

Following the First Punic War, Carthaginian power increasingly reoriented toward Iberia under Barcid leadership, rebuilding Carthaginian strength through control of Iberian resources and manpower. Tensions over the Iberian city of Saguntum, allied with Rome but within the sphere Carthage considered its own, precipitated the outbreak of the Second Punic War in 218 BCE, when Hannibal, having succeeded his father and brother-in-law in Iberian command, led a Carthaginian army, including a substantial contingent of war elephants, on a famous overland march across the Alps into Italy.

Hannibal's early Italian campaigns produced a series of major victories over Roman forces, most notably at the Trebia River, Lake Trasimene, and, most devastatingly, at Cannae in 216 BCE, where a large Roman army suffered catastrophic losses. Despite these victories, Hannibal was ultimately unable to break the resilience of Rome's Italian alliance system or to capture Rome itself, and the war's center of gravity gradually shifted to Iberia and, eventually, to North Africa, as Roman forces under Publius Cornelius Scipio campaigned successfully against Carthaginian positions in Iberia and then carried the war to Carthaginian territory in Africa.

This chapter places particular emphasis on a dimension of the Second Punic War too often reduced to a footnote in accounts centered on Hannibal: the independent political significance of Numidian rulers during this conflict. Two rival Numidian kings, Massinissa and Syphax, pursued their own strategic calculations throughout the war, shifting alliance between Carthage and Rome according to their own assessments of Numidian political advantage rather than functioning simply as auxiliaries serving either Mediterranean power's interests. Massinissa's eventual alliance with Rome, and his powerful cavalry's decisive contribution at the Battle of Zama in 202 BCE, where Roman and Numidian forces defeated Hannibal's army, should be understood as a calculated element of Massinissa's own state-building strategy, examined fully in the following section, rather than as an act of simple loyalty to Rome.

The war's conclusion in 201 BCE imposed severe terms on Carthage: loss of its remaining overseas territories including Iberia, a massive war indemnity payable over decades, restrictions on Carthaginian military capacity, and formal recognition of Massinissa's expanding Numidian kingdom — terms that decisively ended Carthage's status as a major Mediterranean military power while leaving it, for the moment, as a wealthy but increasingly vulnerable commercial state.

Section 11.11

Massinissa and the Kingdom of Numidia

The decades following the Second Punic War saw the Numidian kingdom under Massinissa develop into one of ancient North Africa's most significant states in its own right, and this chapter treats that development as a subject of independent historical importance rather than as a mere footnote to Roman or Carthaginian history. Massinissa, having secured Roman recognition of an expanded Numidian territory at the war's end, spent the following decades consolidating royal authority, developing Numidia's capital at Cirta into a substantial urban center, promoting settled agriculture alongside Numidia's traditional pastoral economy, and building a court culture that drew on Punic administrative and cultural models as well as the wider Hellenistic Mediterranean world, evidenced by Numidian royal coinage, architecture, and inscriptions that combined Punic script and artistic convention with distinctly Numidian royal iconography.

Massinissa used his favorable position with Rome to press repeated territorial claims against a weakened Carthage throughout the following decades, provoking recurring disputes that Carthage, bound by treaty restrictions on independent military action, could not effectively resist without risking Roman intervention — a dynamic that contributed directly to the crisis examined in the next section. Massinissa's long reign, extending until his death in 148 BCE at an advanced age, established Numidia as an enduring regional power whose successors continued to govern a substantial, increasingly urbanized North African kingdom for more than a century afterward.

Understanding Massinissa as an independent state-builder rather than simply "Rome's loyal ally" matters for the chapter's broader argument. Numidia's alliance with Rome served clear Numidian interests — territorial expansion at Carthaginian expense and international recognition of Massinissa's royal authority — and Numidian foreign policy under Massinissa and his successors continued to be shaped by calculations of Numidian advantage, not simple deference to Roman wishes, a pattern that becomes especially clear in the Jugurthine War examined in Section 11.13.

Section 11.12

The Third Punic War and the Destruction of Carthage

Carthage's recovery of commercial prosperity in the decades after the Second Punic War, combined with Massinissa's repeated territorial encroachments and a segment of Roman political opinion increasingly convinced that a prosperous Carthage remained a long-term danger to Roman security, produced the conditions for a final conflict. When Carthage, provoked beyond the limits its treaty obligations allowed, took unauthorized military action against Numidian encroachment, Rome declared war in 149 BCE, initiating the Third Punic War.

Unlike the two preceding conflicts, the Third Punic War escalated deliberately toward Carthage's destruction rather than toward its mere defeat. Once war began, Roman commanders presented Carthage with a series of successively harsher demands — first hostages, then the surrender of arms and military equipment, and finally, when Carthage had already disarmed itself, an order that its population abandon the coastal city entirely and resettle inland, a demand Carthage could not accept without ending its existence as a maritime and commercial city. Roman forces then besieged the city for approximately three years; Carthaginian resistance, though ultimately unsuccessful, was determined and costly for the besieging Roman army. In 146 BCE, Roman forces under Scipio Aemilianus finally breached Carthage's defenses. Much of the population was killed during the final sack of the city, and most of the survivors were enslaved; the city itself was destroyed and its territory formally annexed as the Roman province of Africa.

Ancient and later accounts of Carthage's destruction have accumulated considerable legend, and this chapter addresses one particularly persistent myth directly: the widespread modern claim that Roman forces ritually "salted" Carthage's ground to render it permanently infertile. No reliable ancient source describes this act; it appears to be a much later elaboration, seemingly originating in modern scholarship rather than ancient testimony, and should not be presented to students as established historical fact. The physical destruction of the city in 146 BCE was, by contrast, entirely real and extensively documented.

It is equally important to distinguish the destruction of Punic Carthage as a political entity from the survival of Punic culture more broadly. The Punic language continued to be spoken and, in some contexts, written across North Africa for centuries after 146 BCE — inscriptions in Punic and in a related script called Neo-Punic appear well into the Roman imperial period, and Latin authors as late as the fourth century CE occasionally referenced Punic as a living spoken language in parts of Roman Africa. Punic religious practices, personal names, and cultural forms likewise persisted, in adapted form, within the Roman provincial world examined in Section 11.14. The city-state of Carthage ended in 146 BCE; Punic North Africa did not.

Section 11.13

Rome, Jugurtha, Numidia, and Mauretania

The destruction of Carthage in 146 BCE did not immediately bring the rest of North Africa under Roman control. Rome's new province of Africa initially consisted only of former Carthaginian territory in what is now northern Tunisia, while the Numidian kingdom to its west continued to govern a substantial, independent territory under Massinissa's successors.

Numidian independence entered a significant crisis with the reign of Jugurtha, grandson of Massinissa, who consolidated control over a divided Numidian succession through political maneuvering and violence. His rival claimant Adherbal, who had appealed repeatedly to Rome as a ruler with long-standing ties of Roman friendship, was besieged by Jugurtha's forces at Cirta; when the city finally surrendered, Jugurtha's forces killed Adherbal along with a number of Italian residents of Cirta who had joined the city's defense, an act that inflamed opinion at Rome. Combined with allegations of extensive bribery among Roman senators and envoys sent to investigate the Numidian succession crisis, this episode provoked the Jugurthine War (112–105 BCE), a conflict that exposed significant corruption within Roman political institutions and ultimately ended with Jugurtha's defeat, betrayal by his father-in-law the Mauretanian king, and execution in Rome. The war illustrates both continued genuine Numidian political agency and the increasing willingness of Roman political and military leaders to intervene directly in North African dynastic affairs.

Numidian and Mauretanian political history over the following decades became increasingly entangled with Roman civil conflict. During Julius Caesar's civil war against the faction of Pompey, the Numidian king Juba I allied with Caesar's opponents; following Caesar's victory in Africa and Juba I's death in 46 BCE, much of Numidia was annexed as an expanded Roman province, though parts of the former kingdom were reorganized rather than simply absorbed outright. Juba I's son, Juba II, raised in Rome and married to a daughter of the Egyptian queen Cleopatra and the Roman general Mark Antony, was later installed by the emperor Augustus as a client king over Mauretania, ruling a court renowned for its scholarship and its blending of Numidian, Punic, Hellenistic, and Roman cultural elements. Mauretania continued as a nominally independent client kingdom until the reign of the emperor Claudius, when the death of Juba II's son and successor, Ptolemy of Mauretania, in the early 40s CE, led to the kingdom's direct annexation and reorganization as Roman provinces.

This sequence — provincial Africa in 146 BCE, gradual Numidian annexation through the Jugurthine War and Caesar's African campaign, and finally Mauretania's annexation under Claudius nearly two centuries after Carthage's destruction — should make clear to students that "the Roman conquest of North Africa" was not a single event but an extended, uneven process spanning nearly two hundred years, proceeding through warfare, dynastic crisis, client kingship, and eventual direct annexation rather than one decisive campaign.

Section 11.14

Roman Africa: Cities, Farms, Frontiers, and Layered Identities

By the first and second centuries CE, Roman North Africa had developed into one of the empire's most prosperous and important provincial regions. Carthage itself was rebuilt as a major Roman colonial and later provincial capital, growing into one of the largest cities of the western Roman Empire, while other cities — Utica, Hippo Regius, Cirta (renamed Constantina in late antiquity), Thugga (Dougga), Lepcis Magna, and Sabratha along the Tripolitanian coast, and Volubilis in Mauretania — flourished as centers of urban life, provincial administration, and monumental architecture, much of it funded by local elite patronage rather than imperial expenditure alone.

The North African provinces became crucial to Rome's grain supply, supplementing and, at times, rivaling Egypt's importance in this respect, while also exporting olive oil, wine, and other agricultural products across the Mediterranean on a substantial scale. Roman military frontiers along the desert margin, defended by fortified installations and patrol roads, managed rather than eliminated interaction with Saharan societies; the Garamantes, introduced in Section 11.3, continued to engage in both trade and periodic conflict with Roman North Africa, and archaeological evidence of Roman goods reaching deep into Garamantian territory, alongside evidence of Roman military campaigns against Garamantian settlements, demonstrates a frontier characterized by ongoing interaction rather than a sealed boundary between "Roman" and "Saharan" worlds.

Roman provincial society in North Africa was culturally layered rather than uniformly Latinized. Punic continued to be spoken in parts of the provinces for centuries, as noted in Section 11.12; local indigenous Numidian and Mauri populations retained distinct identities, languages, and, in many rural areas, forms of social and political organization only loosely integrated into Roman civic structures; and provincial elites selectively adopted Latin, Roman citizenship, municipal institutions, and Mediterranean elite cultural practices such as bathing complexes, mosaic decoration, and Latin literary education, often while continuing to maintain Punic or indigenous names, religious practices, and family structures. The emperor Caracalla's Constitutio Antoniniana of 212 CE, extending Roman citizenship broadly across the empire's free population, further deepened this integration without erasing local distinctiveness, and North Africa would go on to produce some of the Roman Empire's most significant intellectual and religious figures, developments belonging chiefly to the late-antique history examined in Chapter 13.

This chapter closes around the end of the third century CE, on the threshold of the profound religious and political transformations — Christianization, the controversies later associated with figures such as Augustine of Hippo, the Vandal invasions, and Byzantine reconquest — that Chapter 13 examines as part of the Arab lands in late antiquity. What should remain with students from this chapter is the longer arc it has traced: indigenous North African societies present before, alongside, and after every wave of outside settlement; a Punic Carthage that was simultaneously Levantine in origin and thoroughly African in its economic and political life; an independent Numidian kingdom whose rulers pursued their own ambitions rather than merely serving Carthaginian or Roman interests; and a Roman provincial world built through incremental conquest that transformed, but never erased, the layered identities of the peoples who called this region home.

Historical Vocabulary
Amazigh, Libyan, Numidian, and Punic

Students frequently encounter these terms as though they were interchangeable, but each identifies a distinct historical category. Libyan was an ancient outside label, used by Egyptian, Greek, and Roman observers, for indigenous populations west of Egypt; it covered many distinct communities rather than a single unified people. Numidian and Mauri refer to more specific regional populations — Numidians in the area of modern eastern Algeria and western Tunisia, Mauri farther west in modern Morocco and western Algeria — who developed increasingly centralized kingdoms during the period this chapter examines.

Punic describes the distinctive culture that developed among western Phoenician-derived communities centered on Carthage: Levantine in linguistic and religious origin, but shaped over centuries by North African and western Mediterranean circumstances. Punic is a cultural and historical category, not a separate ancestry entirely distinct from Phoenician.

Amazigh is the modern term, increasingly preferred by North African communities themselves, for the indigenous peoples of the Maghreb and Sahara and for their language family. It provides a genuine and important continuity with ancient Libyan, Numidian, Mauri, and Garamantian populations, but historians should avoid projecting a single unified ancient "Amazigh nation" backward onto communities that, in antiquity, organized their political identities chiefly around more local kingdoms and lineages rather than a shared pan-regional consciousness.

Historical Vocabulary
Suffetes and Carthaginian Government

The suffetes (from a Semitic root related to the Hebrew shofet, "judge") were Carthage's chief annually elected magistrates, typically two in number, who presided over civic and religious affairs. Their authority operated alongside a powerful council of elders drawn from Carthage's leading families and, under some circumstances, a citizen assembly whose exact powers are not fully documented in surviving sources.

Ancient observers, including later Greek political writers, noted that Carthaginian government combined monarchic, aristocratic, and popular elements in a manner they considered notably stable and durable compared to many contemporary Mediterranean states. Modern historians generally treat this observation as broadly credible while cautioning that most surviving descriptions of Carthaginian government come from outside observers rather than Carthaginian sources themselves, since Carthage's own administrative archives were largely lost in the destruction of 146 BCE.

Because the inscription survives in physical, carved form rather than through a literary intermediary, it also offers evidence more directly connected to Numidian society itself than the Greek and Roman literary accounts on which much of this chapter otherwise depends.

Historical Debate

What Happened at the Carthaginian Tophet?

The question: Ancient Greek and Roman authors repeatedly accused Carthaginians of ritually sacrificing infant children, particularly in times of crisis, at a sacred precinct modern archaeologists call the tophet — a term borrowed from a biblical Hebrew word for a place of sacrificial burning. Excavations at Carthage and other Punic sites have uncovered large quantities of cremated infant and young-animal remains alongside inscribed votive stelae dedicated to Baal Hammon and Tanit. What do this evidence and these ancient accusations actually establish, and what remains genuinely uncertain?

Interpretation A — The tophet evidence substantially supports the ancient accusation of child sacrifice: Some scholars argue that the concentration of cremated infant remains, combined with inscriptions explicitly describing offerings made "because [the deity] heard the voice" of the dedicant's prayer, together with independent ancient testimony from multiple Greek and Roman sources, provides converging evidence that Carthaginians practiced ritual child sacrifice, at least under some circumstances and among some segment of the population, rather than merely burying children who had died of natural causes in a special sacred cemetery.
Interpretation B — The evidence more plausibly reflects infant burial practices, exaggerated by hostile outside reporting: Other scholars argue that ancient Greek and Roman accounts of Carthaginian child sacrifice reflect a long tradition of hostile political and cultural propaganda directed against a defeated enemy, that osteological analysis of tophet remains in some studies suggests a significant proportion of individuals may have died of natural causes such as stillbirth or early infant mortality, and that the tophet may have functioned, at least in part, as a specialized cemetery for infants who died young, rather than as evidence of routine or widespread ritual killing.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on the same core evidence — cremated infant and animal remains, votive inscriptions, and ancient literary testimony — but differ in how they weigh conflicting osteological studies of age-at-death, how much credibility to extend to hostile Greek and Roman literary sources, and how to interpret dedicatory language that explicitly frames the tophet burials as offerings to deities.

Why it matters: This debate illustrates, more starkly than almost any other topic in this chapter, the danger of relying on sources produced overwhelmingly by a society's enemies, and the corresponding importance of archaeological and osteological evidence as an independent check on literary testimony — while also showing that archaeological evidence itself can be read in more than one way and remains the subject of genuine, ongoing scholarly disagreement.

Student task: Using the evidence and considerations described above, explain why historians have not reached full consensus on this question, and identify what additional kinds of evidence might help resolve the disagreement.

Multiple Perspectives

Living Under Successive Powers in North Africa

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns historians and archaeologists have identified across many sources. They are not quotations from any single named historical actor.

From the perspective of a Libyan farming household in Carthage's agricultural hinterland: Taxation and labor demands from Carthaginian authorities weigh heavily in years of war or political strain, even as access to Carthaginian markets and craft goods offers real economic advantages unavailable to communities farther from the coast.
From the perspective of a Punic merchant or craftworker in the city of Carthage: The city's prosperity rests on ships, workshops, and trading contacts stretching from Iberia to the eastern Mediterranean, and political decisions made by the suffetes and council can affect a household's livelihood as directly as any military campaign.
From the perspective of a Numidian cavalry commander serving under Massinissa: Loyalty is owed first to the Numidian king and the kingdom's growing power and territory, not automatically to either Carthage or Rome; alliance with one or the other is a calculated tool for Numidian advantage, not a fixed allegiance.
From the perspective of a Garamantian oasis farmer in the Fazzan: Survival and prosperity depend on the foggara channels that bring water to the fields and on trading relationships that stretch both north toward the Mediterranean coast and south into the wider Sahara, not on the political fortunes of Carthage or Rome, which register mainly through the goods that occasionally reach these southern markets.
From the perspective of a resident of Roman-era Thugga a few centuries later: Latin inscriptions and Roman civic buildings stand alongside households where Punic is still the language spoken at home and where ancestral religious practices continue in modified form, a layered inheritance that no single label — Roman, Punic, or Numidian — fully captures.
Continuity and Change Over Time

Layered North African Identities

What Changed

  • Political authority over North Africa shifted from indigenous chiefdoms to Phoenician coastal settlement, Punic Carthaginian hegemony, independent Numidian kingship, and finally Roman provincial administration
  • Carthage itself was destroyed as a political entity in 146 BCE and later rebuilt as a Roman city with a different civic and legal structure
  • Formal citizenship and legal status expanded significantly, especially after the Constitutio Antoniniana of 212 CE
  • Monumental architecture, urban planning, and elite material culture increasingly reflected Roman provincial conventions over time

What Stayed the Same

  • Indigenous North African populations, languages, and rural social structures persisted beneath every layer of outside political control
  • Punic language and religious practice continued for centuries after Carthage's political destruction
  • North Africa's agricultural productivity, particularly grain and olive production, remained central to the region's wealth and to its importance to outside powers
  • Connections between the Mediterranean coast and the Saharan interior, exemplified by continued Garamantian trade, persisted across every political transition examined in this chapter

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both change and continuity prevents treating North African history as a series of total replacements — Phoenicians replacing indigenous society, Rome replacing Carthage — when in fact each transition layered new political and cultural forms atop persistent indigenous and Punic foundations.

Cause and Effect

The Rise of Carthaginian Power

Long-term causes

Carthage's defensible peninsular location, its inheritance of Phoenician maritime and commercial expertise, and its access to productive North African agricultural land in its hinterland.

Short-term causes

Growing pressure on the eastern Phoenician homeland from Assyrian, Babylonian, and Persian expansion, which shifted the center of Phoenician commercial initiative toward the western Mediterranean and toward Carthage specifically.

Effect

Carthage grew from one Phoenician settlement among several into the dominant western Phoenician (Punic) power, extending commercial and political influence across Sicily, Sardinia, Iberia, and its own North African hinterland by the fifth and fourth centuries BCE.

Historical complexity

Carthaginian power depended throughout on negotiated and sometimes coerced relationships with indigenous Libyan and, later, Numidian populations; it was never simply a Mediterranean maritime achievement independent of Carthage's African setting and African relationships.

Historical complexity: Historians continue to debate how much weight to give commercial versus agricultural foundations of Carthaginian wealth, and how much Carthaginian political stability depended on successfully managing relationships with indigenous North African populations rather than purely Mediterranean factors.

Everyday Life and Social History

Households, Labor, and Devotion in Punic and Numidian Society

For most residents of Carthage and its North African hinterland, daily life centered on household, craft, and agricultural labor rather than the political and military events that dominate most surviving accounts. Carthaginian households engaged in textile production, food processing, and craft work, much of it organized around family workshops; agricultural households in Carthage's hinterland, whether of Punic, mixed, or Libyan descent, worked land that produced grain, olives, and livestock for both household consumption and urban markets. Dependent and enslaved laborers, drawn from war captives, purchased individuals, and, in some periods, indigenous populations under Carthaginian authority, performed much of the physical labor sustaining both agricultural and craft production, though the precise scale and conditions of slavery in Carthaginian society remain incompletely documented compared to later, better-recorded Roman practice.

Religious devotion at the household level, evidenced by small votive offerings, domestic shrines, and inscribed stelae dedicated by individuals and families rather than by the state, suggests that ordinary Carthaginians maintained personal religious relationships with deities such as Tanit and Baal Hammon alongside participation in larger civic and temple worship. In Numidian society, similarly, archaeological evidence of rural settlement, tomb architecture, and grave goods indicates a population engaged in mixed farming and pastoral labor whose material life, while less lavishly documented than that of Numidian royalty, shows increasing engagement with Punic and Mediterranean material culture over the centuries this chapter examines. Because nearly all surviving literary sources were produced by urban, literate, often hostile outside observers, historians rely heavily on archaeology, inscriptions, and comparative evidence to reconstruct the experience of Carthaginian and Numidian households, particularly those of women, children, laborers, and rural populations whose lives rarely appear directly in Greek or Roman political narrative.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined ancient North Africa as a region shaped by indigenous societies, Phoenician and Punic settlement, Numidian state formation, and Roman conquest — none of which should be understood as replacing or erasing the others. Indigenous Libyan, Numidian, Mauri, and Garamantian communities inhabited and organized the region long before Phoenician settlers arrived, and they remained central historical actors throughout the many centuries this chapter has surveyed.

Phoenician settlers founded Carthage as part of a broader pattern of western Mediterranean settlement, and Carthage grew, over several centuries, from one Phoenician-founded city into the dominant western Phoenician, or Punic, power, developing a sophisticated government led by suffetes, a diversified economy combining maritime trade, agriculture, and craft production, and a religious life centered on deities such as Baal Hammon and Tanit. Carthaginian power depended throughout on its relationship with its North African hinterland and with neighboring Libyan and, later, Numidian populations — a relationship of taxation, alliance, conflict, and cultural exchange rather than simple domination.

The three Punic Wars reshaped the ancient Mediterranean. The First Punic War established Roman naval power and cost Carthage Sicily; the Second Punic War, despite Hannibal's remarkable early victories, ultimately strengthened Rome's position and, crucially, elevated the Numidian kingdom under Massinissa to major-power status; and the Third Punic War ended with the destruction of Carthage as an independent city-state in 146 BCE, though Punic language and culture persisted for centuries afterward.

This chapter has given the Numidian kingdom sustained independent attention, tracing its consolidation under Massinissa, its later crisis under Jugurtha, and its gradual absorption into the Roman provincial world through the campaigns of Julius Caesar and the eventual annexation of Mauretania under Claudius. This sequence demonstrates that Roman control over North Africa developed incrementally across nearly two centuries rather than arriving in a single conquest.

The Historical Debate feature examined the genuinely contested evidence surrounding the Carthaginian tophet, modeling the kind of careful evaluation of hostile literary sources and ambiguous archaeological evidence that this textbook asks students to practice throughout. The chapter also addressed and corrected a persistent modern myth — that Rome salted the ground of Carthage — clarifying what the ancient evidence does and does not support.

Finally, the chapter traced how Roman provincial Africa, established through this long process of conquest, remained a culturally layered society: Punic language and religious practice persisted, indigenous Numidian and Mauri populations retained distinct identities, and provincial elites selectively adopted Roman citizenship, institutions, and material culture without abandoning older inheritances. This layered North African world, prosperous and increasingly touched by Christianity by the third century CE, provides the essential background for Chapter 13's examination of the Arab lands in late antiquity.

As throughout Part II of this textbook, none of the peoples examined in this chapter should be understood as Arab. The Arabization of North Africa remains centuries in the future. What this chapter has established is the deep, layered, and independently significant history of the peoples and states — indigenous, Punic, Numidian, and Roman — whose interactions shaped the land that would, many centuries later, become part of the Arab Maghreb.

This chapter has also modeled a method of historical reading that recurs throughout this textbook: weighing evidence produced largely by a society's rivals or conquerors against archaeological and epigraphic evidence more directly connected to the societies themselves, distinguishing secure findings from genuinely contested questions such as the tophet debate, and resisting the temptation to compress uneven, centuries-long processes — Phoenician settlement, Punic state formation, Numidian kingship, and Roman conquest — into a single tidy narrative of civilizational succession.

Key Terms

Amazigh
The modern collective name and language family of indigenous North African peoples, tracing deep roots to ancient Libyan, Numidian, and Mauri populations.
Libyan
An ancient outside label for indigenous populations west of Egypt, covering many distinct communities rather than a single unified people.
Numidian
Indigenous peoples of the region of modern eastern Algeria and western Tunisia who developed a major independent kingdom under Massinissa and his successors.
Mauri
Indigenous peoples of the region of modern Morocco and western Algeria, later associated with the kingdom and Roman province of Mauretania.
Garamantes
A Saharan society of the Fazzan region in southern Libya, known for oasis agriculture using foggara irrigation channels and long-distance trans-Saharan trade.
Punic
The distinctive culture of western Phoenician-derived communities centered on Carthage, Levantine in origin but shaped by centuries of North African and western Mediterranean development.
Suffete
One of Carthage's two annually elected chief magistrates, presiding over civic and religious affairs alongside a council of elders.
Tophet
A sacred precinct at Carthage and other Punic sites containing cremated infant and animal remains and votive inscriptions, subject to significant scholarly debate discussed in this chapter.
Baal Hammon
A major Punic deity, frequently invoked as a father figure and closely associated with tophet sanctuaries; his precise functions remain debated among specialists.
Tanit
A Carthaginian goddess who rose to particular civic prominence from around the fifth century BCE onward.
Barcid
The prominent Carthaginian family, including Hamilcar Barca and his son Hannibal, whose Iberian territorial ambitions led to the Second Punic War.
Hinterland
The agricultural and pastoral territory surrounding a city, in this chapter referring especially to Carthage's North African hinterland and its Libyan inhabitants.
Client kingdom
A nominally independent kingdom, such as Juba II's Mauretania, whose ruler governed under the influence or protection of a stronger power such as Rome.
Province
A territory under direct Roman administrative control, as North Africa became in stages between 146 BCE and 44 CE.
Constitutio Antoniniana
The 212 CE edict of the emperor Caracalla extending Roman citizenship broadly across the empire's free population, including North Africa.
Foundation legend
A traditional story, such as that of Dido/Elissa, explaining a city's origins in culturally meaningful rather than strictly documentary terms.
Trans-Saharan exchange
Long-distance trade and communication networks linking Mediterranean North Africa to societies across and south of the Sahara, sustained in part by Garamantian intermediaries.
Indemnity
A payment imposed on a defeated power, such as the indemnities Carthage paid to Rome after the First and Second Punic Wars.
Annexation
The formal incorporation of a territory into another state's direct control, as occurred incrementally across Numidia and Mauretania.
Neo-Punic
A later form of the Punic script and language, attested in inscriptions across North Africa for centuries after the destruction of Carthage.
Foggara
A gravity-fed underground irrigation channel used by the Garamantes, related in principle to the qanat and falaj systems discussed in Chapter 3.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has examined ancient North Africa as a region of layered, interacting histories rather than a single story of outside conquest. Indigenous Libyan, Numidian, Mauri, and Garamantian societies shaped the region before Phoenician settlers arrived and continued to shape it throughout the Punic and Roman centuries that followed. Carthage, for all its Mediterranean prominence, was an African city whose power depended on its hinterland as much as its harbors. Numidia, too often reduced to a supporting role in the story of Rome and Carthage, built one of the ancient world's genuinely significant independent kingdoms.

Understanding this layered history matters directly for the chapters that follow. Chapter 12 will examine ancient Arabia and the desert crossroads, another region this textbook insists on treating as historically active rather than peripheral. Chapter 13 will pick up North Africa's story again within the framework of late antiquity, tracing how Roman Africa's Christian communities, including the towering figure of Augustine of Hippo, developed atop the layered Punic, Numidian, and Roman foundations this chapter has described. And Part IV of this textbook, when it finally turns to the Arab-Islamic conquests of the seventh century, will find in North Africa a region whose established Amazigh-speaking communities, local political structures, regional networks, and inherited social institutions shaped how Arabization and Islamization would themselves unfold — a story of continuity and transformation, not replacement, consistent with the pattern this chapter has traced from its opening pages.

Students who understand the distinction between Libyan, Numidian, Mauri, and Amazigh identities, the independent significance of Massinissa's kingdom, the incremental rather than sudden nature of Roman conquest, and the persistence of Punic language and culture well beyond 146 BCE are well equipped to recognize similar patterns of layered identity and gradual political transformation throughout the rest of this textbook's narrative.

The story of ancient North Africa is, in the end, a story about the limits of conquest to erase a people's history, and about the danger of writing that history using only the words of the conquerors. The evidence this chapter has surveyed — inscriptions, archaeology, coinage, and the occasional grudging admission within hostile literary sources — allows a fuller, more accurate account: one in which indigenous North Africans, Punic Carthaginians, and Numidian kings all appear as historical actors in their own right.
