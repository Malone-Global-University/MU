Chapter Opening

In December 2010 a confrontation in a Tunisian town became the starting point of a decade of revolutions, counterrevolutions, civil wars, and new demands for dignity and accountability.

On 17 December 2010 a street vendor, Mohamed Bouazizi, set himself on fire in the Tunisian town of Sidi Bouzid following a confrontation with local authorities. Within weeks demonstrations reached the capital and the country’s president departed. News of the events traveled through television, mobile telephones, and human networks across the Arab world. For many observers the Tunisian uprising suggested that political systems long seen as immovable could be challenged by collective public action. The effects of that possibility would be felt in very different ways over the following decade.

From 2010 to 2020 citizens confronted presidents, royal governments, security institutions, and armed organizations. They demanded dignity, jobs, justice, representation, and protection from arbitrary violence. In some countries rulers departed; elsewhere protests were suppressed or met with institutional concessions. In Syria, Libya, and Yemen, political conflict escalated into prolonged wars whose effects traveled far beyond national borders. The chapter investigates the connections among these developments without treating them as one uniform revolution.

Three historical processes must be distinguished. A popular uprising challenges existing authority; a political transition attempts to establish or renegotiate governing institutions; a civil war involves sustained armed conflict among organized parties within a state. The processes may overlap but do not inevitably follow from one another. A successful protest may leave an army and bureaucracy unchanged, a new constitution may not be enforceable, and a ceasefire may not produce peace. These distinctions are essential to comparing events in Tunisia, Egypt, Bahrain, Syria, Libya, and Yemen.

The uprisings grew in settings shaped by decades of post-independence state-building, economic liberalization, expanded education, restricted political rights, and unequal access to services. Women, workers, teachers, students, lawyers, rural residents, professional organizers, and families of detainees often played major roles. Television and digital communication amplified their actions, but their organizing capacities depended on existing relationships and institutions. Economic grievance alone cannot explain why mobilization began in particular places or why coercive authorities responded differently.

International and regional intervention became central to the decade. Saudi Arabia, the United Arab Emirates, Qatar, Turkey, Iran, Russia, the United States, and European governments supported competing security or political arrangements. Armed movements, including ISIS, exploited fragmented authority and committed grave atrocities. Displacement disrupted cities, households, education, and healthcare. Yet the region was not simply a field on which foreign powers acted: domestic politicians, protesters, soldiers, civic organizations, and local administrators continued to make consequential choices, often in conditions of extreme constraint.

Historians draw on constitutions, Security Council resolutions, commission reports, public statements, election records, testimony, photographs, forensic investigations, and humanitarian statistics. Each source has limits. Governments and armed movements promote their preferred accounts; witnesses may be at risk; investigators may lack territorial access; and estimates of deaths or displacement depend on definitions and dates. This chapter identifies editorial paraphrases as such, separates documented acts from disputed claims, and explains how historians can test interpretations without inventing certainty.

The narrative ends on 31 December 2020. It develops the pre-2011 background treated in Chapter 70 only where necessary and preserves longer-term political, economic, environmental, and cultural synthesis for Chapter 72. The aim is not to declare an overall victory or failure for the Arab uprisings, but to explain why their trajectories diverged and what their outcomes reveal about state power, political legitimacy, regional intervention, civilian resilience, and the limits of historical prediction.

Historical Context

During the post-independence decades, Arab states established differing forms of republican and monarchical authority. Some presidents ruled through military institutions and dominant parties; others depended on security services and patronage networks. Monarchies had their own combinations of inherited authority, consultative institutions, organized opposition, and resource distribution. Chapter 70 explains how the post-Cold War period expanded educational access and opened some markets without consistently expanding political accountability. By 2010 these contradictions had accumulated differently across countries.

The political geography was equally varied. Tunisia and Egypt contained large national institutions and urban protest centers; Yemen had long-standing northern and southern grievances and multiple armed networks; Libya relied heavily on personalized rule; Syria combined powerful security services with distinct regional and communal histories; and Bahrain participated in dense Gulf security arrangements. Similar slogans would therefore encounter institutions with different capacities and incentives. In some places troops could refuse to sustain a president; elsewhere security leaders viewed the survival of government as inseparable from their own futures.

The preceding decade also brought rising living costs, uneven regional development, and increasing expectations among educated young people. Formal unemployment did not capture insecure work and the obstacles to establishing households. Satellite media and internet networks enabled circulation of criticism, yet governments continued to censor and monitor political communication. Preexisting labor groups, professional associations, lawyers, human-rights advocates, and religious organizations provided important organizational capacities. It would be inaccurate to credit either economic hardship or technology alone with creating the uprisings.

The 2003 Iraq war, the unresolved Palestinian question, and rivalries among Iran, Gulf states, Turkey, and external powers formed part of the regional context. This chapter does not retell their earlier histories. It explains how governments interpreted the protests in relation to security interests and how intervention changed the conflicts of the 2010s. The study proceeds from first protests and political transitions to the consequences of civil war and renewed mobilization, ending before later political developments assigned to the succeeding chapter.

Division I — The Crisis of Authoritarian Rule and the Uprisings of 2010–2011
Section 71.1

The Conditions Behind the Arab Uprisings

The political background to the uprisings was not uniform. Tunisia and Egypt had presidents whose authority depended on dominant parties, security ministries, patronage, and elections with restricted competition. Syria combined Ba'athist institutions and intelligence organizations with a tightly concentrated presidency. Yemen's political order rested on constantly renegotiated relationships among officers, tribal figures, party leaders, and regional power holders. The Gulf monarchies had different combinations of consultative institutions, oil revenue, family rule, and security partnerships. Yet ordinary citizens encountered related grievances in police stations, licensing offices, courts, and workplaces: arbitrary enforcement, lack of accountability, unequal opportunity, and the sense that personal connections often mattered more than public rules. These conditions created sources of discontent, but none of them alone dictated the date of the uprisings.

Education expanded over previous decades while public employment grew less capable of absorbing new graduates. Economic liberalization provided investment in finance, tourism, real estate, and telecommunications, but did not reliably produce secure jobs across regions. Urban growth disguised neglected interior districts, and formal unemployment rates undercounted informal work and precarious incomes. These pressures made marriage, housing, and family support difficult for many young people. Frustration was not only economic. A university graduate confronted by arbitrary police conduct could experience unemployment and denial of citizenship as parts of one political problem. Calls for bread, freedom, dignity, and social justice expressed this relationship, although protesters did not necessarily agree on an economic program or constitutional design.

An important distinction concerns the legitimacy of the state itself. Some protesters wanted competent and less corrupt administration, while others demanded an entirely new constitutional relationship between citizens and rulers. Religious organizations, trade unions, and professional bodies could cooperate on opposition to police abuse but disagree over law and representation. Later debates over which participants truly represented the revolution often obscured this diversity. Historians should use the words of specific organizations at particular dates instead of assuming that all demonstrators shared a stable ideology. Political identity was shaped during the uprisings as well as before them.

The economic context also varied by generation and location. A capital-city employer might see new investment while a rural graduate found no job; a household receiving remittances could have different incentives from one dependent on a shrinking public payroll. Rising food prices put pressure on the poor, but grievances over favoritism could unite people across income groups. These variations help explain why unemployment rates alone cannot predict the onset of revolt and why attempts to reduce the uprisings to either poverty or internet access are analytically inadequate.

The language of dignity—karāma—linked everyday humiliation to the larger question of who possessed political authority. A street vendor might demand fair treatment rather than a change in ideology; a lawyer could connect police impunity to an unaccountable judiciary; a worker might protest wages, union restrictions, and corruption simultaneously. Such coalitions were powerful because they allowed people with different viewpoints to act together. They were also fragile: once a ruler departed, the same participants might disagree about elections, religion, economic policy, or the removal of officials associated with the previous regime. A coalition against a president did not automatically constitute a governing party.

Political organization preceded the mass protests. Egyptian labor strikes, youth networks, professional associations, and human-rights groups had accumulated experience; Tunisian regional union branches and lawyers had existing networks of local trust. Satellite television and digital platforms allowed footage of police repression to circulate rapidly, but these tools did not replace organizers, neighborhood relationships, physical meetings, and people willing to take risks. Governments blocked websites, disrupted communications, and circulated their own propaganda. Protest activity often persisted despite these measures. Women, students, workers, journalists, and families of people killed or detained contributed organization, testimony, medical assistance, and public legitimacy.

An explanation of timing must ask why an event that could otherwise remain local became a national symbol, why repression sometimes enlarged rather than deterred demonstrations, and how people revised their beliefs about whether others would join. The departure of a president in Tunisia changed expectations elsewhere, but coercive institutions and external allies responded differently. Historians therefore distinguish four analytical problems: underlying grievances, immediate triggers, the organization of protest, and the response of authorities. Police records, demographic surveys, testimony, election data, and photographs can illuminate these questions only if their creators, gaps, and political purposes are understood. The aim is neither to attribute revolution to social media nor to infer predetermined national outcomes from later events.

Historical Vocabulary

Dignity (karāma), authoritarianism, and political accountability

Dignity in the language of the uprisings meant more than public respect: it connected freedom from arbitrary policing, fair treatment in government offices, the ability to earn a living, and participation in political decisions. The Arabic karāma expresses an ethical and civic demand often linked to freedom and social justice.

Authoritarianism describes systems in which leaders and their allies substantially restrict political competition and public accountability. Patronage means distributing opportunities through personal or political connections. These definitions help explain how grievances could cross class and ideological divisions.

Section 71.2

Tunisia: Bouazizi, the Revolution of Dignity, and Ben Ali’s Fall

Sidi Bouzid was a town in Tunisia's interior, far from the coastal investment and tourism districts disproportionately associated with national development. Mohamed Bouazizi's self-immolation on 17 December 2010 followed a confrontation with local authorities and became a symbol of the indignities people associated with unemployment and arbitrary administration. The precise details of his encounter have been recounted differently by witnesses and later writers. What matters historically is how families, local lawyers, union activists, students, and other residents transformed the incident into sustained protest. The movement spread through towns such as Kasserine, where regional inequality and long resentment of police conduct helped mobilize crowds.

The Tunisian General Labour Union (UGTT) was crucial but not monolithic. Local and regional branches acted with varying degrees of autonomy, while national leaders adjusted their position as the crisis deepened. Lawyers, teachers, and neighborhood networks offered public legitimacy and practical organization. The killings of demonstrators drew new people into streets and funerals became sites of collective mobilization. Mobile-phone footage and pan-Arab broadcasting carried news that official channels had minimized. Police violence did not automatically suppress protests; in important places it widened the conviction that the state's conduct required a response beyond ordinary complaints.

The chronology of Tunisia's final weeks matters. Demonstrations emerged in interior regions before becoming large national events. The government's declarations and televised addresses became less effective as evidence of police force circulated. Lawyers and union networks helped turn separate protests into demonstrations of solidarity, while families of those killed transformed mourning into political pressure. An inquiry into who organized strikes, where they occurred, and which offices remained functional would explain the collapse of presidential authority better than the symbolic story of Bouazizi alone.

The transition also raised the issue of continuity in public administration. Removing a president did not automatically change appointments, budgets, or inherited local relationships. Former regime-associated officials retained expertise and influence; activists demanded accountability, but wholesale exclusion risked weakening the capacity to deliver services. Transitional authorities therefore faced genuine dilemmas over law, justice, and stability. These problems make Tunisia useful for comparing revolutionary momentum with the less visible work of constitution-making and bureaucratic reform.

Ben Ali's government had ruled since 1987 through a strong security apparatus, a dominant party, and closely managed elections. The country also had real educational and social achievements, including substantial gains for women's legal status. These did not erase criticism of politically connected business networks, corruption, and limited public freedoms. In January 2011 the movement reached Tunis and the interior ministry. Ben Ali offered cabinet changes, political promises, and employment measures while threatening demonstrators. The promises were undermined by escalating violence and declining confidence. The regular military did not sustain the president through the massive repression that would have been necessary to restore his position; he departed on 14 January.

Descriptions of a single officer heroically refusing a single order are insufficient to explain the institutional outcome. Relations among military leaders, the interior ministry, and political authorities require careful documentary analysis. It is clear, however, that the army's position differed from the durable security alignment later seen in Syria. It is equally clear that Ben Ali's departure did not dissolve the bureaucracy, courts, security institutions, or patterns of economic inequality. Protesters continued to demand a new constituent process, accountability for victims, and removal of officials associated with the previous party. The transition consequently began amid disputes over how much continuity a legitimate state could retain.

Tunisia's revolution gained international symbolic importance because it showed that a long-serving ruler might be removed by mass action. Yet its subsequent politics were not simply a continuation of the demonstrations. Islamists, secular organizations, labor groups, professionals, and former regime-associated actors disagreed over religion, citizenship, rights, and economic priorities. The capacity to organize a protest was distinct from the capacity to administer services or negotiate constitutional rules. Families of people killed, injured, or impoverished continued to seek justice. The sequence from December 2010 to January 2011 was thus both a dramatic political rupture and the opening of a slower struggle over government institutions and social opportunity.

Section 71.3

Egypt: The January 25 Revolution and the Fall of Mubarak

Egyptian protests drew on a history of labor action, the Kefaya movement, the April 6 youth network, and opposition to police brutality. The date of 25 January 2011, National Police Day, directed attention to an institution associated with arbitrary detention and abuse. The killing of Khaled Said in Alexandria in 2010 had already become an emblem of police impunity. Organizers used online pages, but rallies depended on long-developed relationships among labor activists, students, professional associations, rights advocates, and political organizations. The protesters were not one homogeneous party: some supported liberal or leftist reforms, others Islamist organizations, and many primarily demanded accountability, freedom, and economic security.

Demonstrations spread through Cairo, Alexandria, Suez, and other cities. On 28 January, the Friday of Anger, large confrontations occurred and police withdrew or collapsed in several places; the army deployed. The government disrupted internet access, yet public assemblies continued. Tahrir Square became a site of political debate, improvised clinics, protection committees, food distribution, and symbolic occupation of public space. Its televised image did not capture the entire country. Industrial workers organized actions and families weighed dangers of attending marches, while neighborhoods dealt with fears of looting and insecure policing. Women participated as organizers, witnesses, medical volunteers, and demonstrators, not merely as images of a popular crowd.

Egypt's armed forces differed institutionally from the police. Military commanders could withhold support from Mubarak without accepting an end to military autonomy. In the ensuing months officers controlled decisions about elections, constitutional amendments, and public order. The risk of military trials of civilians and the continuing power of interior-ministry personnel became central to post-uprising protest. A historian must examine the military's public statements alongside budgets, command appointments, and interactions with opposition leaders to assess its role rather than infer neutrality from images of soldiers beside demonstrators.

The experience of neighborhoods differed from that of Tahrir Square. After the collapse of regular policing, residents organized checkpoints and protection groups, sometimes amid rumors and insecurity. Workers considered lost income alongside political demands. Religious groups contributed services and organization; women faced both opportunities to act publicly and particular forms of harassment. A national narrative that moves directly from January 25 to February 11 misses the ways the uprising transformed everyday expectations, including the hope that future police and administrative decisions could be challenged openly.

The government attempted to survive through ministerial changes, promises of transition, negotiated contacts, and intimidation. The early-February Battle of the Camel involved pro-government attacks on demonstrators. Violence and deaths qualify any description of the revolt as simply peaceful, even though nonviolent protest remained central to many participants' strategy. The military was also not a neutral observer. Egypt's armed forces possessed substantial economic interests, organizational autonomy, influence over government, and a stake in preserving their corporate role. Military personnel at times stood between protesters and police, yet the institution did not relinquish political authority when it declined to defend Mubarak's continuation as president.

Mubarak resigned on 11 February 2011, and authority passed to the Supreme Council of the Armed Forces (SCAF). Political institutions nevertheless remained contested: the constitution, courts, security services, police, electoral authorities, and economic networks were not recreated in a day. Organizations entered the transition with unequal resources. The Muslim Brotherhood possessed a long-established social and political infrastructure; newer revolutionary movements had impressive mobilizing capacities but lacked comparable nationwide party machinery. Political actors disputed whether elections should precede constitution-making and how to investigate earlier security abuses. Their differences were genuine rather than proof that their shared opposition to Mubarak had been insincere.

The historical distinction between removing a ruler and transforming a regime is central. In February 2011 many different political outcomes remained possible, but military autonomy, institutional distrust, and conflicting understandings of legitimacy sharply constrained negotiations. Electoral victory might establish one claim to office while leaving other institutions resistant to new oversight. Popular demonstrations could change a presidency without establishing durable procedures for sharing authority. Egypt's later crisis in 2012–2013 cannot be read as already inevitable at Mubarak's departure; it requires a history of specific choices, institutions, and political conflict that the next division develops.

Section 71.4

The Uprisings Spread: Regional Mobilization and Divergent State Responses

The uprisings spread because events in Tunisia and Egypt changed perceptions of what collective action could achieve. Demonstrations emerged in Bahrain, Yemen, Syria, Libya, Morocco, Jordan, Oman, and other places, but their demands were not interchangeable. Some called for immediate presidential resignation, others sought a constitutional monarchy, elections, fair treatment by police, government accountability, or improved jobs and services. The rapid circulation of slogans and images created a recognizable regional language of dignity without producing a single political organization. Participants could learn from events elsewhere while remaining rooted in highly specific local conditions.

The geography of protest complicates the focus on capital cities. Tunisia's Sidi Bouzid, Syria's Daraa, Egypt's Suez, and Oman's Sohar each show that demonstrations could begin or gather strength away from national ministries. Industrial relations, neglected infrastructure, cross-border connections, and experiences with local authorities influenced mobilization. National broadcasts amplified some sites and neglected others. A map identifying protest locations can demonstrate geographic spread, but it cannot reveal whether every resident agreed with a movement or whether areas outside the capital experienced the same grievances. Scholars should use dated maps, local testimony, and socioeconomic evidence together.

Different political demands also created different meanings of success. A movement seeking a new president might count resignation as victory; one seeking electoral monarchy would judge constitutional amendments by the powers transferred; a movement seeking local jobs might see a wage increase as insufficient without changes in corruption. These distinctions make simple country rankings misleading. One should compare the stated goals of named organizations with the actions of governments, not project a single standard retrospectively onto every protest wave.

The region's states were linked by migration, broadcasting, diplomacy, and trade, but those connections did not erase sovereignty or local history. Television could show an Egyptian crowd to a Bahraini viewer, yet the Bahraini monarchy's Gulf security commitments shaped a different political response. The meaning of army deployment in Tunisia also differed from military factionalism in Yemen. Comparing the same event category across countries—police violence, military refusal, constitutional promise—allows historians to identify genuine patterns without collapsing distinct national stories.

Government institutions responded differently. Bahrain retained coercive capacity and received GCC security support, while Morocco and Jordan combined institutional concessions and controlled political opening with continued monarchical authority. Oman's government offered employment and administrative changes while also using coercion. In Yemen rival military units and political networks fractured, helping produce a negotiated transfer but not unified authority. Libya's armed institutions split and international intervention altered the military balance. Syria's sustained security repression and emerging opposition militarization contributed to prolonged conflict. These variations cannot be explained only by the availability of oil revenue or a distinction between republics and monarchies.

Broadcasting, mobile phones, mosques, unions, student groups, professional associations, and neighborhood organizations offered different means of mobilization. The same media infrastructure also enabled state surveillance and counter-messaging. Protesters adapted to shutdowns and censorship, but communication alone could not compel a security institution to change course. Leaders had to choose whether to concede, threaten, negotiate, or escalate. External governments weighed risks to allies and their own domestic standing. A comparison of state responses therefore needs to examine both political structures and strategic choices at particular moments.

By the end of 2011 the outcomes already diverged. Tunisia was reconstructing constitutional institutions; Egypt's army governed a contested transition; Bahrain's demonstrations had been suppressed; Libya had experienced armed conflict and international military intervention; Yemen's president had agreed to transfer authority; and Syria's violence was escalating. Historians should distinguish a resignation, negotiated transfer, constitutional referendum, battlefield victory, and ceasefire rather than describe each as a successful or failed revolution. These mechanisms shaped the prospects for accountability, security, and legitimacy after the dramatic first protests.

Division II — Political Transitions, Counterrevolution, and the Limits of Reform, 2011–2020
Section 71.5

Tunisia’s Democratic Transition

Tunisia's transition required political groups that had once opposed the same ruler to agree on new rules for competition. The October 2011 election created a National Constituent Assembly in which Ennahda was the largest party but did not possess unlimited authority. A governing coalition with the Congress for the Republic and Ettakatol attempted to bridge ideological divisions. Disputes concerned executive power, parliamentary oversight, judicial independence, religion, citizenship, women's rights, and the status of former regime figures. The revolution had enabled a plural political sphere; it had not settled the content of constitutional order.

The constitution-making process combined parliamentary negotiations with sustained civic pressure. Labor organizations, employers, human-rights groups, lawyers, and women's organizations debated provisions and challenged elected leaders. Political assassinations in 2013, including those of Chokri Belaid and Mohamed Brahmi, intensified polarization and fears of violence. Opposition organizations questioned the government's ability to protect public life; government supporters emphasized the legitimacy of the election. A crisis that could have deepened institutional breakdown instead moved through a negotiated framework, though not without risks and substantial mistrust.

The constitutional settlement's success depended on actors willing to accept compromises that disappointed some of their supporters. Ennahda had to work with secular parties and civic organizations, while other parties had to acknowledge the results of elections they did not always welcome. The Quartet's mediation mattered because participants could disagree over political legitimacy yet still agree on a procedure for continued negotiation. Civic influence should not be romanticized as complete consensus: bargaining reflected fears about insecurity, economic crisis, and the fate of competing groups.

Tunisia's experience also reveals the difference between formal rights and state capacity. A constitution can protect expression but cannot alone ensure that courts work, schools remain funded, or regional employment gaps narrow. The absence of the envisaged Constitutional Court during the period meant that disputes between branches of government lacked one important arbiter. Students should compare the 2014 constitutional provisions with subsequent governmental practice and regional economic indicators rather than treat either adoption of the document or continued grievances as a sufficient final verdict.

The National Dialogue Quartet brought together the UGTT, the employers' organization UTICA, the Tunisian Human Rights League, and the Tunisian Order of Lawyers. Their mediation offered an agreed roadmap for institutional change, enabling negotiations across political divisions. This was not the disappearance of ideological disagreement; it was a procedure for managing it. The Constituent Assembly adopted the new constitution on 26 January 2014 and the document was promulgated on 27 January. Its provisions expressed ambitions concerning popular sovereignty, rights, and separated governmental powers. Historians must distinguish those commitments from their enforcement through courts, administrative practice, and political bargaining.

Elections in 2014 produced a change of political leadership and confirmed that parties could lose office without a coup. Beji Caid Essebsi became president; Ennahda participated in subsequent political arrangements. Still, important checks were underdeveloped, including a Constitutional Court that was envisaged but not established during the period. Governments confronted security attacks, debt, economic inequality, demands for wages, and continuing protests in neglected regions. The interior towns whose mobilization had helped bring down Ben Ali did not automatically receive reliable jobs or infrastructure. Freedom to organize could grow while the material conditions that had driven protest remained insecure.

By December 2020 Tunisia offered an important case of negotiated transition but not a guarantee of permanent democratic consolidation. The role of civic mediation, competitive elections, and constitutional compromise was distinctive. So too were economic pressures, institutional gaps, and arguments about the powers of the presidency and parliament. Neither later setbacks nor earlier hopes should be projected backward to portray the transition as predetermined. It is historically more useful to ask how particular actors maintained peaceful competition through several crises and why the resulting institutions remained vulnerable to economic and political strain.

Section 71.6

Egypt’s Contested Transition and Restored Military Rule

The Supreme Council of the Armed Forces governed Egypt after Mubarak's resignation and issued a Constitutional Declaration on 30 March 2011. It claimed a transitional role while maintaining military autonomy and substantial governing power. Revolutionary activists protested military trials, police practices, and delayed accountability for earlier violence. Political parties disagreed about sequencing elections and constitution-writing. The Muslim Brotherhood, with longstanding organizational networks, possessed advantages over newer movements whose strength had been mass demonstrations rather than electoral administration. The dispute concerned the distribution of authority as much as a simple contrast between Islamist and secular ideas.

Mohamed Morsi won the presidential election in June 2012, after parliamentary contests and legal struggles over the status of the legislature. The judiciary, military, presidency, media, and political opposition advanced conflicting constitutional interpretations. Morsi removed senior military figures in August but continued to encounter institutions reluctant to accept his authority. His November 2012 constitutional declaration shielded certain presidential acts from judicial review; supporters argued that it protected the transition, while opponents regarded it as executive overreach. A new constitution passed a December 2012 referendum amid controversy. Political polarization combined with economic pressures, insecurity, and fears that future elections alone could not guarantee political inclusion.

An additional institutional conflict concerned the judiciary's position. Court rulings affecting parliament and constitutional arrangements reinforced the perception among some political groups that elected officials could be blocked by unelected institutions. Opponents of Morsi feared the opposite danger: that the presidency would remove checks on its own authority. Because each side believed that losing a constitutional battle could have lasting consequences, short-term compromises became difficult. The military retained coercive resources that civilian parties could not match, and this inequality affected the bargaining environment before and after the July 2013 takeover.

After Rabaa, the state framed public order and counterterrorism as priorities, while families and activists demanded investigations into killings, detentions, and disappearances. Evidence concerning responsibility must distinguish the official statements of ministries, independent testimony, forensic investigation, and conclusions of human-rights organizations. The victims were not an undifferentiated political group; they included people with different degrees of political engagement, and relatives pursued distinct forms of legal recourse. Acknowledging these experiences deepens the account beyond the contest between the president and army leadership.

The Tamarod movement and other opponents organized large demonstrations in June 2013 demanding Morsi's departure. His supporters mobilized in defense of his elected mandate, while other citizens opposed both his administration and army intervention. The existence of substantial popular opposition did not confer on military officers an ordinary constitutional authority to choose the president. On 3 July 2013 the army removed Morsi, suspended the constitution, and installed a new interim administration. Abdel Fattah el-Sisi emerged as the central military decision-maker. The political dispute over whether the events represented a popular correction or military seizure of power remains historically significant, but the institutional mechanism of removal was unmistakable.

Supporters of Morsi assembled sit-ins at Rabaa al-Adawiya and al-Nahda in Cairo. On 14 August security forces violently dispersed them. Human Rights Watch's investigation identified at least 817 people killed at Rabaa alone and considered a figure above 1,000 likely; Egyptian official counts differed. HRW assessed the pattern of killing as likely crimes against humanity. That conclusion must be attributed as an investigative finding, not presented as a judicial judgment. Reports of some armed acts among protesters and deaths of police officers do not negate the need to analyze the scale, planning, and proportionality of state force. Documentation came from witnesses, video, medical records, and investigation of events across several sites.

The subsequent state imposed extensive restrictions on the Muslim Brotherhood and other opposition, detained critics, and reasserted the military's central position. Sisi became president in 2014. Government narratives emphasized stability, large projects, and restored state authority; human-rights organizations described serious limitations on civil liberties and political competition. The outcome was not simply the return of every pre-2011 arrangement, but coercive institutions remained powerful. The Egyptian case reveals why electoral victory, public demonstrations, military intervention, and constitutional legitimacy cannot be collapsed into one measure. It also illustrates the costs when rival institutions cannot negotiate enforceable rules for sharing political power.

Section 71.7

Bahrain and the Gulf: Protest, Sectarian Narratives, and Security Intervention

Bahrain's protests began in February 2011 within a monarchy with elected and appointed political institutions, a history of opposition activism, and disputes over representation and equal citizenship. Crowds gathered at Pearl Roundabout in Manama. Some participants sought a constitutional monarchy with an accountable elected government; others advocated deeper political change. Although many demonstrators came from Shi'a communities, the movement included diverse perspectives and cannot be explained as a uniformly sectarian challenge. Government supporters also mobilized, citing concerns about instability, political exclusion, and regional threats. Understanding their fears does not establish that opposition activists were agents of a foreign state.

The authorities used force during operations against demonstrators, and deaths and injuries broadened anger. Political negotiations became harder as protesters demanded guarantees and the government argued that public order was under threat. On 14 March Saudi National Guard personnel entered Bahrain as part of a GCC force, followed by units from other Gulf states. The arrival of outside forces must be distinguished from the conduct of Bahraini police and security agencies during the crackdown that followed. Bahrain declared a state of national safety, dispersed the protest encampment, detained activists, and later demolished the Pearl Roundabout monument.

The Bahraini government's stated concern about foreign interference should be examined as a claim requiring evidence. The Independent Commission of Inquiry considered allegations about Iranian responsibility and the actions of domestic political organizations; broad sectarian accusations cannot substitute for its actual findings. Likewise, the existence of protesters calling for radical changes does not mean that all demonstrations had one program. Some opposition actors pursued parliamentary reform, others sought accountability for arrests, and still others questioned the legitimacy of the ruling system more fundamentally.

The afterlife of the commission's report is itself a historical topic. It provided an extensive public record and proposed reforms of detention, law enforcement, and accountability. Governments could cite recommendations as evidence of responsiveness, while activists could compare the recommendations with persistent restrictions. Students should ask what administrative data, court cases, and independent monitoring could establish whether reforms occurred. The institution publishing a report and the institutions carrying out its proposed reforms do not automatically have the same interests.

The Bahrain Independent Commission of Inquiry, chaired by M. Cherif Bassiouni, investigated events and published its report in November 2011. It documented excessive force, arbitrary arrests, mistreatment, torture, and failures of accountability. Its findings also examined the presence of GCC personnel, government responses, and allegations of foreign interference. A commissioned inquiry can gather records and testimony, but it cannot by itself compel prosecutions or reconciliation. Historians should compare its recommendations with subsequent evidence of implementation and distinguish documented incidents from claims advanced by either political side.

Regional relationships influenced the monarchy's survival. Bahrain's security cooperation with Saudi Arabia, Gulf neighbors, and the United States shaped government calculations. Yet external security relations did not create the domestic demands for representation or equal treatment. Nor did religious affiliation determine citizens' politics: some Sunni participants opposed government conduct, and Shi'a citizens expressed different strategies and priorities. Sectarian language could polarize conflict by turning political grievances into claims of collective threat, while erasing the individual positions of organizers, journalists, doctors, and families affected by repression.

By 2020 major opposition organizations had been restricted or dissolved, and independent political space remained narrow. Governmental stability and the absence of sustained mass demonstrations did not demonstrate that original grievances had disappeared. Bahrain is an example of monarchical survival aided by domestic coercive capacity and regional security support, but it is not a universal model for all Gulf societies. Its history requires separate attention to protest organization, state violence, external intervention, investigative evidence, and the long struggle over political institutions.

Section 71.8

Morocco, Jordan, Oman, and Reform without Regime Collapse

Morocco's February 20 movement brought together political and civic currents calling for reform, anti-corruption measures, and social justice. King Muhammad VI responded with a constitutional process and a referendum in July 2011. The new constitution introduced changes in governmental and parliamentary responsibilities and recognized cultural rights, while the monarchy retained substantial influence over security, religion, and major appointments. Elections later in 2011 brought the Justice and Development Party into coalition government. These were meaningful institutional events, yet did not constitute unrestricted parliamentary sovereignty. Activists disagreed over whether the reforms represented progress or preserved the decisive power of the palace.

In Jordan demonstrations targeted unemployment, corruption, and the concentration of executive authority. King Abdullah II changed governments and supported constitutional and electoral amendments without ending the central role of the monarchy. Professional associations, political parties, tribal constituencies, and activists of different backgrounds demanded different reforms. Some wanted a government selected by a parliamentary majority, others concentrated on services or public spending. The monarchy sought to accommodate selected demands while retaining control of core political decisions. A smaller number of major protest episodes than in Tunisia does not establish the absence of public dissatisfaction.

The Moroccan referendum of 2011 illustrates a change that was real in text but limited in the distribution of ultimate authority. The king continued to possess decisive constitutional and informal powers, while elected politicians could exercise some increased responsibilities. Jordan's relationship between parliament and the crown differed, as did the position of Oman's consultative institutions. These are not minor constitutional details; they determined which officials could control appointments, security agencies, and legislation after demonstrations subsided.

Another variation lay in citizenship and demography. Gulf states with large noncitizen workforces faced social and labor issues that were not represented by the political rights of citizens alone. Some economic concessions benefited nationals while migrant workers remained subject to restrictive arrangements. Historians should distinguish citizens' demands for representative government from broader questions about labor rights, residency, and migration rather than assume that a monarchy's fiscal capacity resolved every social tension.

Oman experienced demonstrations in Sohar and other places seeking jobs, improved administration, and accountability. Sultan Qaboos announced employment measures, cabinet changes, and selected changes to consultative institutions, while arrests and restrictions showed the limits of permitted mobilization. Other Gulf monarchies differed: Kuwait's parliamentary system gave opposition a distinctive arena; Saudi Arabia used distribution and policing in its own political environment; Qatar and the UAE had different economic, demographic, and security structures. Treating all monarchies as identical would conceal the very national differences needed to explain survival.

Oil and gas resources allowed some governments to offer wages, housing, or employment programs that poorer states could not easily finance. But resources alone do not explain outcomes: Bahrain saw substantial protest despite its Gulf connections, while institutions and political expectations differed among other monarchies. Ruling families, security forces, foreign allies, and constitutional arrangements all affected the available choices. Reform sometimes altered real procedures while preserving a regime's political core; repression could accompany concessions. Historians should compare what governments announced, what laws actually changed, and what citizens could exercise in practice.

These cases challenge simple categories of successful and unsuccessful revolution. Political stability might be maintained through bargaining, distribution, social legitimacy, and coercion in different combinations. Opposition groups could accept partial concessions as valuable or reject them as insufficient. The degree of protest also reflected organizational restrictions and risks faced by participants. A sound comparative history asks why a particular monarch faced specific demands, how the government responded, and what power remained outside elected institutions at the end of 2020.

Division III — Syria’s Uprising, Civil War, and the Rise of ISIS, 2011–2020
Section 71.9

Syria: From the Daraa Protests to Armed Conflict

Syria's crisis began within a political system shaped by decades of Ba'athist government, security surveillance, and extensive concentration of power under Bashar al-Assad. Selected economic liberalization had favored politically connected businesses, while regional inequality and rural hardship persisted. A severe drought affected parts of the northeast before 2011, but the causal relationship among climate, migration, and protest is debated and must not be reduced to one explanation. More immediate grievances involved official corruption, detention, restrictions on expression, and the treatment of citizens by security agencies.

In Daraa, reports that young people had been detained and mistreated for anti-government slogans triggered protests in March 2011. Security forces used lethal force and demonstrations expanded through funerals, solidarity gatherings, and local networks. Protests spread to Homs, Hama, Damascus suburbs, and elsewhere. Early calls often concerned reform and accountability, but demands for the removal of the government grew amid repeated killings and arrests. Local Coordination Committees and other activist networks organized marches and documented repression. They should not be conflated with armed groups that later operated in the same communities.

Documentary evidence from Syria reflects the dangers of working under repression. Local activists documented protests and arrests but could rarely preserve complete official records, while governmental statements framed the crisis in national-security language. Video images might show violence without establishing the precise identity of every perpetrator. Later witnesses could be unable to speak safely. The proper method is corroboration across testimony, geography, dates, hospital records, and independent inquiries rather than uncritical acceptance or dismissal of one side's public statements.

The erosion of trust also became self-reinforcing. People who feared detention or retaliation might prefer armed local protection, but the formation of militias could bring additional fighting and make a negotiated settlement harder. Civilians who wanted reforms could become vulnerable to accusations of collaboration from multiple sides. This development is not adequately explained by a claim that Syria was inherently divided. It was a political process involving coercive decisions, organizational fragmentation, fear, and external opportunities that altered the incentives available to communities over time.

Military defections and local armed formations became more visible during 2011–2012. Groups identifying with the Free Syrian Army did not form one unified organization with consistent command, strategy, or political objectives. Some civilians opposed militarization; others came to believe that armed defense was necessary after government violence. The government used the emergence of armed opposition to frame dissent more broadly as a security conspiracy. Siege tactics, shelling, and fighting in residential areas intensified. Different communities feared retaliation and were divided about which side could protect them, further reducing possibilities for compromise.

Sectarian identities influenced some relationships but did not determine every individual's position. The government included officials from diverse communities despite the prominence of Alawite networks in parts of the security system. Sunni Syrians also supported the government; the opposition included members of different religious and political traditions. Kurdish movements had their own history of discrimination and political aspirations, separate from the priorities of Arab opposition factions. Treating the war as an inevitable struggle among religious groups erases political decisions, geography, regional organization, and economic interests.

The transformation from public protest to war occurred through interacting processes: repression, failed negotiations, defections, competing militias, outside funding and arms, and increasing distrust. An event in Daraa in March 2011 did not mechanically cause the military geography of 2015. Historians must identify the choices that accelerated violence and the moments when alternative responses became less likely. This analysis helps preserve the distinction between the initial protest movement and the armed organizations that subsequently claimed to fight on behalf of Syrians.

Section 71.10

The Fragmentation of Syria

By 2012 Syria had become a struggle over territorial control as well as national political authority. The government retained major institutions and armed forces, while varied opposition groups contested Aleppo, Homs, Damascus suburbs, and provincial towns. Armed organizations differed in ideology, leadership, local ties, access to foreign assistance, and relationships with civilian communities. Some local councils tried to administer education, sanitation, and food distribution; elsewhere armed commanders controlled trade and courts. Dated territorial maps are useful, but control of a district must not be interpreted as proof that every civilian endorsed the ruling armed group.

Islamist armed factions emerged alongside groups claiming nationalist or democratic aims. Jabhat al-Nusra had ties to al-Qaeda, while ISIS developed a separate territorial project from organizational roots in Iraq. Kurdish-led forces built forms of administration in parts of northern and northeastern Syria, with goals shaped by long histories of exclusion and by the changing regional security environment. Their relationships with local Arab populations and with Turkey were complex. An account dividing the country simply between the Assad government and 'the rebels' erases these political and military differences.

Territorial fragmentation affected the economy of war. Checkpoints could become sources of taxation and smuggling revenues, while armed groups competed for fuel, grain routes, border crossings, and aid distribution. Civilian local councils sought administrative legitimacy but frequently lacked resources or freedom from commanders. International humanitarian organizations faced negotiations with several authorities whose interests conflicted. This helps explain why political fragmentation and humanitarian access could not be solved simply by shifting the battlefield between two national forces.

The 2016 battle for eastern Aleppo demonstrates the distinction between territorial victory and political resolution. The recapture altered strategic control, but displaced residents faced destroyed housing, uncertain documents, and risks associated with detention or perceived political allegiance. By 2020 similar dilemmas confronted people from Ghouta, Idlib, and other regions. Security Council resolutions and negotiating tracks provided possible frameworks, but negotiations over detainees, constitutional authority, and safe return remained difficult in the absence of trust and unified enforcement.

The involvement of outside powers altered the balance of war. Iran assisted the Syrian government, and Hezbollah fighters entered important campaigns, including around Qusayr in 2013. Russia began direct air operations in September 2015 and assisted the government's recovery of territory. Turkey supported selected opposition groups and carried out cross-border operations linked to security disputes over ISIS and Kurdish forces. The United States organized anti-ISIS operations with partners including the Syrian Democratic Forces. These activities pursued distinct objectives: fighting ISIS did not automatically promote one agreed political settlement between the government and opposition.

The recapture of eastern Aleppo in 2016 and subsequent government campaigns around other districts changed military control but produced displacement, destruction, and questions about detention and return. Idlib became a destination for displaced people and armed groups evacuated from elsewhere. Humanitarian access depended on checkpoints, border crossings, security guarantees, and political decisions. Diplomatic processes included the Geneva Communiqué, Security Council Resolution 2254 of December 2015, and later negotiations involving Russia, Turkey, and Iran. Resolution 2254 proposed Syrian-led political negotiations and related constitutional and electoral steps, yet its commitments did not yield a comprehensive agreement by 2020.

The survival of rival armed formations and foreign military presences complicated sovereignty. Winning a battle did not settle who could return home, obtain documents, reclaim property, or avoid arbitrary detention. The Syrian government held major population centers by 2020, while other forces controlled areas in the northwest and northeast. Such fragmentation cannot be adequately represented by one political label or a static map. The war must be understood through institutions, armed networks, local governance, external intervention, and the experiences of civilians seeking daily security.

Historical Vocabulary

Refugees, internally displaced persons, and territorial control

An internally displaced person has been forced to flee home but remains within national borders. A refugee is outside the country of nationality or former habitual residence and qualifies for international protection under applicable legal definitions. People may move between categories as they cross borders or return.

A map of territory controlled by an armed organization is not a map of the population’s consent, religious identity, or legal allegiance. Humanitarian agencies count refugees and internally displaced persons using different registration systems, periods, and definitions; the two figures should not be added without checking methodology.

Section 71.11

ISIS: Territorial Rule and Mass Atrocities

ISIS developed from organizations active during Iraq's post-2003 insurgency rather than emerging directly from the Syrian protest movement. Its predecessor groups, including al-Qaeda in Iraq and the Islamic State of Iraq, exploited sectarian conflict, state weakness, and prisons and insurgent networks. The Syrian civil war opened new territory and sources of recruits. Under Abu Bakr al-Baghdadi the organization expanded across the Iraq–Syria border and battled other armed groups. It is inaccurate to equate ISIS with Iraqi Sunni communities, the original Syrian opposition, or Islamist political movements generally. Many Sunni Muslims suffered from its coercive rule and violence.

In June 2014 ISIS seized Mosul after Iraqi security forces collapsed, then proclaimed a so-called caliphate claiming authority over territory in Iraq and Syria. Raqqa was a principal center of its rule on the Syrian side. The organization imposed taxes and confiscations, operated coercive courts and police, regulated schools, and distributed propaganda. It combined armed administration with severe punishment and intimidation. Its videos were political instruments intended to terrorize enemies and recruit followers; they must be assessed alongside independent investigations, captured records, and eyewitness accounts. The extent of its control and cooperation from local populations differed from one place to another.

ISIS's expansion should be examined through political opportunity as well as ideology. The collapse of particular Iraqi security formations, grievances against Iraqi political authority, Syrian territorial fragmentation, and access to resources enabled an extremist organization to seize cities it could not have controlled through propaganda alone. The organization's recruitment and administrative practice varied across locations and involved intimidation as well as coercive recruitment. It is therefore historically inaccurate to treat the population under ISIS control as a bloc of voluntary followers.

The inquiry into Yazidi genocide illustrates the importance of legal categories. Mass killing, separation of children, sexual enslavement, attempts to prevent births, and destruction of communal identity were examined as evidence of intent to destroy a protected religious group. Survivors' accounts of captivity require careful, respectful handling; they are not material for invented dialogue or graphic spectacle. Historians must also recognize the limits of a Syria-focused investigative mandate when discussing atrocities that began in Iraqi territory and continued across borders.

ISIS attacked civilians identified as religiously or politically unacceptable, including Shi'a Muslims, Sunnis who opposed it, Christians, Yazidis, tribal opponents, and others. In August 2014 its assault on Sinjar targeted the Yazidi religious community through mass killing, forced displacement, enslavement, sexual violence, forced conversion, and separation of families. The UN Independent International Commission of Inquiry on Syria concluded in its June 2016 report They Came to Destroy that ISIS committed genocide against Yazidis, along with crimes against humanity and war crimes. The report focused especially on Yazidis held in Syria and used survivor interviews and corroborating documents. The specific intent to destroy a protected group is central to genocide, not merely the number of deaths.

Campaigns against ISIS involved Iraqi forces, Kurdish organizations, Syrian Democratic Forces, local armed groups, and the international coalition led by the United States. Mosul was retaken during destructive fighting in 2017 and the SDF captured Raqqa in October that year. Airstrikes, siege conditions, mines, and urban combat damaged housing and services; civilians suffered from violence by ISIS and also from military operations intended to remove it. Historical assessment requires incident-specific attribution. The fall of urban strongholds did not erase missing persons, detention problems, destroyed schools, or doubts surrounding residents accused of association with ISIS.

The loss of Baghuz in March 2019 ended ISIS's final substantial territorial enclave. That achievement was not the elimination of clandestine networks or its ideological influence. Refugees, displaced families, children, alleged militants, and foreign nationals were held or lived in camps and detention settings with difficult legal and humanitarian questions. Yazidi survivors sought family reunification, identification of missing relatives, and accountability. A serious history therefore links military chronology to social and legal consequences and treats the people who lived under ISIS as individuals rather than assigning collective guilt.

Section 71.12

Syria’s Humanitarian Catastrophe

The Syrian war caused massive displacement within the country and across borders into Turkey, Lebanon, Jordan, Iraq, and beyond. Families fled bombardment, detention, siege, conscription, persecution, and economic collapse, often moving several times. Some lacked resources to cross a border and remained internally displaced; others entered neighboring countries under very different legal and employment arrangements. Loss of identity papers, school records, and housing documents later complicated residence and property claims. The distinction between refugee and internally displaced person is therefore both legal and practical, affecting protection and humanitarian assistance.

UNHCR's assessment of the end of 2020 described roughly 13.5 million Syrians forcibly displaced, including more than 6.7 million internally displaced. These are dated and method-dependent figures rather than exact totals of everyone harmed by war. They do not measure deaths, detention, missing persons, or all migration associated with economic collapse. Casualty estimates vary because investigators differ in the records available, whether combatants are included, and whether unverified reports are counted. Serious teaching should retain this uncertainty while explaining the extraordinary scale of disruption.

Humanitarian documentation can reveal the geography of displacement beyond national front lines. A family moving from Aleppo to Idlib is internally displaced; if it then enters Turkey it may become a refugee or asylum-seeker depending on legal circumstances. Repeated movements may be counted differently across reporting systems. UNHCR figures for the end of 2020 should not be casually added to annual estimates from earlier years, nor should displaced populations be confused with casualties. These distinctions are particularly important when evaluating the scale of the Syrian catastrophe.

The destruction of civilian infrastructure has consequences that survive battlefield change. Interruptions to routine vaccination, schooling, prenatal care, water treatment, and property registration can shape lives for years after an offensive. Families whose relatives disappeared into detention face uncertainty that cannot be captured by a reconstruction budget. Claims about particular attacks should draw on reports identifying the location, method of investigation, and degree of confidence in attribution. Such work is essential for historical responsibility as well as for legal accountability.

Government and allied forces carried out major documented patterns of unlawful bombardment, detention, torture, and siege operations. ISIS and other armed groups also committed grave abuses, including attacks on civilian communities and coercive detention. Schools, clinics, water systems, and hospitals became insecure; aid workers and medical staff faced exceptional risks. It is not sufficient to describe all this as symmetrical suffering without assessing the capabilities and documented responsibility of the actors involved. Satellite imagery, witness accounts, medical documentation, administrative records, and international inquiries offer partial but complementary evidence.

The Ghouta chemical attack of 21 August 2013 prompted international investigation and negotiation. A UN mission confirmed sarin use, but its original mandate did not include identifying the perpetrator. Syria acceded to the Chemical Weapons Convention, and UN Security Council Resolution 2118 of 27 September 2013 endorsed an OPCW framework for destruction and inspection of declared Syrian chemical-weapons capabilities. That did not resolve whether declarations were complete or establish responsibility for every alleged later incident. Detection of chemical agents, attribution to a military organization, and criminal responsibility for an individual order are separate evidentiary questions that later investigations approached using different mandates.

By December 2020 the government held many principal cities, while conflict and divided authority continued elsewhere. Military advances did not remove the obstacles to safe return: destroyed homes, insecure property rights, missing persons, detention fears, poverty, damaged public services, and foreign military presences. Refugees also affected the economies and politics of host countries. The humanitarian experience of Syrians cannot be summarized by battle maps or a single death estimate. Family networks, teachers, doctors, local councils, and aid organizations worked under difficult conditions to sustain civilian life, while the absence of an inclusive political settlement limited long-term recovery.

Section 71.13

Libya’s 2011 Uprising and the Fall of Gaddafi

Libya under Muammar Gaddafi had an unusual political structure of proclaimed popular committees and congresses, but effective authority remained concentrated in personalized leadership, security units, and patronage networks. Oil wealth supported public services as well as political loyalty. Regional and local relationships shaped the armed forces and administration. The February 2011 protests, particularly in Benghazi and other eastern cities, quickly encountered lethal security responses. Some soldiers and officials defected; opposition activists and armed groups formed local organizations, and the National Transitional Council claimed to represent the uprising. The government and opposition competed for towns, infrastructure, and control of military resources.

International reactions developed in two distinct Security Council stages. Resolution 1970, adopted on 26 February 2011, demanded an end to violence, imposed an arms embargo and targeted measures, and referred the Libya situation to the International Criminal Court. It did not authorize a no-fly zone or broad armed civilian-protection measures. Resolution 1973, adopted on 17 March, authorized notified states to employ necessary measures to protect civilians and civilian-populated areas threatened by attack, established a no-fly zone, and excluded a foreign occupation force. This precise legal difference is essential to any analysis of the intervention's legitimacy or implementation.

Libyan opposition politics in 2011 reflected regional and local interests as well as demands for an end to Gaddafi's rule. Some armed units defected from the government; others remained loyal, and civilian leaders tried to represent revolutionary coalitions with different expectations. International protection altered their calculations. Although the National Transitional Council developed diplomatic relationships with outside governments, that recognition did not establish one unified command over the opposition's armed formations. The distinction became crucial during the attempt to create postwar institutions.

A documentary comparison can separate three moments: the Security Council's initial sanctions and ICC referral under Resolution 1970, the authorization of civilian-protection operations under Resolution 1973, and the subsequent military campaign under NATO command. Each had different participants and legal implications. Readers should assess contemporary evidence of threats to civilians and examine particular operational conduct without treating either the fall of Gaddafi or subsequent state fragmentation as a complete explanation of what was authorized in March.

International air operations began in March, with NATO subsequently assuming command. They altered the military balance and supported the conditions under which opposition forces advanced. Supporters of intervention pointed to threats to civilians and the mandate to prevent attacks; critics argued that operations became inseparable from pursuing regime change and neglected the dangers of postwar institutional collapse. The legal scope of Resolution 1973, the conduct of particular strikes, and the longer consequences of intervention are related but analytically separate questions. A later unfavorable outcome does not alone prove every earlier strike was unnecessary; conversely, a humanitarian justification does not immunize military practice from investigation.

Fighting continued through summer 2011, including severe conflict in Misrata and around other communities. Opposition forces entered Tripoli in August; Gaddafi was captured and killed near Sirte in October. His death ended a highly personalized system but did not answer who would control military arsenals, police, oil income, detention facilities, or major towns. Foreign actors including Qatar provided support to selected opposition forces, while local formations developed their own claims to revolutionary legitimacy. Armed groups that could defeat an opponent were not automatically prepared to dissolve into accountable national institutions.

Libya therefore illustrates how revolutionary victory, international authorization, and postwar administration pose different historical problems. Victims of violence needed protection, but the country's future also depended on the difficult creation of a secure legal order. Former officials, revolutionary brigades, urban communities, tribal groups, and regional leaders held conflicting visions and resources. Students must avoid assigning one universal motivation to the uprising, the intervention, or the subsequent violence. The next section examines how the inability to unify armed authority contributed to years of conflict after Gaddafi's removal.

Section 71.14

Libya after 2011: Militias, Rival Governments, and Internationalized War

After Gaddafi's fall, Libya sought to convert military victory into a national political order. The National Transitional Council administered an initial period, and elections for a General National Congress took place in July 2012. Yet revolutionary armed formations remained powerful and often controlled checkpoints, detention facilities, and security in particular towns. Some groups received state salaries or were nominally incorporated into public structures without submitting to unified command. Politicians and commanders disagreed over the exclusion of former regime officials, allocation of oil revenues, and authority over local administration. Electoral legitimacy alone could not compensate for the absence of common coercive institutions.

The crisis deepened in 2014 with disputes surrounding elections, institutions, and armed alliances. Rival governments and parliaments claimed authority; Khalifa Haftar and forces later associated with the Libyan National Army gained prominence, while other armed coalitions operated in western Libya. These labels encompassed shifting local and political alliances rather than two uniform camps. The UN-mediated Libyan Political Agreement signed at Skhirat in 2015 provided for a Government of National Accord. International recognition offered diplomatic status but could not guarantee control over armed forces, oil facilities, banks, and judicial processes in contested territory.

Libya's oil economy created another political problem after 2011. Revenue passed through institutions that competing factions sought to influence, including export facilities and financial authorities. Control of these systems could finance armed power even when a particular group lacked nationwide electoral support. The ability to obtain salaries, ammunition, and local services could reinforce military organizations and undermine attempts to build a unified police service. These dynamics explain why a formal government agreement might coexist with competing commanders able to obstruct its implementation.

The country's position on Mediterranean migration routes also made the civil war international in ways extending beyond foreign military assistance. Migrants and refugees faced detention, exploitation, and unsafe journeys as official and unofficial institutions competed for control. External governments' policies on maritime security and migration intersected with Libyan political conditions. A narrative centered solely on Tripoli's governments or Haftar's offensives misses the lives of people with little legal protection and the incentives of groups profiting from insecurity.

External governments intensified the war through arms, political support, technical assistance, and direct military involvement. Egypt and the UAE supported Haftar-aligned actors in varying ways; Turkey backed the Tripoli-based authorities; Russian-linked military personnel and equipment were reported on the opposing side. Other states pursued their own objectives. Such assistance altered battlefield capabilities and incentives to negotiate. Diplomatic declarations favoring a united Libya coexisted with foreign interventions favoring specific factions. Territorial claims changed repeatedly, so a map must be tied to a particular date and must not imply that every resident consented to the armed organization in control.

Haftar's forces advanced toward Tripoli beginning in April 2019, producing new violence, displacement, and external intervention. The battlefield changed during 2020. On 23 October delegations to the UN-facilitated 5+5 Joint Military Commission signed a countrywide ceasefire agreement in Geneva, including provisions for withdrawal from front lines and departure of foreign forces. The signature did not mean complete implementation, demobilization, or agreement on political authority. It was a meaningful diplomatic opportunity within an unresolved institutional crisis, not proof that the war's deeper problems had already been settled.

For ordinary people, the struggle involved insecure electricity and fuel supplies, detention and abuses by armed groups, displacement, unreliable schools and healthcare, and uncertainty over wages and local administration. Migrants and refugees passing through Libya faced particular dangers, including trafficking and abuse in detention. Civil society groups documented violations but had limited means to enforce accountability. Libya demonstrates how the fragmentation of military authority and control of revenue can survive regime overthrow and electoral events, especially when foreign sponsors support rival claimants to power.

Section 71.15

Yemen’s 2011 Uprising and the Failed Transition

Yemen's 2011 uprising occurred within a republic where Ali Abdullah Saleh had long managed a political coalition of officers, tribal figures, parties, and regional networks. Earlier conflicts in the northern Saada region involving the Houthi movement, southern demands for representation after unification, and severe economic hardship weakened public trust. Demonstrators in Sana'a, Taiz, and other towns included students, women, labor activists, party members, and citizens seeking accountable government. The killing of protesters on 18 March 2011 intensified the crisis and was followed by military defections and greater division of the coercive apparatus. Peaceful activists did not all share the objectives of organized opposition parties or armed commanders.

GCC-sponsored negotiations produced the initiative and implementation mechanism signed in Riyadh on 23 November 2011. The documents provided for a transfer of presidential authority, formation of a unity government, an election, and a national dialogue. Abd Rabbuh Mansur Hadi became president after the February 2012 transitional vote as the sole consensus candidate. The process helped avoid one immediate form of confrontation but did not dismantle military patronage networks or ensure that armed actors accepted unified command. An agreement among powerful elites could create a timetable without resolving the grievances that had brought demonstrators into the streets.

Yemen's negotiated transition was particularly vulnerable because many officers and political elites remained embedded in networks that predated the GCC agreement. The removal of Saleh's formal presidential title did not guarantee that units connected to him would accept authority from Hadi. The coalition government also faced unemployment, subsidies, public debt, and regional distrust. These economic and coercive problems limited the reach of constitutional negotiations. A national dialogue can identify solutions, but it cannot carry them out without administrative capacity and agreements on weapons and revenue.

The political diversity of southern Yemen also matters. Some activists pursued autonomy, others renewed independence, and others sought equitable participation within a unified state. The Houthi movement had its own northern history and grievances, and alliances shifted in response to changing opportunities. Treating all these groups as merely proxies for outside states would erase Yemeni political agency. Conversely, ignoring the subsequent role of foreign arms and support would obscure the regionalization of the war. Both domestic formation and international relationships must be considered over time.

The National Dialogue Conference of 2013–2014 considered constitutional reform, federalism, southern demands, the Saada conflict, and transitional justice. Women, youth, and other representatives participated, yet the proposed arrangements remained contested. Some southern activists rejected the framework; the Houthi movement and other groups opposed elements of political and territorial reorganization. Poor state resources and disputes over subsidies intensified public frustration. Dialogue was a significant political process, but its conclusions required enforcement and agreement by people who retained independent armed capabilities.

The Houthi movement, also called Ansar Allah, had distinctive northern religious and regional roots and a history of fighting Saleh's government. It cannot be reduced to a creation of Iran, although regional ties became increasingly important later. Houthi forces developed an alliance with elements loyal to Saleh and entered Sana'a in September 2014. The relationship was strategically consequential precisely because it joined former enemies for particular political objectives. The movement did not represent every Zaydi believer or every northern Yemeni. Divisions among national and regional actors deepened as the transitional presidency lost leverage.

By early 2015 constitutional negotiations had failed to create a state capable of imposing legitimate authority or integrating armed groups. Saleh's departure from the presidency had not removed the networks and units that remained loyal to him. Economic pressures, unresolved federal arrangements, the existence of regional movements, and the ambitions of competing commanders interacted. Yemen's failure of transition reveals the distinction between an agreed timetable for political office and a negotiated monopoly over coercive force and public resources. The consequence was not predetermined in 2011, but it developed from choices and institutions that the agreement had not resolved.

Section 71.16

Yemen’s Internationalized War and Humanitarian Emergency

The political breakdown intensified in early 2015 as Houthi forces and their allies advanced southward and President Hadi left Sana'a, later departing the country. On 26 March a Saudi-led coalition began military operations in support of the internationally recognized government. The conflict did not involve two uniform sides. Houthi forces, Hadi-aligned units, southern movements, forces tied to former president Saleh, local militias, and Al-Qaeda in the Arabian Peninsula pursued distinct objectives. The coalition's member states also differed in political and military priorities, and relationships among Yemeni partners changed during the war.

Yemen's geography mattered to combat and to civilian survival. Sana'a and the northern highlands, Aden and southern ports, Marib's energy and access routes, Hodeidah's import facilities, the Saudi border, and Bab el-Mandeb's connection to international shipping each had strategic significance. Mountain roads and sparse infrastructure complicated the movement of goods and people. A territorial map dated 2015 would not accurately depict the distribution of authority in 2020. Control of a port could influence food supply far beyond the immediate battlefield, while communities in contested areas faced shifting restrictions and armed checkpoints.

The humanitarian role of ports makes Yemen's geography especially important. Hodeidah and nearby Red Sea facilities were crucial to imports, so damage to roads, restrictions on shipments, and insecurity could harm households hundreds of kilometers from an active front. The Stockholm consultations of December 2018 sought to reduce fighting around these locations and establish confidence-building arrangements. A local decrease in violence might be meaningful even if a national political settlement remained distant. The success of such agreements should be assessed by actual movement, access, and safety rather than the date of signature alone.

Yemen's health emergency also requires separating direct and indirect harm. A civilian killed in an airstrike is recorded differently from someone who dies after a treatable infection because a hospital has closed. Disease outbreaks may reflect damaged water systems, inability to pay public health workers, and interruptions to supply chains. These mechanisms create responsibilities that cannot be understood through a battle casualty list. Credible estimates require explicit time periods and methods, avoiding the misleading practice of combining totals produced for different purposes.

Coalition airstrikes caused civilian casualties and damaged infrastructure in documented cases. Houthi forces and other armed organizations also carried out unlawful attacks, planted mines, and restricted civilians. Ground fighting destroyed homes and caused displacement. Ports and roads were damaged or restricted; the public payroll deteriorated; inflation and currency instability increased hardship. Water and sanitation failures contributed to severe disease outbreaks, including cholera. These effects are not captured by counting deaths directly resulting from weapons alone. International inquiries and relief agencies provided evidence, but attribution of responsibility requires distinguishing incidents and identifying those controlling particular operations.

The Southern Transitional Council, established in 2017, advanced a southern political agenda that sometimes conflicted with Hadi-aligned forces. Al-Qaeda in the Arabian Peninsula exploited security vacuums but should not be equated with southern separatists or the wider Yemeni population. Saleh's alliance with the Houthis collapsed, and he was killed in December 2017. These changing alignments show why a fixed sectarian description is inadequate. Political loyalty often reflected regional demands, military calculation, access to resources, and changing alliances alongside ideology and foreign support.

Security Council Resolution 2216 of April 2015 addressed the conflict and imposed demands on Houthi actors and those obstructing transition. UN mediation generated consultations in Geneva, Kuwait, and Stockholm. The December 2018 Stockholm Agreement contained measures concerning Hodeidah, prisoner exchange, and Taiz, but implementation was partial. Distinguish direct war deaths, excess mortality resulting from collapsed health and food systems, refugees, internally displaced persons, and people requiring assistance; the categories use different methods and dates. By the end of 2020 Yemen remained fragmented and humanitarian conditions were severe. A ceasefire or port arrangement could mitigate suffering without settling the national political conflict.

Historical Vocabulary

Ceasefire, humanitarian access, and excess mortality

A ceasefire is an agreement to suspend specified hostilities; it does not necessarily settle political disputes. Humanitarian access concerns safe delivery of assistance and the ability of people to obtain necessities. A siege restricts movement and supply to an area, with legal obligations governing civilians and relief.

Direct conflict deaths result from violent events; excess mortality estimates deaths above an expected baseline and may include indirect effects of disease, malnutrition, and health-system collapse. These figures use different methods and must not be conflated.

Section 71.17

Regional Intervention, Counterrevolution, and Arab Power

The uprisings changed regional power relationships because governments feared challenges to domestic stability and saw opportunities to support allies. Saudi Arabia and the United Arab Emirates provided political, financial, and security support to selected regimes and factions, regarding some revolutionary and Islamist movements as threats. Qatar pursued different relationships in diplomacy, finance, and media. Turkey supported various opposition actors and later intervened militarily, while Iran reinforced its ties with the Syrian government and partners and developed increasingly significant relationships linked to Yemen. These policies were not entirely consistent: states adapted to conditions in different countries, and their goals shifted over time.

Different forms of intervention require separate examination. Economic assistance to a government, diplomatic recognition of an opposition council, intelligence sharing, weapons shipments, military training, air operations, and direct ground combat change the balance of power in different ways. In Bahrain foreign troops supported a surviving monarchy; in Egypt regional financial assistance affected the conditions under which military-dominated government consolidated; in Libya outside support strengthened rival armed groupings; and in Syria direct military intervention altered control of territory. These mechanisms should be identified rather than attributed vaguely to outside influence.

A regional power might support a foreign government for strategic reasons even while publicly advocating sovereignty or negotiation. This tension was evident in Libya and Syria, where outside forces and financial assistance influenced the capacity of competing actors to continue fighting. Diplomatic recognition could help a governing council obtain loans or institutional authority, whereas military assistance could give local commanders the power to reject a proposed settlement. The mechanisms were different and should be traced separately when assessing why peace negotiations advanced or stalled.

The roles of media organizations require similar precision. Satellite channels and digital platforms recorded significant abuses and enabled dissident voices, but editorial agendas could be shaped by owners, state policy, and competition for audience attention. Historians should verify widely circulated images against dates and locations and should not infer that the most televised city represents a country's entire population. Information became both evidence and an object of political struggle, especially when military actors sought to control accounts of civilian harm.

Religious and regional labels alone explain only a portion of these relationships. Sunni-majority governments supported opposing sides in particular conflicts; Iran acted from state interests as well as ideological and alliance commitments; and Yemeni and Syrian organizations crossed expected communal boundaries. A Saudi–Iranian rivalry was important in several theaters, but did not invent the original demands for jobs, accountability, and freedom in Sidi Bouzid, Daraa, or Sana'a. Domestic governments, militaries, and opposition organizations retained agency, sometimes soliciting outside support and sometimes resisting it. Both foreign intervention and local choices must therefore remain within the explanation.

The United States supported some regional governments, took part in operations in Libya, and later led the coalition against ISIS, without adopting a single policy toward every uprising. Russia's direct entry into Syria in 2015 strengthened the Assad government's battlefield position, while Russia-linked actors operated elsewhere. European states faced refugee movements, security threats, and disagreements over armed intervention. International institutions mediated settlements or investigated abuses but often lacked means to enforce agreement among armed actors. Diplomacy, sanctions, military force, and humanitarian assistance were related yet distinct tools.

Foreign sponsorship could prolong stalemates by making military resistance feasible, even while negotiations offered opportunities to reduce violence. Regional news outlets and digital platforms also reflected political priorities alongside their contribution to the public record. By December 2020 several wars involved actors receiving outside assistance, and sovereignty was itself a central subject of contention. Yet civilians, local administrations, families, and civic organizations remained consequential participants. The historical problem is to identify who intervened, through which mechanisms, with what goals, and with what demonstrable consequences for people living in the affected states.

Section 71.18

The Renewed Uprisings: Sudan and Algeria, 2018–2019

The renewed mobilization of 2018–2019 challenged the idea that the Arab uprisings had ended after the first revolutionary wave. Sudan and Algeria had different political histories, security institutions, and resources, but protesters demanded the departure of long-serving presidents and questioned the systems supporting them. The earlier decade mattered: activists had observed military takeovers, constitutional transitions, and civil wars. Many emphasized sustained peaceful demonstrations and civic organization even as they recognized that removing a president might leave the coercive institutions intact.

Sudanese protests began in December 2018 amid price increases, shortages, and accumulated dissatisfaction with Omar al-Bashir's government, in power since 1989. Demonstrations in provincial towns spread to Khartoum. The Sudanese Professionals Association and neighborhood resistance committees helped organize participation, and women were prominent among organizers and demonstrators. The military removed Bashir in April 2019, but citizens continued protesting because replacing one ruler with a military council did not constitute a civilian transition. The struggle centered on who would exercise coercive authority, represent protesters, investigate abuses, and define the constitution.

Sudan's 2019 Constitutional Charter was negotiated after a period in which protest activists demonstrated sustained organizational capacity but had limited control over coercive institutions. Its framework divided authority between military and civilian actors in order to create a transitional path. The text should be studied for what it committed institutions to do and what remained outside clear civilian supervision. The protests also demonstrated the importance of neighborhood resistance committees and women's organizing, which cannot be reduced to elite agreements alone.

Algeria's Hirak relied heavily on recurring peaceful demonstrations, often on Fridays, and political language about national dignity and civilian government. Its persistence after Bouteflika's resignation showed that the presidency was only part of the political system under challenge. Protesters disagreed about tactics and the sequence of reforms, while the military retained influence over the transition. Comparing the two movements requires attention to their different constitutional structures and armed institutions rather than assuming that both were simply repeat performances of Tunisia in 2011.

The violent dispersal of demonstrators near military headquarters in Khartoum on 3 June 2019 created a grave crisis. Human-rights investigations documented killings and other abuses; estimates require attribution and care over missing people. Negotiations between the Transitional Military Council and the Forces of Freedom and Change resumed with regional mediation. The Constitutional Charter signed on 17 August established a transitional framework involving a Sovereignty Council and civilian government. Its legal language expressed aspirations for rights, political reform, and an eventual civilian order. The document did not itself place every security institution under effective civilian command, nor did it guarantee that its provisions would be implemented.

In Algeria, the Hirak movement emerged in February 2019 against President Abdelaziz Bouteflika's pursuit of a fifth term. Large, often peaceful weekly demonstrations joined students, workers, families, professionals, and activists. Bouteflika resigned in April, but protests continued against the political order associated with his presidency. Military influence over transition remained significant, and the December 2019 election occurred amid disputes and boycott calls. The Hirak's participants did not necessarily share one constitutional program, but many agreed that changing a presidential figure without deeper accountability was insufficient.

Sudan's negotiations created a formal military–civilian power-sharing charter, while Algeria's protests faced institutional continuity within a different political structure. Neither history can be summarized solely by the date of a ruler's departure. Protest organization, military interests, economic pressures, and negotiations all mattered. These cases demonstrate that demands for dignity and accountable government remained politically effective beyond 2011, but also that the power of security institutions and the difficulty of constructing enforceable transitions continued to shape outcomes at the chapter's 2020 cutoff.

Section 71.19

Lebanon, Iraq, and the New Politics of Protest

Lebanon's October 2019 protests developed from anger at proposed taxation and expanded into criticism of corruption, economic mismanagement, weak services, and confessional political arrangements. Demonstrators assembled in Beirut, Tripoli, and other towns, sometimes crossing familiar communal and partisan divisions. Some demanded early elections, others anti-corruption measures, a nonsectarian government, or improved public services. The movement's breadth challenged the idea that citizens could act only through established communal parties, yet entrenched organizations retained influence over institutions and patronage. Protest unity was substantial in some places without amounting to one national governing program.

The financial crisis made institutional failure immediate to households. Banking restrictions, currency depreciation, unemployment, and losses of purchasing power affected people differently according to access to foreign currency and social support. On 4 August 2020 an enormous explosion at Beirut's port killed and injured many people and devastated surrounding neighborhoods. The catastrophe raised urgent questions about dangerous materials, official negligence, and accountability; it was not caused by the protests themselves but intensified public distrust. Volunteers, medical workers, and neighborhood organizations provided assistance while the country was also confronting COVID-19.

Protest organization in Lebanon and Iraq had important cultural and civic dimensions. Murals, songs, public debates, legal aid, and volunteer medical care helped sustain demonstrations and preserve the memory of those killed or injured. These practices created forms of political participation outside formal parties, but they also exposed organizers to intimidation and surveillance. Not every participant shared the same vision of a replacement government, and movements confronted the challenge of translating civic anger into enforceable rules.

Lebanon's port explosion also made the question of state accountability tangible. The immediate human consequences included death, injury, damaged homes, and strain on already weakened health services. Investigating institutional responsibility required records about the storage and supervision of dangerous materials and the decisions of public officials. In Iraq, identifying who shot or abducted demonstrators was complicated by the coexistence of official forces and powerful armed groups. In both countries, demands for justice depended on investigative institutions that were themselves under political pressure.

Iraq's Tishreen protests began in October 2019, particularly in Baghdad and predominantly Shi'a southern cities. Young demonstrators demanded jobs, services, an end to corruption, political accountability, and limits on the influence of armed factions and outside governments. Protest squares became places for political speeches, murals, medical tents, and civic organizing. These were not merely gatherings directed by established parties; demonstrators often criticized the parties themselves. Women and students participated alongside workers, artists, and professionals. The protests showed that grievances over the post-2003 order were not adequately expressed by conventional sectarian categories.

Violent repression by security institutions and armed groups produced deaths, injuries, intimidation, and disappearances. Individual responsibility was not always easy to establish amid overlapping armed organizations, making independent investigation essential. Political pressure contributed to Prime Minister Adil Abdul-Mahdi's resignation in late 2019, but changes of office did not resolve demands for credible security reform. The government of Mustafa al-Kadhimi, formed in 2020, faced constraints from economic hardship, armed power, and the pandemic. The inability to protect activists remained a central problem for public legitimacy.

The social history of these protests includes families searching for missing relatives, students interrupting classes, informal workers balancing political activity with daily income, and women contesting harassment while organizing aid and testimony. Art, photographs, video, and memorials documented experience but also exposed people to surveillance. Lebanon and Iraq were not the same political system, yet both experienced demands for accountable government beyond the first wave of uprisings. By the end of 2020 many institutional questions remained unresolved, but civic networks and public expectations had been reshaped.

Section 71.20

The Arab Uprisings in Historical Perspective

The decade after December 2010 produced divergent outcomes because the same aspiration for dignity encountered different institutions and strategic choices. Tunisia's president departed and parties negotiated a constitution; Egypt's military remained central and later removed an elected president; Bahrain's monarchy survived with security support; Morocco and Jordan offered reforms within continuing royal authority. Syria, Libya, and Yemen entered prolonged armed conflicts. Sudan and Algeria demonstrated later mass protest, and Lebanon and Iraq showed that demands for accountability extended beyond the original list of 2011 revolts. No single label of success or failure explains all these trajectories.

The organization of coercive power was a crucial variable. Tunisia's military did not sustain Ben Ali's presidency; Egypt's army retained substantial corporate autonomy; Syrian government forces largely remained committed to protecting the state leadership; and the fragmentation of armed institutions in Libya and Yemen helped produce competing territorial authorities. But coercion did not explain everything. Civic organizations, party networks, economic resources, constitutional negotiations, international alliances, and the choices of individual leaders also shaped transitions. A convincing comparison must show how several causes interacted rather than treating one as universally decisive.

Historical comparison must avoid the assumption that an uprising was successful if a ruler departed or failed if a political regime survived. A transition may preserve important inequalities while establishing real rights; a revolution may defeat a dictator while opening a prolonged civil war; a constitutional text may offer legal safeguards that institutions cannot yet enforce. These are different outcomes demanding different measures. Students should compare actors' stated goals with dated evidence of institutional authority, personal security, and citizens' capacity to participate in decisions.

The same care applies to the human costs. People who survived displacement, arbitrary detention, economic collapse, and destroyed public services are part of the political history, not an appendix to military events. Their testimony may contradict claims made by both governments and armed opposition groups. By the end of 2020 no single verdict could adequately characterize the Arab region. The greatest historical value of the decade lies in showing how collective action, coercive institutions, political bargains, and regional intervention interacted to create widely divergent futures.

Broad revolutionary coalitions could agree on removing a leader while disagreeing about religion, elections, social policy, justice, and the institutions needed to protect dissent. An election did not by itself restrain security services; a constitution did not automatically create an independent judiciary; a peace agreement required parties capable of enforcing commitments. Tunisia's civic mediation and Yemen's fragile elite transition illustrate different approaches to bargaining. Egypt illustrates the consequences when political authority becomes entangled with an autonomous military. These distinctions show why forming a new government was often harder than generating mass protest.

Regional and international actors altered the opportunities available to local groups. Assistance, recognition, sanctions, airstrikes, and foreign troops could prolong warfare or create space for diplomacy. Nonetheless, outside intervention did not erase the agency of domestic politicians, armed groups, activists, and civilians. The human consequences included extensive displacement, disrupted education, medical shortages, detention, and atrocities, including ISIS's genocide against Yazidis. The experiences of teachers, families, local councils, journalists, and humanitarian volunteers are integral to a history that might otherwise consist only of rulers and battle maps.

By December 2020 political expectations had changed, while authoritarian practices, economic grievances, and disputed sovereignty continued in varied forms. Some states were more institutionally open, others more repressive, and others deeply fragmented. Historical outcomes were contingent, shaped by decisions made under pressure and by structures inherited from previous decades. Chapter 72 will examine broader twenty-first-century developments without treating them as predetermined. Chapter 71 concludes that popular mobilization can transform political possibilities without dictating one institutional future for the region as a whole.

Chapter Summary

The uprisings of 2010–2011 began in societies with different forms of state power but related grievances about corruption, arbitrary policing, economic insecurity, and restricted political participation. Their timing cannot be derived from those conditions alone: local confrontations, established activist networks, organizational opportunities, and changing public expectations helped turn discontent into mass mobilization. Tunisia’s experience demonstrated the possibility of removing a long-serving ruler, but it did not provide a universal model of political change.

Tunisia’s president Ben Ali departed in January 2011, after protests spreading from marginalized interior regions to the capital. Labor organizations, lawyers, students, families, and local networks played substantial roles. The refusal of the military to secure the presidency helped create space for a transition, but a new constitution and political order still had to be negotiated. The 2014 constitutional settlement represented a significant institutional achievement that did not eliminate economic or political strains.

Egyptian mobilization forced Mubarak’s departure in February 2011 while preserving substantial military authority. Constitutional disputes, elections, polarization, and competing claims of legitimacy characterized the transition. Mass demonstrations against Morsi preceded his removal by the military in July 2013; the August dispersal of pro-Morsi sit-ins involved mass killing by security forces. The distinction between popular protest and military seizure of power is indispensable to understanding Egypt’s counterrevolution.

The Gulf and North African monarchies took varied courses. Bahrain’s protests were suppressed with domestic security operations and regional GCC support, and an investigative commission documented serious abuses. Morocco, Jordan, and Oman pursued combinations of concessions, constitutional or administrative reform, and restrictions on dissent. Their differing outcomes challenge claims that all monarchies behaved alike or that religion or oil revenue alone explains survival.

In Syria, peaceful demands for change met severe repression and developed into an increasingly fragmented armed conflict. Opposition organizations and Kurdish-led movements pursued distinct goals; the Syrian government drew on domestic and foreign allies; Russian, Iranian, Turkish, and American interventions altered military and political balances. No comprehensive settlement emerged by December 2020, despite extensive international negotiation and shifting battlefield control.

ISIS expanded from a history rooted in Iraq’s insurgency into Syria’s conflict, established territorial rule, and committed grave atrocities, including genocide against Yazidis. The 2016 UN Commission of Inquiry documented crimes against Yazidis held in Syria, while separate military campaigns retook Mosul and Raqqa and removed ISIS’s final substantial territorial enclave in 2019. Territorial defeat was not the complete disappearance of militant networks or an end to victims’ claims for justice.

The Syrian war displaced millions, destroyed homes, clinics, schools, and infrastructure, and generated profound disputes about responsibility and accountability. Chemical-weapons investigations require distinctions among the detection of use, inspection of declared stockpiles, attribution of attacks, and criminal responsibility. Displacement and casualty figures must be dated, sourced, and categorized rather than combined into one misleading total.

Libya’s uprising became an armed conflict shaped by international intervention. Security Council Resolution 1970 imposed sanctions and referred Libya to the ICC, while Resolution 1973 separately authorized specified civilian-protection measures and a no-fly zone without a foreign occupation force. The fall of Gaddafi did not produce unified security institutions; rival armed coalitions and foreign sponsors helped sustain conflict until the October 2020 ceasefire opened another uncertain political opportunity.

Yemen’s 2011 negotiated transfer under the GCC Initiative did not dissolve military patronage networks or settle regional political grievances. The Houthi movement’s advance, competing governmental claims, southern political movements, and Saudi-led intervention beginning in 2015 produced an internationalized war. Civilian suffering reflected direct violence alongside collapsing economic, sanitation, health, and administrative systems. UN diplomacy secured partial arrangements without ending the conflict.

The renewed mobilization in Sudan, Algeria, Lebanon, and Iraq during 2018–2020 shows that the demand for accountable government continued beyond the first uprisings. By the end of 2020 the Arab region included constitutional transition, military consolidation, surviving monarchies, fractured states, and unresolved wars. The decade changed political expectations and international alignments, but no single explanation accounts for every national outcome and later history cannot be treated as already predetermined.

Chapter Reflection

The Arab uprisings show how ordinary people can change political possibility by acting together, even when they do not share a detailed blueprint for governing afterward. Their demands for dignity, justice, security, and public accountability linked experiences of everyday institutions to national political structures. For historians, an uprising is not only a date on which a president leaves; it is also a process through which citizens test the limits of authority and reshape collective expectations.

The decade also reveals how difficult it is to rebuild institutions after political rupture. A constitution requires enforcement; an election requires acceptance of rules by losing parties and armed institutions; a peace agreement requires command over forces and resources. Governments, opposition groups, militaries, regional organizations, and external actors each made choices that narrowed or expanded the options available to others. Those choices—not an immutable national or sectarian character—help explain divergent outcomes.

Civilian experience remains essential to the historical record. People protested, organized first aid, taught children during displacement, documented atrocities, cared for family members, crossed borders, and sought missing relatives. Their experiences complicate any account centered solely on rulers or battle maps. Historical sources are strongest when the claims of governments and military actors are tested against independent investigation, archival evidence, and testimony that identifies both suffering and the agency of those affected.

The chapter ends in December 2020 without claiming to know what subsequent events must bring. Political expectations had changed; authoritarian practices, inequality, conflict, and external intervention persisted in different forms. Chapter 72 will examine wider twenty-first-century developments, but the historical problem already stands clearly: movements for political dignity can disrupt entrenched power without predetermining a single outcome for institutions, societies, or the regional order.
