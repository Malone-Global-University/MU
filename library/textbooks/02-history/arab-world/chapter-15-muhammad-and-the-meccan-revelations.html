Part III — Late Antiquity and the Rise of Islam

Muhammad and the Meccan Revelations

A History of the Arab World: From Ancient Crossroads to the Modern Arab World

Chapter Opening

According to the later Muslim tradition, in about the year 610 CE a man of roughly forty, a member of the Banu Hashim branch of Quraysh, made a habit of withdrawing for periods of retreat to a cave on a mountain called Hira, a short distance from Mecca, and there experienced the first of the revelations that would be gathered into the Qur'an. Almost everything about that scene, its setting, its timing, its wording, and the reactions of those around him, reaches us through accounts written down generations later. The Qur'an, proclaimed to audiences in Arabia and preserved by the community that gathered around it, is the earliest body of source material associated with Muhammad's preaching and stands much closer to the events than the surviving continuous biographies. The problem of this chapter is how to build a responsible historical account from evidence of these two very different kinds.

Chapter 14 reconstructed Arabia on the eve of Islam: a regionally diverse peninsula of oasis towns, pastoral confederations, sanctuaries, and trade networks, connected but not politically unified, and religiously plural. This chapter enters that world at one of its points, the sanctuary town of Mecca, and follows one life and one message within it. Its task, assigned by the Authoritative Chapter Outline, is to examine Muhammad's early life, Meccan society, the beginnings of the Qur'anic revelation, the opposition it encountered, and the formation of the first Muslim community. Its chronological span runs from the later sixth century to the year 622 CE, the year of the Hijra, which is the handoff point to Chapter 16 rather than an event this chapter narrates.

The chapter's central argument is that Muhammad's Meccan career unfolded within the kinship, sanctuary, commercial, and religious world Chapter 14 described, and that it can only be understood there. The early Qur'anic message challenged important assumptions about divine authority, the final judgment, moral obligation, and the boundaries of communal loyalty. The community that formed around that message, however, remained dependent on the very Meccan institutions, especially kin protection, that its message strained. A movement that began as a household circle became an identifiable community of believers, met sustained opposition, sent some of its members across the Red Sea to Christian Aksum, and finally sought a new social base beyond Mecca.

This chapter treats the problem of evidence more insistently than any chapter so far. Three broad kinds of material are involved. The Qur'an is an early and indispensable text, but it is not a chronological biography, and it names Muhammad only a handful of times. The detailed narrative of his life comes from Muslim biographical and historical writing composed from the eighth century onward, drawing on earlier transmitted reports. Non-Muslim sources of the seventh century mention the new movement but say very little about its Meccan origins. Section 15.2 explains how historians weigh these materials, and every later section labels which kind of evidence supports its claims. Students should avoid two opposite errors: treating the later biographies as a transcript of what happened, and concluding that nothing about Muhammad before Medina can be known.

The chapter also observes a strict division of labor with its neighbors. It does not retell the pre-Islamic Arabian world, which belongs to Chapter 14, and it ends before narrating the Hijra, the political organization of Medina, the wars, the alliances, the legislation, and the formation of the umma as a political community, all of which belong to Chapter 16. Where a Meccan development points forward, the chapter foreshadows briefly and stops. The Meccan phase is treated as significant in its own right, not as a preface to the Medinan phase.

The chapter uses a neutral, historical register. It reports what the Qur'an says and what Muslim tradition reports, attributing each clearly, and it does not adjudicate questions of theological truth, which are beyond the competence of historical method. Believing Muslims understand the Qur'an as divine revelation; historians studying it as a historical source describe how it was proclaimed, received, and contested. A textbook can respect both frames while doing only the second kind of work.

By the end of the chapter, students should be able to describe the life setting, message, and social composition of the early community, explain why opposition arose and intensified, identify the institutions through which pressure and protection operated, and evaluate how confidently each major claim about the Meccan period can be asserted. That preparation is necessary for Chapter 16, where the same community, in a different setting, becomes a political order.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Distinguish the Qur'an, biographical tradition (sira), hadith, later histories, and non-Muslim sources as different kinds of evidence for Muhammad's Meccan life.
  2. Evaluate the confidence with which particular claims about the Meccan period can be asserted, rather than accepting or rejecting the later tradition wholesale.
  3. Describe Muhammad's traditionally reported lineage, upbringing, marriage, and household while marking the dependence of these accounts on later transmission.
  4. Explain the role of Khadija bint Khuwaylid in Muhammad's household, commercial life, and the earliest community.
  5. Distinguish what the Qur'an itself says about revelation from what later tradition narrates about its beginning.
  6. Analyze the principal themes of the early Meccan revelations, including divine sovereignty, judgment, accountability, mercy, and social responsibility.
  7. Assess the social composition of the first believers and explain why the claim that early Islam attracted only the poor and enslaved is inaccurate.
  8. Explain Quraysh opposition as a multicausal development rather than the product of a single economic or religious motive.
  9. Analyze how kin protection (jiwar), clan solidarity, and their withdrawal shaped the safety of Muhammad and his followers.
  10. Trace the migration to Aksum, the Hashimite boycott, the deaths of Khadija and Abu Talib, the Ta'if episode, and the Yathrib contacts as stages in the search for a secure base.
  11. Contextualize the Meccan phase within the world reconstructed in Chapters 13 and 14 and identify what this chapter reserves for Chapter 16.

Historical Context

Chapter 13 placed Arabia inside the late-antique world of Byzantine and Sasanian rivalry, of Christian, Jewish, and local religious communities, and of the Aksumite and Sasanian interventions in Yemen. Chapter 14 turned inward and described the Hijaz, Najd, eastern Arabia, Oman, and Yemen around 600 CE, together with the institutions that structured Arabian life: kinship and lineage, alliance and protection, blood compensation, sanctuary, and oral poetic culture. Students should carry those results forward. This chapter assumes, and does not repeat, the account of Mecca as a sanctuary settlement depicted in later Islamic tradition as governed through negotiated consensus among leading Quraysh families, the debate over the scale of Meccan trade, and the picture of Yathrib as an oasis of several Arab and Jewish communities without a unifying authority.

What Chapter 14 could not do, because of its stopping point, is follow a particular person and a particular text through that setting. Muhammad was born into Quraysh, the Meccan grouping that later Islamic tradition depicts as exercising leadership and custodianship of the sanctuary, and he lived most of his adult life before 622 inside a town whose social life was organized by clans and by obligations of protection. The story told in this chapter therefore requires two kinds of context at once: the general institutions of Chapter 14, and the specific evidence problems of Section 15.2. The chapter also relies on Chapter 13's account of Aksum, since the Aksumite kingdom enters the story as a refuge.

The chapter's boundaries deserve statement. Its lower boundary is Chapter 14's account of Mecca. Its upper boundary is the eve of the Hijra in 622 CE. Chapter 16 begins with the migration to Yathrib and covers political organization at Medina, warfare, alliances, law, and the development of the umma. Chapter 17 covers the consolidation of authority across Arabia. Readers who look here for the Constitution of Medina, the battles of the Medinan period, or legislation will not find them, by design.

Section 15.1

From Pre-Islamic Mecca to the Historical Muhammad

Chapter 14 ended by placing Muhammad's birth within the Quraysh community of Mecca, in a peninsula that was regionally diverse, economically connected, religiously plural, and politically ununified. This chapter begins at that point and does not treat the world it inherits as an "age of ignorance" awaiting correction. The term jahiliyya, as Chapter 14 explained, is a later Islamic theological label. A historian's Mecca is a functioning sanctuary settlement with its own institutions, into which a particular person was born and within which a particular religious message was first proclaimed.

Two features of the task set it apart from writing about most figures in this textbook. The first is the unusual combination of an early primary text and a later narrative tradition described in the Chapter Opening. The second is the historical significance later generations attached to the subject. Muhammad's life became the founding reference point of a religious tradition that eventually spanned continents, and later writers arranged, selected, and interpreted their material in light of that significance. A textbook that wants to describe the Meccan phase on its own terms must therefore read backwards through layers of later meaning without either dismissing them or mistaking them for contemporary reporting.

The chapter's chronological focus is the period from the later sixth century, for Muhammad's birth and upbringing, through 622 CE. Within that span, the thirteen or so years traditionally counted as the Meccan prophetic period, from about 610 to 622, receive most attention. The year 622 is the handoff point to Chapter 16. When this chapter reaches the eve of the Hijra it stops, on purpose, at arrangements and expectations rather than the journey itself.

Section 15.2

How Do Historians Know about Muhammad's Meccan Life?

The following bodies of evidence bear on the subject, and each has its own strengths and limits. Students should learn to ask, of any claim in this chapter, which of them supports it.

The Qur'an is the earliest and most important source for the message proclaimed in Muhammad's name. Muslim tradition holds that it was revealed in portions over roughly twenty-two years, and that it was collected and given a standard written form within a generation or so after Muhammad's death, a process discussed in later chapters. Historians study the text as a document that, in its content and its style, reflects the concerns of the early community, including responses to opposition and to specific objections. Its limits are equally important. It does not narrate Muhammad's life in order; it names Muhammad only a few times; it usually alludes to events without describing them; and it does not date its own parts. Assigning individual suras to Meccan or Medinan periods, and ordering them within those periods, is a scholarly reconstruction that draws on Muslim tradition about the circumstances of revelation, on style, and on content. Nineteenth-century scholarship, notably that of Theodor Nöldeke, developed influential chronological schemes, which later scholars have refined and questioned.

Biographical tradition (sira and maghazi) supplies the detailed narrative. The earliest surviving continuous biography is not preserved in its original form. It was composed by Muhammad ibn Ishaq (d. c. 767–768) and survives chiefly in the edited version of Ibn Hisham (d. 833–834) and in quotations by later historians such as al-Tabari (died 923). Other important works include the compilations of al-Waqidi (d. 822–823) and Ibn Sa'd (died 845). Scholars can sometimes trace earlier transmitters, such as 'Urwa ibn al-Zubayr (d. 712–713) and Ibn Shihab al-Zuhri (died 742), whose reports survive as quoted material. These works were written after the Umayyad conquests and after the first civil wars of Chapter 21, in communities with theological, legal, political, and genealogical interests, and they include material shaped to answer later questions.

Hadith are reports of the sayings and actions of Muhammad, transmitted with chains of named narrators. They were collected and critically sifted in the ninth century and afterward, primarily for legal and devotional purposes. They contain much incidental biographical information but were not organized as biography, and their handling of the Meccan period is scattered.

Non-Muslim sources of the seventh century, in Greek, Syriac, and Armenian, mention a prophet among the Arabs and the conquest movement he inspired. They are valuable as early external testimony that a figure named Muhammad and a new religious movement existed, but they contain almost nothing on the Meccan phase. Archaeology, epigraphy, and manuscripts add a further, slower kind of evidence. Early Arabic inscriptions and coinage, discussed in Chapters 22 and 23, document the movement's public self-presentation from the later seventh century, and radiocarbon dating of some early Qur'anic manuscript fragments places their parchment within the early period, although dating a material object does not by itself date the composition of its text.

Historians therefore work with a gradient of confidence rather than an all-or-nothing verdict. Some claims are supported by the Qur'an and by multiple independent later traditions and can be asserted with high confidence: that Muhammad lived in Arabia, that he proclaimed a text that was recited and preserved by a community, that a monotheistic message was contested in his lifetime, and that the community eventually moved to Yathrib. Other claims, such as the outline of kin-based protection, the emigration to Aksum, and the general sequence of Meccan opposition, rest on a widely attested tradition and are reasonable but should be described as reported. Many specific details, including exact dates, dialogue, numbers, and individual scenes, are much less secure and are best presented as the tradition's account. This chapter uses phrases such as "tradition reports" and "later biography records" deliberately, to mark where a claim sits on that gradient.

Historical Vocabulary
Sira, Maghazi, and Hadith

Sira (from a root meaning "to travel" or "to proceed," hence "way of life" and "biography") is the Arabic term for the biography of Muhammad and, by extension, the genre of biographical writing. Maghazi literally means "campaigns" and originally referred to accounts of Muhammad's military expeditions; the term came to overlap with sira as biographical writing expanded to include earlier and later material. Hadith means "report" or "narrative" and denotes an individual report of what Muhammad said, did, or approved, given with an isnad, a chain of named transmitters back to a witness.

These genres differ in purpose and in method. The sira tells a continuous story; hadith collections arrange material by legal or devotional topic. Students should not treat "the tradition" as one voice. Its authors disagreed with one another on details, and those disagreements are themselves evidence about how memory was shaped. Modern English terms such as "biography" and "hadith literature" can mislead by suggesting the modern historical biography's documentary method; the early Muslim authors worked instead by collecting and arranging transmitted reports.

Historical Debate

How Can Historians Reconstruct Muhammad's Meccan Career?

The question: How much of the detailed narrative of Muhammad's life before 622 can be treated as historical information about that period, given that the surviving biographical narratives were composed one to two centuries later?

Interpretation A — Recoverable early strata: Some scholars argue that careful analysis of chains of transmission and of the texts themselves can identify reports that go back to the late seventh or even earlier seventh century, and that the broad outlines of the tradition, including the Meccan opposition and the emigration to Aksum, reflect genuine early memory. Work of this kind attempts to trace individual reports through their transmitters and to reconstruct earlier collections that survive only in quotation.
Interpretation B — Distance and literary shaping: Other scholars emphasize the interval between the events and the earliest surviving narratives, the tendency of stories to grow in the telling, the presence of material that clearly answers later legal, sectarian, and theological questions, and the difficulty of verifying isnads. Since the Qur'an provides so little narrative detail, they argue, much of what is told about the Meccan period may reflect later storytelling and cannot serve as a reliable basis for reconstruction.

Evidence: Both positions rely on the same corpus: the Qur'an, the later biographies and their known sources, hadith collections, and non-Muslim reports. They differ over how far transmission history can be reconstructed, over the significance of early material surviving only in quotation, and over how to treat reports that fit a later religious purpose. Neither position denies that Muhammad existed or that the Qur'an was proclaimed in early seventh-century Arabia; the disagreement concerns the detailed narrative.

Why it matters: How a historian answers this question determines how much of the Meccan narrative may be stated as fact. Many recent scholars avoid choosing one model wholesale and instead evaluate individual traditions or clusters of reports, asking for each whether it appears in demonstrably independent transmission lines, how its wording changes across recensions, how early its transmission can plausibly be traced, whether it accords with earlier evidence such as the Qur'an, and whether later interests plausibly shaped it. This textbook follows that claim-by-claim approach.

Student task: Choose three claims from this chapter, for example Muhammad's marriage to Khadija, the migration to Aksum, and the visit to Ta'if. For each, decide whether it deserves higher or lower confidence, and explain which criteria above you used and what additional evidence would change your assessment.

Section 15.3

Birth, Lineage, Childhood, and Orphanhood

According to the tradition, Muhammad was born at Mecca into the Banu Hashim, a clan of Quraysh, the son of Abdallah ibn Abd al-Muttalib and Amina bint Wahb. The traditional date of about 570 CE is linked in later writing to the "Year of the Elephant," associated with an Abyssinian expedition against Mecca, an association that Muslim chronographers used to fix the date and that modern historians treat as an approximate anchor, not as a precisely measured year. Chapter 13 discussed the Aksumite presence in South Arabia that forms the backdrop of that tradition.

The tradition reports that Abdallah died before Muhammad's birth or soon afterward, that Amina died when the child was about six, and that Muhammad passed into the care of his grandfather Abd al-Muttalib and then, after the grandfather's death, of his uncle Abu Talib. It also reports a period spent as an infant with a wet nurse from the Banu Sa'd, a widespread Arabian practice. The pattern of orphanhood is reflected obliquely in the Qur'an, which reminds its audience that the Prophet was found an orphan and given shelter (Qur'an 93:6), a verse the tradition understandably connects with his upbringing. That link is a reasonable inference and, unusually for the Meccan biography, has some support in the Qur'anic text itself.

Orphanhood mattered structurally in a society organized by kinship. A child without a father depended on other male relatives for protection, marriage arrangements, and inheritance, and the identity of the guardian determined the child's prospects. Abu Talib, a respected but by most accounts not especially wealthy figure within the Banu Hashim, was Muhammad's guardian during formative years and, as later sections show, remained his protector into the prophetic period. The tradition also reports that Muhammad traveled with Abu Talib to Syria in his youth; the most elaborate accounts of that journey, involving a Christian monk, appear in stories that scholars regard as later elaboration with clear devotional and polemical purposes.

Students should notice what the sources do not provide: any contemporary record of these years. The details are the product of memory, and the tradition, aware of its subject's later significance, plausibly attached signs and anticipations to the childhood that a historian would not treat as biographical fact. A careful account records the broad outline, an orphan of the Banu Hashim raised by relatives in a clan of respectable but not dominant standing, and notes that the outline coheres with what the Qur'an and Meccan institutions independently suggest.

Section 15.4

Khadija, Marriage, Household, and Commercial Life

Tradition associates the Banu Hashim with the sanctuary function of providing water to pilgrims, but by the late sixth century they were reportedly not among the wealthiest or most politically prominent clans of Mecca; the wealthier and better-connected clans, according to tradition, included Umayya (Abd Shams) and Makhzum. Muhammad's own position within his clan was that of a respected but not powerful young man. Tradition reports that he worked in commerce, and that in his mid-twenties he entered the service of Khadija bint Khuwaylid of the Asad clan of Quraysh, a woman of wealth who sent goods with agents on trading journeys.

Khadija is central to the early story and should not be treated as a footnote. The tradition describes her as a widow, or twice widowed, who managed substantial commercial interests, and as older than Muhammad, although the reported ages differ from one account to another. Her proposal or acceptance of marriage, in about 595 by the traditional reckoning, is presented as the initiative of a woman of independent means. Whatever the details, the tradition's consistent picture, that a Meccan woman could own property, employ agents, and take a decisive part in her own marriage, fits Chapter 14's cautions about women's varied economic roles in pre-Islamic Arabia, although it should not be generalized to all women or all classes.

The household reportedly included several children: sons who died young, and daughters, including Fatima, whose descendants later held great importance in the histories of Chapters 21 and 31. The household also included, according to the tradition, Zayd ibn Haritha, a young man who had been enslaved and was freed and treated as a son, and Ali, Abu Talib's son, who is reported to have been raised in Muhammad's home. These reports have devotional significance for later communities and their details, such as who lived with whom at which age, should be treated with corresponding caution.

A historian should resist two temptations in describing Muhammad's economic position. One is to present him as an impoverished outsider whose message arose from marginality. The other is to present him as a great international merchant, an image tied to the older view of Meccan commerce that Chapter 14's debate qualified. The evidence, such as it is, suggests a person of respectable position who gained economic security through marriage into a merchant household, part of a network of kin and commercial connections and not an outsider to Meccan life. That embeddedness, as later sections show, shaped both his access to protection and the character of the opposition he met.

Section 15.5

Mecca in Muhammad's Lifetime

Chapter 14 described Mecca in detail, and this section carries forward only what the biography requires. As Chapter 14 cautioned, much of the detail about Meccan institutions, including consensus government and sanctuary customs, comes from later Islamic tradition, and the description below should be read accordingly. Mecca was a sanctuary settlement without agricultural resources of its own, whose life turned on the Ka'ba, seasonal pilgrimage, markets, and regional trade. Authority rested with the leading Quraysh families and negotiated consensus, not with a king or formal state. The town's clans, including Hashim, Abd Shams, Makhzum, Zuhra, Taym, Asad, and others, cooperated in sanctuary and commercial functions but also competed for status, wealth, and honor.

Several institutions from Chapter 14 shape everything that follows. Kin protection (jiwar) meant that a person's safety depended on the willingness of a clan or an individual protector to defend him. Blood compensation and collective responsibility meant that an injury to a person implicated his group. The sanctuary's customary prohibition on violence allowed inhabitants and pilgrims to live and trade without constant fear, and gave the Quraysh a stake in preserving order. And genealogy, alliance, and reputation governed status. A preacher in this setting who lacked strong protection would be vulnerable; one with protection could speak but at the cost of implicating his kin.

The religious setting was, as Chapter 14 explained, plural and regionally varied. Meccans and visitors honored a range of deities and shrines associated with the sanctuary; Jewish and Christian communities existed in the peninsula and were known at Mecca through trade and contact; and inscriptional evidence shows monotheistic language elsewhere in Arabia. The Qur'an itself is evidence for this setting because it argues with its hearers about their beliefs and reports objections and reactions. Students should bear in mind that later Muslim accounts of Meccan religion are partly polemical, as Chapter 14 cautioned, and that the Qur'an, although early, is itself a partisan voice in the argument.

Section 15.6

The Beginning of Revelation

Muslim tradition dates the beginning of the revelation to about 610 CE, when Muhammad was about forty. The biographical accounts report that he had the habit of spending periods in retreat on Mount Hira, near Mecca, and that there he was addressed by a heavenly being identified in the tradition as the angel Gabriel, who commanded him to recite. According to the reports, he was frightened, returned to Khadija, and was reassured by her; some accounts add that she consulted her kinsman Waraqa ibn Nawfal, described as a man familiar with Christian scripture, who is reported to have said that what Muhammad experienced resembled the experience of earlier prophets.

Students should notice how much of this description comes from the later tradition and how little from the Qur'an. The Qur'an refers to the experience of revelation in various ways, describing itself as sent down, as a recitation, and as delivered through a messenger, and it contains passages usually understood to refer to visionary encounters (for example, Qur'an 53). It does not describe a cave on Hira, and it does not narrate Khadija's response. Muslim tradition associates the opening verses of Qur'an 96 with the first revelation; the tradition is not unanimous, and some reports name other passages. The chronology of the early suras is itself a scholarly reconstruction, as Section 15.2 explained, and modern research continues to refine it.

The tradition further reports an interval after the first experience during which revelation paused, followed by resumption, and it associates passages such as Qur'an 74 with the call to arise and warn. Again, these connections come from later accounts. What historians can say with more confidence is that a distinct series of Qur'anic proclamations began at some point in the early seventh century, that these were recited in Mecca, and that the earliest ones, judged by style and content, were relatively short, rhythmic, and urgent in tone, addressed to a Meccan audience.

Historical Vocabulary
Wahy, Qur'an, and Sura

Wahy is the Arabic term usually translated "revelation" or "inspiration," used in the Qur'an and in later Muslim thought for God's communication to prophets. Qur'an derives from a root meaning "to recite," and the text presents itself as a recitation; the word also names the written collection. A sura is one of the 114 chapters into which the Qur'an is divided, arranged in the standard text roughly by decreasing length and not by the order of revelation.

For the historian, the distinction between the recitation as proclaimed and the text as later collected matters, as does the distinction between the tradition's own claims and the historical evidence for them. Sura headings such as "Meccan" and "Medinan" are part of the later tradition and are useful but not infallible guides to when a passage was proclaimed. Some suras are commonly thought to combine passages from different periods. Translating wahy as "inspiration" or "revelation" does not resolve the theological question of its source, which lies outside historical method.

Section 15.7

The Message of the Early Meccan Revelations

The early Meccan portions of the Qur'an, judged by style and by the tradition's classification, are typically short, rhythmic, and vivid, and they address a Meccan audience in the imagery of its own world: the natural environment, the caravans, wealth and its pursuit, family relationships, and the fear of loss. Reducing their message to an attack on polytheism would be a serious distortion. Several interlocking themes stand out.

Divine sovereignty and the oneness of God. The text insists that the Lord who created the human being is the one to whom the hearer owes gratitude and obedience. The exclusive claim of a single deity, and the criticism of assigning partners to God, became increasingly explicit over the course of the Meccan period, and historians see this monotheism as building on the wider monotheistic currents in late-antique Arabia, of Chapter 14, while differing from them in its claims for the authority of the message itself.

Judgment, resurrection, and accountability. Many early passages describe the last day in dramatic terms, with the sky rent, mountains moved, and human deeds weighed. The theme of individual accountability is prominent: people will answer for what they have done, and neither kin nor wealth will protect them. In a society in which protection and collective responsibility structured life, a message that stressed personal accountability before God was potentially unsettling, although it did not on its face deny the importance of kin.

Mercy, gratitude, and signs. Alongside warning, the early message emphasizes divine mercy and calls for gratitude, pointing to natural phenomena, such as the alternation of night and day, rain, animals, and the human body, as signs of a creator's care. Sura 93 is often read as reassurance directed at Muhammad himself.

Wealth, generosity, and care for the vulnerable. Several early passages criticize the accumulation of wealth for its own sake and the neglect of orphans and the poor; some link denial of the judgment with turning away the needy. Sura 107 and sura 104 are commonly cited in this connection, as is the call in sura 102 not to be absorbed in rivalry over increase. Students should be careful not to read these passages as a modern political program. They express a moral critique of behavior within an existing society, directed at responsibilities of the wealthy, and the tradition's later accounts of who joined and who opposed the message must be read in light of them.

Prophetic warning and earlier prophets. The text presents Muhammad as a warner and situates his mission within a line of earlier prophets. In the Meccan suras, stories of figures such as Noah, Moses, and Abraham recur, together with accounts of Arabian peoples such as 'Ad and Thamud and the messengers associated with them (Hud with 'Ad, Salih with Thamud), often to illustrate that communities that rejected their messengers suffered destruction. The presence of these figures links the message to Jewish and Christian traditions known in Arabia, although the Qur'an reframes and reinterprets that material.

The text also, from early on, reports and answers objections: that its bearer was a poet, a soothsayer, or possessed; that the message was mere tales of the ancients; that resurrection was impossible; that a prophet should be accompanied by an angel or possess a treasure. These references are unusually early evidence for the polemical environment in which the proclamations circulated, although opponents' views reach us through the Qur'an's own representation of them rather than through independent testimony. They show that the message was heard and argued about as a challenge, and they prepare the ground for Sections 15.11 and 15.12.

Section 15.8

From a Household Circle to Public Proclamation

The biographical tradition describes a period of private or limited preaching, traditionally set at about three years, followed by a more public phase. It links the transition to Qur'anic verses commanding the Prophet to warn his nearest kin (Qur'an 26:214) and to proclaim what he had been commanded (Qur'an 15:94). Some reports describe a meal at which Muhammad addressed relatives of the Banu Hashim, and a gathering on the hill of Safa at which he called the people of Mecca and warned them; reports of the latter include the story of an angry response by his uncle Abu Lahab.

The chronology of this progression is reconstructed almost entirely from later tradition, and the specific scenes are the kind of narrative that historians treat with caution: they are vivid, they fit the later story's structure, and they cannot be checked against independent evidence. Yet the general pattern is plausible on other grounds. A message initially recited to a small circle of relatives and friends, and only later addressed to Mecca at large, is what one would expect of a proclamation dependent on kinship networks, and the Qur'an's own turn toward addressing wider audiences, including "people" in general, is evident in the text.

The move to public proclamation mattered because it changed the message's relationship to Meccan institutions. A private belief held within a household presented little threat; a public claim that the town's inherited religious practice was mistaken, and that its leaders would be held accountable, implicated the clans, the sanctuary, and the town's understanding of itself. The tradition's account of hostile reactions, examined in Sections 15.11 and 15.12, follows from that shift.

Section 15.9

The First Believers

The tradition names a small group as the earliest believers. Khadija is reported to have been the first, and the reports about her response are among the more consistent elements of the story. Ali ibn Abi Talib, then a boy or young man living in Muhammad's household, is remembered as among the first; Zayd ibn Haritha, the freed man, is likewise remembered; and Abu Bakr, a Meccan merchant of the Taym clan, is remembered as a leading early convert who brought others with him. Among those associated with him in the tradition are 'Uthman ibn 'Affan, al-Zubayr ibn al-'Awwam, 'Abd al-Rahman ibn 'Awf, Sa'd ibn Abi Waqqas, and Talha ibn 'Ubaydallah.

Later communities disputed who was "the first man," "the first youth," or "the first woman" to believe, and their answers correlate with sectarian and political loyalties that emerged in the conflicts described in Chapter 21. Students should therefore not treat the ranking as historical evidence; it is a record of later debate. What historians can say is that the earliest community included members of Muhammad's household and kin, along with several Meccans from other clans who had connections with him, and that the community's growth in the first years was modest.

The tradition also reports the use of a house, that of al-Arqam ibn Abi al-Arqam, as a meeting place for the believers. This report may reflect a real practice of gathering away from public view; as with most such details, it depends on later accounts. The important historical point is the formation of a community defined by shared belief and ritual practice, and not principally by birth, an unusual feature in a society organized by kinship. Whether it consciously cut across kin lines or did so by accident of who was persuaded is a question the sources do not settle.

Historical Vocabulary
Believers, Muslims, and Islam

The Qur'an uses several terms for the community. Mu'minun, "believers," is among the most frequent. Muslim, meaning one who submits (to God), and islam, meaning submission, also appear, and later became the standard names for the community and religion. Historians debate how these terms were understood in the earliest period and how far they marked a distinct religious identity, as distinct from a general disposition of devotion to God shared with other monotheists.

This textbook uses "believers" for the earliest community where the sources speak of belief, and "Muslim" where the later tradition's usage or the historical development warrants. Students should not project the fully developed communal boundaries of later Islamic centuries backward into the Meccan period. How sharply the earliest believers distinguished themselves from Jews, Christians, and other monotheists remains a matter of scholarly debate.

Section 15.10

Who Joined the Early Community?

An old and widely repeated claim holds that early Islam appealed only to slaves and the poor, a claim sometimes tied to modern theories about religion and social protest. The traditional lists of early believers do not support it. They include figures of wealth and standing such as Abu Bakr and 'Uthman; young men from well-connected Meccan families, including Mus'ab ibn 'Umayr, remembered as having come from a prosperous background; and members of Muhammad's own clan and marriage-related networks. They also include people of low social status: Bilal, an enslaved man of African origin, later remembered for his voice; 'Ammar ibn Yasir and his parents, who belonged to a family of clients or dependents at Mecca and are remembered as having suffered under pressure; Khabbab ibn al-Aratt, a craftsman; and others without the protection of a strong clan.

The pattern that emerges, in the tradition, is a community drawn from several social positions and clans, mainly younger and middle-status Meccans, with a minority of the dependent and vulnerable. The difference between those with strong kin protection and those without mattered more than wealth in one respect: believers with powerful relatives were relatively shielded, whereas dependents, slaves, and foreigners without protection were more exposed to coercion. The tradition preserves stories of purchase and manumission of enslaved believers, such as the reported purchase of Bilal by Abu Bakr; these accounts show that the community's membership, as later remembered, crossed the boundary of slave and free, though the details of individual stories are not securely known.

Women appear in the tradition as early believers and as significant figures, including Khadija; Sumayya, remembered as among those who died under pressure; and Fatima bint al-Khattab, whose conversion is reported in stories about her brother 'Umar. Because the tradition was composed by later, largely male, scholars, women's roles must be recovered with care, but their presence is consistent across the sources. Students should ask what motives people had for joining, a question the sources answer mostly in devotional terms, and should remember that the sources do not record private motives with certainty.

Section 15.11

Quraysh Opposition and Its Causes

Both the Qur'an and the tradition indicate that the message met resistance. The Qur'an reports objections and mocking responses, and the later biographies describe a range of reactions from Quraysh leaders, from ridicule and argument to offers of negotiation and threats. Historians should explain this opposition through several causes working together and should not reduce it to one.

Religious disagreement and ancestral practice. The message criticized inherited ritual practices and claimed that the town's forebears had been mistaken, and the Qur'an records the hearers' reply that they were following the way of their fathers. In a society where religious practice was bound with kinship and memory, an argument against ancestral practice was heard as an argument against one's own family and its dead.

Status, honor, and authority. The claim that one man had received a message from God that others had not implied an authority that cut across the customary distribution of prestige among Meccan clans. Tradition reports leaders questioning why revelation had not come to a man of greater wealth or standing, a complaint the Qur'an itself records, and rivalry among the clans meant that the elevation of one clan's member had implications for the balance among all of them.

Kin politics and the fear of division. Mecca relied on consensus among clans and on the sanctuary's peaceful order. A movement that divided families, drawing sons and clients away from their fathers and patrons, seemed to threaten the solidarity on which security depended. Tradition reports Quraysh leaders describing Muhammad as a divider of the community.

Challenge to the sanctuary's role. The message's stance on the town's shrines and practices had implications for the sanctuary and for those responsible for it. This was a matter of religious and social standing as well as of income, and the chapter does not endorse the older argument that opposition arose simply because monotheism threatened pilgrimage profits. Chapter 14 showed that the scale of Meccan commerce is debated, and the economic explanation is too deterministic to bear the weight once placed on it. Economic anxieties may have contributed in some cases, but the evidence does not allow them to be treated as the principal cause.

Opposition was also uneven. Not every Meccan was an enemy of Muhammad. Some leaders, according to the tradition, argued and negotiated; some kin who did not accept the message protected him; and several individuals who at one time opposed him are reported to have later accepted the message. The tradition names Abu Lahab, Abu Jahl, and others as leading opponents, and the picture of a united hostile Quraysh is partly shaped by the later narrative's need to make a coherent story. Students should keep the range of positions in view.

Section 15.12

Kin Protection, Pressure, and Persecution

Chapter 14 explained that protection (jiwar) and clan solidarity were the practical guarantors of safety in Mecca. The key fact about Muhammad's position is that Abu Talib, the head or leading figure of the Banu Hashim in these years according to the tradition, protected him without accepting his message. Protection did not require agreement; it required that the protector be willing to defend his kinsman and bear the cost. The tradition presents Abu Talib as resisting pressure from other Quraysh leaders to withdraw protection, on the ground that doing so would break the obligations of kinship.

It is worth distinguishing several kinds of pressure. There was social pressure: mockery, argument, and attempts to discredit. There was exclusion: refusal to deal or marry with the believers, or attempts to isolate them. There were threats and negotiation, including reported offers of compromise. And there were remembered acts of physical violence, especially against believers who lacked protection. The tradition preserves specific stories of torture and killing of enslaved and dependent believers, such as reports about Bilal, 'Ammar, and Sumayya. Their detailed narratives derive from later Muslim sources with an interest in commemorating suffering and endurance, and specific scenes cannot be independently verified. But the general pattern, that believers with strong protectors were relatively safe, and those without were more exposed, is consistent with the institutions of Chapter 14 and with the Qur'an's references to persecution and endurance.

Historians therefore describe this period as one of escalating pressure that was constrained by the logic of kinship. Quraysh leaders were reluctant to attack a member of a clan whose members might take revenge, because doing so risked a cycle of retaliation that would damage Mecca's order. That constraint explains why some believers, and Muhammad himself, could remain in Mecca for years, and why, when the constraint weakened, the situation became far more dangerous, as Sections 15.14 and 15.15 show.

Section 15.13

The Migration to Aksum

The migration to Aksum belongs to the Meccan period. It is not the Hijra to Yathrib, which Chapter 16 opens with. According to the tradition, in about 615 CE, when pressure on believers had intensified, a small group left Mecca and crossed the Red Sea to the Christian kingdom of Aksum in the Horn of Africa, centered in what is now northern Ethiopia and Eritrea; a second, larger party is reported to have followed. The leaders named in accounts include 'Uthman ibn 'Affan with his wife Ruqayya, Muhammad's daughter, and later Ja'far ibn Abi Talib, Muhammad's cousin.

The tradition adds that Quraysh sent envoys to the ruler, called by the title Najashi in Arabic sources, to seek the return of the emigrants, and that the emigrants, through Ja'far, explained their faith, including by reciting from the Qur'an, and were granted protection. The specific speeches are the sort of narrative that later tradition embellishes, but the migration itself is among the better-attested episodes of the Meccan period, appearing in multiple early traditions and in reports that scholars can trace to relatively early transmitters. Recent source-critical work identifies the Abyssinian migration as one of the significant early clusters of tradition linking the period of persecution to the later Hijra.

The episode matters for several reasons. It shows that the emerging community was connected to the Christian and African world of Chapters 13 and 14: the Red Sea crossing was an established route, Aksum had been involved in South Arabia, and a Christian ruler was regarded as a plausible refuge. It shows that the believers used the institution of protection at an international level, seeking a foreign patron when domestic protection failed. It also shows that Muhammad, who did not himself go, was willing to send part of his community away, indicating that he expected the movement to survive in more than one place. And it introduces, early, the theme of relations between the believers and Christians that the Qur'an develops. The return of some emigrants and the later development of the Aksumite connection belong to Chapters 16 and 17.

Section 15.14

Escalating Conflict and the Hashimite Boycott

The tradition reports that Quraysh leaders, frustrated by the failure of pressure on individual believers and by the continued protection of Muhammad by his kin, adopted a collective measure against his clan. According to the accounts, clans opposed to Muhammad agreed to boycott the Banu Hashim and their close ally the Banu al-Muttalib, refusing to marry with them or to trade with them, until they surrendered Muhammad. The agreement is said to have been written and hung in or near the Ka'ba, and the boycott is traditionally said to have lasted about three years, from roughly 616 to 619, with the Banu Hashim withdrawing to a quarter of the town, the so-called ravine of Abu Talib.

The story includes the report that Abu Lahab, a member of the Banu Hashim, broke with his clan and sided with the boycotters, and that the boycott ended after some Quraysh figures, uncomfortable with its severity, moved to annul the agreement, which the tradition reports was found damaged. The miraculous details of the last part are a typical example of later embellishment. Historians disagree about how strictly the boycott was observed, how much hardship it caused, and even whether it took precisely the form the sources describe; a few scholars are skeptical of the account. Others regard it as plausible because a measure of collective social and economic exclusion against a clan is consistent with Meccan institutions, and because the Banu Hashim's willingness to support a member who was not obliged to be protected is a striking fact.

Even in its cautious form, the episode illustrates that opposition to Muhammad could take the form of pressure on his kin. It shows a clan and an allied clan standing together in a way that the believers' religious loyalty alone could not have secured, and it suggests that the emerging community's survival depended on the willingness of non-believing relatives to bear costs. The boycott, as remembered, also reveals divisions among Quraysh over how far to go.

Section 15.15

Khadija, Abu Talib, and the Loss of Protection

The tradition dates the deaths of Khadija and Abu Talib to about 619 CE, within a short time of each other, although accounts disagree about which came first. The later tradition remembers this period as the "Year of Sorrow" ('am al-huzn), a retrospective label and not a contemporary calendar designation.

The two losses mattered in different ways. Khadija's death removed Muhammad's closest household partner and the person the tradition remembers as his earliest and most steadfast supporter, and it removed the economic and emotional center of his home. Abu Talib's death removed the kin protector whose willingness to defend Muhammad had constrained Quraysh opposition. The tradition reports that leadership of the Banu Hashim passed to Abu Lahab, who was less willing to protect Muhammad, and that protection was withdrawn or became uncertain. Abu Talib, according to the tradition, is reported not to have accepted the message; later Muslim accounts debated this, and the disagreement reflects later theological concerns.

The chronology and details are uncertain, but the structural point is secure enough to be stated: Muhammad's position in Mecca had depended on a protector, and the disappearance of that protector left him exposed. This helps explain why the last Meccan years are remembered as a search for a new setting. The tradition also places around this period the isra', the night journey conventionally associated with Qur'an 17:1, and a related tradition of heavenly ascent (mi'raj). These narratives were later closely linked, although their textual bases and early development are not identical, and their dating and interpretation have been debated, including within Muslim thought. A historical narrative can record the tradition's placement of the episode without adjudicating the question of its nature, and the details belong properly to religious studies more than to political history.

Section 15.16

Ta'if and the Search for Protection

Chapter 14 introduced Ta'if as a well-watered highland settlement with vineyards and orchards, inhabited by the Thaqif and linked commercially to Mecca. The tradition reports that after the deaths of Khadija and Abu Talib, Muhammad traveled to Ta'if, reportedly accompanied by Zayd ibn Haritha, to seek support from its leaders. It reports that they rejected him, that he was pelted by people of the town as he left, and that he returned toward Mecca and entered under the protection of a Meccan, Mut'im ibn 'Adi, of the Nawfal clan.

The details are preserved in later biography, and this chapter treats them as the tradition's account. The episode's larger significance is nevertheless understandable within the institutions of Chapter 14. A man whose position in his own town had deteriorated sought protection, and possibly a sympathetic hearing, from a neighboring polity of comparable standing. That Ta'if did not respond reflects its own interests: the Thaqif had commercial and political ties to Mecca, and taking in a man with a contested position in Mecca would carry costs. The fact that a Meccan of a different clan then extended protection to Muhammad, and that the tradition remembers Mut'im's protection favorably, shows again the importance of individual protectors, and it confirms that not all Quraysh leaders were hostile.

What the episode reveals, in short, is the shape of the problem of the final Meccan years: a message that had won a small community, faced opposition in its home town, had lost its principal protector, and needed a setting where it could operate without depending on the goodwill of Meccan clans.

Section 15.17

Yathrib Contacts and the Road toward Migration

Chapter 14 described Yathrib as an oasis of several Arab tribal groups and Jewish communities, without a single unifying authority. The tradition adds that its principal Arab groups, the Aws and the Khazraj, had a long history of rivalry that had erupted in serious fighting shortly before this period, commonly associated with a battle at Bu'ath, and that this conflict left the oasis divided and its leaders searching for a means of reconciliation. Whether that description is accurate in detail or a later simplification, the tradition treats the divisions of Yathrib as a reason the town became receptive to an outside mediator.

According to the tradition, during the pilgrimage season of about 620, Muhammad met a small group of men of the Khazraj who listened to his message and were receptive, and in the following year a larger group met him at al-'Aqaba, near Mecca, where they are reported to have taken a pledge (bay'a) of allegiance to him. The tradition says that a teacher, Mus'ab ibn 'Umayr, was sent with them to Yathrib to instruct people, and that, in 622, a much larger group of Yathrib representatives, including women according to some reports, met him again at al-'Aqaba and made a further pledge, including a commitment to protect him as they would protect their own. The tradition reports that Muhammad's uncle al-'Abbas was present and, though not yet a believer by most accounts, negotiated on Muhammad's behalf.

These pledges are best understood as institutions of protection and alliance in Arabian terms, extended from Meccan kin structures to a new group of protectors. That explains why the migration became possible: Muhammad and his followers would not arrive in Yathrib as strangers without standing but as people under a defined commitment. The sources for the pledges are, again, later tradition, although the general outline, that men of Yathrib pledged support at pilgrimage season and that migration followed, is consistent with the later course of events and is widely accepted. The narrative of the migration itself, the circumstances of Yathrib's political arrangements, and everything after belong to Chapter 16.

Section 15.18

The Meccan Phase in Perspective

Viewed across the whole period, the Meccan phase saw the transformation of an individual vocation and a household circle into an identifiable religious community that faced sustained opposition and sought a new social base. The text proclaimed in Mecca, urgent in its warning, insistent on divine sovereignty and accountability, and critical of the neglect of the vulnerable, laid out the intellectual and moral foundations that later generations would develop. The community, remembered as a mixture of kin, allies, dependents, and people from several clans, had learned to operate within the institutions of kinship and protection and had been strained by them.

The chapter has also emphasized what remains uncertain. The dates are approximate, many episodes rest on later narrative, and the social composition and motives of participants can be inferred only partly. Students should carry forward the habit of distinguishing the Qur'an from the later tradition, and of stating confidence levels honestly. The evidence for what the Meccan community believed is stronger than the evidence for what happened to whom on which day.

By 622, arrangements were in place for a move to Yathrib. What happened next, how the migration was carried out, how a community of believers became a political order in a town of several communities, what arrangements governed it, how it fought and made alliances, and how it developed law, is the subject of the next chapter. Chapter 16 begins with the Hijra.

  • Believers came to see themselves, and were seen, as a distinct group

What Stayed the Same

  • Household, lineage, and clan continued to structure safety and status
  • Protection through non-believing kin remained essential
  • Pilgrimage season and oral proclamation remained the principal channels of contact
  • Mecca remained a sanctuary town, depicted in later Islamic tradition as governed through negotiated consensus among leading Quraysh families

Who experienced change most directly? Believers without kin protection and those who left for Aksum experienced the sharpest change. Who experienced greater continuity? Most Meccans, including many non-believers, continued their usual pattern of life, and the town's institutions were still in place in 622.

Why the pattern matters: The movement did not overthrow Meccan institutions in the Meccan period; it worked through them and depended on them, which helps explain both its survival and its strain, and the pattern shows why the later shift to Yathrib was a change of institutional setting.

Cause and Effect

Why Opposition Intensified

Long-term causes

Meccan kinship and sanctuary institutions, the interweaving of religious practice with lineage and honor, and rivalry among clans for status, as described in Chapter 14.

Short-term causes

The shift from small-circle recitation to public proclamation, together with the increasing explicitness of the message on the exclusive claim of one God, judgment, and the neglect of the vulnerable.

Event or transformation

The emergence of organized opposition and pressure, which targeted believers and the clan that protected Muhammad.

Immediate effects

Dependence on kin protection, heightened vulnerability for believers without protectors, the emigration to Aksum, and the boycott.

Long-term consequences

The loss of principal protectors, the search for support beyond Mecca, and the arrangements with Yathrib that led to the Hijra.

Historical complexity

Opposition cannot be attributed to one economic, religious, or political cause. It combined belief, honor, kin politics, and fear of division, varied among individuals, and is known through sources that had their own purposes.
Everyday Life and Social History

Household, Work, and Worship in Mecca

For most Meccans, life in the years of this chapter turned on household work, trade, the demands of the pilgrimage season, and obligations to kin. Households organized labor, property, and marriage, and clients and slaves formed part of many of them. The believers' new practices, including recitation and prayer, took place within this ordinary life and often within households, and the tradition's report of a meeting place in a private house fits that pattern. The regular experiences of ordinary people, such as the demands of work and family, are largely invisible in the sources, which focus on the prophet, his opponents, and the notable early believers.

Women appear as household managers, property owners, believers, and sufferers of pressure in the tradition, though the sources are mostly the work of later male authors. Enslaved and dependent people, whose loyalty and labor belonged to owners or patrons, faced the greatest risk in joining the movement, and the tradition's stories of their endurance and their purchase and release are among its most vivid. Historians must read those accounts as commemorative material as well as evidence, keeping in view that the ordinary experience of the great majority of Meccans, believers and non-believers alike, remains far less documented than the events on which the biography concentrates.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined Muhammad's early life, Meccan society as it bore on his career, the beginnings of the Qur'anic revelation, the opposition it met, and the formation of the first Muslim community, without narrating the Hijra and the Medinan period reserved for Chapter 16. Its central argument is that the Meccan career developed within the kinship, sanctuary, commercial, and religious world of Chapter 14. The message challenged assumptions about divine authority, judgment, moral obligation, and communal loyalty, and the community it created depended on, and was strained by, the institutions of Mecca.

The chapter began with the problem of evidence. The Qur'an is early and indispensable but not a biography; the detailed narrative comes from biographical and hadith tradition composed generations later; non-Muslim sources add little on the Meccan phase; and archaeology and manuscripts contribute at the margins. Historians therefore work with degrees of confidence, and the chapter followed a claim-by-claim approach avoiding both uncritical acceptance and wholesale dismissal. The Historical Debate laid out the difference between scholars who emphasize recoverable early strata and those who stress distance and literary shaping.

On Muhammad's early life, the tradition reports his birth into the Banu Hashim of Quraysh, his orphanhood, and his upbringing by his grandfather and then by his uncle Abu Talib, whose later protection proved decisive. His marriage to Khadija bint Khuwaylid, a woman of independent means, gave him economic security and a household, and she emerges as a central figure of the early community. The chapter avoided portraying Muhammad either as a marginal outsider or as a great international merchant.

The revelation began, according to tradition, in about 610, and the chapter distinguished what the Qur'an says about revelation from the later narrative of Hira, Gabriel, and Khadija's response. The early Meccan message combined divine sovereignty and monotheism, judgment and accountability, mercy and gratitude, moral criticism of wealth and neglect of the vulnerable, and warnings drawn from earlier prophets. The text also records the objections of its hearers, which is early evidence of how it was received.

The movement moved from a household circle to public proclamation, and the chapter's discussion of the first believers and of who joined stressed that the community drew from several clans and social levels, including wealthy and well-connected Meccans as well as enslaved and dependent people, and that vulnerability depended above all on whether a believer had a protector. The old claim that early Islam attracted only the poor and enslaved was rejected.

Opposition, the chapter argued, was multicausal. Religious disagreement and ancestral practice, status and honor, kin politics and the fear of division, and the message's implications for the sanctuary each contributed, and the chapter explicitly declined the older explanation that opposition arose from threatened pilgrimage profits. Opposition was uneven: some Quraysh argued, some negotiated, and some, including non-believing kin and other Meccans, protected Muhammad or his followers.

The chapter then followed the traditions concerning the community's strategies for seeking security: kin protection under Abu Talib; the reported emigration of some believers to Christian Aksum, which connected the movement to the Christian and African world of Chapters 13 and 14; the reported collective boycott against the Banu Hashim and Banu al-Muttalib; the deaths of Khadija and Abu Talib and the resulting loss of protection; the Ta'if episode; and the reported contacts with Yathrib pilgrims and pledges at al-'Aqaba that made migration possible.

Throughout, the chapter labeled the source of each claim and used the language of tradition and reconstruction where the evidence required. It observed that the community's existence across Meccan institutions, rather than against them, explains both its survival and its strain, and it showed that the movement's later transformation depended on a change of institutional setting.

The chapter ended at the threshold of the Hijra. What follows, the migration, the organization of a political community at Medina, the wars, alliances, law, and the formation of the umma, belongs to Chapter 16. The Meccan phase thus stands as the formative period in which a text, a household circle, and a network of protectors became a recognizable community facing the question of where it could live and govern itself.

Qur'an

One of the 114 chapters of the Qur'an, arranged roughly by decreasing length; classification as Meccan or Medinan is part of later tradition.

Biography of Muhammad and the genre of biographical writing, composed in the eighth century and later.

A report of Muhammad's words or actions transmitted with a chain of narrators, collected mainly in the ninth century.
Isnad
The chain of named transmitters attached to a hadith or report, used by scholars to evaluate its reliability.

A group of related reports about an episode, whose spread through different transmitters scholars analyze to identify earlier layers.

The clan of Quraysh to which Muhammad belonged, and whose leading figure Abu Talib is remembered as protecting Muhammad.

Customary protection extended to an individual or group by an established protector, essential to safety in Mecca.

The obligation of members of a kin group to defend one another and bear collective responsibility for their actions.

"Believers," a frequent Qur'anic term for the community, preferred here for the earliest period.

Terms meaning one who submits and submission to God, which later became the standard names of the community and religion.

The later traditional label for the period of the deaths of Khadija and Abu Talib.

The community of believers, developed as a political and religious community in Chapter 16.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a religious movement began within an existing society and worked through its institutions before it transformed them. The Meccan phase did not create a political order, and it did not overturn Meccan life. It produced a text, a community, and a set of relationships of protection and dependence that would shape what came later. Understanding those foundations is necessary for understanding why the community could survive in Mecca for years, why its members turned to Aksum and then to Yathrib, and how the movement understood itself.

The chapter also demonstrates a method. Whether writing about the life of Muhammad or about any figure whose story was told by later generations, historians distinguish among kinds of evidence, state confidence honestly, and read stories for what they show about the tellers as well as the events. That method will matter throughout the chapters that follow, as the developing Islamic historical tradition becomes the main source for the conquests and the early caliphate, and as modern scholars disagree about how to use it.

Several things from this chapter continue after it. The Qur'an, as a text and as a source of law, theology, and language, shapes the Arabic-Islamic culture examined in later parts of the book. The institution of protection reappears in new forms as the community acquires clients and converts. The connection with the Christian world across the Red Sea prefigures later interactions. And the memory of the Meccan years, of opposition, endurance, and emigration, became part of how later Muslims, and later historians, understood the origins of their community.

Chapter 16 now takes up the same community in a different setting. The move to Yathrib will turn a group of believers who depended on the institutions of others into a political community with institutions of its own. The story of the Arab world's transformation in the seventh century cannot be told without that transition, and it cannot be told well without the Meccan foundation this chapter has tried to reconstruct with care.
