Part VI — Regional Powers and the Crusading Age
35

Zengids, Ayyubids, and Saladin

A History of the Arab World: From Ancient Crossroads to the Modern Arab World

Chapter Opening

On 2 October 1187, the Latin defenders of Jerusalem handed the city to Saladin. The date fell, by the Islamic calendar, on or near the anniversary of the Prophet Muhammad's night journey to Jerusalem, and contemporary Muslim writers noticed the coincidence. Yet the surrender was not the work of a single day, a single battle, or a single man. It was the end point of more than four decades in which rulers in Mosul, Aleppo, Damascus, and Cairo had fought one another, bargained with the Franks, courted scholars and caliphs, and slowly assembled the resources needed to overturn the political map that the First Crusade had drawn.

Chapter 34 ended with the fall of the city of Edessa to the atabeg Zengi in December 1144. That event is often read as the moment when a wounded Muslim world began to strike back. The historical record is more complicated. Zengi was not leading a united Muslim front; he was a Turkish military commander seeking to enlarge a dynastic realm at the expense of Muslim and Christian rivals alike. Damascus, the great Syrian city lying between his territories and the Kingdom of Jerusalem, had recently been allied with the Franks against him. The Fatimid caliphate in Cairo, meanwhile, remained a rival Ismaʿili state that most Sunni rulers regarded as illegitimate. "The Muslims" did not act as one body, because they were not one political body.

This chapter traces how that fragmentation was gradually reduced, though never eliminated. It follows three successive projects of consolidation. Zengi built a regional power based on Mosul and Aleppo. His son Nur al-Din turned that inheritance into a larger Syrian state, absorbing Damascus, cultivating Sunni religious institutions, and presenting his wars as jihad. Saladin, a Kurdish officer in Nur al-Din's service, used the struggle for Egypt to build an independent base, ended the Fatimid caliphate, and after 1174 pieced together a realm stretching from the Nile to northern Mesopotamia. His family, the Ayyubids, then ruled Egypt and much of Syria for most of the following century.

The chapter's central question is how rulers in Syria, northern Mesopotamia, and Egypt turned a politically fragmented region into coalitions strong enough to challenge the Frankish states, and how durable that consolidation proved. Its central argument is that the recovery of Jerusalem was not the inevitable result of a spontaneous Muslim reaction to the Crusades. It was the product of dynastic expansion, diplomacy, warfare, ideological mobilization, and institutional development spread over several decades. Even at its peak, Saladin's unity was negotiated, family-based, and incomplete, and it depended on the failures and divisions of his Frankish opponents as much as on his own achievements.

The chapter also insists that conflict and cooperation coexisted. Muslim and Frankish rulers signed truces, traded, exchanged envoys, and sometimes fought beside one another against rivals of their own religion. The Second Crusade, the contest over Egypt, the diplomacy that returned Jerusalem to Frankish control in 1229, and the treaty ending the Third Crusade all show a region in which religious rhetoric mattered but did not replace calculation. Readers should therefore treat "Crusader" and "Muslim" as useful but imperfect labels, and should watch for the Armenians, Greek Orthodox and Syriac Christians, Jews, Kurds, Turks, Arabs, Copts, Sudanese soldiers, and Italian merchants who complicate any two-sided story.

The evidence is unusually rich and unusually partisan. Damascene and Aleppan chroniclers, the letters and chancery writing of Saladin's secretaries, biographies by men who knew him, Latin histories such as William of Tyre's, Arabic memoirs, travelers' accounts, coins, inscriptions, and surviving fortifications and madrasas all survive. Nearly every major narrative source was written by someone who admired or opposed its subject, and the chapter asks students to read them with attention to purpose, audience, and omission.

Geographically, the chapter moves between Mosul and the upper Tigris, Aleppo and the northern Syrian plain, Damascus and its oasis, the Nile Delta and Cairo, the hills above the Sea of Galilee, the walls of Jerusalem, and the coastal ports of Acre and Tyre. It stops in the 1240s, at the threshold of the Mamluk transition. The Mongol destruction of Baghdad, the battle of Ayn Jalut, and the rise of the Mamluk Sultanate belong to Chapter 36, and this chapter prepares for them rather than narrating them.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain what Zengi's capture of Edessa changed and did not change in the political balance of Syria and northern Mesopotamia.
  2. Trace the stages by which Zengi, Nur al-Din, and Saladin built successively larger political coalitions between 1144 and 1193.
  3. Analyze how Nur al-Din used Sunni institutions, patronage, and the language of jihad to legitimize political consolidation.
  4. Explain the Second Crusade's attack on Damascus and why it undermined cooperation between Damascus and Jerusalem.
  5. Assess why Egypt became the decisive prize in regional politics during the last decades of Fatimid rule.
  6. Evaluate Saladin's rise in Egypt and the significance, and the limits, of ending the Fatimid caliphate in 1171.
  7. Analyze the causes of the Battle of Hattin, including Frankish political divisions, Saladin's strategy, and environmental conditions.
  8. Describe the terms of the surrender of Jerusalem in 1187 and identify what Saladin did not conquer.
  9. Interpret the Third Crusade and the Treaty of Jaffa as negotiated outcomes rather than total victories.
  10. Describe the Ayyubid system of family appanages, military households, and iqtaʿ administration.
  11. Compare Saladin's and al-Kamil's diplomacy, including the 1229 agreement that returned Jerusalem to Frankish control.
  12. Evaluate competing interpretations of Saladin as the culmination of a Zengid project or the founder of a new political order.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why is it misleading to describe the fall of Edessa in 1144 as the beginning of a unified Muslim counter-crusade?
  2. How did Nur al-Din's combination of military campaigning, religious patronage, and diplomacy make political consolidation appear religiously legitimate?
  3. Why did Egypt become the decisive prize in the politics of Syria, Jerusalem, and Cairo in the 1160s?
  4. What was gained, and what was not gained, when Saladin ended the Fatimid caliphate?
  5. To what extent was Hattin the result of Frankish political weakness rather than Muslim military superiority alone?
  6. How should historians weigh the religious language of jihad against dynastic ambition in explaining Saladin's policies?
  7. Why did the Ayyubid family system make regional rule flexible and resilient, yet also prone to fragmentation?
  8. What do the treaties of 1192 and 1229 reveal about the limits of "holy war" as an explanation for the politics of the period?
  9. How reliable are sources written by Saladin's own secretaries and biographers, and what perspectives do they omit?

Historical Context

Three earlier chapters supply the background. Chapter 31 explained the Fatimid caliphate and Cairo: an Ismaʿili dynasty whose real power by the mid-twelfth century had passed from the caliph to viziers and military factions. Chapter 33 traced the Seljuk transformation of the eastern Arab lands: Turkish military elites, Sunni revival, the separation of effective sultanic military power from the Abbasid caliphate's continuing role as a source of Sunni caliphal legitimacy and political recognition, and, after the death of Malik-Shah in 1092, the splintering of Seljuk power into rival princes and their tutor-regents, the atabegs. Chapter 34 described the First Crusade, the creation of the Kingdom of Jerusalem, the County of Tripoli, the Principality of Antioch, and the County of Edessa, and the pattern of raiding, truces, and shifting alliances that followed.

By 1144 northern Syria and the Jazira were politically crowded. Aleppo, Damascus, Homs, and Hama were ruled by separate garrison commanders and dynasts; the Artuqid Turkmen princes held Diyar Bakr; Seljuk sultans in Iraq and Iran claimed a nominal overlordship that they could rarely enforce; and the Abbasid caliph in Baghdad was regaining some independence from the sultans but remained militarily weak. The Franks, themselves few in number, survived by fortresses, alliances, and the fragmentation of their neighbors. Nothing made a Muslim recovery of Jerusalem likely in the 1140s.

The chapter's boundaries are deliberate. It starts from Edessa in 1144 and ends in the 1240s, when Egypt's dependence on purchased military slaves has become conspicuous but the Ayyubid state has not yet fallen. The Second Crusade, which Chapter 34 did not narrate, belongs here. The Mongol conquests, the sack of Baghdad in 1258, Ayn Jalut in 1260, and the formal establishment of the Mamluk Sultanate belong to Chapter 36. Students should also remember that "Syria" in this period means the region of Bilad al-Sham, stretching from the Euphrates to Sinai, not the modern state.

Section 35.1

From Edessa to a New Regional Balance, 1144–1146

Zengi's capture of Edessa in December 1144 marked the first loss of one of the principal Frankish capitals and began the collapse of the County of Edessa, whose remaining territories survived for several more years. The news stunned Latin Christendom and prompted the Second Crusade. For Muslims in Syria and the Jazira the event was a major military success, celebrated by poets and religious writers, but its immediate effect was narrower than later memory suggests. Edessa was the most exposed and most thinly held of the Frankish states, a county whose Latin lords ruled a largely Armenian, Syriac, and Muslim population across the Euphrates frontier. The city's fall removed a salient that had long threatened Aleppo and Mosul, and it enlarged Zengi's realm. It did not threaten Jerusalem, Antioch, or Tripoli, and it did not transform the political relationships among Muslim rulers.

Zengi's own career shows why. Born into a Turkish military family, he rose as a Seljuk officer. In 1127 the Seljuk sultan Mahmud II appointed him governor of Mosul and atabeg to Mahmud's princely sons, including Alp Arslan. He seized Aleppo in 1128 and subsequently brought Hama, Homs, and Baalbek under pressure or control. His state was assembled by force and bargaining: he paid and promoted Turkish and Kurdish commanders, exploited the rivalries of Artuqid and Seljuk princes, and repeatedly tried to annex Damascus, the richest Syrian city outside his hands. Damascus, under its atabeg Muʿin al-Din Unur, responded by negotiating with the Kingdom of Jerusalem, and in the early 1140s Damascene and Frankish forces cooperated against Zengi. Muslim chroniclers recorded Zengi's capture of Edessa as a triumph, but they also recorded his attacks on Muslim cities, and they remembered his severity.

In September 1146 Zengi was murdered, reportedly by a member of his household, while besieging a fortress on the Euphrates. His dominions divided almost immediately. His elder son Sayf al-Din Ghazi took Mosul and the Jazira; his younger son Nur al-Din Mahmud took Aleppo. The pattern, in which a state-builder's death produces a partition among sons and commanders, would recur throughout this chapter and is one of its chief structural themes. Zengi had created a precedent and a pool of military personnel, including the Kurdish brothers Ayyub and Shirkuh, who had served him. He had not created a durable centralized state.

Students should be cautious about reading Zengi retrospectively as "the first great jihad leader." Some religious writers did praise him in that language after Edessa, but as Chapter 34 showed, calls for jihad had circulated in Damascus for decades before him, often with limited political effect, and rulers used them selectively. A more accurate description presents him as a ruthless regional dynast whose victories created conditions that his successors could exploit, and whose reputation was refashioned after his death as a forerunner to Nur al-Din.

Historical Vocabulary
Atabeg

The Turkish title atabeg (literally "father-chief") originally designated a military guardian and tutor appointed to a young Seljuk prince. As Seljuk authority fragmented, many atabegs became the real rulers of cities and provinces, sometimes marrying the widow of the prince they tutored and founding dynasties in their own right. Zengi's rule in Mosul and Aleppo began in this way, and the dynasty's later rulers sometimes used the title alongside others.

The term matters because it shows how legitimacy could be assembled from delegated authority. An atabeg governed in the name of a prince, a Seljuk sultan, and ultimately the Abbasid caliph, while commanding his own troops and collecting his own revenues. It is therefore a misleading equivalent to call an atabeg a "governor" in the modern bureaucratic sense, or a "king" in the European sense. He held a flexible position that could be claimed, transmitted, or contested.

Section 35.2

Nur al-Din and the Zengid Project

Nur al-Din (r. 1146–1174) is the crucial bridge between Zengi and Saladin. Inheriting Aleppo as a young man, he faced immediate threats: the Frankish rulers of Antioch and Jerusalem, the ambitions of his brother in Mosul, the hostility of Damascus, and the loyalties of his father's commanders. His early survival owed much to the experienced officers around him, and his later success to his willingness to combine force with patience. He recaptured Edessa briefly when the former count Joscelin II tried to retake it in 1146, then in 1149 defeated and killed Raymond of Antioch at Inab, which left the Principality of Antioch badly weakened. Raymond's death did not lead Nur al-Din to conquer Antioch, since Byzantine claims, the danger of provoking a larger Frankish response, and his other commitments restrained him.

Nur al-Din's distinctive contribution was the fusion of political consolidation with religious legitimacy. He patronized Sunni scholars and jurists, founded or endowed madrasas, a dar al-hadith (a school devoted to the study of the Prophet's traditions) in Damascus, hospitals such as the bimaristan that bears his name there, and Sufi lodges. He presented himself as a pious ruler who lightened what religious scholars regarded as illegal taxes and administered justice in person. He also cultivated the language of jihad. A Damascene legal scholar, Ali ibn Tahir al-Sulami, had lectured on jihad there in the early twelfth century, and the ideas were still available to those who wished to use them. Nur al-Din commissioned an elaborate wooden pulpit (minbar) in Aleppo intended for the Aqsa Mosque in Jerusalem, a statement of ambition that was fulfilled only after his death.

This religious program had political functions. It gave Nur al-Din a claim on the loyalty of Sunni scholars and urban notables in Syrian cities that he wished to bring under his control, it distinguished him from Muslim rivals who cooperated with the Franks, and it legitimized taxation and military mobilization. Historians have long debated how far it was sincere and how far instrumental. Since no source allows us to separate conviction from calculation, a more productive approach asks how the two reinforced each other. A ruler who built madrasas and proclaimed jihad was also a ruler who could rely on networks of religious officials, preachers, and judges who were trained in and committed to his version of Sunni order.

Nur al-Din's political methods were also pragmatic. He concluded truces with the Franks when he needed to attend to Muslim rivals and respected agreements when it suited him. In 1159 he accepted a truce after the Byzantine emperor Manuel Komnenos appeared in northern Syria. After capturing Bohemond III of Antioch and Raymond III of Tripoli at Harim in 1164, he chose to ransom and release Bohemond rather than press on to seize Antioch, which would have invited a more serious Byzantine or Frankish reaction. Raymond remained a prisoner for roughly a decade, a fact relevant to Raymond's later role in Jerusalem's politics. Nur al-Din pursued consolidation by stages, conscious that his resources could not defeat all enemies at once.

Section 35.3

The Second Crusade and the Acquisition of Damascus, 1147–1154

The fall of Edessa prompted Pope Eugenius III to call a new expedition, and in 1147 two royal armies, led by Conrad III of Germany and Louis VII of France, set out through Byzantine territory and Anatolia. Both suffered heavy losses, especially against Seljuk Turkish forces in Anatolia, before reaching the Latin East in 1148. The Latin leadership gathered at Acre in June 1148 and chose to attack not Aleppo or Edessa, where the original grievance lay, but Damascus, a rich Muslim city then allied with Jerusalem.

The choice reflects local Frankish ambitions and competing priorities: Damascus was close, wealthy, and, for some barons, a desirable acquisition. The siege that began in late July 1148 among the orchards west of the city collapsed within days. Reports of relief forces from Nur al-Din and from Mosul, tactical disagreements, and possibly doubts about the allocation of the city among the Franks led the crusaders to abandon the siege. Conrad returned home soon after and Louis followed in 1149. Both the failure and its consequences were significant. The attack badly damaged and politically discredited the Damascus–Jerusalem understanding that Unur had built, making it harder for Damascene rulers to justify cooperation with Franks who had just besieged them, although Damascene rulers later renewed cooperation with Jerusalem under pressure from Nur al-Din.

Nur al-Din benefited. His reputation for defending Syria grew, and he moved cautiously to influence Damascus, marrying a daughter of Unur in 1147 and cultivating the city's notables, scholars, and militia leaders. After Unur's death in 1149, his successor Mujir al-Din Abaq, a weak ruler who needed outside help, turned again toward Jerusalem for support (he called on Frankish assistance in 1151, and Damascus continued to pay tribute), which offended many Damascenes. In April 1154 Nur al-Din entered the city, reportedly with the support of factions inside, and with little fighting. The acquisition mattered because it placed Aleppo and Damascus under one ruler for the first time, forming a bloc that pressed the Franks along their entire eastern frontier from Antioch to the Jordan valley. It also gave Nur al-Din access to Damascus's revenues, markets, scholars, and manpower.

Students should note what the episode teaches about categories. The Second Crusade, nominally a European response to the fall of an Eastern Christian state, failed partly because it misjudged local politics. The Damascus that the crusaders attacked was Muslim but had cooperated with the Kingdom of Jerusalem. The result was not a Christian–Muslim war but a reconfiguration of Syrian politics in which a Muslim state that had dealt with the Franks lost ground to one that opposed them.

Section 35.4

Egypt, the Fatimid Crisis, and the Struggle for the Nile

As Chapter 31 explained, the Fatimid caliphs in Cairo had ruled Egypt since 969, but by the mid-twelfth century they reigned without governing. A series of child or young caliphs stood above viziers who won office through military support, intrigue, or bloodshed. The army included Armenian, Turkish, Sudanese, and Arab contingents that competed with one another for pay and position. Egypt's agricultural wealth, its Nile-fed economy, its Mediterranean and Red Sea commerce, and its ports made it one of the wealthiest and most strategically valuable regions of the eastern Mediterranean. Its rulers, however, could no longer defend it reliably.

The Kingdom of Jerusalem under Baldwin III and then Amalric (r. 1163–1174) saw Egypt as both a threat and an opportunity. The Franks had taken Ascalon in 1153, the Fatimid coastal stronghold, and Amalric wished to impose tribute and eventually perhaps conquer the country. Nur al-Din, for his part, recognized that a Frankish Egypt would encircle Syria from the south, while Egyptian resources in Muslim Syrian hands could transform the balance of power. The Fatimid vizier Shawar, expelled by a rival in 1163, took refuge in Damascus and promised Nur al-Din rewards for restoring him. The situation thus invited intervention from both sides.

Nur al-Din sent his Kurdish commander Shirkuh to Egypt in 1164, accompanied by his nephew Saladin. Shawar, restored to office, quickly turned to Amalric for help against his Syrian benefactors. Between 1164 and 1169 Shirkuh and Amalric struggled for influence in Egypt through a sequence of campaigns. In 1167 the Syrian and Frankish armies fought inconclusively at al-Babayn, after which Saladin held Alexandria under Frankish siege while Shirkuh regrouped. The two sides negotiated mutual withdrawals, and tribute arrangements left a Frankish presence in Cairo. In 1168 Amalric invaded again and sacked Bilbeis; Shawar, fearing the Franks would reach Cairo, had parts of old Fustat burned, and appealed again to Nur al-Din. Shirkuh returned in early 1169. Shawar was seized and killed, and Shirkuh became vizier in January 1169 before dying only weeks later, in March.

The sequence shows that Egypt was not simply seized by a Syrian army. It was drawn into Syrian politics because its own elites sought allies, and the competition between Jerusalem and Nur al-Din turned Egypt into a stake in a larger struggle. An attempt by Amalric and Byzantine ships to attack Damietta in late 1169 failed, partly through poor coordination, leaving Shirkuh's successor in a strong position.

Section 35.5

Saladin in Egypt: From Vizier to the End of the Fatimid Caliphate

When Shirkuh died, the Fatimid caliph al-ʿAdid appointed Saladin vizier in March 1169. Saladin was about thirty, the son of Ayyub, a Kurdish officer from a family associated with Dvin and later Tikrit. To many in Cairo he was a junior, foreign, Sunni commander in charge of an Ismaʿili state. His position was precarious: he depended on Nur al-Din, who was his nominal overlord, on the Syrian troops who had come with Shirkuh, and on the goodwill of Fatimid officials who distrusted him. Over the next two years he consolidated power by patiently removing rivals and building a loyal base. In August 1169 regiments of Sudanese soldiers in Cairo, who had been central to the Fatimid army, rose in revolt and were crushed in street fighting. He brought his own family to Egypt, including his father and brothers, and gave them command positions and iqtaʿ assignments.

In September 1171, with the last Fatimid caliph, al-ʿAdid, ill, Saladin had the Friday sermon, the khutba, delivered in the name of the Abbasid caliph al-Mustadiʿ rather than the Fatimid. The Fatimid caliph died shortly afterward, and the dynasty ended quietly. The moment mattered religiously and politically. It ended the competing Ismaʿili caliphate that had challenged Baghdad's claim for over two centuries, brought Egypt into formal alignment with the Sunni Abbasid caliph, and gave Nur al-Din an ally in a hitherto hostile country. It also gave Saladin access to the Fatimid palace treasures and administrative apparatus.

Historians caution against reading this transition too neatly. Egyptian society was not suddenly Sunni. Ismaʿili belief, loyalty to the Fatimid dynasty, and suspicion of the new rulers persisted, and a conspiracy in 1174 to restore the dynasty was suppressed. Saladin promoted Sunni institutions, especially Shafiʿi and Maliki schools of law, founding madrasas in Cairo and Alexandria and establishing a Sufi lodge in a former Fatimid palace, which gradually changed the religious landscape over subsequent decades. Coptic Christian and Jewish administrators and physicians continued to serve in the bureaucracy. Saladin's relationship with Nur al-Din was also uneasy. He avoided joining Nur al-Din in campaigns against Kerak in the early 1170s, evidently reluctant to place himself where his overlord could call him to account or where a Frankish buffer might be removed. The two men increasingly looked like allies with divergent interests.

Section 35.6

From Zengid Officer to Ayyubid Ruler, 1174–1186

Nur al-Din died in May 1174 and left an eleven-year-old son, al-Salih Ismaʿil. Amalric of Jerusalem died two months later. In a single summer the two principal rulers facing each other across Syria were replaced by minors or by a ruler, Baldwin IV, stricken by leprosy. Nur al-Din's commanders in Aleppo and Damascus competed to control the boy, and the Zengid house in Mosul claimed a share. Saladin used the crisis to intervene, presenting himself as defender of the legacy of his master and guardian of the young heir against selfish emirs. In late 1174 he entered Damascus with little resistance, then advanced on Homs and Hama. Aleppo, which regarded itself as Nur al-Din's true capital, resisted him.

Saladin's campaigns in Syria and the Jazira over the following twelve years are best understood as a combination of war and diplomacy. He defeated Zengid-allied forces at Kurun Hama in 1175, secured his title by obtaining formal recognition from the Abbasid caliph, and survived attempts on his life by Nizari Ismaʿili assassins. He married Nur al-Din's widow, Ismat al-Din Khatun, in 1176, strengthening the claim that he was the heir. He made peace with the Zengid princes when it suited him and fought them when it did not. Aleppo was finally absorbed in 1183, after al-Salih's death, when Saladin granted its ruler, a Zengid relative, compensation elsewhere. Mosul, the Zengid heartland, resisted sieges in 1182 and 1185–1186 and ended in a settlement in which its ruler recognized Saladin's overlordship while retaining local control.

The system that resulted was not a centralized state. Saladin appointed relatives and trusted officers to rule major territories: his brother Turan-Shah took Yemen in 1174, another brother, al-ʿAdil, governed Egypt and later parts of Syria and the Jazira, and his sons and nephews held Damascus, Aleppo, and other cities. These holdings were in effect appanages, territories allotted to family members who ruled them with considerable autonomy and owed the head of the house military support and deference. Subordinate rulers, including the Artuqids of Diyar Bakr and the Zengid lords of Mosul and Sinjar, recognized Saladin by having his name included in the khutba and by supplying contingents. This configuration gave Saladin resources that neither Zengi nor Nur al-Din had commanded, while making every summons to war a negotiation.

Historical Debate

Saladin: Culmination of a Zengid Project or Founder of a New Order?

The question: Was Saladin primarily the heir who completed the political and religious program begun by Zengi and Nur al-Din, or did he create a substantially new Ayyubid political order?

Interpretation A — Continuity with the Zengid project: Saladin inherited Nur al-Din's commanders, his Sunni institutions, his rhetoric of jihad, and his strategic aim of encircling the Frankish states. He worked from Egypt because Nur al-Din sent him there. Much of his language, from the recovery of Jerusalem to the defense of Sunni order, continues Zengid themes, and the Aleppo minbar that he installed in the Aqsa Mosque makes the link tangible.
Interpretation B — A new dynastic order: Saladin used the Zengid inheritance to build his own family state, eventually at the expense of Nur al-Din's descendants. His base in Egypt, his appointment of relatives as governors, his marriage alliances, and his settlement with Mosul produced a structure different in scale and composition from the Zengid realm, and one that long outlasted its founder.

Evidence: Both views draw on the chroniclers: Saladin's secretaries and biographers, who stress his loyalty to Nur al-Din, and Zengid-sympathetic writers such as Ibn al-Athir, who stress his ambition. They also draw on administrative continuity, on titles and inscriptions, and on the institutional record of madrasas and endowments.

Why it matters: The question affects how the recovery of Jerusalem is explained: as the fruit of a patient, decades-long program or as the achievement of a new state-builder who exploited accidents. The evidence supports both views in part. Saladin inherited a great deal, yet he reorganized it around a dynasty, a territorial base, and a family system of rule that Nur al-Din had not created.

Student task: Identify two features of Saladin's rule that a student of Interpretation A would emphasize and two that a student of Interpretation B would emphasize, and explain what additional evidence might help decide how much of each to credit.

Section 35.7

Jihad, Legitimacy, and the Road to Hattin

Saladin inherited a political language that had been decades in the making. Religious scholars such as al-Sulami had framed the Frankish presence as a call for jihad, Nur al-Din had attached that framework to a political program, and Saladin made it central to his public image. His secretaries, notably al-Qadi al-Fadil and Imad al-Din al-Isfahani, wrote letters to the Abbasid caliph and to other Muslim rulers that presented his rule as dedicated to restoring Jerusalem and defending Islam. Such letters were propaganda in the ordinary sense, meant to persuade the caliph and rival princes that Saladin deserved recognition and support, yet they were also sincere expressions of a religious outlook shared by many in his circle. It is misleading to treat jihad as either empty rhetoric or the sole explanation for his policies. It justified his demand for contributions from rivals, and he was criticized by contemporaries for fighting Muslims before the Franks.

Relations with the Frankish states in these years combined raiding and diplomacy. Saladin suffered a serious defeat at Montgisard in 1177 against a smaller force under the young Baldwin IV, and he fought further battles at Marj Ayyun and Jacob's Ford in 1179. Truces followed repeatedly, because Saladin needed to consolidate in Syria and the Jazira and the Franks needed to rebuild. Commercial and pilgrim traffic continued across frontiers. The traveler Ibn Jubayr, a Muslim from al-Andalus who crossed the region in the mid-1180s, noted that merchants and pilgrims moved between Muslim and Frankish territory, while the authorities on both sides fought.

The Kingdom of Jerusalem, meanwhile, was becoming politically fragile. Baldwin IV, afflicted by leprosy, reigned until 1185, with regents and rivals contending for influence. His sister Sibylla's marriage to Guy of Lusignan, a newcomer from Poitou, became the center of a succession dispute. After Baldwin V, a child, died in 1186, Sibylla and Guy were crowned, against the wishes of barons such as Raymond III of Tripoli, who had served as regent and who now withdrew in resentment. Reynald of Châtillon, lord of Kerak and Transjordan, had launched raids on Red Sea shipping in the early 1180s and then, probably in late 1186 or early 1187 (the dating is contested), seized a caravan in violation of a truce. Saladin demanded compensation, Guy could not compel Reynald to comply, and Saladin began to mobilize.

These Frankish divisions gave Hattin its political context. Saladin did not win because the Franks were incompetent or because his army was unstoppable. He won because he had assembled, from Egypt, Syria, and the Jazira, a force larger than any earlier Muslim ruler in the region, and because the Franks, deeply divided and pressed by their own lords, committed their field army to a risky march. Raymond of Tripoli's truce with Saladin in early 1187 led to a Muslim reconnaissance party crossing his lands in May, which led to a Frankish defeat at Cresson that spring. The political fracture of the Latin kingdom was thus not a background detail but one of Hattin's direct causes.

Section 35.8

The Battle of Hattin, 1187

In the early summer of 1187 Saladin gathered an army drawn from Egypt, Damascus, Aleppo, the Jazira, and allied Turkmen and Kurdish commanders, estimated by modern scholars at a substantial fraction of the military resources of his realm, although the contemporary figures vary widely and should not be taken at face value. He crossed the Jordan in early July and besieged Tiberias, where the wife of Raymond of Tripoli was sheltering in the citadel. The Franks had mustered what was probably the largest field army the kingdom had ever assembled, with perhaps around 1,200 knights and a larger number of infantry and light cavalry, although estimates vary. At Saffuriyya, their assembly point, they debated whether to relieve Tiberias. Raymond, whose wife was in danger, reportedly advised against marching, arguing that the heat and the lack of water favored waiting. Others, notably Reynald and the Templar Grand Master, urged action. Guy decided to march.

The army left Saffuriyya on 3 July and moved across the dry upland toward Tiberias, harassed throughout the day by Muslim mounted archers. By evening the exhausted force was still short of the lake and camped near the twin-peaked hill known as the Horns of Hattin, without a reliable water source. During the night Saladin's troops surrounded them and, according to Muslim sources, set fire to the dry brush, which drove smoke across the Frankish camp. On 4 July, in the heat, the Frankish infantry, cut off from the cavalry, withdrew onto the hill, and mounted charges failed to break the ring. Raymond of Tripoli and a few others escaped. The rest were killed or captured, among them Guy of Lusignan, Reynald of Châtillon, and the Master of the Templars. The True Cross, carried with the army, was taken.

Saladin executed Reynald, for reasons that accounts frame in terms of his earlier violations of truces, and spared Guy, who was later released. Captured Templars and Hospitallers, whom Saladin regarded as especially dangerous, were executed, reportedly in large numbers, while ordinary knights and soldiers were generally enslaved or held for ransom. Hattin destroyed the kingdom's field army, leaving its towns and castles defended by skeletal garrisons. In the following weeks Saladin took Tiberias, Acre, Nazareth, Caesarea, Nablus, Jaffa, Sidon, Beirut, and Ascalon, often through negotiated surrenders.

Hattin should not be portrayed as a predestined civilizational showdown. It was a decision by one Frankish commander to march in summer heat, in a divided kingdom, against an army that for once had matching resources and an experienced commander who understood the terrain and exploited his rivals' mistakes. The battle's outcome was significant because of the extent to which the Franks had staked their defense on a single army.

Cause and Effect

Why the Kingdom of Jerusalem Lost Its Army at Hattin

Long-term causes

The Egyptian–Syrian consolidation under Nur al-Din and Saladin, which brought together resources that earlier Muslim rulers lacked; the small size of the Frankish population; the kingdom's dependence on castles and a single field army; and factional divisions over the succession.

Short-term causes

Reynald's seizure of a caravan; the collapse of the 1185 truce; Guy's decision to march from Saffuriyya despite advice to wait; heat, dry terrain, and lack of water; and Saladin's choice of ground and tactics, including the use of mounted archers to isolate infantry from cavalry.

Immediate effects

Loss of the field army; capture of the King and the True Cross; rapid surrender of towns and castles; and a wave of refugees toward Tyre and Jerusalem.

Long-term consequences

The recovery of Jerusalem, the Third Crusade, a severely reduced but surviving Frankish coastal presence, and Saladin's standing as the great Muslim champion of the age.

Historical complexity: Hattin cannot be explained by Saladin's genius, by Frankish errors, or by religious zeal alone. It was the result of long-term structural changes that left the kingdom vulnerable, short-term decisions that exploited that vulnerability, and environmental conditions. A different decision at Saffuriyya might have produced a different outcome, but the broader imbalance of resources would still have confronted the Franks.

Section 35.9

The Recovery of Jerusalem and What Saladin Did Not Conquer

Saladin reached Jerusalem in late September 1187. The city was crowded with refugees, defended by few trained soldiers, and led by Balian of Ibelin, a baron who had escaped from Hattin's aftermath and entered the city to bring his family out, but who stayed at the request of the residents and the Patriarch. After a short siege and a first, rejected set of negotiations, Balian and Saladin reached terms. The city surrendered on 2 October 1187. Latin inhabitants could buy their freedom at fixed ransoms. Accounts differ on the child ransom: commonly cited sources give ten dinars for an adult man, five for a woman, and either one or two for a child, and those able to ransom themselves were given about forty days. Eastern Christians who were not of the Latin community were allowed to stay, and many did. Those who could not pay remained subject to enslavement, and sources suggest that many thousands of poorer Latins ended in slavery, though figures are uncertain. Saladin, his brother al-Adil, and some notables released some captives without payment, and Muslim writers dwell on these acts of clemency. Latin writers dwell on the failures of the Frankish elite to ransom the poor.

Students are often invited to contrast this with 1099, when Frankish forces killed a large part of Jerusalem's Muslim and Jewish populations. The comparison has force, but it is too simple as a moral verdict. The contrast reflects differences in circumstance: Saladin had an interest in a negotiated entry, which avoided the destruction of a holy city and demonstrated restraint, while the crusaders in 1099 had stormed walls after a long march. It also reflects different conventions of war and capitulation: a city that surrendered on terms could expect better treatment than one taken by assault. The surrender still involved enslavement and a great displacement. Saladin restored Muslim worship on the Haram al-Sharif, had the cross removed from the Dome of the Rock, and reconsecrated the Aqsa Mosque. The Church of the Holy Sepulchre remained open to Christian pilgrims under Eastern Christian custody.

Equally important is what Saladin did not conquer. The port of Tyre, reinforced by the arrival of Conrad of Montferrat in July, withstood a siege in late 1187 and became a base for Frankish recovery. Antioch, Tripoli, and several northern fortresses survived, partly because they negotiated truces and partly because Saladin's coalition was tired and increasingly difficult to hold together. In 1188 Saladin released Guy of Lusignan, who would soon begin the siege of Acre. Students who assume 1187 ended the Crusader states will misread what followed. The Frankish presence had been badly damaged and cut off from its interior, but its coastal ports remained, tied to the sea and to Europe's capacity to send reinforcements.

Baha al-Din Ibn Shaddad, al-Nawadir al-Sultaniyya (The Rare and Excellent History of Saladin)

He was present for some of the events he describes, including parts of the Third Crusade, and he drew on Saladin's own circle for others.

Paraphrase (not a verbatim quotation) of the section headed, in D. S. Analysis: The source is indispensable because it is written by an eyewitness and colleague, and it records details of daily routine, personal conduct, and negotiation that no other text provides. It also reflects the author's admiration and his position in the court, and it was written after Saladin had become a model of Muslim kingship, so it participates in the building of a legend. Ibn Shaddad says little about the burden of taxation, about the experience of ordinary Egyptians or Syrians, or about the reasons some Muslim rulers distrusted Saladin. It also reflects a court culture in which the language of jihad was the expected language of praise. Historians therefore use it alongside Ibn al-Athir, who was more critical, al-Isfahani, who served as Saladin's secretary, Latin accounts, inscriptions, and administrative documents.

Section 35.10

The Third Crusade and the Treaty of Jaffa

The loss of Jerusalem provoked the Third Crusade. Frederick Barbarossa, Philip II of France, and Richard I of England took the cross, though Frederick drowned in Anatolia in 1190 and his army largely dispersed. The central episode was the siege of Acre, begun by Guy of Lusignan in 1189 and lasting nearly two years, in which Saladin's army besieged the besiegers while the city's Muslim garrison held out. When Philip and Richard arrived in 1191, the combined pressure proved decisive, and Acre capitulated in July. A dispute over the implementation of the surrender terms led Richard to order the execution of a large number of Muslim prisoners in August, a notorious episode that Muslim writers recorded with outrage.

Richard then marched south along the coast. At Arsuf in September 1191 he repulsed Saladin's attempt to disrupt his column and held the field, and he went on to retake Jaffa and other coastal points. He twice approached Jerusalem but did not attack it, judging that the city could not be held without control of the surrounding country and adequate water and supplies. Saladin, for his part, could not dislodge the Franks from the coast. Both leaders faced political pressure. Richard was anxious about his position in Europe, where Philip had returned and his brother John was scheming. Saladin's coalition was weary, and his lieutenants were reluctant to sustain another campaign. Conrad of Montferrat, the contender for the kingship, was murdered by Nizari assassins in April 1192, and Henry of Champagne became ruler of the kingdom.

In September 1192 Richard and Saladin agreed to the Treaty of Jaffa, a truce that contemporary sources generally give as lasting three years and eight months. The Franks kept the coast from Tyre to Jaffa; Ascalon's walls were to be dismantled; Jerusalem remained in Muslim hands; and unarmed Christian pilgrims were to be allowed to visit the holy places. The treaty corresponds to the recurring pattern of the chapter: war concluding not in total victory but in negotiated coexistence. Richard left in October. Saladin had held Jerusalem and ended the threat of Frankish conquest in the interior, while the Franks retained a strengthened coastal presence centered on Acre, which became the effective capital of the kingdom.

Continuity and Change Over Time

The Eastern Mediterranean in 1144 and 1193

What Changed

  • Egypt, Damascus, Aleppo, and much of northern Mesopotamia came under one family's leadership
  • The Fatimid caliphate disappeared, and Egypt aligned with the Abbasid caliph
  • Jerusalem returned to Muslim rule, and the Kingdom of Jerusalem became a coastal state centered on Acre
  • Sunni madrasas, colleges, and Sufi institutions expanded across Egypt and Syria
  • Jihad became a central language of political legitimacy

What Stayed the Same

  • Political authority remained divided among dynastic households and military elites
  • Truces, trade, and diplomacy continued between Muslim and Frankish territories
  • Frankish states survived on the coast and in the north
  • Local populations, including Christians, Jews, and rural cultivators, continued their lives under changing rulers
  • Military rule depended on Turkish and Kurdish commanders and iqtaʿ revenue

Who experienced change most directly? Military and religious elites, Fatimid officials, Jerusalem's Latin residents, and people displaced by the campaigns felt the changes sharply. Cultivators and many townspeople saw more continuity, with a new ruler's tax collectors and garrison replacing the old ones.

Why the pattern matters: It shows that Saladin's achievement was real and that it was bounded. The region became more coordinated without becoming unified.

Section 35.11

Saladin's Death and the Ayyubid System of Government

Saladin died in Damascus in March 1193, only months after the treaty. He left numerous sons and brothers and an empire that was a family coalition rather than a unified administration. His eldest son al-Afdal took Damascus and held a nominal seniority, al-Aziz Uthman took Egypt, and al-Zahir Ghazi took Aleppo, while Saladin's brother al-Adil held territories in the Jazira and Transjordan. Within a few years the family had quarreled. Al-Aziz and al-Adil combined against al-Afdal, al-Adil took Damascus in 1196, and after al-Aziz's death in 1198, al-Adil took Egypt by 1200 and became the dominant Ayyubid ruler, governing through sons and relatives installed in major cities. This process illustrates the normal mechanics of Ayyubid politics: rule meant holding a territory, recognizing a senior relative, and competing for greater shares when opportunities arose.

Administration built on earlier Egyptian and Syrian practice. The revenues of the agricultural land, tolls, markets, and mints supported the ruler and his household troops. Much of the army was paid through the iqtaʿ, a grant of the right to collect the revenue from a specified territory in return for military service, which continued practices established under the Seljuks and, in a different form, under the Fatimids. Citadels, which Saladin and his successors built or rebuilt in Cairo, Damascus, Aleppo, Bosra, and elsewhere, housed garrisons, treasuries, and the ruler's administration. Fortifications went up across Syria, and the walls of Cairo were extended. Jerusalem's own walls were rebuilt and then dismantled in 1219 by al-Muʿazzam of Damascus, who feared that the Franks might capture a fortified city.

Ayyubid rulers patronized Sunni religious and educational institutions on a large scale. They founded madrasas, colleges of law, Sufi lodges, hospitals, and endowments (waqf), and the spread of these institutions changed Egypt's religious landscape and strengthened the class of scholars who provided judges, teachers, and administrators. Christians and Jews continued to live under the protection arrangements of Islamic law, paid the poll tax, and served in administration and in trades such as medicine, finance, and scribal work. The physician and philosopher Moses Maimonides, a Jewish scholar who settled in Fustat and served in the circle of Saladin's officials, exemplifies the place such people held. Commerce flourished in Alexandria and Damietta, where Venetian, Pisan, and Genoese merchants traded under agreements that Saladin and later rulers renewed. The Ayyubids therefore combined war against Frankish states with lively commercial ties to Italian city-states.

Historical Vocabulary
Iqtaʿ

An iqtaʿ was an assignment of the right to collect revenue, usually from agricultural land, made by a ruler to a military officer in lieu of a cash salary. The holder, the muqtaʿ, used the income to support himself and a body of soldiers. Under the Seljuks the system became widespread in Iraq, Syria, and Iran, and under the Ayyubids it was a major means of paying the army in Egypt and Syria.

The term is sometimes translated as "fief," but the comparison is misleading. The holder did not normally own the land, exercise hereditary lordship over its peasants in the manner of a European lord, or pass it automatically to heirs. Assignments could be reassigned, and rulers used that flexibility to reward, discipline, or balance commanders. It mattered because it tied military loyalty to revenue, but it also gave officers an interest in the regions they held, and it was one of the mechanisms that made Ayyubid politics decentralized.

Everyday Life and Social History

Markets, Madrasas, and Changes of Ruler

For most inhabitants of Cairo, Damascus, Jerusalem, Aleppo, and Acre, the great events of these decades were experienced through taxes, markets, garrisons, and the availability of grain. Cairo and Fustat supported large populations of artisans and merchants, and Alexandria and Damietta tied Egypt to Mediterranean commerce. Damascus, watered by the Barada and surrounded by orchards, was a city of scholars, merchants, and craftsmen, and Nur al-Din's and Saladin's patronage of schools and hospitals gave local residents new institutions. Aleppo was a major commercial and military center in the north, repeatedly affected by earthquakes, including a severe one in 1170. Jerusalem's pilgrim economy, sustained by visitors of several faiths, was disrupted by each change of ruler. Acre, a busy port, hosted Italian, Syrian, and Jewish merchants under Frankish jurisdiction.

Religious minorities, women, and non-elite groups appear in the sources only indirectly. Endowment deeds, which record women as founders and supporters of schools and lodges, suggest that elite women held property and patronized institutions, and the women of ruling households, such as the wives and sisters of Nur al-Din and Saladin, sometimes held political influence. The experience of rural cultivators, who bore the burden of taxation and sometimes of military campaigning across their fields, survives mainly in fiscal and legal records and in complaints by officials. Enslaved people, notably the military slaves and domestic servants of the elite, formed a growing presence, and the captives taken at Hattin and Jerusalem are a reminder that war produced both ransom and slavery.

Section 35.12

Ayyubid Diplomacy, the Limits of Unity, and the Mamluk Horizon

Under al-Adil (r. 1200–1218) and his son al-Kamil (r. 1218–1238 in Egypt), the Ayyubids turned from conquest to diplomacy. Al-Adil preferred truces with the Franks and cooperation in trade, and the Franks did the same, partly because the Latin kingdom was weakened by its own succession crises and lacked the resources to threaten the Ayyubids directly. The Fifth Crusade, launched in 1217, targeted Egypt, on the reasoning that Egypt was the source of Muslim power. Crusaders besieged Damietta from 1218, al-Adil died during the siege, and al-Kamil, who inherited a difficult position, offered to trade Jerusalem in return for withdrawal. The crusading leadership refused, and Damietta fell in November 1219. In 1221, however, the crusading army advancing up the Nile was trapped by the flood and the Ayyubid army and had to surrender and evacuate Damietta.

Al-Kamil's most striking negotiation was with the Holy Roman Emperor Frederick II, who arrived in the Levant in 1228 while under papal excommunication. Al-Kamil was engaged in a dispute with his brother al-Muʿazzam's successors in Damascus and wished to avoid a war with a powerful European ruler, while Frederick had few troops and wished to fulfil his vow to go on crusade. In February 1229 they agreed to a treaty under which Jerusalem, along with Bethlehem, Nazareth, and a corridor to the coast, was returned to Frankish control for a period of ten years, while the Muslim sanctuaries on the Haram al-Sharif remained under Muslim administration. The agreement outraged many Muslims, who criticized al-Kamil, and many Latin Christians, who objected to the arrangement and to the excommunicated emperor. It is nonetheless an essential reminder that Jerusalem's status was negotiable for rulers who judged the benefits of peace to outweigh the symbolic cost, and that holy war did not dictate Ayyubid policy. Jerusalem was lost to Frankish control again in 1244, when Khwarazmian military bands, remnants of forces displaced westward by Mongol expansion and then allied with the Egyptian Ayyubid ruler al-Salih Ayyub, took the city; it remained in Muslim hands thereafter.

The family system that supported Ayyubid diplomacy also produced instability. Every succession produced quarrels among brothers, sons, and nephews. Rulers of Damascus, Aleppo, Homs, Hama, Diyar Bakr, and Egypt made alliances with each other and with Frankish states against rival Ayyubids, and in the 1230s and 1240s Ayyubid princes sometimes negotiated with Franks for support. The system permitted local adaptation and gave rulers a stake in their territories, but it lacked a stable rule of succession and encouraged competition. It also depended on loyal household troops, since provincial rulers needed soldiers they could trust more than the regional levies or allied Turkmen.

One response was to purchase military slaves, mamluks, who were trained, freed, and attached to their master's household. Ayyubid rulers had used such troops from Saladin's time, but the practice increased. Al-Salih Ayyub, who took Egypt in the 1240s after years of family struggle, relied heavily on regiments of Turkish mamluks, and he also employed Khwarazmian mercenaries. The reliance brought short-term benefits: the soldiers were reliable, professional, and dependent on him. It also created a military elite with its own interests that would, within a few years, transform Egypt's politics. The emergence of that elite, the Mongol invasions that changed the region's geopolitics, and the rise of the Mamluk Sultanate are the subject of Chapter 36. The decades of Ayyubid rule covered here left a region in which authority was still negotiated and multi-centered, with new institutions and new military dependencies that the next chapter will show at work.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has shown that the recovery of Jerusalem in 1187 was the culmination of decades of political consolidation rather than an isolated heroic reversal. Zengi's capture of Edessa in 1144 was a regional success by a dynastic ruler who fought Muslims as readily as Franks, and his realm divided at his death. The deeper story is of successive efforts to assemble larger coalitions, each building on the last and each vulnerable to succession crises.

Nur al-Din took the decisive step of joining Aleppo and Damascus and of tying political power to Sunni institutions and the language of jihad. The Second Crusade's failed attack on Damascus helped him by badly damaging a Muslim–Frankish understanding that had protected Damascus's independence. His project illustrates that religious legitimacy and political calculation reinforced each other.

Egypt's role was pivotal. The Fatimid caliphate's weakness, the competition between the Kingdom of Jerusalem and Nur al-Din, and the ambitions of Shirkuh and Saladin transformed Egypt into the strategic center of the eastern Mediterranean. Saladin's rise to the vizierate, his consolidation of power, and his ending of the Fatimid caliphate in 1171 brought Egypt into the Sunni Abbasid sphere and gave him a base, though he still had to win over a society that remained religiously diverse.

After Nur al-Din's death in 1174 Saladin used diplomacy, marriage, military force, and appeals to religious duty to assemble an Ayyubid coalition that included Egypt, Damascus, Aleppo, and most of northern Mesopotamia. It was a confederation of family holdings rather than a centralized state, and each summons to war involved negotiation.

Hattin resulted from that consolidation and from Frankish vulnerability. The Kingdom of Jerusalem was divided by a succession dispute, its field army was its main defense, and Reynald's actions and Guy's decision to march in summer heat compounded the danger. Saladin's victory cannot be reduced to individual brilliance or to religious zeal, and it does not show the outcome was inevitable.

The surrender of Jerusalem was negotiated, involved ransom and enslavement, and left Tyre, Antioch, Tripoli, and several other Frankish holdings intact. The Third Crusade and the Treaty of Jaffa confirmed this outcome: Muslim Jerusalem, a Frankish coast, and pilgrim access. The treaty reflects the period's recurrent pattern of negotiated coexistence rather than total victory.

After Saladin's death the Ayyubid family divided his territories, quarreled, and reconstituted a dominant branch under al-Adil, who ruled through relatives in major cities. Ayyubid government relied on iqtaʿ, citadels, military households, and patronage of Sunni institutions, and continued earlier Egyptian and Syrian practice while supporting lively commercial ties with Italian merchants.

Al-Kamil's negotiation with Frederick II in 1229 returned Jerusalem to Frankish control for a time. This underscores that holy war did not dictate policy. The limits of Ayyubid unity, together with reliance on purchased mamluk troops, prepared the way for the political changes of Chapter 36. The deepest lesson is that even after 1187 the region remained multipolar, negotiated, and institutionally diverse.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Atabeg
A Turkish military guardian of a Seljuk prince who often became the real ruler of a city or province, as Zengi did in Mosul and Aleppo.
Zengids
The dynasty founded by Zengi, ruling Mosul, Aleppo, and, under Nur al-Din, Damascus.
Ayyubids
The dynasty of Saladin's family (from his father Ayyub), ruling Egypt until 1250 and major Syrian and upper-Mesopotamian principalities into the mid-thirteenth century, with some local branches surviving longer.
Jihad
Striving in the path of God, including armed struggle; in this period a language used to justify and mobilize political and military campaigns against the Franks and sometimes against Muslim rivals.
Iqtaʿ
An assignment of revenue from a territory to a military officer in lieu of salary, in return for military service.
Vizier
The chief minister of a ruler; in late Fatimid Egypt often the real holder of power.
Khutba
The Friday sermon, in which the ruler's name was mentioned as a sign of recognition of his authority.
Appanage
A territory assigned to a member of a ruling family, who governed it with autonomy under the head of the family.
Madrasa
A college of Islamic law and related subjects, usually supported by an endowment.
Waqf
An endowment of property for a pious or charitable purpose, such as a school, hospital, or mosque.
Bimaristan
A hospital, often with teaching functions, funded by endowments.
Mamluk
A purchased and trained military slave who, upon manumission, served as a soldier in his master's household.
Fatimid caliphate
The Ismaʿili dynasty ruling Egypt from 969 until Saladin ended it in 1171.
Franks
The term used by Arabic sources for Latin Christians from Western Europe and their descendants in the Levant.
True Cross
A relic venerated as a fragment of the cross of Christ, carried by the Frankish army and captured at Hattin.
Horns of Hattin
A twin-peaked volcanic hill near Tiberias, where the Frankish army was defeated in 1187.
Hudna
A truce or armistice between rulers, regularly used in relations with the Franks.
Nizari Ismaʿilis
A branch of Ismaʿilism based in fortresses in Iran and Syria, known to Crusaders as Assassins, which targeted several political leaders.
Dhimmi
A non-Muslim subject (Christian, Jewish, and others) under Muslim rule, entitled to protection in return for payment of the poll tax.
Citadel
A fortified complex within a city, housing the ruler, garrison, and treasury.
Minbar of Nur al-Din
A carved wooden pulpit commissioned in Aleppo for the Aqsa Mosque and installed there after 1187, a symbol of a long-standing ambition.
Kitab al-Jihad
The early twelfth-century treatise by al-Sulami of Damascus on jihad, read in retrospect as a precursor of Nur al-Din's ideology.
Treaty of Jaffa (1192)
The truce between Richard I and Saladin leaving the Franks the coast and Muslims Jerusalem, with pilgrim access.
Treaty of 1229
The agreement between al-Kamil and Frederick II returning Jerusalem to Frankish control without a battle.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it explains how a fragmented region produced a coalition capable of retaking Jerusalem, and in doing so it corrects two common simplifications. One treats 1187 as a spontaneous Muslim uprising against the Crusades; the other treats Saladin as a singular genius who overturned history. The evidence instead shows long processes of dynastic expansion, institution-building, diplomacy, and ideological development, combined with Frankish weaknesses and contingent decisions.

The chapter also reveals a region that was multipolar and negotiated. Muslim rulers fought one another, allied with Franks, and made peace for reasons of state, and Frankish rulers did the same. Egypt, Syria, and the Jazira were connected through family networks, military households, scholars, merchants, and pilgrim routes that did not stop at confessional boundaries. Attention to Armenians, Eastern Christians, Jews, Copts, Kurds, Turks, and Italian merchants helps prevent an overly binary picture.

Later developments were shaped by what happened here. The Ayyubid reliance on family appanages and on purchased military slaves, the spread of madrasas and other Sunni institutions, the changed status of Jerusalem, and the coastal Frankish presence all carried forward into Chapter 36, where Mongol power and the Mamluk Sultanate transformed the region once more. Saladin's memory, in turn, acquired a long afterlife in both Muslim and European tradition, which students should treat as a historical subject in its own right.
