Syria and Lebanon under the French Mandate

A History of the Arab World: From Ancient Crossroads to the Modern Arab World

Chapter Opening

On 1 September 1920, in Beirut, the French High Commissioner proclaimed a state called Greater Lebanon. Five years later, French artillery and aircraft struck rebel-held quarters of Damascus. Twenty-six years after the proclamation, in 1946, the last foreign troops left territories that had by then become two separate sovereign republics. Between those dates lies a history that cannot be reduced to imperial domination alone, or to a story of two nations waiting for their independence to arrive.

Accounts of the Beirut proclamation describe a ceremony at the High Commissioner's residence attended by the Maronite Patriarch, Elias Hoayek, and by Muslim religious leaders of the city. The scene condenses much of this chapter's subject. It was an act of French imperial authority, issued by a French general acting under a decree. It also answered a petition that Lebanese Christian leaders, with the Patriarch at their head, had pressed on the Paris Peace Conference in 1919. And it enlarged the old autonomous Mount Lebanon with coastal cities, the Beqaa Valley, and southern and northern districts whose inhabitants, many of them Sunni and Shi'i Muslims, had not asked to join it and in many cases opposed it. A single event thus contained French strategy, Lebanese aspiration, and Lebanese dissent.

The Damascus of 1925 shows the other side of the mandate. The previous chapters explained how a short-lived Arab kingdom in Syria was destroyed at Maysalun in July 1920 and how the League of Nations confirmed French authority over Syria and Lebanon. This chapter begins where those chapters leave off. It asks what France actually did with the authority it had taken, what institutions it built, what it divided, whom it relied on, and how the people it governed responded. Responses ranged from armed revolt to constitutional bargaining, from boycotts and general strikes to quiet cooperation, from literary and educational projects to the everyday work of farming, trading, and migrating.

The chapter treats four problems in turn. The first is territory: why France divided the Syrian territories into several political units, enlarged Lebanon, and repeatedly redrew internal boundaries. The second is political authority: how French High Commissioners, local governments, army officers, and nationalist organizations competed to control the institutions of the state. The third is society and identity: how religious communities, regional interests, economic structures, and intellectual movements shaped state formation without determining any individual's political loyalty in advance. The fourth is independence: why agreements negotiated again and again failed to deliver sovereignty, and how the Second World War altered the balance of power in 1941 and after.

The chapter's central argument is that French rule was neither an uninterrupted sequence of repression nor a benevolent process of preparing territories for self-government. French administration was marked by territorial fragmentation, administrative experimentation, selective cooperation with local elites, severe violence when authority was challenged, and constitutional promises hedged by reservations. Syrians and Lebanese were not passive recipients of these policies. They revolted, negotiated, petitioned, organized parties, founded schools and newspapers, and argued bitterly with one another over what the countries they lived in ought to be. Two states emerged from one mandate system, and the differences between them were produced by both French design and local politics.

Historians reconstruct this period from an unusually rich and unusually partisan documentary record. French sources include High Commissariat reports, military records, and annual reports to the League of Nations' Permanent Mandates Commission. Syrian and Lebanese sources include constitutions and parliamentary debates, newspapers, memoirs of political leaders, petitions, and later oral testimony from participants in the revolt. British and American diplomatic correspondence adds outside perspectives, especially for the crises of 1943 and 1945. Every one of these sources has an angle. French reports often justified French policy, nationalist memoirs sometimes enlarged the unity of the movements they celebrated, and diplomatic records reflected the strategic interests of the powers that wrote them. Students should therefore read the evidence presented in this chapter critically.

The chapter also observes boundaries set by the textbook's division of labor. Chapter 55 explained wartime Arab politics and the collapse of Ottoman authority. Chapter 56 explained the peace settlements, the mandate system as international law, and the battle of Maysalun. Chapter 57 treated the Hashemite and British states of Iraq and Transjordan. This chapter summarizes those earlier developments only as needed, and it concludes in 1946. Palestine, the subject of Chapter 59, and the wars that followed in 1948, treated in Chapter 61, appear here only where they directly touched Syria and Lebanon.

Historical Context

Before 1918, the territories discussed in this chapter belonged to the Ottoman provinces of Syria, Aleppo, and Beirut and to the special autonomous district of Mount Lebanon (the Mutasarrifate, created in 1861 under the guarantee of European powers). Chapter 54 explained how Ottomanism, Arab cultural nationalism, and Syrian regional loyalties coexisted before the war. Ottoman administration had divided the region into provinces and districts that did not coincide with later state boundaries, and its towns were linked by trade to the Mediterranean, to Anatolia, to Iraq, and to Egypt.

The First World War shattered those arrangements. Wartime requisitioning, conscription, a British blockade, and a catastrophic famine in Mount Lebanon and elsewhere killed large numbers of people and left a lasting memory of suffering. In October 1918, British and Arab forces occupied Damascus and Aleppo, and Faisal's government governed inland Syria while the British and French militarily controlled other zones. Chapter 55 and Chapter 56 explain how the Anglo-French agreements and the San Remo conference allocated Syria and Lebanon to France, and how in July 1920 French forces defeated the Arab army at Maysalun. The chapter you are reading begins in the aftermath of that defeat.

Three features of the inherited landscape are essential. First, society was religiously and regionally diverse, although the diversity was uneven and cannot be reduced to a simple map of sects. Sunni Muslims formed the largest community of Syria's cities and much of the countryside. Alawites, Druze, Ismailis, Twelver Shi'a, Greek Orthodox, Greek Catholics, Maronites, Armenians, Jews, Kurds, Circassians, Turkmen, and Bedouin groups were distributed in different proportions in different regions. Second, the economy was a patchwork. Damascus and Aleppo were commercial centers with long-standing links to their agricultural hinterlands, Beirut and the coast had grown as Mediterranean ports, Mount Lebanon was a densely populated region of silk, terraces, and emigration, and the steppe and desert supported pastoral groups. Third, the postwar order was decided by outside powers, but local elites, notables, merchants, clergy, officers, and village leaders had their own interests, and most of them tried to bargain with whoever held power.

Two cautions guide the chapter. Historians sometimes describe the mandate territories as if French policy invented their communities from nothing. That is wrong: religious and regional differences existed before the French arrived, and some of them were politically significant. It is equally wrong to treat such differences as self-evident, permanent, or automatically determining political action. Mandate institutions gave particular political meaning to some divisions, and local actors also chose to emphasize them or to challenge them. The chapter pays attention to both the structures imposed from above and the choices made from below.

Division I — Occupation, Mandate Government, and Territorial Reconstruction · Section 58.1

From the Syrian Kingdom to French Rule, 1920

The French army that entered Damascus on 25 July 1920, the day after the battle of Maysalun, found a city whose politics had been shaped by eighteen months of Arab government and by intense public debate over independence. The Syrian Kingdom, proclaimed in March 1920 under Faisal, had never secured international recognition, and its ministers had spent much of their energy trying to negotiate with France and Britain while preserving unity among rival factions. Chapter 56 explained the military and diplomatic sequence: General Henri Gouraud's ultimatum, the disbanding of Syrian forces, the battle at Maysalun in which War Minister Yusuf al-Azma was killed, and Faisal's departure from Syria. This section concerns what followed.

The transition from military occupation to civil administration was not instantaneous. During 1920 and 1921, French officers governed through military governors and advisers, and several regions resisted. In the north, armed bands under Ibrahim Hananu fought around Aleppo until 1921. In the coastal mountains, Salih al-Ali led an Alawite rebellion that also resisted the French. Elsewhere, resistance took the form of strikes, political organizing, and refusals to cooperate. The French response to these challenges combined negotiation with selective force, and it laid down habits of governance that persisted: relying on local intermediaries, dividing opponents, and using military columns against defiant districts.

French policy also drew on a long tradition of French interest in the Levant. French merchants, Catholic missions, schools, and the protection of Eastern Christians had been features of French influence since the nineteenth century. After 1920 these interests became state policy. Some French officials, especially those with military backgrounds, saw Syria as a strategic position for the French empire in the eastern Mediterranean. Others, in the Foreign Ministry and in colonial circles, emphasized economic opportunity or the cultural prestige of a French presence. A third current, associated with the Catholic and conservative right, emphasized France's historical role as protector of Levantine Christians. These currents did not always agree, and French policy in the Levant often reflected domestic politics in Paris as much as conditions in Beirut or Damascus.

The new regime also inherited the people and the institutions of the Syrian Kingdom. Many former officials, officers, and politicians went into exile in Egypt, Palestine, Transjordan, or Europe, and from there they organized opposition. Some, such as the Damascene nationalist Abd al-Rahman Shahbandar, were sentenced in absentia. Others stayed and made their peace with the new order, becoming ministers, mayors, or provincial officials. The mandate government drew from both groups over the following years. The tension between those who cooperated and those who refused would remain a constant of Syrian politics, and the labels "collaborator" and "patriot" were often applied more sharply in retrospect than they were lived at the time.

Division I · Section 58.2

The League of Nations Mandate and French Imperial Authority

The mandate was a new legal form. Article 22 of the League of Nations Covenant provided that certain territories detached from the defeated empires were not ready to stand alone, and that their administration should be entrusted to advanced nations acting as "mandatories" on behalf of the League. Syria and Lebanon were classed among the communities whose existence as independent nations could be provisionally recognized, subject to administrative advice and assistance until they could stand alone. The allocation to France was agreed at San Remo in April 1920. The League Council confirmed the text of the Syria and Lebanon mandate in July 1922, and it entered into force in September 1923 after the dispute over the Turkish settlement was resolved.

The mandate's provisions combined obligations with powers. France was required to frame an organic law for Syria and Lebanon within three years, in consultation with local authorities and respecting the rights, interests, and wishes of the inhabitants. It was to facilitate local self-government, protect religious freedom, ensure that personal status matters were left to the jurisdiction of religious communities, support education, and avoid discrimination. In return, France held responsibility for foreign relations and could station troops and maintain order. The mandate also provided for annual reports to the League's Permanent Mandates Commission, which could question the mandatory power and receive petitions from inhabitants.

In practice, the executive centered on the High Commissioner. The office was held by a senior soldier or diplomat appointed in Paris, reporting to the French Foreign Ministry, and residing in Beirut. The High Commissioner issued legislative decrees, appointed or approved governors and ministers, and could suspend constitutional bodies. Beneath him a Secretary General and a network of delegates and advisers shadowed local administrations. Local officials held formal titles, but French advisers frequently decided policy. It is therefore misleading to read the existence of presidents, parliaments, and cabinets as proof of Syrian or Lebanese self-government; their real authority depended on what the High Commissioner permitted.

The language of the mandate also carried an ideology. "Tutelage" implied that the peoples of the territory were not yet capable of governing themselves and that France's task was to guide them. Many French officials sincerely held this view, and it justified paternalism in education, public works, law, and the army. Syrians and Lebanese responded in different ways. Nationalists argued that the Syrian and Lebanese peoples had already demonstrated their capacity for self-government under Faisal and in earlier Ottoman reform, and that the mandate was a pretext for colonial rule. Many Lebanese Christians, by contrast, viewed French protection as a necessary guarantee. The mandate was therefore both an imposed framework and a contested one.

Historical Vocabulary
Mandate and Tutelage

A mandate was a form of international trusteeship created after the First World War under Article 22 of the League of Nations Covenant. A victorious power administered a former Ottoman or German territory under terms approved by the League and subject to League review. Tutelage was the associated idea that the inhabitants required guidance until they reached political maturity.

The mandate is easily mistaken for colonial rule under another name, or for a genuine preparation for independence. Historians increasingly argue that it was both and neither. The mandate bound France to promote self-government and permitted international scrutiny, yet it also gave France broad powers and an open-ended timetable, and the French authorities often treated the territories in practice like colonies. Understanding this double character explains why Syrian and Lebanese leaders invoked the mandate's own language to demand that France keep its commitments.

Primary Source Window

The Mandate for Syria and the Lebanon (League of Nations, 1922)

Creator: The Council of the League of Nations, on the basis of a text drafted by France and revised in consultation with other powers · Date: confirmed 24 July 1922; in force 29 September 1923 · Type: International legal instrument

Context: The mandate document defined French powers and obligations. It was drafted in Paris and Geneva by officials of the mandatory power and the League, not by Syrians or Lebanese, who were not parties to it.

Paraphrase: The mandate directs France to prepare, within three years, an organic law for Syria and the Lebanon in agreement with local authorities. It entrusts foreign relations and the maintenance of order to France, protects freedom of conscience and the jurisdiction of religious communities over personal status, and requires the mandatory power to report annually to the League.

Analysis: The text shows how the mandate framed French power as a trust and imposed obligations that nationalists later cited. It also shows what the document does not contain: any fixed date for independence, any guarantee of a single Syrian state, or any role for Syrian and Lebanese representatives in drafting it. Its provisions for religious jurisdiction helped entrench communal institutions. Historians read it alongside French administrative practice to see how far the stated obligations shaped what actually happened.

Division I · Section 58.3

The Partition of Syria into Separate Political Units

In 1920 and 1921 France divided the territory that had been the Syrian Kingdom's domain into several units. A State of Damascus and a State of Aleppo were created, each with its own governor and administration. Along the coast, an Alawite Territory centered on Latakia received its own organization and, later, the title of Alawite State. In the southern volcanic highlands, Jabal Druze was constituted as a separate administrative unit with special arrangements. Greater Lebanon, proclaimed in September 1920, was a further division. Meanwhile, the Sanjak of Alexandretta in the northwest, after the Franco-Turkish agreement of 1921, acquired a special administrative regime within the State of Aleppo to protect the Turkish-speaking population.

The arrangement was not static. In 1922 the States of Damascus, Aleppo, and the Alawite Territory were grouped into a Syrian Federation with a federal council and a president, a structure that gave regional governments considerable autonomy but also complicated administration. The federation proved fragile. The Alawite State withdrew in 1924, and in December 1924 Damascus and Aleppo were merged into a unified State of Syria, while the Alawite State and Jabal Druze remained separate. These changes show that French policy was experimental and reactive. Officials adjusted boundaries according to security needs, budget pressures, and local politics, and they were influenced by competing views within the French administration.

What motivated partition? French officials offered several justifications. They argued that Syria lacked national unity, that religious and regional communities had distinct interests that required protection, and that decentralization would permit more efficient government. Strategic considerations also operated. Smaller units were easier to control, and dividing the area weakened the nationalist center in Damascus, where the Arab government had been based. Economic reasoning appeared too, especially in the case of Lebanon, where French and Lebanese advocates argued that the mountain required fertile and port territories to survive. Local actors also took part: some minorities, notably some Alawite and Druze leaders, welcomed separate status as a protection against rule from Damascus, while others, such as Aleppo's nationalist notables, feared that partition would weaken the commercial relations of their city.

Nationalists denounced the division as a French strategy to fragment the Syrian nation and keep it weak. Syrian unity became one of their principal demands, expressed in every constitutional draft and negotiation. Yet it would be an oversimplification to treat the territorial design as simply a French ethnographic map. Several units did not correspond neatly to religious majorities: Damascus and Aleppo were themselves religiously mixed, and the Alawite Territory contained Sunni, Christian, and Ismaili minorities alongside an Alawite majority. The boundaries emerged from a combination of French security calculation, administrative convenience, and negotiations with local leaders, and they continued to be revised through the 1930s.

Historical Debate

Why Did France Divide Syria?

The question: Was the territorial division of mandate Syria principally a deliberate strategy of "divide and rule," or a more improvised response to security, administrative, and local pressures?

Interpretation A — Deliberate fragmentation: Syrian nationalist historiography, and many later scholars, argue that France systematically exploited religious and regional differences to weaken Syrian nationalism, pointing to the concentration of minority recruitment in the army, to the separation of the Alawite and Druze regions, and to the timing of boundary changes in relation to nationalist activity.
Interpretation B — Improvised pragmatism with real local input: Other historians emphasize that French officials disagreed among themselves, that boundaries were changed repeatedly, that some local leaders actively sought autonomy, and that financial and security constraints shaped decisions at least as much as a master plan. They argue that "divide and rule" captures a real tendency but overstates coherence.

Evidence: Both views draw on French administrative correspondence, decrees, High Commissioner reports, and the memoirs and petitions of local leaders. Disagreements concern how to read intent from documents that reflect internal French debates and the public language of administration.

Why it matters: If partition was a coherent strategy, French rule appears more systematically manipulative; if it was improvised, French policy appears reactive, but its consequences for later politics remain the same. The question also affects how historians treat minority communities, whether as instruments of French policy or as actors with their own agendas.

Student task: Identify one piece of evidence that would distinguish deliberate design from improvised response, and explain what you would look for in French archives.

Division I · Section 58.4

The French Security System

French authority rested ultimately on force. The Army of the Levant was composed of metropolitan French units, colonial troops recruited in North and West Africa, and locally recruited auxiliaries. In 1921 France created the Troupes spéciales du Levant, local units commanded by French officers, which grew into the main instrument of internal security. The Troupes spéciales recruited heavily from rural districts and from minority communities, including Alawites, Druze, Circassians, Armenians, Kurds, and Christians, and also included Sunni Arabs. Recruitment drew on economic need as well as French preference: military pay was a reliable income in poor rural regions with limited opportunities.

Alongside the army, the French built intelligence and police structures. The Service des Renseignements stationed French officers in districts, where they collected information, advised local officials, and intervened in disputes. The Sûreté Générale monitored political activity, and military courts tried political offenders. In desert and steppe regions, specialized bureaus managed relations with Bedouin groups. Air power became important in the 1920s, as bombers and reconnaissance aircraft allowed rapid response to unrest in remote districts, and the French used them in the revolt.

The political consequences of military recruitment were significant and complicated. Nationalists accused France of using minority soldiers against Sunni urban populations, and the army's composition fed the idea of an alliance between France and minorities. Historians now emphasize that the reality was more varied: recruits had diverse motives, many were drawn by wages, and the same communities supplied both soldiers and rebels at different moments. Nonetheless, an army shaped by mandate recruitment became a lasting political legacy. When Syria and Lebanon gained sovereignty, they inherited military forces in whose officer corps and rank-and-file particular communities were heavily represented, a problem that Chapter 64 will examine in connection with later coups.

French security practice also relied on collective punishment. Villages believed to harbor rebels could be fined, searched, and in extreme cases shelled or burned. Such measures were meant to deter resistance but also generated resentment. The tension between a stated mission of guidance and a practice of coercion was visible in everyday encounters with soldiers and officers, and it provided nationalists with powerful material for denunciation.

Historical Vocabulary
Troupes spéciales du Levant

The Troupes spéciales (Special Troops of the Levant) were locally recruited military units, organized from 1921 under French officers, that served alongside metropolitan and colonial French forces. They were the mandate's principal instrument of internal security and, after 1945, the nucleus of the Syrian and Lebanese national armies.

The term should not be read as implying a single ethnic or religious composition. Recruits came from many communities and regions, though some minorities and rural districts were heavily represented. The transfer of command of these units from France to the Syrian and Lebanese governments was one of the decisive issues of the independence struggle, because control of armed force was understood as the test of real sovereignty.

Division I · Section 58.5

Administration, Law, Finance, and Local Government

French administration combined inherited Ottoman structures with French institutions. Ottoman legal codes, land categories, and religious courts continued to operate in many matters, while French officials introduced new codes, reorganized the judiciary, and began cadastral surveys to register land. French advisers sat beside Syrian and Lebanese officials in ministries, and a French-trained civil service gradually emerged. Mandate administration also created new local institutions: municipal councils, representative councils, and provincial administrations with limited powers. Such bodies gave local notables a stage and a source of patronage, but their decisions could be overridden.

Financial administration was organized around shared institutions. The customs revenue and certain public services of Syria and Lebanon were handled by a joint French-controlled department known as the Intérêts communs (Common Interests), which collected customs duties and divided receipts among the territories. A single currency, issued by the Banque de Syrie et du Liban, was tied to the French franc, which linked the local economy to French monetary conditions. Taxes continued Ottoman categories such as the tithe on agricultural produce, supplemented by new levies, and tax collection remained a point of friction in rural districts. Budgets reflected the cost of the military establishment and administrative staff, a burden that critics said served French rather than local needs.

Local political intermediaries were essential. French officials relied on landowning notables, tribal shaykhs, clergy, and urban merchants to implement policy, collect taxes, and keep order. In return, intermediaries obtained appointments, subsidies, protection of property, and influence over local resources. This arrangement strengthened some notable families, which used mandate institutions to consolidate their position, while ordinary villagers and urban workers had little voice. Historians of the mandate often describe the system as one that conserved and rearranged existing social hierarchies rather than transforming them.

The mandate also reshaped relations between the state and religious communities. Personal status law, covering marriage, divorce, inheritance, and family matters, remained with the religious courts and community authorities of each recognized community. This preserved communal institutions and gave religious leaders political importance, but it also meant that legal rights differed according to religious affiliation, a feature that would later complicate debates about citizenship and equality. These patterns of law and administration formed the institutional background for both the Syrian revolt and the Lebanese constitutional experiment treated in the following divisions.

Division II — The Great Syrian Revolt, 1925–1927 · Section 58.6

Jabal Druze and the Origins of Revolt

Jabal Druze, a volcanic highland in southern Syria whose population was predominantly Druze, had a long tradition of local autonomy under the leadership of a few prominent families, among them the al-Atrash. In 1921 France recognized the region as an autonomous unit and, under an agreement with local leaders, installed a Druze governor, Salim al-Atrash. In 1923, after his death, France appointed a French officer, Captain Gabriel Carbillet, as governor. Carbillet launched a program of public works, schools, and administrative reform, and he curtailed the authority of the traditional leadership. Many of the leading families resented this displacement, and the use of forced labor for road building and other projects added to the grievance.

By 1925 the political situation in the Jabal was tense. Local leaders sent delegations to the High Commissioner, Maurice Sarrail, who had arrived in January 1925, to complain about Carbillet and request a more representative arrangement. Accounts of these meetings disagree on details. What is generally agreed is that the delegations obtained no satisfaction, and that several Druze notables who went to Damascus or Beirut to press their case were detained or treated in a way that heightened distrust. Sarrail, a general with republican and anticlerical convictions, was unpopular with many conservative French officials and had little patience for negotiation. His government's decisions during 1925 were also shaped by French domestic politics and by the pressure of a limited military budget.

The documentary record for these months is thinner than the later nationalist narrative suggests, but it is not empty. American consular reporting in August 1925 recorded the arrest and deportation of Druze notables who had been invited to a conference, which supports the broad outline of the story that French authorities treated the Druze delegation with bad faith, even though the precise sequence of invitations, assurances, and arrests remains disputed between French and Druze accounts. The incident mattered because it persuaded many Druze leaders that negotiation with the High Commissioner could not protect them. In a society in which the personal honor of a leading family was a political asset, the detention of notables was a public insult as well as a security measure.

Sultan Pasha al-Atrash emerged as the leader of the resistance. Born into one of the most prominent Druze families, he had fought against Ottoman authorities and had participated in the Arab revolt of 1918. In July 1925 his forces, supported by other Druze leaders, defeated a French column at the battle of al-Kafr, and on 2–3 August they defeated a larger relief expedition at al-Mazra'a. These victories surprised the French command, captured substantial weapons, and demonstrated that a locally recruited force could defeat French regulars in difficult terrain. They also persuaded other communities and leaders in Syria that rebellion was possible.

Terrain and local knowledge explain much of the early success. The Jabal is a rugged volcanic upland whose villages, stone houses, and narrow approaches favored defenders who knew them, while French columns, sent out from garrison towns on the plain, were heavy with artillery and supplies and dependent on a few roads. The Druze fighters were not a standing army. They were village contingents under local commanders, gathered for specific actions and dispersing afterward, a pattern that made them hard to destroy but also hard to sustain over a long campaign. The French command, which had underestimated the rising and relied on units of uncertain quality, found that defeats in the Jabal damaged its prestige across the whole mandate.

Historians debate how local and how national the origins of the revolt were. Some stress the particular grievances of Jabal Druze and the actions of Carbillet and Sarrail, which they treat as the trigger. Others emphasize the broader nationalist ferment in Damascus and the role of political networks, in particular the People's Party founded in 1925 by Abd al-Rahman Shahbandar and the groups that had been organizing since Maysalun. The best accounts hold both together: the revolt began from local grievances, but it spread because the grievances found an existing nationalist language and organization.

Division II · Section 58.7

From Regional Rebellion to Syrian National Uprising

In late summer 1925 Sultan al-Atrash issued a proclamation calling on Syrians to rise for independence and unity of the country. The proclamation framed the rebellion in national rather than communal terms, and in the following months the revolt expanded beyond Jabal Druze. Bands formed in the Hawran plain, in the orchards of the Ghuta surrounding Damascus, and in the Qalamun mountains. In the Ghuta, Hasan al-Kharrat, a Damascene from the Maydan quarter, organized armed groups that combined urban volunteers and rural fighters. In the Hama region, a former Ottoman and Syrian army officer, Fawzi al-Qawuqji, led insurgents who took the city in early October.

The spread of the revolt followed existing networks. The Hawran plain lay beside the Jabal and shared its grain economy and family ties; the Ghuta orchards and the Maydan quarter were connected to Damascus markets; and Hama and Homs, with their own landowning notables and former Ottoman officers, were receptive to a movement that spoke of independence and unity. Political organizers in Damascus, among them members of the People's Party, supplied money, contacts, and a nationalist program. The revolt thus had two faces: a rural insurgency that drew on village loyalties and a political movement that drew on urban nationalist circles, and the two did not always agree on aims or methods.

The revolt reached into Lebanon as well. Fighting spread to the Hasbaya and Rashaya districts on the slopes of Mount Hermon, where rebel groups with Druze participation clashed with French forces in the autumn. The French garrison at Rashaya was attacked, and French reprisals provoked sharp criticism. These episodes show that the revolt crossed the new border between Syria and Lebanon, though the Lebanese state's institutions and its Christian-majority political leadership mostly remained aligned with France.

Leadership of the revolt was never unified. Sultan al-Atrash commanded the Druze fighters and held symbolic authority, but regional commanders acted with considerable independence. Urban politicians and exiles tried to coordinate political and financial support from Egypt, Palestine, and Transjordan, and a revolutionary government was announced at one stage. There were disagreements among leaders over objectives, over relations with the Druze, and over how far to cooperate with the French after the initial victories. Some notables of Damascus supported the revolt cautiously, protecting their property and reputations; others joined openly. This variety complicates later nationalist narratives of a single united movement.

Winter brought a change in French policy but not an end to the fighting. Henry de Jouvenel, who arrived as High Commissioner at the end of 1925, offered negotiations and the promise of political reform, and he sought to separate moderate nationalists from the armed bands. Some notables were tempted, others suspected the offer, and the rebels continued operations around Damascus and in the Hawran into 1926. Meanwhile, exiled nationalists in Cairo, Palestine, and Transjordan appealed to the League of Nations and to public opinion in Europe and the Arab world, presenting the revolt as a national struggle against a mandatory power that had broken its trust. These appeals did not change French policy directly, but they kept the issue before the Permanent Mandates Commission and in the press.

The revolt's social composition was equally varied. Peasants, small landowners, shopkeepers, students, veterans of the Faisal army, Druze and Sunni fighters, some Christians, and Bedouin groups participated, although in different proportions and at different times. The revolt was therefore not a sectarian uprising, but neither was it uniformly supported. Many people remained cautious or neutral, and some, especially those who feared retaliation or who depended on French protection, opposed it. The revolt's reach and limits reveal how fragmented Syrian society remained and how the nationalist cause had to work through local loyalties.

Division II · Section 58.8

French Military Repression and the Bombardment of Damascus

The French response escalated through the autumn of 1925. Reinforcements arrived from North Africa and France, including troops with tanks, artillery, and aircraft, and General Maurice Gamelin took command of the field operations. On 4 and 5 October, after rebels seized Hama, French artillery and aircraft bombarded the city, with significant damage to its quarters. The operation was intended to restore control and to terrorize rebel districts, and its destructiveness drew attention both in Syria and abroad.

The most notorious episode occurred in Damascus. On 18 October, rebel bands entered parts of the city, and the French military and High Commissioner's government responded with artillery fire and air attack in the days that followed. The bombardment struck the old city and parts of the Hariqa and Maydan districts, and fires destroyed many shops and houses in what had been a commercial and residential center. Estimates of civilian deaths vary widely, from several hundred to well over a thousand, and the number of people left homeless was large. The memory of the bombardment became a permanent element of Syrian political memory, symbolized in the city's destroyed quarters and in the stories of families displaced.

The surviving evidence for Damascus consists of competing kinds of source. A telegram sent from Beirut by the American consul, Knabenshue, on 19 October 1925 passed on a telephone report from the American consular officer in Damascus that French forces were bombarding the city with field guns and aircraft and that it was on fire. The same telegram, which is discussed in this chapter's primary source window, also reported that a French expedition against bands near the city had killed about two hundred people, that bodies had been displayed in a public square, and that villages had been burned. Its author wrote at second hand from Beirut and used the French term "brigands" for the fighters, a reminder that even outside observers often adopted the language of the authorities. French officials, for their part, gave lower casualty figures than local sources, and legal commentators writing in 1926 debated whether the bombardment of a town could be justified as a response to an armed rising. The extreme discrepancy among the estimates should be treated as part of the historical problem rather than resolved by selecting the figure that best suits one's sympathies.

International reaction was swift and sharp. Newspapers in Britain, France, and elsewhere reported the bombardment, and the French Chamber of Deputies debated it. The Permanent Mandates Commission of the League of Nations, which met in the following months, questioned the French representative closely and expressed grave concern about the use of force against a town and the treatment of civilians. Sarrail was recalled in late October, and his successor, the civilian politician Henry de Jouvenel, arrived at the end of the year with a mission to restore order and conduct negotiations. The League did not, however, intervene to restrain French military operations; its power was limited to scrutiny and criticism, a limit that Syrians noticed.

The League's treatment of the episode deserves careful reading. The Permanent Mandates Commission could question the French representative, examine the High Commissioner's reports, and receive petitions, and in 1926 it did press France on the destruction at Damascus and on the principle that a mandatory power owed the inhabitants protection rather than collective punishment. But the Commission had no means of independent investigation, relied heavily on French documentation, and could not compel France to change course. Its effect was therefore moral and political, shaping the way France justified its conduct and the way Syrian nationalists later cited the League's criticism, rather than operating as a restraint on French military operations.

French officials defended their actions as necessary to protect lives and property and to suppress an armed rebellion. Their reports emphasized the threat to Christians and to order and minimized the destruction caused by their own shelling. These claims were disputed by Syrian, British, and American observers. The disagreement illustrates a recurring problem for historians of colonial violence: the sources written by those who inflicted the damage are most numerous, and those written by the people who suffered it are fewer and often later. Careful use of newspapers, consular reports, and petitions to the League helps to correct the imbalance.

Division II · Section 58.9

Rural Resistance, Urban Networks, and the Social History of War

The revolt's endurance depended on social networks. In the Ghuta and Hawran, villagers supplied food, shelter, and information to fighters, and many villages bore the cost of French reprisals. Fighters moved between orchards, villages, and the mountains, using local knowledge to evade French columns. Supply lines ran from Damascus merchants and from the exile networks in Transjordan and Palestine, which raised money and procured weapons. These connections show how rural resistance and urban nationalism, often portrayed as distinct, relied on each other.

The refugee dimension is among the best documented. In October 1925 the American consul recommended that the American Red Cross send substantial funds for refugees in the Hawran, a recommendation that reflects both the scale of displacement in that district and the way humanitarian relief entered mandate politics. Families fled burned villages toward Damascus, the Lebanese coast, Palestine, and Transjordan, where relatives and sympathetic authorities provided uneven shelter. The Transjordanian and Palestinian borderlands also served as rear areas for the rebels and as places of exile for leaders after 1927, so that the revolt's geography extended beyond the mandate's own frontiers.

Women's roles are documented in fragments. They carried supplies and messages, sheltered fighters, tended the wounded, and kept households going when men were killed, imprisoned, or in hiding. Women also bore a disproportionate share of displacement and hardship when villages were burned and families fled. Because most surviving sources were written by men, women's experiences must often be reconstructed from memoirs, oral testimony, and incidental references. Later nationalist narratives sometimes honored these contributions in general terms, but the particular stories of individual women remain less documented than those of leaders.

The war's costs were severe. French operations destroyed houses, orchards, and livestock in the Ghuta and Hawran, and many families became refugees, seeking safety in Damascus, in Lebanon, in Palestine, or in Transjordan. Collective fines, confiscations, and executions followed the reoccupation of districts. The disruption to agriculture and commerce had economic effects that lasted beyond the fighting, and it left some communities impoverished and others indebted.

Reprisal followed a pattern. French forces would reoccupy a village, search for fighters and weapons, impose fines or confiscations, and sometimes burn houses or cut orchards to deprive rebels of cover. The Ghuta, whose dense orchards offered concealment close to Damascus, suffered repeatedly, and the loss of trees took years to repair because orchards, unlike grain, cannot be replanted in a season. Troop movements and requisitions also disrupted markets and trade routes, with consequences for merchants and for the cost of food in the cities. For peasants already indebted to landowners and moneylenders, a lost harvest could mean a permanent loss of land, which helps explain why the political memory of the revolt in the countryside differs from the more celebratory memory of the cities.

Different communities experienced the revolt very differently. Many Druze villages bore the heaviest burden and were also the heart of the rebellion. Christian communities in Damascus and in some villages feared reprisals from rebel bands and from the breakdown of order, and some sought French protection, though others joined or supported the revolt. Merchants and landowners in Damascus weighed their economic interests against nationalist sentiment, and many hedged. These divergent experiences matter because they shaped later memories: the revolt could be remembered as a national epic, as a Druze war, or as a catastrophe, depending on who told the story.

Division II · Section 58.10

Suppression, Political Consequences, and Historical Memory

Under Jouvenel and then Henri Ponsot, who became High Commissioner in 1926, French policy combined continued military pressure with limited political offers. French forces recaptured the Ghuta and Hawran during 1926 with heavy fighting, and by 1927 organized resistance in most districts had collapsed. The Druze in the south held out longest, but they were eventually overcome by superior force, supply shortages, and exhaustion. Sultan al-Atrash and other leaders fled to Transjordan, where they lived in exile; many were sentenced in absentia, and a general amnesty was not granted until 1937.

The end of the fighting did not bring immediate normality. Rebel leaders were tried in absentia or pursued, property was confiscated in some cases, and French officers continued to administer the countryside through the Service des Renseignements and the Troupes spéciales. At the same time, the cost of the campaign, the criticism in Geneva and in the French press, and the evident strength of nationalist feeling pushed Ponsot toward a policy of controlled political opening. This is the link between the revolt and the elections of 1928: the same authorities who had bombarded Damascus now permitted nationalist candidates to campaign, because they hoped that elected representatives would accept the mandate's framework.

The revolt failed militarily because French forces possessed superior firepower, air power, and logistics; because rebel leadership was fragmented and lacked sustained supply; and because many Syrians remained uncommitted. But it was not a political failure. It demonstrated the strength of nationalist feeling, embarrassed the French government internationally, and convinced many French officials that governing Syria would require political concessions. In that sense it opened the way to the constitutional phase that followed, in which the nationalists who had avoided or opposed the revolt's violence came to dominate Syrian politics.

The revolt's historical memory has been contested. Syrian nationalist and later state narratives, especially after independence, celebrated it as a national struggle and as the founding moment of modern Syrian patriotism. Sultan al-Atrash became a national hero, and the revolt is commemorated in school curricula and public monuments. Historians have complicated this picture by documenting the revolt's local origins, its internal disagreements, and the limited participation of some regions and communities. They also emphasize that the revolt's meaning has been used by different political groups to advance different claims, including claims about Druze, Sunni, rural, or urban leadership.

Memory has also been shaped by what happened to the participants. Sultan al-Atrash returned from exile under the amnesty of 1937 and remained a prominent figure in Jabal Druze, where his leadership was still significant in 1945. Many fighters who had been peasants returned to their villages without public recognition. The urban politicians who inherited the nationalist movement, particularly in the National Bloc, claimed the revolt's legitimacy while distancing themselves from its violence, and critics from other currents argued that they had profited from sacrifices made by others. Historians therefore read the revolt both as an event and as a resource that later political groups used to define their own authority.

Division III — Syrian Constitutional Nationalism and State Formation · Section 58.11

Nationalism after the Revolt and the Rise of the National Bloc

The suppression of the revolt left Syrian nationalism with a choice of strategies. Armed resistance had demonstrated its limits, and French authorities, anxious to restore calm and answer international criticism, signaled willingness to permit political activity within the mandate's framework. Nationalist leaders, many of them urban notables from Damascus, Aleppo, Homs, and Hama, gradually concluded that the most promising route to independence lay through organized political pressure, constitutional demands, and negotiation. This approach, sometimes called constitutional or "positive" nationalism, characterized Syrian politics for the next decade.

In 1927 a coalition formed that became known as the National Bloc (al-Kutla al-Wataniyya). Its leadership included Hashim al-Atassi, a former Ottoman and Faisal-era official from Homs, Ibrahim Hananu of Aleppo, who had fought the French in 1919–21 and was later prosecuted and then released, Shukri al-Quwatli and Jamil Mardam Bey of Damascus, Faris al-Khuri, a Christian politician and lawyer, and Sa'dallah al-Jabiri of Aleppo. The Bloc was less a disciplined party than a coalition of notables and urban political networks that shared the goals of Syrian unity and independence. Its members were drawn from landowning and merchant families, the professions, and the old Ottoman administrative class, and its leadership reflected that social base.

The Bloc's origins were in a series of meetings and negotiations among notables in 1927 and after, and its organization remained loose, with local committees in the major cities and a leadership that deliberated informally. It had no mass membership in the modern sense, but it had a recognized network of leaders who could call a strike, issue a statement, or send a delegation, and its authority rested on the standing of those leaders in their cities. Ibrahim Hananu's trial in 1922, in which he was acquitted, and his death in 1935 gave the movement a respected figure from the generation that had fought the French in 1919–1921. The Bloc's rivalry with the People's Party and with Shahbandar, who was in exile for much of the period, shaped the debates over whether to accept the constitutional framework or to hold out for a more radical break.

The Bloc's strategy combined cooperation and refusal. It accepted participation in elections and parliaments and negotiated with French authorities, while refusing to endorse arrangements that compromised unity or independence. It used the press, public meetings, strikes, and boycotts, and it cultivated international opinion in Britain, in the League of Nations, and in the Arab press. Rival currents challenged this approach. Shahbandar's People's Party and exiled revolutionaries criticized the Bloc as too accommodating, while some local leaders pursued their own bargains with the French. The Bloc's cohesion was always fragile, and its coalition of notables sometimes split over questions of patronage, treaties, and leadership.

Nationalist politics also developed new forms. Youth organizations, scouts, and student groups, along with trade unions and professional associations, brought new participants into public life, and the nationalist cause acquired a mass dimension in demonstrations and strikes that the notables led but could not entirely control. These developments foreshadowed the more radical movements of the late 1930s and 1940s and the ideological parties examined in Chapter 64.

Division III · Section 58.12

The Constituent Assembly of 1928

In 1928 High Commissioner Ponsot allowed elections for a Constituent Assembly to draft a Syrian constitution, an implementation of the mandate's call for an organic law. The elections, held in the spring, were managed by the French authorities but resulted in a victory for nationalist candidates, and Hashim al-Atassi was elected president of the assembly. A committee drafted a constitution that affirmed Syria as a republic, with a parliamentary system and a president elected by the legislature. The draft also asserted the unity of the Syrian territories, treating the Alawite, Druze, and other separately administered regions as parts of a single Syria.

The constitutional drafting immediately collided with French reservations. The draft contained provisions that, in French eyes, infringed on the powers reserved to the mandatory power. Several articles dealt with the presidential authority to command the army, the conduct of foreign relations, and the relationship between the Syrian state and the mandate. The assembly's refusal to alter these provisions led to a deadlock. Ponsot demanded changes, the nationalist majority refused, and the assembly was adjourned in August 1928 and not allowed to resume.

The assembly's work was done in a climate of high public attention. Elections to it were held in stages in the spring of 1928, and nationalist candidates won most of the seats. A drafting committee prepared a constitution based on parliamentary models and on the principles that Syrian nationalists had advanced since 1920, and the draft was debated article by article. The contested provisions concerned the supreme command of the army, the right to conduct foreign relations and conclude treaties, and clauses that implied that Syria's territory included the regions administered separately. The High Commissioner insisted on modifications and, when the majority refused, adjourned the assembly. The outcome showed that France was willing to grant the form of representative government only so long as its mandatory prerogatives were unchallenged.

The controversy over Syrian unity was also central. The French government saw the separate status of Jabal Druze and the Alawite State as part of its commitments to local populations and as a lever of influence. Nationalists regarded unity as a core principle and rejected treating regional separation as permanent. The Constituent Assembly's debates thus reveal the central conflict of the mandate: who had the right to define the territorial shape and sovereign powers of the Syrian state? The assembly asserted that Syrians did; France insisted that its responsibilities limited them.

Historians study the assembly because it shows what the nationalists wanted at their moment of greatest organized legal strength: a unified, parliamentary Syria with control over its army and foreign policy. It also shows the limits of mandate liberalism. France permitted elections and debate, but would not tolerate a constitution that questioned the legal basis of its authority.

Division III · Section 58.13

The Constitution of 1930 and the Syrian Republic

After nearly two years of impasse, Ponsot dissolved the Constituent Assembly in May 1930 and imposed a constitution by his own decree. The High Commissioner's decree (no. 3111) is dated 14 May 1930, and the constitution was published and promulgated at Damascus on 22 May 1930; the two dates are often run together, and students should distinguish the legal act from its publication. The text kept most of the nationalists' draft but removed or changed the contested provisions and added Article 116, which specified that nothing in the constitution would contradict the obligations the mandate imposed on France. The same day, separate decrees established statutes for the Alawite and Jabal Druze territories, confirming their distinct administrations.

Under the constitution, Syria was a republic with a parliamentary chamber, a president elected by the parliament, and a council of ministers. The structure provided for a degree of parliamentary politics. Elections were held in 1932, in which the National Bloc obtained a significant share of seats, and the parliament elected Muhammad Ali al-Abid as president. Parliament debated legislation, budgets, and relations with France, and the press and political parties operated with considerable freedom in comparison to the pre-1925 period. Syrians gained experience in parliamentary procedure, coalition building, and electoral campaigning.

But the constitution's authority remained limited. The High Commissioner retained legislative powers over matters connected with the mandate, French advisers continued to shadow ministries, and the Troupes spéciales and customs administration remained under French control. Parliamentary decisions could be overridden, and the High Commissioner could suspend the assembly. The mandate's reservation clause made the entire constitutional order conditional on French consent. Nationalists were therefore divided over how to use the constitution: some saw it as a platform for gradual gains, while others thought participation legitimized French rule.

French negotiators tried repeatedly to secure a treaty that would regulate the relationship between France and Syria. The most important attempt before 1936 came in 1933. A treaty negotiated under the French High Commissariat was signed on 16 November 1933 by the Syrian prime minister, Haqqi al-Azm. It offered a measure of recognition of Syrian sovereignty and a path toward entry into the League of Nations, but it preserved extensive French military and economic privileges and left the separate status of the Alawite and Druze regions in place. Its reception in Damascus was hostile. On 21 November the Syrian parliament confronted the government, and the National Bloc led its rejection. The records preserved in the British Library's India Office files document the objections raised at the time: the privileges reserved to France, the open-ended character of the French presence, and the territorial separation of parts of Syria. Syrian political memory placed this refusal, like the 1928 assembly, in the tradition of resistance to a settlement that would have kept the substance of mandate control behind the language of independence.

Division III · Section 58.14

The Social and Economic Foundations of Syrian Politics

Syrian politics rested on a particular social order. Large landowners, many of them living in Damascus, Aleppo, Homs, and Hama, controlled vast agricultural estates worked by tenants and sharecroppers. The privatization of land and the registration of landholdings under late Ottoman and mandate law often favored those with influence and capital, and communal village lands were increasingly absorbed into large properties. Peasants bore heavy rents and debts, and rural inequality shaped political attitudes and later radical movements. Landowning notable families also dominated the National Bloc, which meant that nationalist politics reflected and protected their interests.

This social order helps explain the nature of nationalist politics. The National Bloc's leaders were landowners, merchants, lawyers, and former Ottoman officials, and their ability to negotiate with the French depended on their local standing. Their political program emphasized independence and unity rather than social reform, and they were cautious about land reform or labor demands that might disturb their own interests. At the same time, the nationalist movement drew on popular grievances, and the pressures of the Depression, which pushed the poor toward protest, forced the leaders to take account of a broader public. In this respect the movement of the 1930s differed from the elite politics of the early mandate.

Agricultural production varied by region. Wheat and barley were grown on the Hawran and the plains around Homs and Hama, cotton in the Aleppo region and later in the Jazira in the northeast, and fruit orchards in the Ghuta around Damascus. The Jazira was increasingly settled in the 1920s and 1930s by agricultural colonization, with the participation of Kurdish and Arab tribal groups and refugee Assyrians and Armenians. Damascus and Aleppo had long been commercial and craft centers, but their textile industries faced competition from imports, and the mandate's tariff and currency arrangements affected merchants differently, benefiting those tied to French trade while harming some artisans.

The world economic crisis of the early 1930s struck hard. Falling prices hurt cultivators and exporters, indebtedness spread, and unemployment increased in towns. Economic distress contributed to the sharpening of nationalist politics and to unrest, and it also gave new weight to labor organizations. Trade unions, some led by communists or nationalists, organized transport, tobacco, and textile workers. The French authorities monitored these activities and sometimes repressed them. A French tobacco monopoly established in 1935 became a particular target of nationalist criticism as an example of economic dependence.

Economic change also affected the urban environment. Damascus and Aleppo expanded beyond their old walls with new neighborhoods and French-style administrative buildings, and Beirut grew rapidly. Roads and railways linked regions more closely. But the benefits were unevenly distributed, and many of the rural poor experienced the mandate chiefly through taxes, conscription for military service, and the loss of land and security.

Division III · Section 58.15

Regional Interests and the Limits of Syrian Unity

Syrian nationalists proclaimed unity, but the country's regions had different interests and histories. The Alawite coastal region, administratively separate, was poor, largely rural, and dominated by landlords and urban creditors who were often Sunni. Alawite leaders divided over their future. Some, including a number of notables, wanted to join Syria, while others, notably the charismatic religious figure Sulayman al-Murshid, favored continued autonomy under French protection and later asked for guarantees. The question of how an Alawite population long marginalized by Sunni urban elites would be integrated into a Syrian state was real and unresolved.

Aleppo deserves particular attention because its political culture differed from Damascus's. The city had been oriented toward Anatolia and Iraq, and the severance of those links by the new borders damaged its commerce; its leaders, among them Ibrahim Hananu and Sa'dallah al-Jabiri, emphasized the economic cost of the division of Syria and resisted any settlement that would leave their city on the periphery of a Damascus-centered state. The Alawite coast and Jabal Druze had to be integrated into a Syrian system, and the experience of 1936 and after showed that this required political bargaining, not merely proclamation. The Jazira's mixed society, with Kurdish, Arab, Assyrian, and Christian communities, added a further problem of local identity and security. National unity, in other words, was a political project in which the strategies of several regions had to be reconciled.

In Jabal Druze, the revolt's heritage and the authority of the al-Atrash family shaped local politics. After 1927 the region returned to French-supervised autonomy, and Druze leaders debated whether to accept integration with Syria or to preserve their distinct status. In the north, Aleppo's commercial elite had economic and political interests that sometimes diverged from Damascus, partly because of Aleppo's historic trade links with Anatolia and Iraq, which had been severed by new borders. Rivalry between the two cities, and between Aleppo's National Bloc leaders and Damascus's, marked the movement. In the northeast, Kurdish, Assyrian, Arab, and Armenian communities in the Jazira included autonomist currents that sought to preserve local rights under French protection.

These regional differences did not mean that Syria lacked national feeling. On the contrary, grievances against the mandate and the symbols of the revolt and the strike created shared political language. But they show that national unity was a project that required negotiation, not a given condition. Nationalist leaders tried to address minority concerns by guaranteeing rights, by recruiting minority members, and by using the language of equality. French policy, in turn, sometimes encouraged separatist sentiments when it served strategic aims. The competition between these dynamics shaped the treaty negotiations of 1936 and the Alexandretta crisis examined later.

Division IV — Greater Lebanon: Territory, Identity, and Political Institutions · Section 58.16

The Creation of Greater Lebanon in 1920

The Lebanon of the Ottoman Mutasarrifate had been a mountain district with a Christian, and especially Maronite, majority, governed under a European-guaranteed statute since 1861. It was small, densely populated, dependent on silk and on emigrant remittances, and cut off from the coastal ports and the grain-producing Beqaa. During the First World War, blockade and requisitioning produced a famine in the mountain that killed a large share of its inhabitants, and the memory of that catastrophe strengthened arguments that an economically viable Lebanon required more land and ports.

Maronite Patriarch Elias Hoayek led a delegation to the Paris Peace Conference in 1919 that requested an enlarged and independent Lebanon, under French protection, including the coastal cities and the Beqaa. Other Lebanese delegations, including Muslim and Syrian nationalist representatives, argued for union with an Arab Syria or for different arrangements. French officials in the Levant, including Gouraud, were sympathetic to the Maronite case, in part because of long French ties with the Maronite Church and in part because an enlarged Lebanon promised a loyal base and a good port in Beirut.

By an arrêté of 31 August 1920, Gouraud proclaimed the State of Greater Lebanon, effective 1 September. It annexed to Mount Lebanon the cities of Beirut, Tripoli, Sidon, and Tyre, the Beqaa Valley, and the districts of the north (Akkar) and south (Jabal Amil). The resulting territory was religiously mixed, with a narrow overall Christian majority of the resident population in later censuses and large Sunni, Shi'i, and Druze communities. In the annexed districts, many Muslim leaders, including some in Beirut and Tripoli, opposed incorporation into a Christian-led state and preferred union with Syria. Opposition took the form of petitions, boycotts of official events, and sometimes armed incidents in the early 1920s.

The new state's borders were drawn with a mixture of historical argument and administrative convenience. The eastern line followed the crest of the Anti-Lebanon range, separating the Beqaa from the Damascus region, while the northern district of Akkar and the southern district of Jabal Amil extended the state to the frontiers of Syria and Palestine. The southern boundary with Palestine was settled in a series of Anglo-French agreements between 1920 and 1923, which also determined which villages and water sources fell on which side. Those negotiations were conducted between the two mandatory powers, with no representation of the villagers whose lands were divided. The French authorities also reorganized local government, creating an Administrative Council and then, in 1922, a Representative Council, which gave Lebanese notables a role in the new state while the High Commissioner kept executive control.

The decision therefore illustrates the combination of French calculation, Maronite initiative, and local contestation that characterized the mandate. French authorities imposed the decision, but it answered a long-standing Lebanese Christian project. Meanwhile the new state's boundaries incorporated communities that had not asked to be included, and the work of reconciling them to the state occupied Lebanese politics for decades.

Division IV · Section 58.17

Competing Visions of Lebanese National Identity

There was no single Lebanese national idea in 1920. Several competing conceptions coexisted. A Lebanese particularist current, strongest among Maronites but not limited to them, held that Lebanon had a distinct historical identity as a mountain refuge and a Christian-influenced society oriented toward the Mediterranean and Europe. A related intellectual current, called Phoenicianism, traced Lebanese identity to the ancient Phoenicians, a maritime and commercial people. Writers such as Charles Corm and the banker and journalist Michel Chiha developed versions of this idea, which emphasized Lebanon's difference from the Arab hinterland and its Mediterranean heritage. Critics argued that Phoenicianism was a selective and modern invention, a use of the ancient past to support a contemporary political claim, and that it excluded Lebanese who considered themselves Arabs.

Arab nationalism provided another vision. Many Lebanese Muslims, and some Christians, regarded themselves as part of a broader Arab nation and favored union with Syria or at least close ties. Sunni leaders in Beirut, Tripoli, and Sidon were prominent among them in the early years. A third current, Greater Syrian nationalism, was articulated most influentially by Antun Sa'adeh, a Lebanese Greek Orthodox thinker who founded the Syrian Social Nationalist Party in Beirut in 1932. It proposed a secular Syrian nation, including Lebanon, Palestine, and neighboring territories, and rejected both sectarian politics and pan-Arabism. Meanwhile, the Communist Party, founded in 1924, offered a class-based critique.

The intellectual currents were tied to institutions. Michel Chiha, one of the principal exponents of a Lebanon oriented to commerce, the sea, and cooperation among its communities, took part in drafting the 1926 constitution, so that Phoenicianism was not only a literary idea but an influence on state design. In Jabal Amil, Shi'i religious scholars and reformers, among them Sayyid Abd al-Husayn Sharaf al-Din, were prominent in the debate over how the Shi'i community should relate to the new state and to the Arab nationalist movement. From Geneva, the Druze notable Shakib Arslan promoted Arab and Islamic solidarity and the cause of Syrian independence, and his writings were widely read in both countries. These figures show that Lebanese opinion cannot be reduced to a Christian-Muslim divide: there were Christian Arab nationalists, Muslim supporters of Lebanese statehood, and communities, such as the Shi'a, whose political loyalties were still taking shape.

These views were not frozen. Individuals and groups moved among them as circumstances changed. Many Lebanese Muslims who initially opposed the state gradually accepted it while demanding fair representation, and some Christian leaders came to emphasize Lebanon's Arab identity as a way of securing independence from France. Several organizations of the 1930s, including the Maronite-led Kataeb (Phalange) party, founded in 1936, and the Muslim youth group Najjada, expressed communal and national identities in new forms. In March 1936 a gathering of Muslim leaders from coastal towns, the Coastal Conference, demanded union with Syria, an episode that shows the continued strength of that position even as the treaty negotiations of that year proceeded.

The result was a state whose citizens held different ideas about what it was. This fact was central to the political compromise that later produced the National Pact: it reconciled the Christian desire for an independent Lebanon, not absorbed into Syria, with the Muslim desire for an Arab Lebanon not dependent on Western protection.

Division IV · Section 58.18

The Constitution of 1926 and the Lebanese Republic

Lebanon moved toward a constitution earlier than Syria. During the early 1920s, the French created an Administrative Council and then a Representative Council with limited powers. In 1926, the Representative Council, acting with the High Commissioner's encouragement, drafted a constitution, which was promulgated on 23 May 1926. It declared Lebanon a republic with a parliament and a president and provided for civil liberties and equality before the law. The first president, Charles Debbas, a Greek Orthodox, was chosen the same month.

The constitution had a bicameral legislature, a Senate and a Chamber of Deputies, though the Senate was abolished in a revision the following year. The president was chosen by parliament and shared executive authority with a council of ministers. The High Commissioner retained broad powers, notably over foreign relations, defense, and the supervision of institutions, and the constitution's provisions had to be understood within the mandate's framework. Under this arrangement the government could be suspended, as happened in 1932 and again in 1939.

Practice quickly modified the text. The Senate, designed to give additional weight to community interests and to older families, was abolished in 1927, and the amendments of that year also adjusted the president's term and powers. The High Commissioner's authority operated through decrees, through the influence of French advisers, and through the power to suspend institutions that he judged dangerous to the mandate. Charles Debbas, a Greek Orthodox Christian chosen as the first president, governed in cooperation with the French authorities and with a parliament whose deputies were elected on a basis that took account of the religious communities. The result was a political system that was parliamentary in form, communal in the distribution of power, and subject to mandate oversight in the last instance.

The constitution also addressed religious communities. It recognized the freedom of religious belief and the authority of the communities over personal status, and it included a transitional provision, widely known by its article number, 95, that called for equitable representation of the communities in public employment and in the government, as a temporary measure meant for the sake of justice and concord. This provision, written to ease concerns about the Muslim and Christian communities' respective places, later became the constitutional anchor for the confessional distribution of offices. Historians stress that it was introduced as a provisional device, and that its subsequent permanence was the result of political practice rather than of a design for permanent sectarian allocation.

Lebanese deputies took the constitution seriously and used it. Parliament was a forum for debate, patronage, and negotiation, and the political parties and blocs that formed around leading families and personalities developed a distinctive style of coalition politics. At the same time, French authority limited what the institutions could do, and Lebanese leaders navigated between cooperation with the High Commissioner and assertions of independence.

Division IV · Section 58.19

The 1932 Census, Citizenship, and Confessional Politics

In 1932 the French authorities conducted a census of Lebanon, the only official census in the country's history. Enumerators recorded citizens by religious community, and the exercise was bound up with the question of who counted as a Lebanese citizen at all. The figures, as analyzed in the scholarship of Rania Maktabi, show how finely balanced the outcome was: among resident citizens there were 396,946 Christians and 396,450 non-Christians, a margin of only 496, or almost exactly half and half. The statement that Christians formed a "majority" of residents is therefore technically true but could hardly be more misleading. When registered emigrants, most of them Christian, were added to the citizen count, the Christian share rose to roughly 58.5 percent. Maronites were the largest single community, followed by Sunni and Shi'i Muslims, with Greek Orthodox, Druze, and other communities making up the remainder. (These figures come from the secondary analysis cited in this chapter's source register; students should treat them as the standard scholarly reconstruction of a census whose own categories were contested.)

The census mattered because it fixed in numbers the relative weight of the communities, and numbers underpinned the allocation of seats and offices. Its accuracy was disputed. Some Muslim leaders argued that it undercounted Muslims and overcounted emigrants, and the treatment of nomadic and mobile populations and of recent migrants raised questions. Subsequent political practice nonetheless used the 1932 figures as the basis for the ratio of representation, usually expressed as six Christian seats for every five Muslim and Druze seats in parliament. No later census has been conducted, partly because of the political sensitivity of the results.

The census's numerical closeness explains why its politics were so sensitive. A population almost evenly divided between Christians and non-Christians among residents could be presented as having a Christian majority only if emigrants were counted, and the case for counting them rested on the argument that emigrants remained part of the Lebanese nation and kept ties of property and family to the country. Muslim leaders objected that many emigrants had been away for years, that some had acquired foreign citizenship, and that the registration of emigrants was open to manipulation. Citizenship criteria also excluded or complicated the position of groups such as nomads, recent migrants from Syria, and refugees from Anatolia, so that the "Lebanese" counted in 1932 was a legal and political construct rather than a simple inventory of the people living in the country.

Citizenship was bound up with these questions. Nationality legislation of the mid-1920s defined who counted as Lebanese, and it gave rights to certain emigrants of Lebanese origin while leaving some residents, for instance among the Armenians, Kurds, and others who had arrived after the war, in uncertain legal positions until they were naturalized in later decisions. The issue shows that Lebanese citizenship, like the state itself, was constructed through administrative and political decisions that carried consequences for communities and families.

Historians therefore treat the census and the 1926 constitution as instruments of state formation rather than simply as steps toward the 1943 formula. They defined who belonged, how power was to be measured, and how communal identities would enter public life. They also give support to a critical interpretation: that the system made religious affiliation the principal framework of political participation, which made communal bargaining the main route to influence.

Historical Vocabulary
Confessionalism (Ta'ifiyya)

Confessionalism is a political system in which public offices, legislative seats, and sometimes civil service posts are distributed according to recognized religious communities (ta'ifa, plural tawa'if). In Lebanon, the 1926 constitution's transitional article on community representation, the 1932 census, and the 1943 National Pact together produced a confessional order.

The term differs from religious government and from the Ottoman millet arrangements, although it drew on that older experience of communal self-administration. Confessionalism assigns political weight to community membership, which can protect minority communities and also entrench sectarian identities. Whether its origins lie chiefly in Ottoman precedent, French colonial classification, or Lebanese political choice is a matter of continuing scholarly discussion.

Division IV · Section 58.20

Lebanese Political Leadership and Constitutional Crisis

Lebanese politics in the 1930s was organized around prominent personalities and the networks of kin, clients, and sects that supported them. Two figures dominated Maronite politics: Bishara al-Khuri, who led the Constitutional Bloc and favored a more nationalist line, and Emile Eddé, who led a rival bloc and was closer to the French. Sunni leaders such as Riad al-Solh, who had been a prominent critic of Lebanon's separation from Syria, and leaders of other communities built their own followings. Politics was therefore a competition among elite factions, with frequent shifts of alliance.

The relationship with the French authorities fluctuated between cooperation and confrontation. The presidential election of 1932 brought a crisis. Parliament was divided between rival Maronite candidates, and a compromise candidacy of the Sunni Shaykh Muhammad al-Jisr emerged. High Commissioner Ponsot, alarmed at the prospect of a Muslim president and at the political deadlock, suspended the constitution in May 1932 and extended the existing president's term. Over the next years the French authorities appointed presidents and ministers by decree, and the constitution was restored only partly. In 1936 Eddé became president, a result that favored the pro-French line.

Beneath the headline crises lay a politics of patronage and coalition. Deputies represented districts through family networks, religious authority, and local services, and ministers were chosen to balance communities as much as parties. After the 1932 suspension, Charles Debbas continued in office until 1934, when Habib Pasha al-Saad was appointed president, and the constitution was only gradually restored. New organizations appeared in the middle of the decade, among them the Kataeb and the Najjada, which drew in younger activists and gave the communal parties a mass base that the older notable factions lacked. The Lebanese Communist Party, small but active in the labor movement, and the Syrian Social Nationalist Party, which operated across the border, added further currents that the mandate authorities watched closely.

These episodes reveal both the vulnerability of Lebanese institutions and the strategies of Lebanese leaders. Many accepted French intervention as a way of settling rivalries or protecting their positions, while others used the crises to build nationalist credentials. They also show the difficulty of building a political community in a state where communities competed for representation and where the external arbiter held the ultimate power. The politicians who emerged from these struggles would be the ones who negotiated independence a decade later.

Division IV · Section 58.21

The Economy and Society of Mandate Lebanon

The Lebanese economy centered on Beirut, whose port, banks, trading houses, and service sector grew under the mandate. The port handled the transit trade of the interior, and Beirut became a center of finance, education, and publishing. The Banque de Syrie et du Liban, the issuing bank, operated from there. A pipeline built in the 1930s to carry oil from Iraq to the Mediterranean ended at Tripoli, giving that northern city new economic importance, though the benefits to local workers were modest.

In the mountains, silk production declined sharply in the interwar years, partly because of competition and falling prices, and farmers shifted toward fruit, tobacco, and other crops. Emigration continued, with Lebanese traveling to the Americas, West Africa, and elsewhere, and remittances remained vital to households and to the balance of payments. The Beqaa Valley was an agricultural zone of grain and livestock with large estates, and Jabal Amil in the south, populated largely by Shi'i farmers, was poor and underdeveloped. Tobacco growers in the south were affected by the monopoly arrangements that favored the concessionary company.

The social geography of the country shaped its politics. Tripoli, Sidon, and Tyre were commercial and religious centers with Sunni leading families whose ties ran naturally toward the Syrian interior, and their political posture in the 1920s and 1930s reflected the loss of that hinterland. Zahle and the Beqaa combined Christian and Muslim communities in a region of grain and vineyards whose estates were large and whose peasants were poor. In Beirut, the port, banks, and professional classes formed a cosmopolitan elite, while migrants from the mountain and the south crowded into poorer quarters, among them the refugee districts of Armenians who had survived the war and the genocide. Tobacco cultivation in the south, regulated by a concession company, tied the livelihood of Shi'i farmers to decisions made by a distant administration, and the weakness of schools and roads in the periphery created a sense of neglect that politicians would later exploit.

Inequality shaped the Lebanese experience. Coastal merchants and bankers, Mount Lebanon's landowners and clerical establishments, and urban professionals prospered, while peasants, laborers, and small producers in peripheral regions had fewer opportunities. Some regions, particularly the south and the north, were underserved by schools, roads, and clinics, and this unequal distribution of resources later gave rise to political grievances and movements. Understanding mandate Lebanon therefore requires attention to the differences between the cosmopolitan coast, the densely settled mountain, and the poorer margins.

Historical Debate

Who Made Lebanon? French Design and Lebanese Agency

The question: Was the Lebanese state principally a creation of French imperial policy, or the outcome of a Lebanese political project and of negotiations among Lebanese actors?

Interpretation A — A French creation: Some historians, especially from Arab nationalist and critical perspectives, emphasize that France drew the borders, designed the institutions, and used Lebanon as a base, and that the confessional arrangements reflected colonial classification of society by religion.
Interpretation B — A Lebanese project negotiated with France: Other scholars emphasize that Maronite and other Lebanese leaders pursued enlargement and independence before and during the mandate, that Lebanese politicians shaped the constitution and bargained with French officials, and that the 1943 settlement was reached among Lebanese actors.

Evidence: Historians use French diplomatic and administrative records, the memoirs of Lebanese leaders, petitions presented in Paris in 1919, parliamentary debates, and press coverage. The interpretations diverge in the weight they assign to different kinds of sources and to the intentions behind decisions.

Why it matters: The answer affects how one understands the legitimacy of the state, the origins of confessionalism, and the responsibility for later conflicts. Most recent scholarship treats the two views as overlapping rather than exclusive: the state resulted from French imperial structures and from Lebanese initiatives that sometimes aligned with and sometimes resisted them.

Division V — Society, Economy, Culture, and Communities under the Mandate · Section 58.22

Trade Networks, Labor, and Economic Integration

Political division did not sever economic relations. Syrian and Lebanese markets remained linked by the common customs system administered by the Intérêts communs, by the shared currency, and by transport routes connecting Beirut to Damascus, Aleppo, and the interior. Grain, livestock, and agricultural produce moved from Syria to the coast, while imported goods and finance flowed inland. Merchants in Damascus and Aleppo depended on Beirut's port, and Beirut's prosperity depended on the interior's demand. Yet the new borders also created frictions: tariffs, quotas, and differing regulations affected cross-border trade, and the division of revenue from the customs union became a recurring dispute.

The mandate's economic policy was influenced by French interests. French firms obtained concessions in utilities, transport, and tobacco, and the franc peg tied local prices and credit to French monetary movements, which became a source of difficulty when the franc was devalued. Syrian and Lebanese critics argued that policy favored French capital over local producers, and economic grievances became part of nationalist rhetoric. Local entrepreneurs nevertheless took advantage of new opportunities in construction, light industry, banking, and import trade.

The tobacco monopoly illustrates how economic and political grievances combined. In 1935 the administration granted a concession for the sale and manufacture of tobacco to a French company, an arrangement that affected growers in the Latakia region and in the south of Lebanon, as well as the many small manufacturers and shopkeepers who had lived from the trade. Nationalists denounced the concession as evidence that French interests were being preferred to local ones, and it was one of the immediate grievances behind the unrest of 1936. A different kind of development came with the building of the pipeline carrying Iraqi oil across Syria to Tripoli and Haifa in the 1930s: it brought construction work, new revenues, and new strategic importance to the Levant, but the profits flowed principally to the international consortium that operated it. Such developments show how the mandate economy was integrated into imperial and international systems that the Syrians and Lebanese could not control.

Labor history during the mandate is less well documented than elite politics, but evidence from union records, strike reports, and press coverage shows growing organization among workers in transport, tobacco, printing, and textiles. Wages were low, hours long, and the legal protection of workers limited. Strikes occurred, particularly in the 1930s, and some unions allied with nationalist or communist organizations. The migration of rural workers to the cities, and of Lebanese and Syrians abroad, reshaped families and communities.

Division V · Section 58.23

Education, Language, Journalism, and Intellectual Life

Education was one of the most contested fields of mandate policy. The region had an unusually dense and varied school system by the 1920s: Ottoman state schools, Arabic-language private and communal schools, and Western missionary institutions, including the American University of Beirut (founded as the Syrian Protestant College) and the French Jesuit Université Saint-Joseph. The French authorities promoted the French language and French-curriculum schools, while also funding Arabic-language public schooling in the mandate states. In Damascus, a Syrian University was formed in the early 1920s by merging earlier professional schools, and the Arab Academy, established under the Arab government, continued to be an important center for language and scholarship.

Language was a political question. Arabic was the language of most Syrians and Lebanese, and its place in education and administration was a nationalist demand. At the same time, French was the language of the mandate government, of elite schools, and of much of the professional and commercial world, particularly among Lebanese Christians and the educated middle class. Bilingual and trilingual education created a cosmopolitan intelligentsia, but it also created cultural differences among communities. Debates about the language of instruction and the content of history curricula reflected competing national narratives.

Policy toward schools reflected these tensions. The French authorities supported Arabic-language public education while giving prestige and funding to French schools, and French cultural missions, the Jesuit university, and Catholic religious orders educated many Lebanese Christians and a smaller number of Syrians. American and British Protestant institutions offered an alternative, and Arabic private schools run by Muslim and Christian associations competed with both. Syrian nationalists insisted that Arabic must be the language of the state, and in Lebanon the constitutional amendments of 1943 would later make Arabic the sole national official language. In the intervening years the history curricula became a battlefield: Syrian textbooks emphasized the unity of Syria and the Arab nation, while Lebanese Christian educators often stressed a distinct Lebanese past, and the content of schoolbooks was a recurring subject of dispute with the mandate authorities.

The press flourished despite censorship. Newspapers in Arabic and French appeared in Beirut, Damascus, and Aleppo, reflecting the full range of political and cultural views. The Arabic daily al-Nahar, founded in Beirut in 1933, and French-language papers such as L'Orient, founded in 1924, are examples that became durable institutions. Publishing houses, literary societies, and journals provided platforms for poets, novelists, and essayists. Debates over reform, language, religion, and national identity took place in these forums, and intellectuals such as those associated with the Arab Academy and the Beirut presses shaped public opinion. The emigrant literary movement of the Americas also linked Lebanese writers to wider currents.

Intellectuals were therefore central to the construction of national identities. Historians and educators wrote textbooks, poets and journalists framed political events, and the nationalist narrative of the revolt, of Arab history, and of Lebanese history was written and rewritten. Students should consider how these narratives, produced for particular political purposes, influence the sources available to historians.

Division V · Section 58.24

Women, Families, and Social Reform

Women's roles in mandate society varied by class, region, and religion. In rural households, women worked in agriculture, livestock, and household production, while urban middle-class women increasingly attended schools, and some entered teaching and nursing. Women's organizations proliferated, many of them charitable, educational, or nationalist in orientation. Women participated in demonstrations and strikes, and prominent women, such as Naziq al-Abid, who had served in the Arab government, became symbols of female nationalist participation.

The late 1920s and 1930s saw a growth in organized activity. Women's associations ran schools, clinics, and charities, and in Syria women took part in the strikes and demonstrations of 1936, in some cases organizing their own marches in support of the national cause. Legal inequality remained fundamental. Nationality law assigned citizenship through the father, personal status law left marriage, divorce, and inheritance to the religious courts of each community, and suffrage was reserved to men. Women of the educated middle classes found new openings in teaching, nursing, journalism, and charitable work, while for the majority of rural and working-class women the central realities remained agricultural labor, household work, and dependence on male relatives. Because the written record is dominated by educated and urban voices, historians must read memoirs, school records, and periodicals alongside local evidence to avoid mistaking the experiences of a few for those of the many.

Public debate about women's status was vigorous. Reformers advocated education, changes in dress, and legal reform, and conservative voices defended existing practices. In 1928 Nazira Zayn al-Din, a Lebanese woman from a Druze family, published a book arguing, on the basis of religious texts, against compulsory veiling, which provoked wide controversy. Others argued for women's suffrage, though it was not achieved in Syria and Lebanon within the mandate period. Personal status law, controlled by the religious communities, meant that marriage, divorce, and inheritance rights differed according to religion and often disadvantaged women, and debates about codifying or reforming these laws continued after independence.

Family structures changed gradually. Education, migration, urbanization, and economic change altered household arrangements, but extended kinship networks remained central to social and political life. Historians reconstruct women's experiences from memoirs, periodicals, school records, charity records, and oral histories, recognizing that most women, especially in rural and poor households, left few written traces.

Division V · Section 58.25

Religious and Ethnolinguistic Diversity

The mandate territories contained a wide range of communities, and their political experiences varied. Sunni Muslims, the largest group in Syria, provided most of the urban political elite and dominated nationalist leadership, though not uniformly. Alawites, concentrated in the coastal mountains, were largely poor villagers under landlord domination and had a distinctive religious tradition; their position in the mandate was shaped by their separate administration and recruitment into the Troupes spéciales. The Druze of Jabal Druze and Mount Lebanon had their own religious and political leadership, and their experiences differed on either side of the Syrian-Lebanese border. Twelver Shi'a lived mostly in Jabal Amil, the Beqaa, and some northern villages; Ismailis lived in a few Syrian districts.

Christian communities were also diverse. Maronites, concentrated in Mount Lebanon, were the largest Lebanese Christian community and had a close relationship with France. Greek Orthodox, Greek Catholics, Syriac Orthodox and Catholics, and Protestants lived in cities and regions across both states. Armenian refugees, who had come after the genocide of 1915 and the upheavals of the war, formed distinct communities in Aleppo, Beirut, and other places, with their own schools, churches, and political parties. Jewish communities in Damascus, Aleppo, and Beirut were small but long established. Kurds lived in the north and northeast of Syria and in Damascus, and Circassians and Turkmen formed other minorities. Assyrians fleeing violence in Iraq settled in the Jazira in the 1930s.

Several developments illustrate the variety of communal experience. In 1936 some Alawite separatist leaders petitioned French prime minister Léon Blum against incorporation in a unified Syrian state and requested continued French protection. The document's transmission became controversial: modern scholarship has identified an archival original differing from widely circulated versions, while other Alawite petitions favored Syrian unity. This petition therefore demonstrates one political current rather than the position of the whole community. Kurdish communities in the northern districts and the Jazira developed their own cultural and political organizations, and Kurdish nationalist activists found a base in the mandate's tolerance of some forms of Kurdish expression. The Armenian Catholicosate of Cilicia moved its seat to Antelias in Lebanon, so that the mandate became a center of Armenian church life. Jewish communities in Damascus, Aleppo, and Beirut engaged in commerce, crafts, and the professions and, in Lebanon, were recognized as a community under the confessional system. In all these cases, the intersection of religion, language, and region shaped how communities perceived the mandate and the future state.

These communities did not simply act as blocs. Class, region, family, and ideology divided each of them, and individuals made choices that cut across communal lines: Christians joined Arab nationalist movements, Sunnis supported Lebanese independence, and Alawites and Druze took part in both resistance and cooperation. At the same time, the mandate's institutions, including its administrative divisions, recruitment practices, courts, and census, gave communal identity real political weight. Students should therefore avoid both assuming that sects determined behavior and ignoring the ways that institutions made them politically relevant.

Everyday Life and Social History

Living under the Mandate: Streets, Villages, and Households

For most inhabitants, the mandate was experienced less through treaties than through practical encounters. A villager in the Hawran met it in the tax collector's assessment, the requirement to provide labor or transport, and the district officer's attention. A Damascus shopkeeper met it in tariffs, currency changes, and street closures during demonstrations. A Beirut schoolchild might study in French in the morning and learn Arabic poetry at home. A Lebanese mountain family might depend on a relative's remittance from Brazil, and an Armenian refugee family in Aleppo might rebuild community life in a new quarter around a church and school.

The rhythms of life also followed older patterns: harvest and market cycles, religious festivals, pilgrimage, and family events. Cafés, newspapers, and radio, which spread in the 1930s, created new shared experiences, and cinema became a popular urban entertainment. Historians reconstruct these experiences from memoirs, local newspapers, school records, court records, and photographs, while recognizing that peasants, workers, and women are underrepresented in the written record.

Division VI — The Crisis of the Mandate in the Late 1930s · Section 58.26

The Syrian General Strike and the Franco-Syrian Treaty of 1936

By the mid-1930s, parliamentary politics had produced little progress, and economic distress, the tobacco monopoly, and the earlier treaty failure fed discontent. In January 1936 the arrest of nationalist figures in Damascus triggered a general strike, beginning on 20 January and lasting about fifty days. Shops closed, demonstrations took place in many cities, and the movement was supported by merchants, students, workers, and the National Bloc, which coordinated the protest. French forces used force against crowds, and there were deaths. The strike was a demonstration of the nationalist movement's mass base and of its capacity for disciplined, nonviolent pressure.

The strike's organization showed the maturity of the nationalist movement. It began on 20 January 1936 in Damascus after the arrest of nationalist figures, and it was sustained by committees that coordinated closures of shops, markets, and workplaces, by the support of merchants who accepted the loss of business, and by students and young activists who mobilized crowds. It spread to Aleppo, Hama, Homs, and other towns, and the French authorities responded with martial law, arrests, and the use of troops against demonstrators. Deaths occurred, and each funeral became an occasion for further protest. What made the strike unusual was its discipline and its social breadth: it united notables, shopkeepers, workers, and students behind a single demand for negotiation, and it was far less easy for France to portray it as brigandage than the armed revolt of a decade earlier.

The strike ended after the French authorities agreed to negotiate, and a National Bloc delegation went to Paris. The victory of the Popular Front in France in 1936, led by Léon Blum, created a more sympathetic government, although it was constrained by the resistance of colonial and military circles. In September 1936, a treaty of friendship and alliance was signed. It provided for Syrian independence and entry into the League of Nations after a transition period, the end of the mandate, and a long alliance giving France military bases and other privileges, and it provided for the reunification of the Alawite and Druze regions with Syria, with special guarantees for their administration.

The negotiations themselves were shaped by French politics. The National Bloc delegation, led by Hashim al-Atassi, arrived in Paris in the spring, as the French elections were bringing the Popular Front to power, and the new government charged Pierre Viénot, a junior minister at the Foreign Ministry sympathetic to colonial reform, with the Syrian and Lebanese files. The treaty was signed in Paris on 9 September 1936. It provided for an alliance of twenty-five years, the termination of the mandate and Syrian entry into the League of Nations after three years, the stationing of French forces and air bases for a period, and the return of the Alawite and Druze regions to Syrian administration under special guarantees. Syria's parliament approved it, and Atassi was elected president in December. The French parliament, however, never brought it to a ratifying vote. The Quai d'Orsay and the army doubted the wisdom of leaving the Levant, colonial lobbyists mobilized against it, and the fall of the Blum government in 1937 removed its principal sponsor. By 1938 the treaty was a dead letter, a fact that Syrian nationalists, who had staked their strategy on it, could not escape.

The treaty seemed a breakthrough. Hashim al-Atassi was elected president in December 1936, and the Bloc held power, enjoying great popularity. But the treaty required ratification by the French parliament, and it never received it. French political changes, including the fall of Blum's government in 1937, the return of more conservative governments, military and colonial opposition, and the diplomatic pressures of European tensions, meant that ratification was postponed and finally abandoned. The failure undermined the National Bloc's strategy, since it had staked its credibility on French promises, and it left Syria in limbo: governed by a nationalist cabinet but still under the mandate.

Division VI · Section 58.27

The Franco-Lebanese Treaty and the Politics of Independence

Lebanon's negotiations paralleled Syria's but took a different course. President Emile Eddé's government, more closely aligned with France, negotiated a treaty signed in November 1936. It promised Lebanese independence after a period of transition, an alliance with France, and French military facilities, and it recognized Lebanon within its existing borders. This last point was significant to Lebanese leaders who feared that Syrian nationalists, who had never fully accepted the separation, might seek to reopen the question of Lebanon's boundaries.

The Lebanese treaty was signed on 13 November 1936 by President Emile Eddé, whose government had a different relationship to France from that of the Syrian nationalists. Eddé and his supporters regarded the French connection as protection for Lebanon's independence from Syria, and they valued the treaty's recognition of Lebanon's existing borders. Their opponents were not united in rejecting it, but they differed on grounds. Khuri's Constitutional Bloc, Sunni leaders such as Riad al-Solh, and others criticized the French privileges and the extent to which the arrangement kept Lebanon within the mandate in all but name. The Coastal Conference in March 1936 had already shown that part of the Muslim leadership wanted union with Syria, and the Syrian treaty's provisions for the Alawite and Druze regions left Lebanese Muslims asking why their own relationship to Syria had been settled differently. In the end the Lebanese treaty met the fate of its Syrian counterpart: it was not ratified, and the state remained a mandate in practice.

Lebanese reactions were divided. Many Christian leaders welcomed a treaty that confirmed the state's borders and French support. Many Muslim leaders regarded it with suspicion, seeing it as a bargain that cemented separation from Syria and French influence, and the Coastal Conference earlier that year had demanded union with Syria. Others thought it a stepping stone to full independence. The treaty, like its Syrian counterpart, was never ratified by France, so its provisions did not take effect.

Comparing the two treaties clarifies the different political situations. Syria's nationalists negotiated from a position of mass mobilization but against the problem of territorial unity and the legacy of the revolt. Lebanon's leaders negotiated within a state whose very borders were contested, and the question of independence was tied to the question of what Lebanon was. These differences would reappear in 1943.

Division VI · Section 58.28

Political Disillusionment and the Reassertion of French Authority, 1937–1939

After the treaty's failure, Syrian politics became tense. The National Bloc government struggled with economic problems and with internal rifts, and opposition grew from both sides: from those who thought it had conceded too much and from rival politicians. Separatist tendencies revived in some regions, and in the Jazira and in the Alawite and Druze areas, French officials sometimes encouraged local autonomy movements. The government's authority was undermined by the realization that France would not deliver on the treaty.

The most visible fractures appeared in the regions. In the Jazira, Kurdish, Assyrian, Christian, and some Arab leaders pressed for local autonomy under French protection, and in 1937 there were disturbances there that French officers exploited to argue that Syria was not united. In the Alawite and Druze regions, separatist currents revived, and in Jabal Druze there were clashes between supporters and opponents of integration with Damascus. The National Bloc governments, led by Jamil Mardam Bey, argued that unity was the nation's rightful condition and that France was manipulating minorities, while minority leaders argued that Damascus had not yet offered credible guarantees. The government also faced hostility from rival nationalists, including supporters of Shahbandar, who returned to Syria after the amnesty and criticized the treaty strategy as a failure.

French policy hardened in the late 1930s. European tensions, the threat of war with Germany, and French concern for the security of its imperial communications made the retention of Levantine bases more important, and the High Commissioner, Gabriel Puaux, who succeeded Martel at the end of the decade, followed a firmer line. In 1939, after the loss of Alexandretta, President Atassi resigned in July. The High Commissioner suspended the constitution and governed through a council of directors under Syrian officials, and the autonomous status of the Alawite and Druze territories was restored. In Lebanon, too, the constitution was suspended at the outbreak of war in September 1939.

The period shows the limits of a strategy of negotiation with a power that retained the ultimate means of control. It also shows how developments in Europe shaped Levantine politics: the outcomes were determined by French domestic and international calculations at least as much as by Syrian and Lebanese actions.

Division VI · Section 58.29

Alexandretta, Hatay, and the Turkish-Syrian Frontier

The Sanjak of Alexandretta, on the Mediterranean coast in the northwest corner of the mandate, had a population of Arab Alawites, Sunni Arabs, Turks, Armenians, Greek Orthodox, and others. Under the Franco-Turkish agreement of 1921 it received a special administrative regime. After Turkey consolidated its republic under Mustafa Kemal Atatürk, Ankara pressed claims on the district on the grounds that its Turkish population required protection, and it saw the Sanjak as strategically important. Syrian nationalists insisted that it was an integral part of Syria.

When the 1936 treaty raised the prospect of Syrian independence, Turkey demanded guarantees for the Sanjak, and the issue went to the League of Nations. In 1937 the League Council approved a statute for the Sanjak as a distinct entity with internal autonomy and under special arrangements. Elections in 1938, held under conditions of disputed registration and tension, produced a Turkish majority in the assembly. In September 1938 the Hatay State was proclaimed, and in June 1939 the assembly voted for union with Turkey, formalized by an agreement between France and Turkey. France, eager to secure Turkish friendship as war approached, agreed to the transfer.

The League's role is a useful case study of its limits. A commission supervised the registration of voters in 1938 in a district where the population categories were themselves in dispute: Turks, Alawites, Sunni Arabs, Armenians, Greek Orthodox, and others, with competing claims about the size of each. Turkish pressure, including troop movements near the border, and disputes over who was entitled to vote produced a result that gave the Turkish side a narrow majority in the assembly, and the French and Turkish governments concluded agreements that removed the issue from the League's effective control. Opponents saw a manufactured outcome; Turkish officials described it as the democratic expression of a Turkish-majority population. Meanwhile, Armenians from villages around Musa Dagh left for Lebanon in 1939, where many were settled at Anjar, and Arab Alawites and Sunni Arabs who opposed the change faced pressure, with some going to Syria.

Perspectives differed sharply. Turkey presented the outcome as the self-determination of a Turkish population; Syrian nationalists regarded it as a French betrayal and the amputation of Syrian territory, and they have not accepted it since; many Alawites and Arabs in the district opposed or feared the change; Armenians, many of whom left the district, resettled in Lebanon, with some founding the town of Anjar in the Beqaa; and French officials justified it as strategic necessity. The episode shows that territorial sovereignty remained unresolved while Syrians negotiated for independence, and it deepened distrust of France.

Cause and Effect

Why the 1936 Treaties Failed to Deliver Independence

Long-term causes

French strategic interest in the Levant, the structure of mandate authority, and the reliance of nationalist strategy on French goodwill.

Short-term causes

The fall of the Popular Front, domestic opposition in France, military and colonial lobbying, and the approach of European war.

Immediate effects

Non-ratification, the collapse of the National Bloc's credibility, the resignation of President Atassi, and the suspension of constitutions in 1939.

Long-term consequences

A turn toward more radical nationalism, deep distrust of French promises, and receptivity to the changed conditions created by the war.

Historical complexity: The failure cannot be attributed to a single cause. French domestic politics, imperial calculation, Turkish diplomacy over Alexandretta, and divisions among Syrians and Lebanese all contributed.

Division VII — World War II and the Achievement of Independence · Section 58.30

The Fall of France, Vichy Rule, and the Levant Campaign, 1940–1941

The German defeat of France in June 1940 transformed the Levant. The High Commissioner and the Army of the Levant at first hesitated, then the authorities in Beirut aligned with the Vichy government, headed by General Henri Dentz from December 1940. Vichy rule brought stricter censorship and economic hardship, and it raised the question of whether Axis powers would gain access to the Levant. In May 1941 a pro-Axis uprising under Rashid Ali in neighboring Iraq, which Britain suppressed, and Vichy's permission for German aircraft to use Syrian airfields on their way to support it, convinced the British that they needed to control the territory. (Chapter 57 ends before 1941, so the Iraqi episode is noted here only for its effect on Syria.)

Vichy administration in the Levant was authoritarian and defensive. Officials suppressed nationalist agitation, enforced censorship, and attempted to maintain French prestige, while Axis armistice inspectors visited the territory, and rationing and shortages made daily life harder. The British, controlling the neighboring territories and the sea, imposed a blockade that exacerbated scarcity. Within the French command there were officers who wished to join de Gaulle, and some left to do so, but the High Commissioners remained loyal to Vichy. The result was a period in which Syrian and Lebanese leaders watched the conflicts of the great powers and calculated that the weakness of France might offer opportunities they had not had since 1920.

In June 1941 British, Australian, Indian, and Free French forces invaded Syria and Lebanon. The campaign, which lasted until mid-July, involved sharp fighting between Allied and Vichy French troops, and it ended with an armistice signed at Acre. Vichy forces were withdrawn, and the Free French under General Charles de Gaulle took charge of the administration under the general oversight of the British. Syrians and Lebanese were not primary actors in this campaign, but its outcome altered their prospects: France was no longer a self-confident, undivided power, and Britain, the dominant military force in the region, had its own interests and commitments to Arab opinion.

The campaign, which began on 8 June and lasted until mid-July, was a bitter struggle, because Vichy forces resisted more strongly than the Allies had expected, and Frenchmen fought Frenchmen. The armistice signed at Acre in mid-July ended the fighting and provided for the repatriation of Vichy troops, though many chose to remain or to join the Free French. The settlement left a question of authority unresolved: Britain held the military command and the practical power, while the Free French claimed the political heir's position of France. De Gaulle and the British negotiated agreements in the summer and autumn of 1941 that gave the Free French the civil administration while reserving to Britain control of military operations and the right to protect wartime interests. These agreements were a source of continual friction, because each side suspected the other of seeking to dominate the Levant after the war.

The war also affected daily life. Shortages, inflation, wartime requisitions, and the presence of large Allied armies created economic strain and new opportunities, such as wartime contracts and a rise in local industry. The famine memories of 1914–1918 made food supply an acute political issue. Political activity revived as the Free French allowed parties to operate in the hope of winning support.

The war economy created new patterns. The Allied armies purchased food and labor, the Middle East Supply Centre in Cairo coordinated the allocation of scarce goods across the region, and inflation eroded the income of wage earners and fixed-income households. Grain shortages and the price of bread were political issues in 1942 and 1943, and the memory of famine in the First World War gave them special force. Wartime contracts, remittances, and the expansion of small industry benefited some merchants and entrepreneurs, who accumulated capital that would be important in the postwar economy. The social effects of the war thus increased both the discontent that fed nationalist politics and the resources of a new generation of business families.

Division VII · Section 58.31

Independence Declarations and the Limits of Allied Commitments

As the invasion began, the Free French representative, General Georges Catroux, proclaimed that the mandate would end and that Syria and Lebanon would be independent and sovereign. The proclamations were repeated in 1941: Syria's independence was announced in September, and Lebanon's in November. Britain endorsed the promises. In effect, however, the Free French retained control of key powers, including defense, customs, and security, pending treaties, and they asserted that France's position would be protected in a future settlement.

The proclamations were repeated and elaborated in 1941. Syria's independence was announced on 27 September 1941 and Lebanon's on 26 November, and in each case Britain publicly guaranteed the declaration. The Free French nonetheless claimed that the mandate's legal basis could be altered only by agreement, and they refused to regard the declarations as ending the League framework. Local leaders took a different view. For them the declarations were binding commitments that France could not take back, and every later negotiation was measured against the promises of 1941. The conflict between these two understandings, one treating the proclamations as final and the other as statements of intention, ran through the next five years.

The distinction between nominal recognition and effective sovereignty is central here. The declarations created legal and moral commitments that nationalists used, but they did not transfer authority. Free French officials argued that wartime conditions precluded fuller transfers and that the League mandate, though suspended in practice, could be ended only by agreement. The British, who maintained a large military presence and a diplomatic mission under Major-General Edward Spears, pressed France to honor its promises in order to maintain Arab goodwill during the war, but they also wished to protect their own interests and avoid a rift with the Free French.

Syrian and Lebanese leaders exploited the opening. They demanded elections, the restoration of constitutions, and the transfer of powers. Heads of state were appointed by the French in 1941: in Syria, Shaykh Taj al-Din al-Hasani, and in Lebanon, a succession of presidents including Alfred Naccache. Nationalist pressure, wartime inflation, and British encouragement pushed the Free French toward holding elections in 1943.

The British position was ambivalent. The Spears Mission, headed by Major-General Edward Spears, acted as Britain's representative to the two governments and pressed France to honor its promises, in part because British officials believed that a settled Levant served the war effort and Arab goodwill. Spears also appeared to many French officials to be encouraging nationalist demands in order to displace France, and the suspicion of British designs hardened the attitude of the Free French delegation. In Lebanon, appointed presidents and cabinets under the Free French were replaced in 1943, when the constitution was restored and an election was announced, in part because of British insistence and in part because Catroux judged that continued delay would worsen the situation.

Division VII · Section 58.32

Lebanon's Independence Crisis of 1943

Elections in Lebanon were held in August and September 1943 and brought to power a coalition favoring independence. Bishara al-Khuri was elected president, and Riad al-Solh became prime minister. The two men, a Maronite and a Sunni who had earlier been on opposing sides of the debate over Lebanon's separation from Syria, negotiated an unwritten understanding, later called the National Pact. By its terms, as generally understood, Lebanon's Christians would renounce reliance on French or other foreign protection, and Lebanon's Muslims would renounce union with Syria and accept Lebanon's independent existence with an Arab face and Arab ties. In practice, it was also interpreted to allocate the presidency to a Maronite, the premiership to a Sunni, and the speakership of parliament to a Shi'i, and to maintain a ratio of six Christian to five Muslim parliamentary seats. The Pact's terms were not written in a single document, and later accounts of its content differ in detail.

The preparation for the Pact had been long. Bishara al-Khuri, a Maronite who had led the Constitutional Bloc, and Riad al-Solh, a Sunni who had been a leading opponent of Lebanon's separation from Syria in the 1920s and had been associated with the demand for union, arrived at their understanding through negotiations among leaders of several communities. The agreement was reported and interpreted in the ministerial declaration that the new government presented to parliament in October, which emphasized independence, friendly relations with the Arab states, and cooperation with other nations. The Pact's lasting importance lay in its acceptance by leaders who had previously been on opposite sides, and in its recognition that Lebanon's survival required that each community give up part of its maximal aims.

The new government moved quickly. On 8 November 1943 parliament voted amendments to the constitution that removed the articles referring to the mandate and to French privileges; the corresponding constitutional law is dated 9 November 1943 and was published in the Official Gazette on the following day. The French delegate-general, Jean Helleu, regarded the step as unacceptable. On 11 November, French forces arrested President Khuri, Prime Minister Solh, and several ministers and deputies, and held them at Rashaya. Helleu suspended the constitution and appointed Emile Eddé as head of state. Yet the arrests produced mass demonstrations, strikes, and clashes in Beirut and elsewhere, and a provisional government formed in the mountains at Bshamoun, led by Majid Arslan and Habib Abu Shahla, which kept the resistance organized.

The amendments themselves were wide-ranging. Besides removing the references to the mandate and the powers of the High Commissioner, they gave constitutional status to the use of Arabic as the sole official language, with French permitted only in cases fixed by law, a change with cultural as well as political meaning for a country whose elites used French so extensively. The French delegation interpreted the amendments as a unilateral act contrary to the continuing legal framework and as a challenge to French authority at a moment when the Free French sought to hold the Levant until treaties could be made. The arrests of 11 November were ordered at dawn, and the detainees were taken to the citadel at Rashaya. The rapid reaction, with strikes, demonstrations, and the formation of a provisional government in the mountains, showed that the Lebanese population was ready to defend the new government across communal lines.

Britain, backed by the United States and the Arab states, demanded the release of the prisoners and threatened to intervene. The French committee in Algiers, under pressure, reversed its decision. The prisoners were freed on 22 November, a date that Lebanon celebrates as Independence Day, and Helleu was recalled. The episode, which blended nationalist mobilization, cross-communal solidarity, and British leverage, demonstrated that France could not reimpose its authority. But it did not by itself end the French military presence or transfer all powers, which required further negotiation.

The external pressure was as important as the internal. The British minister in Beirut, Spears, pressed his government to act, and London delivered an ultimatum to the French authorities, while Arab governments, including Egypt and Syria, protested. Charles de Gaulle sent Catroux back to Beirut to restore the situation, and Catroux arranged the release of the detainees and the reinstatement of the government. The affair illustrates the three layers of the independence process: a domestic coalition prepared to take risks, a population willing to mobilize, and a changed international environment in which Britain and the United States no longer wished to support French claims by force. Lebanon's independence thus came by a combination of constitutional action and a political victory, not by a formal treaty or a grant.

Division VII · Section 58.33

Syrian Nationalist Government and the Independence Struggle, 1943–1945

In Syria, elections in the summer of 1943 gave the National Bloc (whose Damascus wing would form the National Party only in 1947), a decisive victory. Shukri al-Quwatli was elected president in August, and a government dominated by veterans of the nationalist movement took office. The new leadership sought to secure a real transfer of powers. In 1944 the Free French handed over most administrative and public services to the Syrian and Lebanese governments, but retained control of the Troupes spéciales and the customs and common-interest administration, which they sought to link to a future treaty guaranteeing French cultural and military privileges.

The transfer of powers took place in stages. In the winter of 1943–1944 the French agreed to hand over most administrative and public services, and by January 1944 the Syrian and Lebanese governments controlled the greater part of their civil administration. The French reserved the Troupes spéciales and the common-interest services, linking their transfer to a treaty. The Syrian government under President Quwatli rejected this linkage, passing laws to establish national armed forces and negotiating with the French to take over the Troupes spéciales without conditions. The same governments pursued an Arab diplomacy of their own, participating in the discussions in Alexandria in 1944 that prepared the way for the League of Arab States in March 1945, and thus established themselves as sovereign actors in regional politics before the French had fully evacuated.

Syrian leaders resisted a treaty that would preserve such privileges. They also pressed to obtain command of the army, which they regarded as a precondition of sovereignty, and they worked to win recognition from other states. The Soviet Union, the United States, and other powers recognized Syria and Lebanon in 1944, and the Arab states, in the process of forming what became the Arab League in 1945, supported them. Syria declared war on the Axis early in 1945, a step that qualified it to participate in the founding of the United Nations.

French officials, meanwhile, faced domestic pressures and imperial anxieties. They did not want to be seen as expelled from the Levant and saw a treaty as a way to preserve influence. The impasse over troops and over the terms of a treaty grew sharper through the spring of 1945, as the war in Europe ended.

Division VII · Section 58.34

The French Bombardment of Damascus and the Crisis of 1945

American diplomatic reporting gives a clear chronology of the origins of the crisis. French reinforcements landed in the Levant on 6 May and again on 18 May 1945, even though the United States and Britain had urged France not to increase its forces, and on 18 May the French delegate-general presented notes to the Syrian and Lebanese governments demanding concessions that included strategic bases. Because the note was delivered while a French warship was in Beirut harbor, many Syrians and Lebanese understood it as an attempt to intimidate them into accepting French terms. The arrival of the troops aroused alarm. Demonstrations broke out in Syria and Lebanon, and Syrian authorities refused to negotiate under pressure. On 29 May, French forces and Troupes spéciales units fired on the Syrian parliament building in Damascus and bombarded parts of the city by artillery and aircraft. The parliament building was damaged, and there were numerous casualties, though estimates of the death toll differ.

The reaction was immediate. Britain, whose forces were still in the region, intervened. After a message from Prime Minister Winston Churchill, British troops under General Bernard Paget moved to impose a ceasefire, confining French forces to their barracks. The episode humiliated France and revealed that British support was decisive. The United States and the Soviet Union criticized the French action, and the Arab governments protested. American diplomatic correspondence and British records document the crisis and the sharp exchanges among the Allies.

The surviving documentation allows the scale of the crisis to be seen from several angles. On the evening of 29 May the American minister, Wadsworth, reported by telegram that American and British officials in Damascus had telephoned him about heavy machine-gun and rifle fire near the French barracks, that a British officer told him the French were shelling Damascus, and that a British legation officer saw a French aircraft drop bombs on and machine-gun the city. The Syrian president sent a message on 30 May asking where the principles of the Atlantic Charter had gone, and a memorandum prepared for President Truman on 31 May summarized American information that disorders were developing into open warfare, that French garrisons in outlying districts had been captured, that several hundred Syrians had been killed, and that the crisis was disturbing the atmosphere at the San Francisco conference. The memorandum also reported that locally recruited troops in Jabal Druze had gone over to the Syrian governor. These sources are not neutral, but taken together they show that Syrian, British, and American observers agreed on the main sequence, even though they differed on responsibility and on casualties.

The consequences were far-reaching. France lost any realistic hope of retaining its position by force. In the following months France transferred the Troupes spéciales to Syrian and Lebanese command, and negotiations began over the withdrawal of French and British troops. In the broader international context, the new United Nations, in which Syria and Lebanon were founding members, provided a forum for pressure.

The crisis also showed the weight of the emerging postwar order. The United States, which was then hosting the San Francisco conference on the United Nations, did not wish a colonial war to discredit the new organization, and the Soviet Union was ready to exploit French difficulties. Syria and Lebanon, whose governments had declared war on the Axis early in 1945 and thus qualified to attend, saw their grievance raised in the setting of the new world body. For France the humiliation was acute. Metropolitan opinion divided, with many insisting that France must keep its position in the Levant, but the practical choice had narrowed: continued military presence meant open conflict with Britain and with local populations.

Division VII · Section 58.35

Final Evacuation and Independence, 1946

After the crisis, Britain and France agreed in December 1945 on the principle of withdrawing their forces, and the question was taken to the United Nations Security Council in February 1946, where a resolution calling for withdrawal was blocked by a Soviet veto, though the debate helped keep diplomatic pressure on both powers. French troops left Syria in April 1946, and 17 April is celebrated as Syria's Evacuation Day. Most French combat units left Lebanon by the end of August 1946, while the final French technical and liquidation personnel departed by the end of December.

The negotiation of the withdrawal was complex. The Anglo-French agreement of December 1945 provided for staged evacuation, but the Syrian and Lebanese governments, fearing delay, took their complaint to the Security Council in early 1946 and invoked the Charter's principle of the sovereign equality of states. In the debate on 16 February, a draft resolution on the withdrawal of foreign troops was vetoed by the Soviet Union and therefore not adopted, though the discussion pushed France and Britain to complete the evacuations. French and British forces completed their departure from Syria in April 1946. In Lebanon the principal French military units withdrew by the end of August, while a residual group of officers and technicians remained temporarily for liquidation and transport work, with the final withdrawal completed by December.

The chronology of independence is therefore layered. Lebanon marks 22 November 1943 as its independence day, when its leaders were released and the constitutional amendments stood. Syria dates effective independence to the withdrawal of April 1946, with earlier milestones in 1941, 1943, and 1944. In both cases, formal declarations, constitutional change, transfers of administration, and the departure of foreign troops occurred on different dates, and students should distinguish them. Sovereignty was effective only when the foreign armies left and when the governments controlled their own forces.

The layering matters for assessing what independence meant. Lebanon's 1943 amendments and the release of its leaders gave it a sovereign constitutional order, but foreign troops remained until the end of 1946 and French influence continued. In Syria, effective control of the civil administration was transferred in 1944, the Troupes spéciales came under Syrian command in 1945, and the evacuation of April 1946 ended the physical presence of foreign armies. For each country the year in which independence was "achieved" is a matter of definition: the date of a declaration, of a constitutional act, of a transfer of services, or of the last soldier's departure. Historians therefore speak of a process of independence that unfolded between 1941 and 1946, and a student who writes a single date should specify what it marks.

The new states inherited significant problems: borders drawn under the mandate, political institutions with limited experience of autonomy, armies shaped by French recruitment, economies integrated through a customs union that would soon be dissolved, communal divisions that politics had deepened, and unresolved issues such as Alexandretta. They also inherited an Arab world in transition, in which the Arab League and the question of Palestine, taken up in Chapters 59 and 61, would soon dominate regional politics.

Division VIII — Comparative Synthesis: Two States from One Mandate System · Section 58.36

Comparing Syrian and Lebanese State Formation

Placing the two histories side by side clarifies what was distinctive in each. Syria's territory was divided, and its nationalists defined their cause as the restoration of unity and the end of mandate control. Lebanon's territory was enlarged, and its politics turned on the question of what the new state was and how its communities would share power. Syria's decisive formative experience was the revolt and its aftermath, and Lebanon's was the negotiation of constitutional and communal arrangements within a French-supervised republic.

Several causal contrasts follow from this. Where Syria's dominant problem was territorial unity, its nationalists defined success in terms of ending partition and subordination, and they tended to see minority autonomy as a French device; where Lebanon's dominant problem was communal coexistence, its leaders defined success in terms of a bargain among communities, and they tended to see the unitary national state as a source of risk. The mandate's institutions reinforced these orientations. In Syria, the army and the intelligence service were instruments of imperial control that later became prizes in domestic politics; in Lebanon, the civil institutions of representation absorbed the energies of political competition and were slower to be displaced. Even the common economic framework, the customs union and the Beirut port, served different political purposes in the two countries.

Political institutions also differed. Syria developed a nationalist movement led by urban notables who aspired to a centralized parliamentary republic, while Lebanon developed a consociational pattern of communal representation, rooted in the 1926 constitution, the 1932 census, and the 1943 Pact. The role of the army differed as well: Syria inherited a force that would soon become a central political actor, while Lebanon's army remained small. Both economies were linked through the customs union and Beirut's port, yet their political economies diverged, with Lebanon's emphasis on commerce and finance and Syria's on agriculture and domestic markets.

The strategies of resistance and negotiation varied as well. Syrians combined armed revolt, strikes, and parliamentary politics, and many were willing to confront France directly; Lebanese leaders more often combined negotiation with limited confrontation, and it was a joint Christian-Muslim front that produced the 1943 crisis. These contrasts should not be exaggerated, because there were Lebanese who joined Syrian revolts and Syrians who cooperated with France.

Division VIII · Section 58.37

The Legacies of French Rule and the Meaning of Independence

The mandate left durable legacies. The borders of Syria and Lebanon, though contested at times, endured. Institutions such as the parliamentary systems, civil codes, administrative structures, and educational networks continued. The mandate also left the legacy of communal representation in Lebanon, an army shaped by recruitment patterns in Syria, and a political culture marked by suspicion of foreign promises. French cultural and economic influence persisted, especially in Lebanese elite education and commerce.

Some of the legacies were intended and others were not. France intended to leave a French-oriented elite, a set of legal and administrative models, and a continuing influence over language, education, and commerce, and in Lebanon especially it largely succeeded. It did not intend to create the nationalist movements that eventually expelled it, or the habit among both peoples of treating French promises with deep suspicion. It did not intend that the army it had built would become an independent political actor. Students should keep in mind that Chapters 61, 64, and 68 will take up these legacies, including the coups, the Arab-Israeli conflict, and the Lebanese Civil War, and that the mandate period explains the setting of those events without determining their course.

Independence resolved some questions: foreign troops departed, and sovereign governments controlled their administration and foreign relations. It left others open. The meaning of citizenship, the relation of religious communities to the state, the unity of Syria and its relation to Lebanon, the future of the economic union, the status of Alexandretta, and the role of the army remained unsettled. Both states entered independence amid a regional environment shaped by the approaching conflict over Palestine.

How historians weigh these outcomes depends on the question asked. Those interested in institutions note that both states entered independence with functioning ministries, courts, schools, and parliaments, which the mandate had built, however imperfectly and for its own purposes. Those interested in legitimacy note that the strongest claims to authority in 1946 belonged to leaders who had confronted France, not to those who had cooperated with it, and that the memory of the revolt, the strike of 1936, and the crisis of 1943 supplied each state with a founding narrative. Those interested in society note that independence changed the identity of the rulers more than the distribution of land, income, and communal power. A careful assessment keeps all three in view: the mandate left real institutions, real grievances, and real unfinished business, and independence did not settle which of these would shape the new states.

Students should therefore treat independence not as an ending but as a transition. The mandate period shaped the arrangements through which later conflicts would be fought, but it did not determine their outcomes. Chapters 61, 64, and 68 trace how those arrangements evolved.

Who experienced change most directly: Politicians and officials, townspeople affected by strikes and bombardment, and communities redefined by new borders. Who experienced more continuity: Many peasants and pastoral groups, whose obligations to landowners and tax authorities changed less. Why the pattern matters: It shows that political transformation did not automatically transform social structures.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced how French rule in the Levant produced two states, Syria and Lebanon, out of the Ottoman territories assigned to France after the First World War. Its central argument is that the mandate was neither a simple story of repression nor a benevolent preparation for independence. It combined territorial fragmentation, administrative experiment, selective cooperation with local elites, severe violence when challenged, and constitutional arrangements hedged by reservations.

French authority began with the defeat at Maysalun in 1920 and rested on the League of Nations mandate, which gave France responsibility for foreign relations and security and obliged it to frame an organic law and promote self-government. The High Commissioner in Beirut held real executive power. France divided the Syrian territories into separate units, enlarged Lebanon, and relied on a security system, including the Troupes spéciales, that drew heavily on rural and minority recruits. Historians debate whether this division was a deliberate strategy or an improvised response, and the evidence suggests elements of both.

The Great Syrian Revolt of 1925–1927 was the largest armed challenge to the mandate. It began in Jabal Druze under Sultan al-Atrash, spread across Syria and into parts of Lebanon, and was suppressed with heavy violence, including the bombardment of Hama and Damascus. It failed militarily but demonstrated the strength of nationalist feeling, drew international criticism through the League's Permanent Mandates Commission, and encouraged the shift toward constitutional nationalism.

After the revolt, the National Bloc led a strategy of constitutional politics and negotiation. The Constituent Assembly of 1928 asserted Syrian unity and sovereignty, but the High Commissioner imposed a constitution in 1930 containing an explicit reservation of mandate obligations. Syrian politics rested on a social order dominated by landowners and merchants and was shaped by regional differences in the Alawite coast, Jabal Druze, Aleppo, and the Jazira.

Lebanon followed a different path. Greater Lebanon was created in 1920 in response to a Maronite project and French strategy, and it incorporated communities, especially Muslims, who had not asked to join it. Competing conceptions of national identity, from particularism and Phoenicianism to Arab nationalism and Greater Syria, coexisted. The 1926 constitution, with its provisional article on community representation, and the 1932 census created the basis for a confessional political order, which the 1943 National Pact later consolidated through a compromise between Lebanese independence and Arab identity.

Society and culture under the mandate were varied. Economic links continued across the new borders through the customs union and the port of Beirut, labor and agrarian inequality persisted, and schools, universities, and a vigorous press fed competing national narratives. Women participated in nationalist and social movements and sparked debates about law and custom, while communities of diverse religious and ethnolinguistic identities experienced the mandate differently depending on region, institution, and social position.

The late 1930s exposed the limits of negotiation. The Syrian general strike of 1936 forced France to the table, and treaties were signed with Syria and Lebanon, but neither was ratified by France. The cession of Alexandretta to Turkey in 1939 showed that Syrian territorial sovereignty remained unresolved, and constitutions were suspended as war approached.

The Second World War changed the balance. After the fall of France and the British and Free French campaign of 1941, declarations of independence were made but did not transfer effective control. The elections of 1943, the Lebanese constitutional amendments, the French arrests of November 1943, and the crisis of May 1945, in which French forces bombarded Damascus and Britain intervened, undermined France's capacity to hold the territories by force. Independence took distinct forms: Lebanon celebrates 22 November 1943, and Syria celebrates the evacuation of April 1946, with the final foreign troop withdrawal from Lebanon completed by the end of 1946.

Comparing the two states shows how one mandate system produced different political institutions, nationalisms, and strategies. The legacies of French rule, including borders, institutions, army composition, communal representation, and cultural ties, survived independence, while unresolved questions of identity, citizenship, and sovereignty shaped the later histories treated in Chapters 61, 64, and 68.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how the modern state system of the Levant was made, and made by many hands. French officials drew boundaries and designed institutions, but Syrians and Lebanese rebelled, petitioned, bargained, and built parties, schools, and newspapers that gave those institutions meaning. The result was two states whose similarities and differences cannot be explained by either imperial design or local agency alone.

The chapter also reveals processes that recur across Part X. Mandate rule shows how international law could legitimize imperial control while creating standards that subjects invoked against it. It shows how colonial administrations relied on local intermediaries and how that reliance shaped social hierarchies. And it shows how the failures of negotiation, from the 1930 constitution to the 1936 treaties, helped convert constitutional nationalism into more radical politics. Students will meet comparable dynamics in the British-ruled territories of Chapters 57 and 59 and in the North African cases of Chapter 60.

Many of the chapter's legacies endured. Lebanon's confessional arrangements, the role of the army in Syrian politics, the status of Alexandretta, and the cultural and economic ties to France all outlasted the mandate and informed the crises examined in Chapters 61, 64, and 68. The chapter connects regions as well: it links Beirut's Mediterranean commerce to Damascus's interior markets, the rural Hawran to metropolitan Paris and Geneva, and the Levant to the wider wartime world.

Finally, the chapter invites careful thinking about independence itself. Independence arrived through declarations, elections, constitutional amendments, confrontations, and evacuations, not in a single moment. Understanding the differences among those steps helps students read later claims about sovereignty with greater precision, and it helps them see the history of the Arab world as a sequence of contested and incomplete transformations rather than a simple movement from subjection to freedom.
