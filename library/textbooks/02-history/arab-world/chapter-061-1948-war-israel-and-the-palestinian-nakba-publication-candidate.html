Part XI — Independence, Revolution, and the Arab Cold War
61

1948: War, Israel, and the Palestinian Nakba

A History of the Arab World: From Ancient Crossroads to the Modern Arab World

Chapter Opening

Chapter 59 ended on November 29, 1947, with a vote in the General Assembly of the United Nations recommending that Mandate Palestine be divided into a Jewish state, an Arab state, and an internationally administered Jerusalem. Within eighteen months the recommendation had been overtaken by civil war, the end of British rule, the proclamation of the State of Israel, the intervention of five neighboring Arab states, the flight and expulsion of a majority of Palestine’s Arab population from the territory that became Israel, and a set of armistice lines that matched the partition map in almost no respect. This chapter explains how that happened, and why a war that ended in armistice rather than peace left behind a conflict that remained unresolved.

Begin with a single day, April 9, 1948. In the morning, near the village of al-Qastal on the road to Jerusalem, Palestinian fighters were still absorbing the death of Abd al-Qadir al-Husayni, the most prominent field commander of the Palestinian Arab forces around Jerusalem, who had been killed the previous night while trying to retake the hill. At dawn, a short distance away, Irgun and Lehi fighters, working with the knowledge of the Haganah, attacked the village of Deir Yassin on the western edge of Jerusalem. By the time the fighting was over, a substantial number of the village’s inhabitants were dead, many of them noncombatants. Within days, news of the attack, in versions that exaggerated and disputed its details, was circulating through Palestinian towns and villages. On that same day, in other parts of the country, convoys were being ambushed, Jewish and Arab neighborhoods were exchanging fire, and British soldiers who still formally governed the country were preparing to leave. No single event of that day determined what followed, but the day is a useful reminder that the war of 1948 was not one conflict but several overlapping ones: a struggle over roads and villages, a contest between two national movements, a collapse of civil government, a series of local tragedies, and, later, a war between states.

The chapter’s organizing problem is therefore one of explanation and distinction. The civil war between Palestine’s Jewish and Arab communities that began after the partition vote (November 30, 1947, to May 14, 1948) was related to, but distinct from, the interstate war that began when Arab armies entered Palestine after the British departure (May 15, 1948, onward). The military history of each phase must be told, but not alone. A second strand concerns political and state institutions: the Jewish Agency and the Yishuv’s institutions that became the government of Israel, the Palestinian leadership organized around the Arab Higher Committee and local committees, and the divergent political aims of Egypt, Transjordan, Iraq, Syria, and Lebanon. A third strand, which this chapter treats as equal in weight to the military narrative, is the social history of the Palestinian Nakba (Arabic for “catastrophe”): the displacement, expulsion, and dispossession of roughly three-quarters of a million people, the destruction or emptying of hundreds of communities, and the creation of a refugee population whose status has remained a central political question. A fourth strand is diplomacy: the United Nations, mediation by Count Folke Bernadotte and Ralph Bunche, General Assembly Resolution 194, and the armistice negotiations of 1949.

The chapter’s principal chronology runs from November 30, 1947, to July 20, 1949, the date of the last of the four armistice agreements, with limited discussion of refugee relief and diplomacy through December 1949. Its subject matter is bounded by its neighbors in the textbook. The origins of the conflict, the mandate, and the partition resolution itself belong to Chapter 59. The consequences of the defeat for Egypt’s politics, including the revolution of 1952, belong to Chapter 62, and the later history of Palestinian political organization, later wars, and regional interventions belongs to later chapters. Chapter 61 explains the war itself, the Nakba, the armistice settlement, and the immediate shape of the unresolved conflict.

The chapter also asks students to confront a problem of terminology and narrative. The same events are called the “War of Independence” (Milhemet ha-Atzma’ut) or “War of Liberation” in Israeli usage and the Nakba in Palestinian and wider Arab usage; in English-language scholarship they are also called the 1948 Arab–Israeli War, the First Arab–Israeli War, or the Palestine War. These names are not neutral labels. They reflect distinct experiences: for most Israeli Jews, 1948 was a war of survival that produced a state; for most Palestinians, it was the moment of national dispossession. Serious history can hold both facts. It need not treat every claim made about 1948 as equally well supported, and this chapter distinguishes carefully among documented events, interpretations of causes and intentions, and later national narratives.

The evidence available to historians has changed greatly since the 1980s. Older accounts depended heavily on memoirs, official statements, and the records of the United Nations and the British government. From the late 1970s and 1980s, the opening of Israeli state and military archives, the British Public Record Office, and the archives of the United Nations and the American government permitted new scholarship, often associated with the “new historians,” as well as continuing work by Palestinian historians relying on oral histories, village memorial books, captured and Arab-language records, and local documentation. Some Israeli files have since been reclassified or restricted. Arab state archives, by contrast, remain much less accessible, a limitation that affects what can be said with confidence about Arab decision-making. Students should read every statement in this chapter with these limits in mind.

By the end of the chapter, students should understand why the outcome in 1949 differed so sharply from the partition recommendation of 1947: Israel was established and, by the end of the fighting, controlled roughly three-quarters of the territory of Mandate Palestine, a considerably larger share than the partition resolution had allotted; the Palestinian Arab state the resolution envisaged was not created; the West Bank came under Jordanian rule and the Gaza Strip under Egyptian administration; Jerusalem was divided; and the question of the refugees, along with the status of Jerusalem and the borders, remained unresolved. The 1949 armistice agreements ended the main interstate fighting without producing a political peace, and that distinction is among the most important facts in the chapter.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Distinguish the civil war of November 1947–May 1948 from the interstate war that followed, and explain how the two phases were connected.
  2. Trace the collapse of British authority and the failure of the United Nations transition plan, explaining why partition was not implemented as recommended.
  3. Compare the political organization, military resources, and strategic decisions of the Yishuv and the Palestinian Arab leadership between November 1947 and May 1948.
  4. Explain the proclamation of the State of Israel, the formation of the Israel Defense Forces, and the Arab League’s decision to intervene, including the different aims of the Arab governments.
  5. Reconstruct the principal military operations and truces from April 1948 to March 1949, using maps to explain changes in territorial control.
  6. Analyze the causes of Palestinian displacement, distinguishing expulsions, flight from combat, fear after massacres, local evacuation orders, and the collapse of civil life, and explain why no single cause accounts for every case.
  7. Describe the experiences of refugees, of Palestinians who remained in Israel, and of internally displaced people, including the loss of property and the prevention of return.
  8. Evaluate the roles of the United Nations, Count Folke Bernadotte, and Ralph Bunche, and distinguish humanitarian relief, political mediation, and legal claims in Resolutions 194 and 302.
  9. Explain the terms and limits of the four 1949 armistice agreements and distinguish armistice demarcation lines from final political borders.
  10. Assess how Israeli, Palestinian, British, Arab, and United Nations sources shape historical interpretation, and distinguish well-documented facts from contested questions of intention and responsibility.
  11. Connect the outcomes of 1948–1949 to later developments in Palestinian society, Israeli state formation, and Arab politics.

Historical Context

By late 1947 Palestine had been governed by Britain for a quarter-century under a League of Nations mandate, whose terms, examined in Chapters 56 and 59, committed Britain both to facilitating a Jewish national home and to safeguarding the civil and religious rights of the non-Jewish communities. The mandate period transformed both communities. The Jewish population, the Yishuv, grew through immigration from perhaps 10 percent of the population in 1918 to roughly one-third by 1947, building national institutions under the Jewish Agency, the Histadrut labor federation, and a clandestine army, the Haganah. The Arab population, about two-thirds of the total, remained overwhelmingly rural and was politically organized around notable families, parties, and the Arab Higher Committee headed by Hajj Amin al-Husayni. The Arab Revolt of 1936–1939 had been suppressed by Britain with the participation of Zionist auxiliaries, and the Palestinian leadership emerged from it exiled, divided, and without a unified armed force. Chapter 59 treats this history in detail; the present chapter relies on it without retelling it.

The Second World War and the Holocaust transformed the diplomatic setting. Jewish displaced persons in Europe, the British restriction on immigration in the 1939 White Paper, the illegal immigration campaign, and the Zionist insurgency against British rule in 1945–1947 pushed Britain, in February 1947, to refer the Palestine question to the United Nations. A special committee, UNSCOP, recommended partition, and on November 29, 1947, the General Assembly adopted Resolution 181 by a vote of 33 to 13, with 10 abstentions. The plan proposed a Jewish state and an Arab state linked by an economic union, with Jerusalem and its surroundings governed by a special international regime. The Jewish state would have received a little over half of the land, including the Negev, in a territory in which the Jewish population was a bare majority and the Arab population very large. The Zionist leadership accepted the plan, while the Arab Higher Committee and the Arab League rejected it as a violation of the rights of the majority population.

Resolution 181 was a recommendation. It specified the end of the mandate, the withdrawal of British forces by August 1, 1948, and the establishment of a United Nations Palestine Commission to oversee a transition and the creation of provisional governments. Britain, however, declined to share authority with the Commission, announced that it would end the mandate at midnight on May 14–15, 1948, and refused to facilitate a gradual handover. The United Nations had no armed force of its own, and the great powers that had endorsed partition, chiefly the United States and the Soviet Union, were not prepared to enforce it. That gap between a recommendation and the capacity to carry it out is the context in which the war began.

The regional setting also mattered. The Arab states had won formal independence only recently, most within the previous decade, and several, including Egypt, Iraq, and Transjordan, remained tied to Britain by treaty or by military dependence. Their governments faced domestic pressure over Palestine, rivalries among one another, and a reputation for rhetorical commitment greater than their capacity. Abdullah of Transjordan, who had long sought a role in Palestine and had held contacts with the Zionist leadership, pursued aims different from those of Egypt, Syria, or the Palestinian leadership. Chapters 56 through 60 describe the construction of this state system, and the present chapter shows its first great test.

Evidence and terminology. This chapter uses “Palestinian Arabs” and “Palestinians” for the Arab population of Mandate Palestine and its descendants, and “the Yishuv” for the Jewish community before May 1948 and “Israeli” for the state’s population after its establishment. It uses “Nakba” for the Palestinian experience of 1948, “expulsion” where documents show people were compelled to leave by military or official action, and “flight” where people left in response to combat, fear, or the collapse of daily life. Figures for casualties and refugees are given as ranges with their sources, because the numbers are contested and some are uncertain. “Arab Legion” refers to the army of Transjordan, which was commanded by British officers, a fact that matters for interpretation. Place names are given in the forms most commonly used in English-language scholarship; Palestinian villages are generally named in their Arabic forms and Israeli towns in their Hebrew forms.

Division I

From Partition to Civil War, November 1947–March 1948

How fighting developed inside Palestine before any neighboring Arab army crossed the border.

Section 61.1

The Partition Vote and the Beginning of Armed Conflict

The General Assembly vote of November 29, 1947, was followed by celebrations in Jewish neighborhoods and a general strike and demonstrations in Arab ones. Violence began almost at once. On November 30, Arab gunmen attacked a bus on the road near Petah Tikvah and a second vehicle near Lydda, killing several Jewish passengers, and on December 2, during a general strike called by the Arab Higher Committee, an Arab crowd attacked the Jewish-owned commercial center at Mamilla, near the Jaffa Gate, looting and burning shops. Arab-owned property was also attacked in the reprisals of the following days, but those were separate incidents. Jewish militias, in particular the Irgun, responded with bombings in Arab crowds and neighborhoods in Jerusalem, Jaffa, and Haifa. By the end of December, perhaps a few hundred people had died. The numbers are uncertain, but the pattern was clear: roads, mixed neighborhoods, and the borders between Jewish and Arab quarters of towns became zones of fear.

The two national movements reacted to the vote in sharply different ways. The Jewish Agency accepted the resolution, while privately regarding its borders, and especially the position of Jerusalem and the large Arab population inside the Jewish state, as a difficulty to be managed rather than a final settlement. The Arab Higher Committee, the Arab League, and the Arab governments rejected partition as illegitimate, arguing that the United Nations had no right to divide a country against the will of its majority population. Many Palestinians also believed, that Arab strength and British indifference would prevent the plan from taking effect. The refusal to accept the plan did not, in itself, produce an organized plan of Arab military action, and the early fighting was largely improvised.

The Arab states responded within weeks. A meeting of the Arab League in Cairo in December 1947 voted to supply the Palestinians with money and arms, on the order of ten thousand rifles and a million pounds according to commonly cited accounts, and to organize a volunteer force, the Arab Liberation Army, in preference to committing regular armies. The decision reflected the governments’ hesitation: it signaled support for the Palestinians while avoiding responsibility for a war that most of their armies were not prepared to fight. By the end of March, contemporary estimates put the death toll in the low thousands, with civilians accounting for a large share, and the pattern of reprisal, in which each attack on civilians was followed by another, was well established.

The immediate political consequence was the disintegration of the framework within which the two communities had lived together under British rule. The mandate government retained formal authority but not the will or the capacity to impose order on a country divided by a decision it did not support. Police and officials left their posts, and local authority passed gradually to community organizations. In the vacuum, each community mobilized: the Yishuv through its established national institutions and the Haganah, and the Palestinian Arab population through local committees, volunteers, and the political leadership around the Husayni family. From the outset, the conflict therefore had two dimensions that must be kept distinct in the narrative: a communal war on the ground, with its ambushes, reprisals, and civilian casualties, and a struggle over who would inherit state power when Britain left.

Section 61.2

British Withdrawal and the Failure of the Transition

Britain’s policy in the winter of 1947–1948 was governed by a determination to leave Palestine without becoming entangled in implementing a plan it had not endorsed. It announced that the mandate would end on May 15, 1948, and that it would withdraw its forces in stages, retaining control of the ports and the Haifa area for as long as possible to preserve an orderly departure. It refused to let the United Nations Palestine Commission enter the country before the end of the mandate, to transfer administration to provisional Arab and Jewish authorities early, or to establish the joint arrangements for transport, communications, and security that the partition plan assumed. British officials also had concerns that were mainly imperial: relations with Arab governments, the security of their own troops, and the need to avoid an open commitment to either side.

A decisive difference between a withdrawal timetable and an actual transfer of government is the management of police files, courts, hospitals, and municipal revenue. In a planned succession, officials would have handed functioning services to recognized successors; in Palestine, the two future governments envisaged by the UN plan could not even be jointly installed. The commission established under Resolution 181 needed access to ministries and ports, protection for its personnel, and cooperation from British officers who still commanded the territory. London’s refusal to undertake partition left these prerequisites unmet. Local councils improvised food distribution, policing, and transport, but their capacity depended on the neighborhood and the strength of armed organizations nearby. In mixed towns such as Haifa, the timing of British unit withdrawals became a material fact of battle rather than a neutral administrative detail. The documents should therefore be read in parallel: Britain’s Cabinet and Colonial Office correspondence explains its strategic priorities; the Palestine Commission’s reports show what it could not execute; and municipal records or contemporary diaries describe the costs of the gap to inhabitants.

The practical result was a progressive disintegration of mandate government. British forces continued to intervene occasionally, in some cases to stop attacks, in others to disarm one side or another, and in Haifa and elsewhere to protect their own withdrawal routes. But the rule of law eroded steadily. Postal services, railways, courts, and local administration weakened or ceased; the economy of the towns was disrupted by strikes and fighting; and the Palestine Commission, which Britain never permitted to take up administration inside the country and which worked from Lake Success, reported to the Security Council that it could not carry out its task without an armed international force that no state was willing to provide. Britain’s policy has been interpreted in contrasting ways. Some historians emphasize a deliberate withdrawal that allowed the conflict to be resolved by arms; others emphasize improvisation, exhaustion, and the pressures of a larger imperial retreat. The records show a government more interested in extrication than in any particular outcome, and the result of that stance was a vacuum that the better organized side was best prepared to fill.

Section 61.3

Palestinian Arab Leadership, Local Committees, and Volunteer Forces

The Palestinian Arab leadership entered 1948 organizationally weaker than its Jewish counterpart. Its central body, the Arab Higher Committee, had been reconstituted in 1945 under the auspices of the Arab League and was dominated by Hajj Amin al-Husayni, who lived in exile in Cairo and had a reputation for rivalry with other Arab leaders, in particular with King Abdullah of Transjordan. His wartime association with Nazi Germany and the fact that Britain had banned him from Palestine since 1937 further limited his role. The leadership was divided between the Husayni camp and the opposition associated with the Nashashibi family and other notables, and it lacked a unified administration, a regular army, a reliable system of taxation, or a stable source of arms.

Local organization partly compensated for these weaknesses. In towns and villages, national committees collected funds, organized guards, and tried to maintain services and order. In the Jerusalem region, Abd al-Qadir al-Husayni formed the Holy War Army (Jaysh al-Jihad al-Muqaddas), and in the south and elsewhere other commanders organized local forces. The Palestinian fighters were largely part-time, short of weapons, and tied to their home villages, a pattern that made village defense effective in some places and mobile offensive operations difficult. Many men had served in the 1936–1939 revolt or in British forces during the Second World War, but there was no systematic training, nor a general staff.

It is nevertheless misleading to reduce Palestinian organization to the rivalries of prominent families. The committees operating in Jaffa, Jerusalem, Haifa, and smaller localities negotiated with merchants, sought contributions from property owners, organized watches, and sometimes arranged the removal of vulnerable residents from exposed streets. Their tasks varied sharply: one committee had a functioning telephone network and access to a nearby Arab town, while another depended on intermittent volunteers or supplies carried over a road under fire. These differences complicate the retrospective description of a single Palestinian command failure. Some committees had considerable local legitimacy but could not compel cooperation beyond their districts. The Arab Higher Committee’s claims to national authority did not solve shortages of ammunition, trained officers, transport, or communication, nor did support from neighboring Arab governments replace those capacities. Local historical reconstruction should distinguish a committee’s stated military responsibility from the forces it could actually mobilize on a particular day.

In January 1948 the Arab Liberation Army, a volunteer force recruited under the Arab League’s Military Committee, began entering Palestine from Syria. Commanded by Fawzi al-Qawuqji, a veteran of the earlier revolt, it comprised some thousands of men from Syria, Iraq, Lebanon, and other countries, along with some Palestinians. The Army took positions in the north and center of the country, but its relations with the Palestinian leadership were uneasy, and its operations, such as the attack on Kibbutz Tirat Zvi and the Battle of Mishmar HaEmek in April, achieved little. It was a symptom of the Arab states’ approach: they wished to support the Palestinians without committing their regular armies, and they preferred volunteers they could control to a Palestinian force under the Husaynis. The gap between rhetoric and capacity that characterized Arab policy, and to which Chapter 59 pointed, is clear in this period.

Section 61.4

The Yishuv’s Military Mobilization: Haganah, Irgun, and Lehi

The Yishuv was better prepared. It possessed a government in embryo, with a Jewish Agency and National Council (Va’ad Leumi) that could raise taxes, mobilize labor, and coordinate defense; a unified, though not monolithic, military organization in the Haganah, with its elite striking force, the Palmach; a modern industrial base capable of producing light weapons; and a population that included many veterans of the British army and of the Second World War, together with refugees and survivors who saw the conflict as existential. David Ben-Gurion, who as head of the Jewish Agency’s defense portfolio took charge of military organization from late 1946, expanded conscription, reorganized the Haganah into brigades, and sought arms abroad.

The Haganah in December 1947 had on the order of tens of thousands of trained or partly trained personnel, but few heavy weapons, little artillery, and almost no armor or aircraft. Its main strategic problem was that Jewish settlements were scattered across the country and dependent on roads that passed through Arab territory. Ben-Gurion’s decision to hold every settlement, including isolated ones in the Negev and the Etzion bloc south of Jerusalem, imposed serious burdens on the army but preserved a political presence across the territory. From early 1948, the Yishuv also pursued arms purchases, in particular from Czechoslovakia, through agents in Europe and the United States; the arrival of Czech rifles, machine guns, and later aircraft in the spring and summer was to be decisive for the capacity of the Israeli forces.

The first months also demonstrated the Haganah’s vulnerability. On January 16, 1948, a Palmach party of thirty-five men on foot to reinforce the besieged Etzion bloc, known as the Lamed-Heh, was surrounded and killed to the last man near Hebron, and in March a large supply convoy to the Etzion bloc, the Nebi Daniel convoy, was destroyed. The Haganah’s policy until March was largely defensive, supplemented by retaliatory actions under a plan adopted in January that authorized reprisals against villages and neighborhoods from which attacks came. At the same time Ben-Gurion extended conscription to all young men and women, intensified the purchase of weapons in Europe and the United States, and, from the spring, organized the transport of Czechoslovak arms by air and sea, steps that transformed the army’s capacity by May.

The Irgun Zvai Leumi and Lehi (the Stern Group) represented a different political tradition. Both were products of the right-wing Revisionist movement and of the underground struggle against Britain, which they conducted with more violence and less restraint than the mainstream Zionist leadership. The Irgun, under Menachem Begin, and Lehi, a smaller and more radical group, had conducted attacks on British personnel and on Arab civilians, and the Jewish Agency had at times cooperated with the British in suppressing them. In 1948 they operated alongside, and sometimes against, the Haganah. The tension was not resolved until the new state’s government demanded their integration into the Israel Defense Forces and, in June, forcibly ended the Irgun’s independent arms shipment on the vessel Altalena. Understanding the distinctions among these organizations matters because many of the events discussed in later sections, including Deir Yassin and the assassination of Bernadotte, involved the dissident groups, and because the national narratives of Israeli and Palestinian historians treat their role very differently.

Section 61.5

Urban Fighting, Roads, Convoys, and the Fragmentation of Palestine

The characteristic form of the civil war was the struggle for roads. Jewish settlements and neighborhoods needed supplies, and Arab forces were able to block movement on roads that ran through Arab-populated territory. Jerusalem, whose population was roughly 100,000 Jews and some 65,000 Arabs, depended on the road to the coast, and convoys were ambushed repeatedly on the road through Bab al-Wad. Convoys to the Etzion bloc and to the Galilee were attacked, and in March the convoy known as the Nebi Daniel convoy was lost. By the end of March, Jerusalem’s Jewish population faced shortages of food, water, and fuel, and the road was effectively closed.

Jerusalem was at the center of the struggle. Its Jewish population of about 100,000 included the residents of the Old City’s Jewish Quarter, of Mount Scopus, and of districts surrounded by Arab neighborhoods, and the Arab population of about 65,000 lived in the east, north, and south, including the prosperous quarters of Katamon, Talbieh, and Baqa. Fighting in the city took the form of bombings, sniping, and attacks on convoys and on the buses that linked the Jewish districts. The Haganah forces were also under pressure to hold the Jewish Quarter and Mount Scopus, and the British, who controlled the security zones, intervened only occasionally.

Road warfare also meant a struggle over ordinary civilian movement. A lorry carrying flour, a bus bringing workers to a port, and a military supply convoy often used the same stretches of highway, so an attack on transport could deprive civilians of necessities even when a nearby settlement remained militarily secure. Protective escort systems depended on vehicles, fuel, reliable intelligence about ambush points, and coordination between drivers and armed guards. In Jerusalem, road closures increased the price and scarcity of staples, forced medical institutions to plan for shortages, and made water distribution a matter of security. In Arab cities, severed routes to agricultural villages reduced food supplies and the market outlets on which farmers relied. Neither side experienced the road war identically: Jewish communities increasingly benefited from centralized planning, while Palestinian towns and villages often defended their own approaches without comparable interregional command. The distinction helps explain why battlefield control of a junction could rapidly change civilian life across an entire district.

In the towns, fighting was less systematic and more terrible. In Jerusalem, bomb attacks and counterattacks between Jewish and Arab neighborhoods killed civilians; in January, a Jewish attack on the Semiramis Hotel killed residents, and on February 22, Arab-led bombings on Ben Yehuda Street killed dozens of Jews, with the involvement of British deserters, according to later accounts. In Haifa, the oil refinery incident in December, the Balad al-Shaykh raid by the Haganah, and other attacks and reprisals escalated tension. In Jaffa and Tel Aviv, snipers fired across the boundary between the two cities, and the border neighborhoods emptied of residents. The effects on commerce were serious: Arab commercial districts lost their Jewish customers, and the economic interdependence that had marked the mandate’s mixed towns dissolved into mutual boycott and siege.

Fragmentation had a political meaning. A country in which each community could not move safely beyond its own area could not be governed from the center. The Palestinian leadership could not prevent local deals, such as those between some villages and Jewish neighbors, nor could it impose discipline on local commanders. The Yishuv, by contrast, could treat the roads as a single strategic problem, with the Haganah’s command free to decide which operations to launch. That asymmetry became decisive in April.

Section 61.6

The Military and Political Crisis of Early 1948

By March 1948, both communities were in a crisis. For the Yishuv, the crisis was military and psychological: convoys were being destroyed, Jerusalem was under siege, the Etzion bloc and parts of the Galilee were isolated, and the expectation of an invasion by the Arab armies after the British left made the prospect of survival uncertain. Many in the Haganah leadership feared that the Jewish state, once declared, would not survive the first weeks. At the same time, the political situation of the Yishuv worsened when, on March 19, the American representative at the Security Council proposed that partition be suspended and replaced by a temporary United Nations trusteeship. The proposal shook the Zionist leadership, who feared the loss of American support for a state.

For the Palestinians, the crisis was social and political. Civilians who could afford to leave the towns had begun to do so, and the process of departure accelerated as the economy deteriorated and the sense of order disappeared. By the end of March, tens of thousands of Palestinians, according to the estimates historians have offered, and which depend on the sources they use, had left Haifa, Jaffa, Jerusalem, and other mixed towns, often to nearby Arab areas or to neighboring countries, expecting to return after the fighting ended. Many of those who left were from wealthier and more mobile classes, including families with relatives abroad, and the emigration of the urban elite deprived the Palestinian leadership of the resources and social cohesion necessary to organize resistance. The numbers and the dynamics of these early departures are discussed in Division VI.

Both national movements changed strategy. For the Arab side, the failure of volunteers to win the war and the weakness of local forces led to a gradual shift of responsibility to the Arab states, a process that culminated in the Arab League’s decision to authorize intervention. For the Zionist side, the combination of the road blockade, the arrival of arms from Czechoslovakia, and the failure of the Arab forces to exploit their early advantage led to a decision to take the offensive. These changes, taken in March and April, mark the passage from the first phase of the civil war to the second, the subject of Division II.

Section 61.7

Plan Dalet and the Strategic Reorganization of the Haganah

Plan Dalet (Plan D), completed in March 1948 under the direction of Yigael Yadin of the Haganah’s operations staff, was a general operational plan intended to prepare the Haganah for the anticipated invasion by Arab armies after the British left. It replaced earlier plans that had been defensive in character. Its immediate purpose was to secure the territory allotted to the Jewish state by Resolution 181 and Jewish settlements outside it, along with the roads between them, to establish a defensible territorial base before the Arab armies entered. To do so, it instructed commanders to seize strategic points, including police stations and British installations as the British withdrew, and to establish control over the main routes.

The plan’s language on Arab villages and towns is the part that matters most for the history of the Nakba. It directed that enemy population centers located within or near the defensive system be dealt with so that they could not serve as bases for attack. In the case of villages that offered resistance, the plan envisaged the destruction of the village and, if they resisted, the expulsion of the population outside the borders of the state. Where there was no resistance, the plan allowed garrisons to be installed and the population to remain under military administration. The text is general and leaves the decision to local commanders to determine what constituted resistance.

The text of Plan Dalet needs close reading rather than retrospective shorthand. It set out missions for protecting Jewish localities and routes in anticipation of the British departure and Arab intervention, while allowing forces to occupy or destroy villages deemed military threats and to expel inhabitants where resistance was encountered. A general headquarters plan is not itself proof that every local commander followed one identical policy or that every departure was ordered centrally. Conversely, a defensive justification for the plan does not erase the powers concerning destruction and expulsion that were written into it or how those powers were used in particular operations. Historians must ask three separate questions: what the instructions authorized; how brigades interpreted them; and what happened to named villages and their residents. Operational orders, intelligence summaries, village testimony, and reports of later return attempts may answer different portions of those questions. Dividing evidence by level of command is more useful than treating the entire dispute as a choice between 'mere defense' and a single comprehensive expulsion order.

How the plan was understood and carried out is the subject of one of the central historiographical debates of the period, presented in the Historical Debate feature below. What is not disputed is the plan’s organizational importance. It marked the transition from a force whose commanders thought in terms of defending settlements to one that thought in terms of controlling territory, and it supplied the framework for brigade-level offensives in April and May. Operations conducted in its spirit, including those in the Jerusalem corridor, at Haifa, and in eastern Galilee, brought about the capture of Arab towns and villages in the weeks before the Arab armies entered. Whether the plan’s authors intended depopulation, or whether depopulation was a consequence of its military logic, is a different question, which scholars answer differently.

Section 61.8

Operation Nachshon and the Battle for the Jerusalem Corridor

Operation Nachshon, launched on April 2, 1948, was the first large Haganah offensive of the war. Its purpose was to open the road to Jerusalem, and in particular to bring a large convoy of supplies, arms, and personnel to the besieged Jewish city. About 1,500 troops were assembled, drawn from the Haganah’s brigades, including a Palmach element, and the force was supplied, in part, with weapons that had recently arrived from Czechoslovakia. The operation involved the capture of positions along the road, including the hill of al-Qastal, a village on the road to Jerusalem.

In the first days of April the Palmach took al-Qastal; the Palestinian forces under Abd al-Qadir al-Husayni, who had come back from Cairo to command the Jerusalem front, attempted to retake it, and on the night of April 7–8 he was killed in the fighting. His death, and the funeral at which large crowds gathered in Jerusalem on April 9, deprived the Palestinian side of its most capable local commander at a decisive moment, and the Arab force that had held the hill dispersed. The Haganah, in turn, recaptured the position on April 9, and by April 20 it had cleared much of the corridor and sent through several convoys.

The tactical problem was a chain of heights and villages overlooking a narrow highway, not simply a shortage of trucks. An escort could make the journey only if hostile positions had been suppressed or bypassed, and any gain might be temporary unless troops remained to hold the ground. The struggle around al-Qastal, where Abd al-Qadir al-Husayni was killed on April 8, exemplified both the military urgency and the symbolic importance of leadership. Palestinian forces sought to regain commanding ground at great cost even while the Haganah concentrated men and arms for the corridor campaign. A successful passage of food vehicles did not mean that all approaches to Jerusalem were thereafter safe; later convoys still faced danger, and the village positions along the route remained contested. The record of each village should therefore be examined chronologically, noting first attacks, actual capture, departures, destruction, and any attempts at return. Bayt Mahsir, captured during Operation Maccabi in May, is a case in which conflating successive operations produces an incorrect account of the road campaign.

The operation had consequences for the Arab villages along the road, among them Qastal, Qaluniya (Colonia), Saris, and other communities in the hills west of Jerusalem. Many were depopulated during the operation, some as a direct result of attacks and some as people fled in fear, and their homes were in a number of cases destroyed or occupied. The corridor remained open only briefly, because the Arab forces reorganized and again closed the road in May, and the road to Jerusalem was not secure until the later Burma Road alternative route was constructed in June. The village of Bayt Mahsir, which is sometimes included in accounts of Nachshon, was attacked in April but was not captured then; it fell to the Harel Brigade in Operation Maccabi in the second week of May, together with several neighboring villages whose inhabitants fled or were expelled. Operation Harel and other operations that followed Nachshon extended Haganah control over the corridor in April, and Operation Maccabi in May, which was designed to widen the corridor and to capture positions around Latrun, was one of the operations that prepared for the Arab Legion’s arrival. The relief of Jerusalem and the depopulation of its approaches were thus linked, and later sections return to both.

Section 61.9

Deir Yassin: Violence, Massacre, and Historical Controversy

Deir Yassin, a village of some 600 to 750 inhabitants on a hill west of Jerusalem, lay near the road and the Jewish suburb of Givat Shaul. Its leaders had reportedly made an agreement of non-aggression with their Jewish neighbors, and it had not been a major center of Arab fighters, though it had been included in the sphere of operations of the Haganah in the Nachshon plan. In early April, the Irgun and Lehi, with the permission of the Haganah’s Jerusalem command, which attached conditions to it, planned an attack on the village. The operation, on April 9, involved about 120 men from the two organizations. The village’s defenders were few and poorly armed, and they resisted, and the Jewish fighters, who also took casualties, fought house to house.

What happened in and after the fighting is the center of the controversy. It is established beyond serious dispute that a considerable number of villagers, including women, children, and elderly men, were killed, that some were killed after the fighting had effectively ended, that survivors, including prisoners, were paraded through Jerusalem before being released, and that the attackers’ own leaders, as well as the Jewish Agency and the Haganah, quickly acknowledged that atrocities had occurred. The Jewish Agency sent a letter of condemnation to King Abdullah. The Haganah and the Jewish Agency denounced the killings, and the Irgun’s leader, Menachem Begin, later wrote that the attack contributed to the panic that helped the Jewish side.

The casualty figures are contested. Early reports by the Irgun and by Jewish and Arab spokesmen gave a figure of about 250, and that number was widely repeated for decades. A Red Cross delegate, Jacques de Reynier, who visited on April 10, reported on the bodies he found. A later Palestinian study of the village, carried out by researchers at Bir Zeit University in the 1980s, identified fewer than 120 victims by name, and many scholars, Israeli and Palestinian, now accept a figure in the range of 100 to 120, which is lower than the early claim but still an unambiguous massacre of civilians. The disagreement about the number should not obscure the fact that a massacre took place. Nor does the revision of the casualty figure reduce the significance of the event in the minds of the Palestinians who heard of it. Some Palestinian leaders, according to later accounts, had also exaggerated the events in the hope of rallying Arab opinion, with the unintended effect of increasing fear.

The wider consequences were political and psychological. The news of Deir Yassin spread through Palestine, and was repeated in broadcasts and leaflets, sometimes with added atrocity details, and later memoirs by Palestinians repeatedly invoke it as one of the causes of flight. Four days later, on April 13, a convoy of doctors, nurses, and staff traveling to Hadassah Hospital on Mount Scopus was ambushed in what is generally understood as a reprisal, and seventy-eight people were killed, according to the hospital’s own history. The two events demonstrate how reprisal and fear drove one another. Chapter 61’s later treatment of the causes of flight (Section 61.33) shows that Deir Yassin was important but not the sole or even the most common cause of departure.

Section 61.10

The Battle for Haifa and the Displacement of Its Arab Population

Haifa, in April 1948, was a port city with an Arab population of some 60,000 to 70,000 and a Jewish population of similar size, divided by topography: Jewish residents lived on Mount Carmel and Arab residents in the lower town and in neighborhoods near the harbor. The British, who controlled the port and used it for their withdrawal, retained a garrison there. In the weeks before April 21, incidents between the communities escalated, and Arab residents who could afford to had been leaving.

On April 20–21, the British commander, General Hugh Stockwell, informed the Haganah that British forces were withdrawing from positions between the Arab and Jewish quarters. The Haganah’s Carmeli Brigade, under Moshe Carmel, which had prepared for the moment, launched an attack, sometimes called Operation Bi’ur Hametz, on April 21–22. Using mortars and infantry attacks from the heights, it overran the Arab neighborhoods in a day and a half. Resistance was poorly coordinated; some Arab fighters from the Arab Liberation Army had withdrawn, and the local leaders were unable to hold the lower city. A delegation of Arab notables requested a truce through the British; they were presented with terms by the Haganah that included the surrender of arms, and the Arab leadership declined to sign them and left the city.

Haifa reveals why political outcomes cannot be inferred solely from a unit’s military objective. The port was indispensable to British evacuation and also to future immigration and commerce; its mixed residential geography put workers’ streets, railway approaches, and neighborhoods close to military positions. The April fighting unfolded as British troops were withdrawing from important areas and as Arab institutions lost the ability to reassure residents about their safety. Public appeals by Jewish representatives for Arab inhabitants to stay are part of the record, but they must be assessed alongside shelling, fear, armed attacks, the absence of effective protection, and the decisions taken by Arab political and religious leaders. Some inhabitants left by ship, others by road, and some remained. The historical question is not whether a single speech or order explains the departures, but how the combined effects of battle, local authority, and available escape routes made staying appear dangerous or impossible to different households.

The population then left in large numbers. The largest part moved by sea, in boats supplied or permitted by the British, toward Acre and Lebanon, and others left by road. In the following days and weeks, only a few thousand Arabs remained in the city. The reasons are documented in some detail and show the complexity discussed in Division VI: fear of the fighting, the collapse of the Arab leadership, the departure of the local committee and of wealthier residents, the British decision to facilitate evacuation, an appeal from Jewish municipal leaders for Arab residents to remain, and the effect of Haganah propaganda and actions. Some Haganah leaders encouraged departure and some opposed it. Whatever the mixture, the outcome is clear: the Arab population of Haifa was reduced from tens of thousands to a small remnant, and the city was transformed into a predominantly Jewish city within days.

Section 61.11

Jaffa, the Coastal Plain, and the Collapse of Palestinian Urban Centers

Jaffa, the largest Arab city in Palestine, with a population of some 70,000 before the war, lay alongside Tel Aviv, and the two cities’ border had been a front since December. The partition plan had assigned Jaffa to the Arab state as an enclave surrounded by the Jewish state, and the Jewish leadership regarded the enclave as a strategic problem. In late April the Irgun, with Haganah knowledge, launched a mortar bombardment and attack on Manshiya, the quarter between the two cities, beginning on April 25. British forces intervened to stop the attack and then the Haganah, in a series of operations, took over in the surrounding villages and closed the city’s links with the interior.

The city’s defense, by this point, was fragmented. The local committee had little authority, discipline among the fighters was poor, and some fighters left or demanded pay. As in Haifa, those who could leave the city did so, many by sea. By May 13, the day before the British left, Jaffa’s leaders signed a surrender to the Haganah, which entered the city, and only several thousand of the original residents remained. The loss of Jaffa had an enormous economic and symbolic importance: it was the commercial center of Arab Palestine, home to a number of newspapers and business families, and its fall demonstrated that the Palestinian political and economic infrastructure could not survive without protection.

Jaffa’s importance reached far beyond the port district. Citrus cultivation employed farmers, packers, dockworkers, merchants, and transport operators; when export routes broke down, the effects spread through the surrounding villages and into urban households dependent on seasonal wages. The adjoining city of Tel Aviv was both a commercial competitor and a strategic center for Jewish forces, so the line between economic rivalry and armed separation was exceptionally close. By late April the pressure of attacks, shortages of food and ammunition, and the failure of outside relief convinced many residents that their city could not be defended. Families leaving by boat could carry little, and those moving overland did not necessarily regard their departure as permanent. Subsequent military control over homes, shops, and orchards converted temporary-seeming flight into long-term dispossession. To reconstruct this process adequately, an account should combine military diaries with municipal revenue records, business correspondence, and testimonies of households whose decisions were shaped by livelihood as well as personal safety.

The collapse of the urban centers is among the central facts of the war. Palestinian society had been undergoing a major social transformation in the mandate period, in which towns such as Jaffa, Haifa, Acre, and Jerusalem were the centers of commerce, education, press, and political organization. Their fall, and the departure of much of their leadership, disrupted the networks that connected rural and urban communities. When Arab armies entered in May, they were not entering a country with functioning Palestinian institutions to support, but one in which these institutions were largely in ruins, a fact that shaped both the political aims and the military potential of the Arab intervention.

Section 61.12

Galilee, Rural Palestine, and the Eve of British Withdrawal

In the north, Haganah and Palmach operations between April and May extended Jewish control across eastern and western Galilee. Tiberias, whose Arab population was evacuated with British help after the Haganah captured it on April 18, was the first Arab town to fall. Operation Yiftach, from late April, secured the area around the Hula Valley and Safed. Safed, a town of mixed population in which Jews were outnumbered, fell on May 10–11 after a battle in which Palmach forces defeated the Arab defenders, and most Arab residents left. Beisan (Beit She’an) was taken on May 12–13, and its inhabitants were largely expelled or left. In the west, Operation Ben-Ami, from May 13, relieved the isolated Jewish settlements and led to the fall of Acre on May 17–18, after the state was already declared. In these operations, nearby villages were captured, with varying consequences for their inhabitants. The sequence of dates is itself informative. Tiberias fell on April 18, Haifa on April 22, Jaffa’s surrounding villages and Jaffa itself between late April and May 13, Safed on May 10–11, Beisan on May 12–13, and Acre on May 17–18, and in each case the pattern was one of a military victory followed by the departure of most of the Arab population within days. In Jerusalem, the Arab district of Qatamon, largely deserted since January, was occupied at the end of April in a Haganah operation, and its houses were soon occupied by Jewish residents. Villages in the coastal plain, such as those near Tel Aviv and in the Sharon, were emptied in April and May in the course of Operation Hametz and other operations, and in the Jordan Valley and Lower Galilee villages near Jewish settlements, such as those around Tabor and Beisan, were cleared during Operation Gideon and similar actions.

In the center, the Haganah’s battle at Mishmar HaEmek in April defeated Qawuqji’s Arab Liberation Army and led to the displacement of several Arab villages around the kibbutz. Along the coastal plain, Operation Hametz in late April and May captured villages near Jaffa, and Jewish forces consolidated the corridor between Tel Aviv and Jerusalem. The pattern was not uniform. In some regions, villages negotiated non-aggression agreements and were left alone; in others, Haganah units attacked and cleared them. Villages were frequently emptied of civilians when fighting approached, and evidence from Israeli military records and from Palestinian accounts shows many individual circumstances.

Not all the fighting went the Haganah’s way. In the Etzion bloc south of Jerusalem, a group of four Jewish settlements, defenders had held out since the start of the war, but on May 12–13 the bloc fell to a combined attack by the Arab Legion and local irregulars. A number of the defenders who had surrendered, as well as some who had not, were killed by irregular forces, and the Jewish survivors were taken prisoner; the memory of the massacre at Kfar Etzion became part of Israeli national memory, much as Deir Yassin did of Palestinian. By May 14, the Haganah controlled the areas of the Jewish state allotted by Resolution 181, apart from certain pockets, and also a number of areas beyond it. Historians offer estimates, which differ by method, of 250,000 to 300,000 Palestinian Arabs who had left or been expelled from their homes by the time the state was declared, before any Arab army had entered Palestine. That fact, more than any other, distinguishes the civil war from the interstate war that followed and demonstrates the importance of separating them.

Statehood as an act of institutional construction under fire, and the different purposes of the Arab governments that intervened.

Section 61.13

The Declaration of the State of Israel, May 14, 1948

On the afternoon of Friday, May 14, 1948, in the Tel Aviv Museum, David Ben-Gurion read the Declaration of the Establishment of the State of Israel before the People’s Council, the provisional legislative body of the Yishuv, and a gathering of invited guests. The decision had not been unanimous or easy. On May 12, the small executive known as the People’s Administration had voted six to four to proceed with a declaration after hearing military advisers who offered gloomy assessments of the chances of survival and after the American Secretary of State, George Marshall, had warned the Zionist leadership, through Moshe Shertok (Sharett) and other emissaries, against declaring a state and risking war. Ben-Gurion, who argued that the Yishuv could not afford to wait, prevailed.

The Declaration itself is a carefully balanced political document. It grounded the state’s legitimacy in several sources: the historic connection of the Jewish people to the land, the international recognition embodied in the Balfour Declaration and the League of Nations mandate, the Holocaust and the need for refuge, and Resolution 181 of the General Assembly. It declared the establishment of a Jewish state to be called Israel, to take effect upon the end of the mandate, and it promised equality of social and political rights for all inhabitants regardless of religion, race, or sex. It appealed to the Arab inhabitants to keep the peace and take part in the building of the state, and it offered peace to the neighboring states. It did not define the borders of the state; the drafters, after debate, left out any reference to boundaries, in part because the war was about to determine them. The Declaration is a primary source examined in the feature below. It was proclaimed by the People’s Council, a body of thirty-seven members drawn from the Zionist parties, and it was signed by the members who could reach Tel Aviv, in the shadow of an expected air raid. Chaim Weizmann, the elder statesman of Zionism, who was in Geneva, was named president of the Provisional State Council, and Ben-Gurion became head of the Provisional Government. The decision to proclaim on Friday afternoon, before the Sabbath and the formal end of the mandate at midnight, meant that the state would exist before the first Arab army crossed the frontier.

The meaning of the Declaration has been interpreted in different ways. Israeli historians have emphasized that it enacted a right to self-determination recognized by the United Nations and carried out under threat of invasion. Palestinian historians have emphasized that it was issued without Palestinian consent and effected a proclamation of sovereignty over territory in which a large Arab population lived, some of it outside the partition borders. The Declaration’s promise of equality and the subsequent flight and expulsion of most Arab inhabitants are an important contrast to keep in view. The immediate effect of the Declaration was the creation of a provisional government, with Ben-Gurion as prime minister and defense minister, which assumed control of the institutions of the Yishuv and set out to turn them into the machinery of a state in the middle of a war.

Section 61.14

The End of the British Mandate and International Recognition

At midnight on May 14–15 the mandate ended. The High Commissioner, Sir Alan Cunningham, left Haifa by sea on May 14, and the last British forces in the country were withdrawn in stages, with the final contingents remaining in Haifa until the end of June. Britain transferred no authority to a successor and did not recognize either an Arab or a Jewish state at the time, though Jewish forces had been preparing to take over British installations as they were evacuated. The British position was that it was ending the mandate, not partitioning the country; its government later extended de facto recognition to Israel in January 1949 and de jure recognition in April 1950.

International recognition came quickly. President Harry Truman, who had been under pressure from both domestic Jewish organizations and some of his own advisers, extended de facto recognition to the provisional government within minutes of the proclamation, a decision against the advice of the State Department. The Soviet Union recognized Israel de jure on May 17, and a number of other states, including Poland, Czechoslovakia, and Guatemala, followed in the next days. The motives of the great powers were different: American recognition reflected domestic politics, humanitarian sympathy, and the desire to preempt a Soviet advantage, while Soviet policy sought to weaken British influence in the Middle East and appeared sympathetic to the Zionist cause at that moment. Both powers, though, imposed arms restrictions on the combatants during the war, and the principal supply of weapons to Israel came from Czechoslovakia, which acted with Soviet acquiescence.

The Arab states refused to recognize the legality of the new state. In their view, the General Assembly had no authority to partition a country, the mandate had been intended to lead to the independence of a unified Palestine, and the declaration of a Jewish state on part of the territory was a violation of the rights of its inhabitants. The legal arguments, which were expressed in the Arab states’ communication to the Secretary-General of May 15, are discussed in the primary source below. This difference over legitimacy was the diplomatic counterpart of the fighting, and it persisted long after the armistice agreements.

Section 61.15

The Israel Defense Forces and the Machinery of Statehood

A state at war needs a single army. On May 26, 1948, the provisional government issued an ordinance establishing the Israel Defense Forces (IDF), and the Haganah ceased to exist as an independent organization. The act was both administrative and political. It affirmed the principle that the armed forces would be subordinate to the civilian government and that no other force could operate. Ben-Gurion, who regarded the private armies of the Yishuv as a danger to national authority, pursued the principle consistently, including against his own political allies in the Palmach and its parent party.

Integrating the dissident groups was the first test. The Irgun in most areas agreed to merge its units into the IDF, but it retained a separate command in Jerusalem for a time and attempted to land a large shipment of arms from France on the ship Altalena on June 20–22, in violation of the terms of the truce and the integration agreement. When negotiations over the distribution of the weapons failed, the army fired on the ship off Tel Aviv, and it was destroyed with some loss of life on both sides. Begin ordered his men not to retaliate and a civil war among Jews was avoided. After Bernadotte’s assassination in September the government moved decisively against Lehi, and the Palmach, whose leaders had been affiliated with the left-wing party Mapam, was disbanded as a separate command in November 1948. By that point the army had become a single national force.

Unifying the armed organizations was also a contest over the relationship between the cabinet and the military command. Units trained under clandestine conditions had developed loyalties to political parties, local commanders, and the Palmach’s distinctive command culture; incorporation required regular ranks, common supply accounting, military law, and a monopoly over recruitment and armaments. The June 1948 confrontation over the Irgun arms ship Altalena made the issue unmistakable. The dispute involved whether weapons delivered to the new state could remain under independent political control, and it ended in armed confrontation and loss of life. Ben-Gurion’s insistence on a single chain of command was a state-building decision as well as a battlefield measure. At the same time, formal incorporation did not erase differences over tactics or the treatment of civilians. The existence of an IDF command structure should not lead students to assume that every local action had been planned in advance by the provisional government.

The army’s growth was rapid. At the time of the declaration, the Haganah and its associated forces had perhaps 30,000 troops at their disposal; conscription of men and women, and the arrival of immigrants, including Holocaust survivors who were sometimes sent into combat with minimal training, as well as foreign volunteers (Mahal), including World War II veterans from Britain, the United States, and other countries, swelled its strength. By the winter of 1948–1949, estimates put the number of personnel under arms at roughly 100,000. The provisional government also created ministries, a civil service, courts, and a system of military government over Arab-populated areas, and held elections for a constituent assembly in January 1949. State formation was therefore not a single act but a process conducted during war, and it relied on the institutions the Yishuv had built during the mandate.

Section 61.16

The Arab League and the Decision to Intervene

The Arab League’s decision to intervene emerged from months of debate. At the end of 1947, the League had approved the supply of arms and volunteers to the Palestinians, a policy that suited most governments, which wished to avoid direct commitment. By April 1948, with the Palestinian side losing ground, the League’s political committee, meeting in Cairo, concluded that intervention by regular armies was necessary when the mandate ended. Some governments hesitated. The Egyptian prime minister, Mahmud Fahmi al-Nuqrashi, was reluctant, and his army’s leaders warned of its lack of preparedness; it was only in the second week of May, under pressure from King Farouk and from public opinion, that the cabinet and parliament approved the entry of troops.

The governments entering Palestine did not share an aim. Transjordan’s King Abdullah, who had long sought to extend his authority over the Arab parts of Palestine, regarded the intervention as a chance to secure them, and he had discussed with Jewish Agency representatives, including a meeting with Golda Meir on May 10, the possibility of an arrangement that would avoid a war with the Yishuv. He did not succeed in that attempt, but his army’s later operations indicate that its priorities were the Arab areas assigned to the Arab state by the partition plan and Jerusalem, rather than an attack on the Jewish state proper. Egypt’s king and government wanted to demonstrate Egypt’s leadership of the Arab world, to prevent Abdullah from taking Palestine, and to establish an Egyptian presence in the south. Syria, whose government under President Shukri al-Quwatli feared that Abdullah’s dream of a Greater Syria endangered its own sovereignty, acted partly to forestall Hashemite expansion. Iraq, a Hashemite monarchy allied with Britain, sent an expeditionary force whose political direction was not clearly subordinated to the others. Lebanon, divided by confessional politics and militarily weak, sent a small force and was reluctant to take part. Saudi Arabia and Yemen sent token contingents.

The Arab League’s military committee, headed by an Iraqi general, Ismail Safwat, had drawn up a plan for coordinated attacks by the Arab armies, but the governments did not adopt it in practice, and the actual deployments followed the separate decisions of each government and its generals. The Arab League named Abdullah the nominal commander of the combined forces but did not establish a real joint command, and each army acted according to the orders of its own government. This division mattered. The Palestinian leadership was not given a decisive role, and the Arab governments distrusted both the Mufti’s ambitions and each other’s. The Arab states entered a war for which they had not prepared a common plan and for which their military and political preparations were poor. The gap between public pledges, which spoke of saving Palestine, and the actual capabilities and priorities of the governments is a theme to which the chapter returns. The Arab states’ own explanation of their intervention, in a communication to the United Nations on May 15, is analyzed in the Primary Source Window below.

Section 61.17

Palestinian Armed Organizations and the Limits of Independent Command

The Palestinian armed organizations had suffered a severe blow in April: the death of Abd al-Qadir al-Husayni and the defeats of other local forces. By May the Holy War Army was fragmented, and the Arab Higher Committee had limited capacity to coordinate the remaining irregulars. The Arab League, which had in the winter refused to give the Mufti’s forces a central role, chose in May to let the regular armies take command of operations. In practice, Palestinian fighters were either absorbed into the Arab regular armies, fought as local militias beside them, or continued as semi-independent bands. In the Jerusalem area and around Hebron, Palestinians fought alongside the Arab Legion and Egyptian forces, among them volunteers from the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood; in Galilee, they were supplemented by the Arab Liberation Army under Qawuqji.

Palestinian irregulars faced a structural problem after May 15 that was distinct from their battlefield losses. The intervening armies spoke in the name of defending Palestine, but they reported to sovereign governments and controlled their own sectors, supplies, and diplomatic contacts. A Palestinian commander operating in the hills near Jerusalem could not reliably direct an Egyptian column in the south or a Legion unit in the central highlands. The Arab Liberation Army, already divided from the Husayni-aligned forces before the invasion, also retained an independent command. Disagreement concerned not just tactics but the political destination of the war: would the Arab-held territory become a Palestinian state, join Abdullah’s kingdom, or remain under a provisional Arab League authority? The All-Palestine Government proclaimed in Gaza later in 1948 displayed those contradictions rather than resolving them. Understanding the weakness of Palestinian sovereignty requires following the relationship between local military capacity and the diplomacy of neighboring states, not treating the latter as automatic representatives of the former.

The limits on Palestinian independence also reflected the political goals of the states that intervened. The Arab Legion in the areas it controlled disarmed some Palestinian irregulars and discouraged the formation of separate Palestinian administrations. Egypt, for its part, retained control over its occupied areas of the south, though it later accepted the formation of an All-Palestine Government in Gaza in September 1948, a body whose real authority was slight. The result was that the Palestinians, who had been the principal actors in the civil war, became secondary actors in the interstate war. Their leadership was divided, their forces lacked heavy weapons and command structures, and key decisions about the course of the war were taken in Amman, Cairo, Damascus, and Baghdad. The consequence, a Palestinian national movement without sovereignty or territory under its control at the end of the war, is one of the central results of 1948.

Section 61.18

Military Resources, Strategic Objectives, and the Balance of Forces in May 1948

Estimates of the balance of forces in May 1948 vary, and historians depend on different sets of sources and definitions of who counts as a soldier, so figures should be treated as approximate. A widely used estimate holds that the Israeli forces numbered about 30,000 mobilized personnel at the start of the interstate war, with the number rising rapidly through the summer, while the Arab regular armies that entered Palestine in May numbered in total in the low tens of thousands, with Egypt and Transjordan fielding the largest contingents, Iraq and Syria smaller ones, and Lebanon very few. Egypt’s expeditionary force was initially several thousand troops, expanded later; the Arab Legion had perhaps 4,500 to 6,000 men in Palestine; Iraq, Syria, and Lebanon each committed forces numbered in the low thousands or below. The widespread image of a small Jewish force confronting overwhelming Arab numbers is not supported by the figures on the ground at the start of the invasion, though in total population and potential manpower the Arab states were vastly larger than the Yishuv, and Israeli commanders at the time feared the outcome.

Over the course of the war the balance changed. Israel’s forces grew rapidly, as the truce allowed mobilization and rearmament, and weapons acquired in Czechoslovakia, and later in other places, gave them advantages in artillery, aircraft, and armor that they did not possess in May. The Arab armies, by contrast, were hampered by supply problems, limited manpower, restrictions on British arms deliveries resulting from the United Nations embargo, and the lack of a joint command. By the end of 1948, estimates suggest that Israel’s forces outnumbered the Arab forces in the field, a pattern which scholars have used to argue that Israel’s victory was due not to a sudden advantage but to better organization, more effective mobilization, and unity of command.

The distinction between nominal strength and strength in the field matters. Israel’s army of about 30,000 in May included thousands of support personnel, poorly equipped units, and troops tied to defending settlements, and could concentrate only limited forces on any one front, while each Arab army, advancing on its own line, brought a smaller number to the point of contact. The decisive fact, in most sectors, was that Israeli units were not outnumbered and defended prepared positions. A further factor was the quality of command: the Arab Legion’s British officers gave it a professional edge, but the Egyptian, Iraqi, and Syrian armies lacked experience, and none had a plan for a joint campaign.

The strategic objectives of each side mattered as much as the number of troops. The Israeli command’s objectives were to secure the territory of the state and its settlements and to prevent a coordinated Arab attack; its commanders understood that the war would be decided by the first weeks. The Arab commanders’ objectives were more varied, as noted, and often limited: Egypt’s was to occupy southern Palestine and Gaza, Transjordan’s to secure the Arab areas, Syria’s and Iraq’s to take positions in the north and center. Few were prepared for a decisive campaign against the Jewish state, and such coordinated plans as existed were set aside in the confusion of the first days. These differences help explain why the Arab armies, individually, were stopped in battles around the borders of the Jewish state, as the next division shows.

Section 61.19

The Egyptian Advance through Gaza and Southern Palestine

Egypt committed the largest Arab force to Palestine, but it did so late and with the least preparation. Prime Minister Mahmud Fahmi al-Nuqrashi had resisted entering the war until the second week of May, and the army that crossed the Sinai border on May 15, 1948, under Major General Ahmad Ali al-Mawawi, was assembled in a few days. Estimates of its initial strength differ, from roughly 5,000 to about 10,000 men with armored cars, light tanks, artillery, and a small air force, and it was reinforced through the summer, with Sudanese and Saudi volunteers attached to its command and Muslim Brotherhood volunteers operating alongside it. Egyptian staff officers had little current intelligence about Israeli positions and had expected something closer to a police action than a campaign. The army’s supply line ran across the Sinai desert, along a single coastal road and railway, and that constraint governed everything it did.

The Egyptian advance moved in two directions. The main column followed the coastal road through Rafah, Khan Yunis, Gaza, and al-Majdal, in territory that Resolution 181 had assigned to the Arab state, while the Israeli settlements that lay close to the road became the first obstacles. Kfar Darom and Nirim were attacked on May 15, and the Egyptians besieged rather than overran them. A second column moved from al-Auja through Beersheba and Hebron toward Bethlehem and the southern edge of Jerusalem, where it made contact with the Arab Legion, and by about May 21–24 Egyptian forces were fighting around the kibbutz of Ramat Rachel. Egyptian aircraft meanwhile bombed Tel Aviv and other targets in the opening weeks, killing dozens of civilians and demonstrating, before Israel had effective fighters of its own, how exposed the new state’s population centers were.

The decisive early engagements took place at the Negev kibbutzim. At Yad Mordechai, a small kibbutz on the coastal road, some 130 defenders held out against the Egyptian assault from May 19 until May 24 and then withdrew, having delayed the advance by several days. Negba, further inland, withstood repeated attacks in early June, and Nitzanim, on the coast, fell on June 7 when its Israeli defenders, outgunned, surrendered. The importance of these settlements was not that they stopped the Egyptian army, which was able to bypass them, but that they absorbed its attention and time while Israel mobilized.

The Egyptian thrust toward Tel Aviv reached its northern limit on May 29. Egyptian forces occupied the village of Isdud (Ashdod), and in the afternoon of that day Israeli observers at Nitzanim reported a column of tanks, armored vehicles, and artillery moving north along the coastal road; accounts of its size vary widely, from hundreds to over a thousand vehicles. The bridge over the Lakhish River at Ad Halom, which the Givati Brigade had blown up on May 12 in anticipation of precisely this movement, delayed the column. That evening Israel flew its first fighter mission, four Avia S-199 aircraft assembled from Czechoslovak parts and flown by pilots that included overseas volunteers. The aircraft bombed and strafed the Egyptian column near the bridge, and one, flown by Eddie Cohen, was shot down. The attack inflicted limited physical damage and became an important symbol of the new Israeli air arm. The Egyptian advance stopped in the Ad Halom area amid the destroyed bridge, logistical limits, resistance by nearby Israeli positions, and concern about further Israeli attacks; the evidence does not establish that this single sortie caused the halt. The place came to be known by the Hebrew phrase meaning “up to here.”

What followed must be distinguished from that first halt. The Givati Brigade attacked the Egyptian positions at Isdud in a series of operations between May 29 and June 3, collectively known as Operation Pleshet. These ground attacks were poorly coordinated and short of ammunition and did not dislodge the Egyptians, who dug in and held Isdud until October. Historians therefore describe the outcome as an Egyptian tactical success within a strategic halt: Egypt kept its forward position, but it advanced no further, and the initiative gradually passed to Israel. The Egyptian army, stretched along its long supply line and facing a growing opponent, did not attempt another large advance in the first phase of the war. Egypt’s war aims, set by King Farouk and the cabinet, were in any case limited: to establish an Egyptian presence in southern Palestine, to prevent Abdullah from taking the whole country, and to demonstrate Egyptian leadership in the Arab world. Chapter 62 examines how the officers who fought here, and the political leaders who sent them, interpreted the experience.

Section 61.20

The Arab Legion, Jerusalem, and the Central Highlands

The Arab Legion was the best-trained and best-disciplined Arab army, but it was a small one. Its total strength was perhaps 8,000 to 10,000 men, of whom some 4,500 to 6,000 entered Palestine in the first weeks, organized in infantry brigades with armored cars and artillery. It was commanded by John Bagot Glubb (Glubb Pasha), and many of its senior officers were British officers seconded to Transjordan under the Anglo-Transjordanian treaty of 1946. The British government, which paid a subsidy to Transjordan and kept a military mission there, had to balance its relationship with Abdullah against its stated neutrality and against the risk that its officers would find themselves in combat with Israeli forces. After the opening days, British officers were not to take part in operations in the area allotted to the Jewish state, and the Legion’s ammunition supply from Britain was restricted, a constraint that shaped its operations.

King Abdullah’s aims determined the Legion’s deployment. He had long wished to extend his rule to the Arab parts of Palestine, and he had held discussions with the Jewish Agency, including a meeting with Golda Meir on May 10, about avoiding a collision. The Legion therefore moved into the areas that Resolution 181 had assigned to the Arab state, taking Ramallah, Nablus, and Hebron, and into Jerusalem, whose status under the resolution was international. Even before the formal invasion, on May 12–13, the Legion and local irregulars overran the Etzion bloc south of Jerusalem, and its defenders, including some who had surrendered, were killed in a massacre that became a defining memory in the Yishuv.

The struggle for Jerusalem was fought simultaneously at different levels. In the Old City, fortified positions, narrow streets, and the confinement of the Jewish Quarter limited the value of larger formations; its surrender on May 28 involved residents as well as combatants and subsequently generated competing accounts of protection, damage to religious sites, and captivity. Outside the walls, the Legion’s positions around Sheikh Jarrah and the approaches from the east helped prevent Israeli forces from linking all Jewish neighborhoods into one continuous military area. Abdullah’s generals also had to conserve a relatively small professional force rather than sacrifice it in every battle requested by other Arab governments. The result was not a decisive conquest of Jerusalem by either party but the emergence of a divided city. British officer John Bagot Glubb’s involvement was militarily significant and politically controversial: critics suspected Britain of directing Arab policy, while the Legion’s own records describe operational judgments constrained by troop numbers and supply. Those competing assessments should be tested against orders and deployments, not presumed from the nationality of officers alone.

The struggle for Jerusalem was a struggle for two cities. In the new city and its suburbs, Israeli forces held the Jewish neighborhoods but had to take the Arab neighborhoods that lay between them, and in the Old City, which contained the Jewish Quarter and the principal holy places of three religions, the Haganah garrison was cut off. On May 17–19 the Legion entered the Old City, and the Israelis, who had briefly opened a link to the Quarter through Mount Zion on May 18, were pushed back. The Quarter, defended by a few hundred fighters, many of them poorly armed, and sheltering about 1,500 civilians, was shelled and gradually reduced. It surrendered on May 28. About 1,300 civilians were expelled to the Israeli-held part of the city and a few hundred men were taken prisoner. Buildings were damaged or destroyed in the following months, and Jews were barred from the Old City for the next nineteen years.

For King Abdullah the Old City and the Haram al-Sharif had enormous symbolic weight; he styled himself guardian of the holy places, and his claim to Palestine rested in part on that role. The Legion avoided attacking the territory assigned to the Jewish state, concentrating instead on holding Jerusalem’s Arab areas, the Latrun salient, and the road to Ramallah. Israeli forces, for their part, tried and failed to take the Old City in July and held on to Mount Scopus and the western districts. How far British policy shaped Abdullah’s restraint is debated; the documents show that London encouraged limited aims, but Abdullah’s own calculations, including his understanding with the Zionist leadership that the Legion would not seek to destroy the Jewish state, appear to have been the primary cause.

Section 61.21

Latrun, the Jerusalem Corridor, and the Struggle over Supply

Jerusalem’s survival depended on the road to the coast. The city held about 100,000 Jews, who had been cut off by Arab control of the road since the spring, and its supplies of food, water, and fuel were nearly exhausted by late May. The Legion occupied the Latrun salient, the hill with a British-built police fortress and a Trappist monastery that dominated the main road, in the days after May 15, and it denied the road to Israeli convoys. Capturing Latrun became an Israeli priority, and the effort to do so was one of the war’s most costly failures.

The first attack, on May 25–26, was made by the newly formed Seventh Brigade, whose troops included recently arrived immigrants, among them Holocaust survivors, who had been given minimal weapons training. It failed. A second attempt on May 30–June 1, with more artillery but still limited intelligence, and a third on June 9–10 also failed. In the various attacks the Israelis lost well over a hundred dead, with some estimates higher, and the Legion, fighting from prepared positions and short of ammunition rather than men, held the fortress. The episode left lasting bitterness in Israeli military history, and many of the inexperienced soldiers who died there had arrived in the country only weeks before.

Latrun should be mapped as a fortified position guarding the principal road from the coastal plain toward Jerusalem, with the surrounding hills and the approaches to Ramallah and the central highlands shown separately. Israeli operations failed repeatedly in May and early June, but the attackers changed: some formations included newly arrived immigrants with limited training and insufficient familiarity with the terrain, while the Legion occupied prepared positions and could bring concentrated fire on the approaches. Casualty totals differ among operational studies and should not be treated as precisely established by a single memorial tradition. The construction of the so-called Burma Road, an improvised route through hills south of the blocked highway, did not make the Latrun fortress disappear; it supplied an alternative passage that helped relieve shortages while the front remained contested. Thus a tactical defeat at the fortress and a logistical success in sustaining Jewish Jerusalem could coexist. The distinction illustrates why judging an operation by whether a hill was captured may miss its wider consequences.

The alternative was a road that avoided Latrun. Israeli engineers and laborers found a track through the hills to the south and, between late May and the middle of June, widened it into the so-called Burma Road, constructed largely by hand and by improvised machinery under occasional fire. The first jeeps passed in early June and trucks by the time the first truce began on June 11, and a pipeline carrying water to Jerusalem followed in the autumn. The route relieved the siege, and it meant that Jerusalem’s western districts could be held, although Latrun itself remained in Jordanian hands until 1967. The struggle shows how a few kilometers of road could outweigh larger territorial gains elsewhere, and how the Legion’s limited aim, to hold the Arab area, allowed it to defend the position without trying to exploit it.

Section 61.22

Syrian Operations on the Northern Front

Syria’s army was small, recently formed, and equipped with a mixture of French and other material that included a few old tanks and armored cars. It had been independent only since 1946, and the Syrian government, headed by President Shukri al-Quwatli, feared both the Zionist movement and the ambitions of Abdullah of Transjordan, whose Greater Syria project threatened Syrian sovereignty. Its aims were limited, and it mobilized only a part of its army. Some Syrian units crossed into Palestine on May 15–16, in the Jordan Valley, where they took the village of Samakh on May 18 and positioned themselves for an attack on the kibbutzim south of the Sea of Galilee.

On May 20–21 Syrian armor and infantry assaulted Degania Alef and Degania Bet, the oldest kibbutzim in Palestine. The defenders, assisted by units from the Golani Brigade, stopped the Syrian tanks with improvised weapons, including Molotov cocktails, and with a small mountain gun brought forward at the last moment; one Syrian tank was destroyed at the gate of Degania Alef and others retreated. The Syrians withdrew and did not renew the attack on the Degania settlements, a defeat that had political as well as military effects in Damascus. In June they shifted north and, in a surprise attack on June 6–10, captured the kibbutz of Mishmar HaYarden, west of the Jordan River in the Galilee Panhandle, establishing a bridgehead that Israeli counterattacks failed to eliminate.

The Syrian position at Mishmar HaYarden had particular significance because it put troops west of the Jordan and threatened roads and irrigation works in the Huleh region. The fortified bridgehead survived Israeli attempts to retake it before the first truce, giving Damascus a bargaining position it later relinquished during armistice negotiations. Yet the Syrian army’s poor communications, changing priorities, and concern for the domestic political situation discouraged a sustained offensive from that foothold. Israeli commanders faced a separate problem: concentrating on the Syrian salient might weaken positions facing Iraqi forces farther south or the Arab Liberation Army in Galilee. The largely static front after June therefore reflected a regional allocation of limited forces, not simply inactivity or a lack of strategic objectives. For civilians, villages on both banks of the river faced shelling, restricted cultivation, damaged infrastructure, and fear of renewed fighting; those difficulties continued because the later demilitarized-zone provisions did not settle questions of landownership or sovereignty. The Syrian campaign is consequently inseparable from the border disputes that followed the armistice.

The Syrian record illustrates the limits of Arab coordination. The army operated on its own line, with little communication with the Iraqis to the south or the Arab Liberation Army to the west, and its commanders, preoccupied by the possibility of a coup or of Hashemite expansion, did not press its advantage. The consequences were political: the army’s poor showing contributed to the popular criticism of the regime and, in March 1949, to the first of a succession of military coups, examined in Chapter 64. The northern front stayed largely static from June until the armistice.

Section 61.23

Iraqi and Lebanese Forces and the Wider Northern and Central Fronts

Iraq’s army, one of the largest in the Arab world, had a long route to Palestine and sent a smaller force than its size would suggest. An expeditionary corps of about 3,000 men crossed the Jordan River in mid-May and later reinforcements, which brought the total to several thousand more, according to estimates that differ by source. Its first target was the Israeli settlement of Gesher and the hydroelectric works at Naharayim, which it attacked on May 15 and was repulsed. Redirected by the Arab Legion’s command, the Iraqis moved into the hills of Samaria and took positions around Jenin, Nablus, and Tulkarm, a triangle from which they threatened the coastal plain at its narrowest point, only a few kilometers north of Netanya and some twenty kilometers from the sea.

An Israeli effort to take Jenin in early June, mounted by the Golani and other brigades, failed, and the Iraqis held the northern West Bank for the rest of the war. Iraq’s position was curious: its government was Hashemite, allied with Britain, and sympathetic to Abdullah, yet its command acted with considerable independence from Amman, and its army was never integrated in the Legion’s chain of command. In 1949, after Israel and Transjordan concluded their armistice and agreed to adjust the line in the Triangle, Iraqi troops withdrew and handed their sector to the Legion, which meant that Iraq could leave the war without negotiating an armistice of its own.

The central front also exposes the limited usefulness of nominal troop totals. Iraqi battalions operating far from their bases had to coordinate ammunition, transport, and casualty evacuation across Transjordan while holding a line with many possible approaches from the coastal plain. Israeli formations faced their own constraints: securing the narrow coastal corridor remained more urgent than launching repeated assaults into the hills, and the failed June attempt around Jenin demonstrated the costs of attacking organized positions without adequate preparation. The Iraqi withdrawal in 1949 was thus a diplomatic and command transfer, not the result of a battlefield surrender. In Lebanon, the fighting near Malkiya involved Syrian and Arab Liberation Army activities as well as Lebanese units; broad claims that 'Lebanon fought at Malkiya' conceal changes of control and the identity of troops in individual engagements. The most useful military comparison identifies the responsible formation, the route by which it arrived, and the objectives its national government authorized, rather than merging all Arab soldiers into an undifferentiated coalition.

Lebanon’s part was small and symbolic. The Lebanese army, a few thousand men, put about a thousand in the field, and the government under President Bishara al-Khuri, which feared the war’s effect on the country’s confessional balance, had been reluctant to take part. Its units fought chiefly along the frontier near Malkiya, a village that changed hands more than once in the opening weeks, and supported the Arab Liberation Army, which held positions in central Galilee under Fawzi al-Qawuqji. The ALA, joined by volunteers from several countries, was never integrated into the regular armies and was defeated in October. The contrast among these contingents makes the point that the “Arab army” of 1948 was five armies, each with its own government, objectives, and problems, and that none of them was designed or trained to coordinate with the others.

Section 61.24

The Battlefield by June 1948: Advances, Defenses, and Strategic Limitations

By the first week of June the front lines had settled into a pattern that no side had planned. In the south, the Egyptians held Gaza, the Negev road, and the Isdud salient but had been halted well short of Tel Aviv; in Jerusalem, the Legion held the Old City, the Latrun fortress, and Sheikh Jarrah, while Israel held the western city; in the center, the Iraqis held the Jenin–Nablus–Tulkarm triangle; in the north the Syrians held a salient at Mishmar HaYarden and the ALA held central Galilee. Israel held the main area assigned to the Jewish state, together with western Galilee, Jaffa, and a corridor to Jerusalem, but it had lost the Etzion bloc and the Jewish Quarter and was cut off in parts of the Negev. The Arab armies had captured little Israeli-held territory, and nowhere had they broken through.

The costs were severe for both sides. By the end of the war about 6,000 Israelis, roughly one percent of the Jewish population, had died, about two-thirds of them soldiers, and a large share of the losses came in the first weeks. Arab military casualties are much less certain: estimates for Egypt, Transjordan, Iraq, Syria, and Lebanon together range from a few thousand to well over ten thousand, and Palestinian civilian and combatant deaths are also uncertain. Both sides were short of ammunition, of trained commanders, and of heavy weapons.

Any map showing the first-truce front must distinguish territory held continuously from roads merely traversed by mobile forces. Jerusalem’s western districts were linked imperfectly to the coastal plain; Israeli agricultural settlements could be encircled even when their region appeared to be controlled by Israel; and the Arab Liberation Army’s central-Galilee positions did not form a single continuous line comparable to a European battlefield. These gaps shaped both military planning and civilian movement. The first phase also demonstrated the different advantages of professional regular units and rapidly expanding irregular forces: the Arab Legion could defend fortified ground effectively, while Israel could move reserves among several fronts as its central command matured. Control of the air began to change once Israel acquired modern aircraft, but air superiority was not immediate or uniform. Students should therefore read the June map as an interim military situation, not as a prediction of the October victories or a proxy for what boundaries the parties would ultimately accept.

Security Council Resolution 50, adopted on May 29, called for a four-week cessation of hostilities and an embargo on the introduction of war material and fighting personnel, and the parties accepted it in early June, with Israel agreeing first and Egypt and Transjordan hesitating until about June 8–9. The truce favored the side with the better capacity to mobilize and to import weapons. Historians draw different conclusions about the first phase, but they largely agree on several points: the Arab armies were not unified and had limited aims; Israel’s forces were smaller than the combined Arab armies’ nominal strength but, in the main sectors, not outnumbered on the ground; and the first month showed that the Arab armies could not destroy Israel but that Israel could not yet defeat them, which is why the truce, when it came, was a pause between two phases of the war rather than the end of it.

Section 61.25

The First United Nations Truce, June 11–July 8, 1948

The truce was the first test of the United Nations as a military as well as a diplomatic actor. Security Council Resolution 50 of May 29 called on the combatants to cease fire for four weeks and to refrain from introducing fighting men and war material into Palestine and the Arab states, and it charged the mediator, Count Folke Bernadotte, with supervising the arrangements with the assistance of military observers. Israel accepted on June 1 and began to prepare for the ceasefire. Egypt and Transjordan, hesitant because their armies were still advancing or defending positions, agreed only on June 8–9 after pressure from Britain and the United States. The truce took effect on June 11.

Bernadotte recruited observers, mostly officers from Belgium, France, Sweden, and the United States, initially a few dozen and later several hundred, to patrol the lines and investigate complaints. They were unarmed and dependent on the cooperation of the parties, and they had no authority to compel anyone to do anything. From the first days, both sides accused each other of violations, and a number of the complaints were well founded. Israel received arms shipments and aircraft in contravention of the embargo, including Czechoslovak weapons and the pieces of fighter aircraft assembled in Israel, and it continued to mobilize men of military age, who were registered as workers or trainees. The Arab states reinforced their forces, though more slowly, and Egypt and Transjordan moved troops and supplies behind their lines. In Jerusalem, the completion of the Burma Road and the building of a pipeline were treated as supply measures, which Arab spokesmen disputed. The Altalena incident of June 20–22, when Israeli forces sank an Irgun arms ship off Tel Aviv, occurred during the truce and was both a violation by the Irgun and a demonstration of Ben-Gurion’s determination to end independent armies.

In the Negev, the truce produced a different dispute. The Israeli settlements there were isolated by Egyptian forces, and Egypt refused to let Israeli supply convoys pass freely, arguing that convoys were not covered by the truce. Bernadotte and the observers attempted to arrange supply under United Nations supervision, with limited success, and the issue was a recurring pretext for later Israeli action. The truce also produced the first systematic estimates of the refugee crisis: a UN relief official, Sir Raphael Cilento, estimated in August that 300,000 to 350,000 Palestinians were already in need of relief, a number that later reports revised upward. On June 16, the Israeli cabinet decided to bar Palestinian refugees from returning while the war lasted, a decision that Bernadotte protested and that later became the foundation of Israel’s policy, as Division VI explains.

The mediator used the truce to propose a political settlement. His suggestions of June 27–28, discussed in Division VII, were rejected by both sides, and as the four weeks drew to an end it was clear that fighting would resume. On July 7–8 the Arab governments, led by Egypt, refused to extend the truce, and on the morning of July 8 Egyptian forces attacked Israeli positions in the Negev. The pause had lasted twenty-eight days, and it had altered the balance in ways that the observers were not equipped to prevent.

Section 61.26

Rearmament, Military Reorganization, and the Second Truce

Israel used the truce to transform its army. The Haganah’s units had been organized as brigades with different levels of equipment and training, and its commanders, notably Yigael Yadin as chief of operations, now reorganized them into about a dozen numbered brigades with common staff structures, uniforms, and discipline under Chief of Staff Yaakov Dori. Conscription, extended to men and women up to the age of about forty, and the arrival of thousands of immigrants, many of them from displaced-persons camps in Europe, together with foreign volunteers, increased the number of personnel under arms from roughly 30,000 on May 15 to approximately 60,000 or more by early July, according to Israeli sources and estimates by historians that differ in detail. A growing share of these were equipped with Czechoslovak rifles and machine guns, and the army gained armored cars and half-tracks, artillery, mortars, and in July a handful of bombers. The air force, which had begun operations on May 29 with a few improvised fighters, acquired more aircraft, including Spitfires and Czechoslovak-built Messerschmitt derivatives. The navy, a few converted vessels, began to attack Egyptian shipping.

The Arab forces rearmed more slowly. Egypt, which had the largest army, was dependent on British and other European suppliers whose deliveries were slowed by the embargo and by their own governments’ caution, and its commanders, with long supply lines and units spread from Rafah to Bethlehem, faced mounting logistical difficulties. The Arab Legion’s principal problem was ammunition, as British shipments were restricted, and Glubb was cautious about offensive operations as a result. The Iraqis and Syrians were limited by distance, and the Lebanese by politics. No joint command was created, and the Arab League’s efforts to coordinate resulted in few concrete decisions. By July, the nominal strength of the Arab forces was not much smaller than Israel’s, but the Israelis were better organized, had a shorter supply line, and had a single chain of command.

The second truce was not merely an interval in which both sides waited for permission to resume operations. It required the UN truce staff to investigate claims that men and weapons had crossed boundaries despite restrictions, and accusations depended on incomplete observation in ports, border crossings, and remote airfields. Israel’s purchases abroad and the growth of its mobilized forces were central to the transformation of the military balance, but procurement did not instantly translate into effective formations; crews needed training, aircraft required maintenance, and newly recruited troops had to learn how to fight with established units. Arab governments also tried to resupply or rotate forces, with different degrees of success. The political stakes were equally important. Diplomats debated whether control of a village gained after a ceasefire should be recognized in subsequent negotiations, while commanders sought better positions before the next line was frozen. The truces therefore belong inside the causal military chronology and not only in a separate UN diplomatic narrative.

When the truce ended on July 8, Egypt resumed attacks on Israeli positions in the Negev and Israel launched a series of operations, known as the Ten Days. The Security Council, in Resolution 54 of July 15, determined that the situation in Palestine constituted a threat to the peace within the meaning of the Charter and ordered a ceasefire within days, with the threat of further measures. This was the first time the Council invoked the language of Chapter VII in relation to Palestine. The second truce took effect on July 18 and, unlike the first, was of indefinite duration; it was supervised by the observers, who were now organized in a permanent body, and it remained in place, with frequent violations, until the autumn. By its start, Israeli forces had captured Lydda, Ramle, Nazareth, and parts of Lower Galilee, and held the initiative on every front, although they had failed to break the Egyptian line in the south or to take the Old City.

Section 61.27

The Ten Days and Operation Dani: Lydda, Ramle, and Central Palestine

The Ten Days, from July 8 to 18, were a set of Israeli operations on several fronts. In the south, Operation An-Far and subsequent attacks on the Egyptian positions in the northwestern Negev were intended to relieve the Negev settlements, but they failed to dislodge the Egyptians, who held their line and, in some sectors, advanced. In the north, Operation Dekel took Nazareth on July 16 and cleared much of Lower Galilee. In Jerusalem, Operation Kedem, an attempt to capture the Old City on July 16–17, was unsuccessful. The most consequential operation, however, was Operation Dani in the center, whose aim was to eliminate the Arab-held salient around the airport at Lydda and the towns of Lydda and Ramle, which lay near Tel Aviv, and to widen the corridor to Jerusalem.

Operation Dani was commanded by Yigal Allon, with the Yiftach and Harel Palmach brigades, the Eighth Armored Brigade, and other forces. The attack began on the night of July 9–10; Israeli units took the airport and surrounding villages on July 10 and entered Lydda on July 11. The town’s population was large. Before the war it had perhaps 18,000 to 20,000 residents, and refugees from the surrounding villages and from the coastal plain had swelled the number to 30,000 or more. It was defended by local militia and a small detachment from the Arab Legion, which had withdrawn most of its troops from the area on the orders of its British commanders, a decision for which Glubb and Abdullah were criticized by Palestinians. On the afternoon of July 11, a column of jeeps from the 89th Commando Battalion, commanded by Moshe Dayan, drove through the town firing, and the resulting confusion and the fighting that followed caused numerous casualties.

The events at Lydda on July 11–12 require careful distinction. It is well documented that there was fighting in the town, that a substantial number of Arab civilians and fighters were killed, and that Israeli accounts put the total deaths on those two days at around 250, a figure that other historians have disputed in both directions. Within that, there is a separate and more disputed set of claims about the Dahmash mosque, where a number of Arab men had gathered. Israeli accounts describe a surrender followed by firing from the mosque and a response by Israeli troops; Palestinian accounts, based on testimonies by survivors and by those who buried the dead, describe the killing of people who had taken refuge or surrendered, and give numbers of about 175 or more. Historians who have examined the evidence differ on whether, and how many, people were killed inside the mosque itself, on whether those killed were combatants or had surrendered, and on the sequence of events; the precise toll cannot be established with confidence, and the mosque-specific claims should not be conflated with the overall death toll for the town.

The decision that followed is not in dispute. On July 12, after Ramle had surrendered, Allon and Yitzhak Rabin, who was operations officer of the Yiftach Brigade, sought and received Ben-Gurion’s approval for the removal of the population. The brigade order, as published in the scholarly literature from Israeli military archives, directed that the inhabitants of Lydda be expelled quickly, without regard to age, in the direction of Bayt Nabala, and similar orders applied to Ramle. In the following days, the 50,000 to 70,000 inhabitants of the two towns were forced out, in many cases on foot in the July heat, toward the Arab lines near Ramallah. Some of the weakest, especially the elderly and small children, died on the way from exhaustion or thirst; how many cannot be said. Rabin’s own account of the decision was removed from his memoirs by Israeli censors, and published in the United States in 1979. Israeli troops looted the abandoned towns, and only a few thousand people remained, concentrated in small areas under military administration. Lydda and Ramle were also subsequently settled by Jewish immigrants.

Two other cases show that Israeli practice was not uniform. At Nazareth, which surrendered on July 16 to a force under a Canadian-born Israeli officer, Ben Dunkelman, who reportedly refused an order to expel the inhabitants, most of the population remained. This contrast, with Lydda and Ramle only a few days earlier, shows that outcomes depended in part on the decisions of individual commanders and political leaders, and that by July, the army’s treatment of Arab populations had become more permissive of expulsion than it had been in April, a change that Division VI discusses.

Section 61.28

Operation Yoav and the Struggle for the Negev

The southern front was quiet in late summer, but the Israeli leadership regarded the Negev as indispensable. The partition plan had assigned it to the Jewish state, Bernadotte had recommended giving it to the Arabs, and Israeli settlements there were isolated. After weeks of dispute over supply convoys and an Egyptian refusal to allow them to pass, Israel launched Operation Yoav (also called Operation Ten Plagues) on October 15, 1948. The pretext was an attack on a convoy, an incident whose circumstances are disputed, but the operation had been prepared well in advance, and Ben-Gurion had been persuaded to approve it in the expectation that the Security Council would not be able to react in time.

Operation Yoav was directed by Southern Command under Yigal Allon, with an Israeli force of several brigades and with air support that destroyed Egyptian airfields and bombed positions. Its plan was to break the Egyptian line in the coastal plain between Majdal and the Negev, isolate the Egyptian units in the interior, and open the road to Beersheba. The Egyptian army, deployed over a long and thin front and short of ammunition and reserves, was broken into pockets. Beersheba fell on October 21, the Israelis took Beit Jibrin and other positions in the following days, and a force of some 4,000 Egyptian soldiers was surrounded in the Faluja pocket, where among the besieged was a young officer named Gamal Abdel Nasser, whose experience is discussed in Chapter 62.

Operation Yoav illustrates the relationship between a battlefield breakthrough and a humanitarian transformation. The Israeli command sought to link the Negev settlements with the rest of the state, undermine Egyptian positions east of the coastal corridor, and reverse the isolation of southern garrisons. Its attacks toward Beersheba combined maneuver, concentrated fire, and control of road junctions, while Egyptian forces struggled to maintain mutually supporting positions along an extended supply line. The capture of Beersheba on October 21 shifted control of the principal inland town and displaced much of its remaining Arab population. Other communities experienced different sequences: some residents fled advancing troops, others were expelled after capture, and still others moved repeatedly toward Gaza as nearby fighting expanded. To assess the campaign, the text should distinguish the military objective of opening a corridor from the treatment of inhabitants of the places taken. Territorial victory did not itself determine whether civilians could stay, and later restrictions on return helped turn the military movement of 1948 into a lasting refugee geography.

The Security Council, in Resolution 61 of November 4, called for a return to the lines of October 14, and in Resolution 62 of November 16, for armistice negotiations, and Bunche, then acting mediator, pressed for compliance. Israel complied only partially: it kept the territory it had taken in the northern Negev and bargained over the Egyptian forces at Faluja. By November the Israelis had also taken the road to the Negev settlements, the hills around Hebron’s approaches, and the former Egyptian positions around Isdud and Majdal, and Egypt’s army had been pushed back toward Gaza. In late October, as part of the same campaign, Israeli forces captured the village of al-Dawayima, west of Hebron, on October 29, where, according to Israeli and Palestinian sources, a large number of villagers were killed; the numbers are disputed, ranging from dozens to several hundred in different accounts.

The consequences for Palestinian communities were severe. Most of the inhabitants of Beersheba, which had about 4,000 residents, had already left, and the people of Isdud, al-Majdal, and many villages of the southern coast and Hebron foothills were driven out or fled. The Negev Bedouin, who had numbered several tens of thousands, were largely displaced to Gaza and Transjordan or restricted to a small area, and only a minority remained. For Israel, the operation transformed the military position: it had broken the Egyptian army in Palestine, secured the road to the Negev, and made it clear that no Arab army could restore the situation by force.

Section 61.29

Operation Hiram and the Conquest of Northern Galilee

In the north, the Arab Liberation Army remained in central Galilee, holding a pocket of villages around Sasa, Jish, and Tarshiha, and in October it attacked Israeli positions near Manara, an incident that gave Israel the justification it needed. Operation Hiram began on the night of October 28–29 under Northern Command, led by Moshe Carmel, with several brigades, including the Seventh, the Carmeli, and units from the Oded and Golani brigades. It lasted about sixty hours. The ALA, short of supplies and unsupported by the regular Arab armies, collapsed, and Israeli columns pushed north to the Lebanese border and, in places, into Lebanon, occupying numerous Galilee villages as well as a smaller group of localities inside southern Lebanon, from which Israeli forces subsequently withdrew under the Lebanese armistice.

The operation was accompanied by a number of well-documented atrocities. At Safsaf on October 29, Israeli soldiers shot a number of villagers, including prisoners, in groups, and the number of dead is estimated at between fifty and seventy by different sources. At Jish (Gush Halav) and Eilabun, villagers were expelled, and at Eilabun some men were killed. At Saliha on October 30, dozens of civilians were killed when a house into which they had been herded was blown up, and at Hula, a village in Lebanon, on October 31, dozens of men were shot. Sources include Israeli military records, letters from soldiers and officials, and testimonies; each site’s numbers need separate attribution, and sources differ. Protests came from within Israeli political circles, including the left-wing Mapam, and the authorities opened investigations, which led to prosecutions that had limited results; Ben-Gurion, in correspondence, described the events as damaging to Israel’s image.

The pattern of settlement after Hiram also cautions against equating military conquest with universal depopulation. Christian villages such as Jish, Druze villages, and mixed communities sometimes retained substantial populations, while neighboring Muslim and Christian villages experienced flight, expulsion, or killings. Religion alone cannot explain these outcomes: local surrender arrangements, commanders’ decisions, political contacts, and the presence or absence of active resistance all mattered. At Safsaf, Saliha, Eilabun, and Hula, specific allegations and documented killings must be examined individually, using Palestinian testimony, Israeli military inquiries, and other contemporary records rather than assigning one casualty figure to the entire operation. The Israeli army’s occupation of some villages inside southern Lebanon adds a separate territorial dimension; withdrawal from Lebanese territory under the 1949 armistice should not be confused with the fate of Galilee communities that came under lasting Israeli control. The historical result was both the defeat of the Arab Liberation Army as an effective regional force and an uneven transformation of Upper Galilee’s population.

The treatment of the population varied. Some Christian and Druze villages, including Rama and Peqi’in, were allowed to remain, and in some cases the army did not interfere with villagers; elsewhere, commanders ordered, or encouraged, residents to leave. Tens of thousands of Palestinians went to Lebanon, and others were displaced within Galilee. The campaign extended Israeli control well beyond the partition borders, to the whole of the Galilee, and the combination of military victory with expulsions and killings shaped the demography of northern Israel and the refugee population in Lebanon. Division VI places these events in the wider pattern of displacement.

Section 61.30

Operation Horev and the Final Southern Operations, 1948–1949

The Egyptians had been defeated but not driven from the Gaza area, and in December the Israelis moved to finish the job. Operation Horev (also called Operation Lot) began on December 22, 1948, after a period of preliminary attacks and in response to a dispute over the Egyptian-held positions in the Negev. Southern Command, again under Allon, broke the Egyptian lines near Gaza and Rafah and moved southwest, across the border into Sinai. Israeli forces took Abu Ageila in late December and approached al-Arish, threatening to cut off the Egyptian forces in the Gaza area from their base.

The offensive into Egyptian territory caused an international crisis. Britain, which had a treaty relationship with Egypt, warned Israel to withdraw and sent aircraft to patrol, while the United States also pressed Israel to withdraw. Under this pressure Israel pulled back from Sinai in the first days of January 1949. A ceasefire took effect on January 7, the day on which Israeli aircraft shot down several British reconnaissance planes, an episode that deepened tension with London, and Egypt agreed to armistice talks, which opened on Rhodes on January 13. The Egyptian government, which had been shaken by the assassination of its prime minister in December and by the army’s defeat, was ready to end the war.

The final southern operations were shaped by geography as much as by political timing. Gaza's coastal corridor, the inland Beersheba road, the Sinai approaches, and the sparse routes toward the Gulf of Aqaba were distinct axes, and control of one did not guarantee control of the others. During Operation Horev, Israeli units crossed into Sinai at points, drawing British and international attention to the danger that fighting might spread beyond Palestine. Egypt’s willingness to negotiate followed military setbacks but also depended on domestic politics and the prospect of extricating surviving forces without a public capitulation. Operation Uvda in March 1949 was an advance into a sparsely populated southern region, with relatively little direct combat compared with the great battles of 1948; it nevertheless mattered enormously for the eventual armistice map and Israeli access to the Red Sea. A full operational account must distinguish dates of the advance, dates of occupation, and dates of diplomatic acknowledgment. Otherwise a map can mistakenly suggest that the armistice negotiations simply recorded positions established months earlier.

The last operation took place after the armistice with Egypt had been signed on February 24. In Operation Uvda, between March 5 and 10, Israeli forces advanced through the southern Negev against negligible opposition, and on March 10 they reached Umm Rashrash on the Gulf of Aqaba, where they raised a flag at the site of the future port of Eilat. The operation gave Israel an outlet to the Red Sea and strengthened its position in the negotiations with Transjordan, which were then in progress; Israeli representatives made clear that Jordan’s refusal to concede changes in the Triangle might lead to renewed fighting. The dates and campaign names used in this division are those most commonly found in the scholarly literature, which draws on Israeli military records, UN truce documentation, and Arab accounts; where these sources differ about operational details, the text identifies the difference.

Division VI

The Palestinian Nakba: Displacement, Dispossession, and the Destruction of Communities

What happened to Palestinian towns, villages, and families, reconstructed place by place rather than by a single formula.

Section 61.31

The First Waves of Palestinian Displacement, 1947–Early 1948

The Nakba did not begin with the arrival of Arab armies or even with the fall of Haifa. It began in the winter of 1947–1948 with a quieter exodus of people who left because life had become unsafe and unworkable. The first to go were usually those with the means and connections to do so: landowning and merchant families, professionals, and officials in Jaffa, Haifa, and Jerusalem, who moved to Beirut, Cairo, Amman, Damascus, or to the Arab-held hill country, expecting to return when the fighting subsided. Banks closed or restricted withdrawals, public services deteriorated, shops lost their Jewish customers and suppliers, and the mandate police and officials withdrew from one district after another. In a city like Jaffa, a port of some 70,000 people whose citrus export trade had made it the commercial center of Arab Palestine, the blockade of the border with Tel Aviv and the sniping that closed streets drove out those who could leave by sea or by road in the first months. In Jerusalem, the prosperous Arab neighborhood of Qatamon, where many wealthy Christian and Muslim families lived, emptied steadily after the bombing of the Semiramis Hotel on January 5, 1948, and the Haganah occupied it at the end of April.

Contemporary intelligence reports, and the historians who have used them, estimate that between 75,000 and 100,000 Palestinian Arabs had left by the end of March 1948. The Arab Higher Committee, alarmed, stopped issuing exit permits at the end of March and later threatened penalties for those who left, and local committees and the Arab Liberation Army sometimes urged families to remain; at other times, local leaders and commanders arranged the evacuation of women, children, and the old from villages near the fighting, a practice found in many wars. Pressure from the other side is also documented. In February 1948, for example, the villagers of Caesarea (Qisarya) on the coast left in circumstances that Haganah records describe as the result of a deliberate effort to induce departure, and in the coastal plain and near Jewish settlements some villages were abandoned after threats or after incidents. At the same time, other communities signed local agreements with their Jewish neighbors that held for a time, and many villagers stayed.

The first departures should not be mistaken for a unified, voluntary evacuation organized by Arab leaders. Urban business families with savings and connections could arrange temporary refuge in Beirut, Cairo, or Amman; workers, small traders, and village households generally had fewer options. Even a household intending to return after a short interruption might lose the practical ability to do so when streets were blocked, rent payments ceased, or property changed hands. In some districts people moved from one Palestinian town to another without initially crossing an international border, and a temporary host community could itself become a battlefield weeks later. Contemporary Israeli intelligence assessments and Palestinian personal accounts are useful precisely because they capture these multiple stages of movement, although neither source type records every household. Asking when a village 'became depopulated' may consequently obscure a longer sequence of partial evacuation, attempts to return, temporary reoccupation, and final exclusion.

Two features of this early phase must be emphasized. First, most of those who left did not regard the departure as permanent. They left furniture, livestock, tools, and standing crops behind, and many carried only a few days’ belongings and the keys to their houses. Second, the emigration of the urban elite weakened Palestinian society as the war developed. Merchants, bankers, teachers, doctors, and local leaders were the people who might have organized relief, sustained local committees, and negotiated with the British and the United Nations, and their departure deprived the communities that remained of leadership and capital. When the Haganah’s offensives began in April, the structure that might have resisted them had already been hollowed out. The first historians of the exodus, among them Palestinian writers such as Nafez Nazzal, who collected testimony from refugees from the Galilee, stressed this sense of a country being left without protection.

Section 61.32

The Mass Displacement of Spring and Summer 1948

The scale of displacement changed in April and May, when the Haganah’s offensives, described in Division II, took Tiberias, Haifa, Jaffa, Safed, Beisan, and Acre, together with scores of villages in the corridor to Jerusalem, the coastal plain, Galilee, and the Jordan Valley. The IDF Intelligence Service, in a report dated June 30, 1948 and entitled “The Emigration of the Arabs of Palestine in the Period 1/12/1947–1/6/1948,” counted about 391,000 Palestinians displaced by June 1, with a margin of error of 10 to 15 percent, of whom some 239,000 came from areas that Resolution 181 had assigned to the Jewish state. The report divided the exodus into stages and identified May as the decisive stage, in which what it called a psychosis of emigration, a collapse of confidence in Arab strength, took hold. The figure for early June should be compared with the contemporaneous estimates of United Nations officials, who put the number of Palestinians in need of relief at 300,000 to 350,000 in August, and the Acting Mediator’s report of October 18, which gave 472,000, and with the 726,000 estimated by the UN Economic Survey Mission in 1949.

The movement of people took place in waves, and each tracked a phase of the war. In April and May, the fall of the towns sent tens of thousands to nearby Arab areas or by sea and road to Lebanon and Transjordan. Tiberias’s Arab residents were evacuated under British protection on April 18 after the Haganah took the town; Haifa’s population, of whom some 50,000 or more left within days, was largely evacuated by sea and road in late April; Jaffa emptied around the time of its surrender on May 13; and in the eastern Galilee, Safed’s Arab community left in the second week of May after the Palmach’s capture of the town, and Beisan’s inhabitants were removed after May 12. By May 15, when the Arab regular armies entered, a substantial portion of the people who would become refugees had already fled or been expelled, although later movements in May, July, and the autumn accounted for much of the eventual total. This chronology qualifies any account in which the exodus is treated as a consequence of the Arab invasion.

Counts must be tied to their observation dates. The Israeli intelligence assessment compiled on June 30 estimated roughly 391,000 people had moved by June 1, but its authors acknowledged substantial uncertainty and did not provide a precise count for May 15. Other retrospective historical estimates for the months before the regular Arab invasion are lower; none justifies asserting that most of the eventual 1948–1949 refugee population was already displaced by the moment of statehood. Comparing an end-of-May intelligence estimate with the later UN estimates of approximately 700,000 to 750,000 without explaining different coverage, dates, and definitions creates a false impression of numerical precision. Equally important, the June report describes distinct routes of flight: from rural areas to a nearby town, from that town to another community, and only later across a border. These repeated movements mean that reports counting people in temporary destinations can overlap or miss families altogether. The correct account is that the exodus began before May 15 and expanded in successive waves afterward, with major later displacements associated with the July and October campaigns.

The second wave, in July, came with Operation Dani and the expulsion of Lydda and Ramle, which added some 50,000 to 70,000 in the space of days. A third wave came in October and November, when Operations Yoav and Hiram cleared the northern Negev, the Hebron foothills, and central and upper Galilee, displacing the people of Beersheba, Isdud, al-Majdal’s hinterland, and dozens of villages, and sending tens of thousands more to Gaza, the Hebron hills, and Lebanon. A final phase continued into 1949 and later, as Israeli forces cleared border villages, and as the remaining population of al-Majdal, a town that had become a refugee center, was moved to Gaza in 1950. Of the Negev’s Bedouin, who had numbered several tens of thousands, a small minority remained within Israel.

The total is uncertain and depends on definitions. A careful assessment would say that between about 600,000 and 760,000 Palestinians left or were expelled from the territory that became Israel, with the United Nations figure of 726,000 for 1949 the most widely used. Many of them moved more than once, from a village to a town, to a camp, and then to another country. The summary conclusion that nearly all of the towns and most of the villages of the area that became Israel were emptied before the armistices is one on which historians with otherwise sharply different views agree.

Section 61.33

Expulsions, Massacres, Fear, and the Causes of Flight

The best historical work does not seek a single cause. It distinguishes several, which operated together in most places, and asks which was decisive where, and what the documents show.

Direct expulsion is the clearest category. At Lydda and Ramle, as Section 61.27 shows, written orders, memoirs, and military reports document an Israeli decision to remove the population. There are other cases, in many of which commanders ordered villagers to leave or removed them by force: in parts of the coastal plain, in the Galilee villages cleared during Operation Hiram, in several villages around Beersheba and Hebron, and in border zones during 1949. The IDF intelligence report of June 1948 attributed at least 55 percent of the exodus to Haganah operations and their influence, with a further share, summarized by some analysts as about 15 percent, to the Irgun and Lehi, so that the report’s own accounting placed roughly 70 percent on Jewish military action, and about 5 percent on Arab orders. Historians such as Benny Morris, who first published and analyzed the report, found it reliable on the whole but noted some errors, and its methodology and categories require critical reading.

Flight from combat is the second category, and it was common. Haifa’s residents left during and after a battle fought around them; villagers in the path of an advance left in anticipation of shelling or attack; others fled when the mortar rounds began. Fear after atrocities is the third. The massacre at Deir Yassin on April 9, spread with exaggeration by Arab as well as Jewish sources, was cited by many survivors as a reason for leaving, and later massacres, at Safsaf, Saliha, Hula, and al-Dawayima, among others, had a similar effect in their regions, though the numbers killed at each place differ among sources. Psychological warfare is a fourth: leaflets, loudspeakers, and rumors, deliberately used by Haganah and Palmach units, as in the campaign around Safed in May, to encourage civilians to leave before an attack. The fifth is the collapse of civil life: the loss of food supplies, water, employment, and local authority, and the flight of leaders, which left populations exposed.

A sixth explanation, long advanced in Israeli accounts, holds that Arab leaders and radio stations ordered the Palestinians to leave. Historians who have examined the radio monitoring of the period, including the Palestinian historian Walid Khalidi and others, and later Israeli scholars working in the archives, have found no evidence of a general order to evacuate, and the Haganah’s own intelligence recorded Arab institutions trying to stop the exodus. There were local instances in which Arab commanders or committees advised or ordered the evacuation of civilians, as at some villages near Tiberias and in parts of Haifa, and these are documented. They do not explain the exodus as a whole.

The question of intention is distinct. The idea of “transfer” of the Arab population had been discussed by some Zionist leaders before 1948, and Ben-Gurion’s diaries and correspondence from the period show that he considered the demographic consequences of the war with approval. Historians disagree about whether the expulsions resulted from a systematic plan, from the permissive effect of Plan Dalet, or from decisions taken locally by commanders with the approval or acquiescence of political leaders. What the record shows is that once the exodus was under way, the Israeli government and army decided to prevent return and used the emptied territory for settlement, and that a committee including Yosef Weitz of the Jewish National Fund was constituted in the summer of 1948 to plan the use of abandoned land and, in Weitz’s own diary, to bring about permanent change. The Historical Debate feature examines these interpretations.

Section 61.34

Depopulated Villages, Property Loss, and the Prevention of Return

By the end of the war several hundred Palestinian communities in the territory that became Israel had been emptied of their inhabitants. The count depends on how a “village” is defined and which sources are used. Walid Khalidi’s All That Remains (Institute for Palestine Studies, 1992), a village-by-village survey based on mandate records, maps, and testimony, documents 418 villages that were depopulated and occupied or destroyed; Palestinian compilations, such as those of Salman Abu Sitta, include more settlements and urban quarters and reach more than 500; and Israeli researchers using military records, such as Benny Morris in The Birth of the Palestinian Refugee Problem, count somewhat fewer, around 370 localities, while acknowledging the same general scale. The mandate government’s Village Statistics 1945 remains the standard baseline for population and land ownership by locality.

What happened to a village after its inhabitants left varied. Some were demolished at once, in some cases by explosives, to prevent the return of the population, a policy recommended in military and settlement circles; some were taken over by kibbutzim and new immigrant towns; some had their buildings reused; a few, such as Lifta on the outskirts of Jerusalem, remain largely standing as ruins. In cities, Arab homes in Jaffa, Haifa, Acre, and Jerusalem’s Qatamon were occupied by Jewish immigrants, soldiers, and state agencies. Looting by soldiers and civilians was widespread and documented in Israeli sources, and Ben-Gurion complained of it, but it was not stopped effectively. Orchards, groves, and fields were harvested or planted by Jewish farmers, and in the late summer and autumn of 1948 the Israeli authorities organized the use of the abandoned agricultural land for the next harvest.

Loss of property occurred through more than a single legal instrument. Military occupation and immediate use of houses or farms came first; emergency administrative arrangements addressed unattended buildings, harvests, and businesses; and a more elaborate statutory system emerged in subsequent years. Students must not project the later 1950 Absentees' Property Law unchanged backward into every 1948 event. The question of whether a house was empty on the date troops arrived, whether the owner was in a nearby village, and whether a family had been prevented from crossing the armistice line could determine its later classification. Orchards required irrigation and maintenance, while livestock and stored grain could be lost before any formal transfer took place. Even where a village building remained standing, the social institutions that made a return possible—schools, mosques, churches, market links, and village councils—might have disappeared. Property registers, military orders, and family deeds offer different kinds of evidence for reconstructing that transformation, and each must be linked to a clearly dated claim.

The state regularized this process by law. Emergency regulations on abandoned areas and absentee property were issued in 1948 and early 1949, and the Absentees’ Property Law of March 1950 defined an “absentee” as, in essence, anyone who had left their place of residence for another part of Palestine outside Israel or for an Arab country after November 29, 1947, and placed their property under a state Custodian, who was empowered to transfer it to a Development Authority. The definition also captured Palestinians who stayed in Israel but had been displaced within the country. The effect was that large quantities of Arab-owned land and buildings passed to the state and to Jewish users, and that the departure of owners became legally irreversible.

The prevention of return was a policy and not only a consequence. On June 16, 1948, the Israeli cabinet decided not to allow the refugees to return while the war lasted, and subsequent decisions linked the question to a general peace. Military practice reinforced the policy: villages were destroyed, borders were closed, and in 1949 and after, refugees, many of them peasants trying to harvest crops, recover possessions, or rejoin family members, who crossed the armistice lines were treated as infiltrators, and the army fired on those who were caught. The outcome was a permanent change in the ownership of land and the composition of the population of the country.

Section 61.35

Refugee Routes, Camps, Families, and the Humanitarian Crisis

The refugees’ journeys followed the geography of the war. From Haifa and Acre, many went by sea or road to Lebanon; from Galilee, across the northern frontier to Lebanon and Syria; from the central coast and the interior to the hill country of the West Bank, held by the Arab Legion and the Iraqis; from Jaffa, the southern coast, and the Negev, to Gaza and, to a lesser extent, to Egypt; and from the Jordan Valley across the river into Transjordan. Approximate distributions in 1949–1950, based on relief registrations and rounded here because the sources differ, were about 280,000 in the West Bank, around 200,000 in Gaza, where the population of the area roughly tripled, some 100,000 in Lebanon, perhaps 75,000 to 90,000 in Syria, and several tens of thousands in the East Bank of Transjordan.

The humanitarian situation in the autumn and winter of 1948–1949 was grave. Families lived in caves, schools, mosques, abandoned buildings, orchards, and open fields, and in the camps that were later set up they lived in tents. Cold, rain, and flooding caused suffering and deaths in the winter, and disease and malnutrition struck children and the elderly. Families were separated, and many people lost contact for years. The host communities, themselves poor, shared what they could, and the governments of the host states had little capacity. Relief first came from local charities, the Red Cross societies, and religious bodies, including the American Friends Service Committee (the Quakers), which took responsibility for the relief of refugees in Gaza in 1948–1949. The General Assembly established the United Nations Relief for Palestine Refugees in November 1948, which coordinated the voluntary agencies and provided food and shelter, and, from May 1950, UNRWA took over.

Emergency relief operated on several scales that are easy to conflate. Local committees and families provided food and lodging in the first weeks, while organizations such as the Red Cross and American Friends Service Committee undertook broader programs under difficult logistical conditions. UN agencies later attempted to set standardized requirements for rations, tents, medical care, sanitation, and winter shelter. A registration for bread distribution was not necessarily an accurate census of permanent residence, and it could change as households moved, children were born, or formerly independent families exhausted their resources. The economic burden on receiving communities also mattered: Gaza's sudden increase in population strained water, employment, housing, and municipal services, while Lebanese and Syrian towns faced different legal and social arrangements. Refugees preserved ties to home villages through family networks, records of land, oral accounts, and political associations. Relief was thus essential to survival but could not substitute for an agreement about return, compensation, or lasting civic status.

The refugee experience differed by class, gender, and place. Peasant families, who had lost land, their principal source of livelihood, faced the greatest difficulty, and the transition to dependence on rations marked the loss of a way of life. Wealthier urban families could rent rooms, draw on relatives, and move abroad for work in Beirut, Amman, and the Gulf, and educated refugees, often schoolteachers, found work in the expanding schools and civil services of neighboring states. Women, who in the village economy had performed agricultural, domestic, and craft work, were often the ones who held families together in the camps, queued for rations and water, and kept children in school. The legal position of the refugees varied: Jordan extended citizenship to most of them in 1949–1950, Syria granted rights short of citizenship, Lebanon did not integrate them and imposed restrictions, and Egypt kept Gaza’s refugees in a status that limited their movement. These choices reflected the policies of the host states and the refugees’ own insistence on their right to return, which they feared would be undermined by resettlement.

Section 61.36

Palestinians Who Remained: Minority Communities and Internal Displacement

Not all Palestinians left. At the end of 1949 some 150,000 to 160,000 Palestinian Arabs lived in the territory under Israeli control, about one-eighth of its population. Among them, the largest groups lived in Galilee, including Nazareth, whose population largely stayed; in the Little Triangle, the strip of villages along the armistice line, such as Umm al-Fahm, that Israel obtained under the agreement with Transjordan in April 1949; in the remains of the mixed towns of Haifa, Jaffa, Acre, Lydda, and Ramle, where a few thousand people were left in a small area of the former Arab quarters; and in the Negev, where only a minority of the Bedouin remained. Palestinian Christians, Muslims, and Druze found themselves in different situations: Druze commanders had joined the Israeli side in 1948, and the community’s relationship with the state developed differently from that of other Arab citizens.

A substantial number were displaced within the state. Estimates of internally displaced Palestinians, referred to in Israeli law as “present absentees,” range from about 30,000 to over 45,000, and they include the inhabitants of villages such as Ikrit and Bir’im in northern Galilee, whose Christian residents were told to leave temporarily in November 1948 and promised that they could return; the promise was not kept, even after the High Court of Justice ruled in 1951 that they should be allowed to go back. The people of al-Majdal, whose remaining inhabitants were moved to Gaza in 1950, and of villages in the Triangle, were among those who experienced further displacement. The status of their land followed the absentee property law.

For Palestinians who remained inside Israel, the distinction between physical presence and effective control over land was often decisive. Some families lived in their own houses but could not reach fields on the other side of a newly established military zone; others were internally displaced within Israel and found themselves treated as absent from their original property for legal purposes. Military administration and restrictions on movement were established in the early years of the state, alongside formal citizenship arrangements. Experiences varied between Galilee communities that had surrendered, mixed towns whose Arab quarters had emptied, and Bedouin groups in the south who faced particular problems of mobility and land recognition. The existence of an Arab minority therefore complicates any description of 1948 as the complete removal of Palestinians from Israel, while the large-scale dispossession makes equally untenable any suggestion that those who remained simply resumed their former lives. Their histories are part of the Nakba and the institutional history of Israel alike.

Those who stayed lived under a military government that restricted movement and political activity, which was established during 1948–1949 and lasted until 1966, and they were a minority in a state defined by Jewish national institutions. The Citizenship Law of 1952 settled their formal status, a subject for later chapters. They too experienced the Nakba, in the loss of relatives, land, leadership, and a society that had been their larger context; their position as Palestinian Arabs and citizens of Israel is a theme in the history of both peoples that this chapter introduces and later chapters develop.

Section 61.37

Counting the Nakba: Demography, Evidence, Memory, and Historical Interpretation

Estimates of the number of refugees vary because of definitions and sources. The United Nations Economic Survey Mission for the Middle East reported 726,000 refugees in 1949, and the Conciliation Commission later revised the figure to 711,000; by 1950 UNRWA had registered more than 900,000 people, a number that included needy residents who were not refugees and children born after the exodus. Benny Morris, in The Birth of the Palestinian Refugee Problem (1987), estimated 600,000 to 760,000; Israeli government figures at the time were lower, and Palestinian estimates were higher, 800,000 or more. The differences arise from the absence of a census in 1948, the difficulty of counting people who moved more than once, and political motives, and no single figure can be treated as established beyond a range.

The sources are of several kinds. Israeli military and government archives, opened in part from the 1980s, record operations, intelligence assessments, and cabinet decisions; British records document the end of the mandate; United Nations records include the observers’ reports and the reports of the mediators and relief officials; Arab state archives are largely closed; the records of relief agencies describe the refugees’ condition; and newspapers, radio monitoring, and photographs supply contemporary detail. Oral history is essential for what official documents rarely record: how a village decided to stay or go, what happened on the road, what women and children experienced. Collections of testimony, from Nafez Nazzal’s early work on the Galilee to more recent archives, and memorial books produced by refugees from destroyed villages, preserve these accounts. Memory fades and shifts, and oral testimony must be tested against other evidence, but historians who have done so have found that it often corresponds closely with documents.

A useful demographic exercise should place at least three figures side by side rather than treating them as answers to one identical question. Contemporary UN relief agencies recorded people registered as needing assistance; the 1949 Economic Survey Mission estimated people displaced from the territory controlled by Israel, including those not drawing rations; and later historical reconstructions attempt to identify individuals from village populations, departures, internal displacement, and the size of the Arab minority that remained. Differences do not automatically reveal manipulation: a person could be a refugee without being registered for aid, and a family displaced within Israel could meet one historian’s definition but not another agency’s operational criterion. The Mission’s first interim report of November 1949 estimated about 726,000 people who had fled from Israel and were unable to return, plus approximately 25,000 special borderline cases. The fact that those numbers derive from dated estimates, rather than a door-to-door census, should be explicit whenever they are cited.

The Nakba became central to Palestinian identity. The word gained currency after 1948 through the Syrian historian Constantin Zurayk, whose pamphlet Ma’na al-Nakba (“The Meaning of the Disaster”), published in Beirut in 1948, argued that Arab societies had to transform themselves after defeat. Over time it came to designate the loss of homeland and the dispersal of the community, and the symbols associated with it, such as the keys of abandoned houses and the names of lost villages, became part of Palestinian culture and politics, with the claim to return as its central demand. In Israeli public memory, 1948 has been remembered as the War of Independence, and the history of the displacement of Palestinians occupied a marginal place for decades, a situation that changed as archival research and public debate developed. The divergence of memory is itself a historical fact that students must understand, while evaluating the evidence on which each account rests.

Division VII

International Diplomacy, Mediation, and the Refugee Question

Three tracks that must be kept apart: political mediation, legal claims, and humanitarian relief.

Section 61.38

The United Nations and the Failure to Implement Partition

Resolution 181 gave the United Nations Palestine Commission, composed of representatives of five states (Bolivia, Czechoslovakia, Denmark, Panama, and the Philippines), the task of supervising the transition. In practice the Commission could do almost nothing. Britain refused to allow it to take over the administration of any area before the end of the mandate, the Arab states and the Arab Higher Committee refused to cooperate, and the Commission lacked armed force. In February 1948 it reported to the Security Council that the plan could not be implemented peacefully unless armed forces were provided, and it asked the Council to supply them. The great powers, which had voted for partition, were not prepared to do so. The American government in particular, which had worried about the strain on its resources and its relations with Arab oil-producing states, concluded that partition could not be enforced without military commitments it was unwilling to make.

The American proposal of March 19 to place Palestine under a temporary trusteeship reflected this reversal. It was not adopted, but it temporarily undermined the Jewish Agency’s confidence and strengthened the sense among Arab leaders that partition might be abandoned. The General Assembly met in special session in April and May, and on May 14, the day the State of Israel was proclaimed, it voted to appoint a United Nations Mediator and discharge the Palestine Commission. The Security Council, in the meantime, set up a Truce Commission composed of the consuls in Jerusalem of the United States, France, and Belgium. In the Security Council, resolutions of April 17 and 23 and of May 22 called for truces and created the Truce Commission, and the Council’s tools were appeals rather than orders. These arrangements show the limits of United Nations authority at that time. The organization was only three years old, it depended on the great powers, and the Charter’s enforcement powers under Chapter VII were not used to impose partition. They were first invoked on July 15, 1948, in Resolution 54, and then only to order a cease-fire.

The proposed UN Palestine Commission and the later UN Mediator were different instruments formed for different circumstances. The first was intended to supervise the transition to two states under Resolution 181; because Britain would not cooperate in transferring effective authority and no member state supplied the necessary enforcement force, it never acquired control on the ground. The mediator was appointed after violence had already overwhelmed the transition arrangements and faced the narrower immediate problem of arranging ceasefires, ensuring access to relief, and proposing terms for a settlement. Even a successful truce did not revive the specific constitutional machinery of the partition plan. This institutional discontinuity matters because textbooks sometimes write as though 'the UN' maintained one coherent implementation program from November 1947 to July 1949. In reality, the General Assembly, Security Council, Palestine Commission, mediator, truce observers, and Conciliation Commission had different authorities and frequently worked under incompatible political constraints. Each institution's documents must be read in light of what it could legally recommend, observe, or enforce.

The failure to implement partition, then, was not primarily a failure of a UN body but a failure of the states that had voted for the plan to commit the means to carry it out, compounded by the refusal of the Arab leadership and states to accept it and by the military decisions of the two sides. The Assembly’s recommendation retained a legal and moral status that the parties continued to invoke, the Israelis citing it for the legitimacy of their state, and the Arabs citing it for the creation of an Arab state, but it did not itself determine the outcome.

Section 61.39

Count Folke Bernadotte and the Search for a Political Settlement

The man appointed as mediator, Count Folke Bernadotte, was a Swedish nobleman, president of the Swedish Red Cross, and a nephew of the Swedish king, who was known for his negotiations in 1945 to rescue prisoners from German concentration camps. He arrived in the region in late May 1948, helped to arrange the first truce, and then worked to produce a plan for a political settlement. His first proposal, delivered at the end of June, suggested a union of Palestine and Transjordan, under Abdullah’s crown, with the Jewish and Arab parts retaining autonomy; it proposed to assign the Negev to the Arabs and Galilee to the Jews, to place Jerusalem under Arab control with autonomy for the Jewish inhabitants, and to make Haifa a free port and Lydda a free airport. Both sides rejected it. The Israelis objected to the loss of the Negev and Jerusalem and the union with Transjordan, and the Arab governments objected to any recognition of a Jewish state.

His second set of recommendations, completed on September 16 as part of a progress report, revised the earlier ideas. It dropped the proposed union between the Jewish state and Transjordan, though it still recommended that the Arab part of Palestine be merged with Transjordan, proposed that Galilee be allocated to Israel and the Negev to the Arab side, and called for Jerusalem to be placed under United Nations control with municipal autonomy for Jewish and Arab communities, and for Haifa and the Lydda airport to be free zones. It proposed that the final boundaries be determined by the United Nations. Bernadotte also gave strong attention to the refugees. He argued that Palestinians who had fled from their homes should be given the right to return at the earliest possible date and to receive compensation for the property of those who chose not to return, and he wrote that it would be an offense against the principles of elemental justice to deny the right of return to these victims of the conflict while Jewish immigrants were flowing into Palestine. His observations, which became a basis for the General Assembly’s resolution three months later, are analyzed in the Primary Source Window.

Bernadotte’s mediation also shows how battlefield geography affected proposals that presented themselves as political compromises. Moving the Negev from one proposed state to another would change access to the Red Sea and alter the strategic relation between Egypt, Transjordan, and Israel. Jerusalem's disposition was more than symbolic because divided roads, hospitals, religious sites, and municipal services required practical arrangements. His humanitarian recommendations were shaped by direct evidence of refugee destitution, yet he could not compel Israel to readmit people or require Arab states to accept his territorial proposals. In July he suggested that limited repatriation might begin while wider settlement was negotiated; Israel refused substantial return during active hostilities. The documentary record thus separates a mediator’s view of what was urgent from the parties’ willingness to implement it. His September progress report is evidence of the negotiating options before his assassination, not evidence that any one of them had already been agreed.

Bernadotte’s approach has been judged in different ways. Some historians emphasize his moral seriousness and the realism of his proposals, in particular on the refugee issue. Others point out that his proposals ignored the facts on the ground: Israel had by then established its government and army and was unwilling to give up territory it controlled or to accept the loss of the Negev. His plans also were opposed by hard-line groups and, by some accounts, by Ben-Gurion and his colleagues. They illustrate the difficulty of any plan that required one of the parties to give up what it had won in battle.

Section 61.40

Bernadotte’s Assassination and the Continuation of UN Mediation

On September 17, 1948, the day after he completed his report, Bernadotte was shot dead in Jerusalem as his convoy was driving through the Katamon neighborhood in the Israeli-held part of the city. The French officer traveling with him, Colonel André Serot, was killed as well. The attack was carried out by a group of Lehi members who acted under the name “Front of the Homeland,” and its perpetrators believed that his plan, particularly on Jerusalem and the Negev, threatened Israel’s interests. Lehi’s leaders, among them Nathan Yellin-Mor and Yitzhak Shamir, were not tried for the killing during the war, and the assassins were not publicly identified until later.

The international reaction was one of shock and condemnation. The government of Israel, under Ben-Gurion, denounced the murder, declared Lehi illegal, and arrested hundreds of its members, but it did not prosecute the killers. The Lehi leaders who were convicted of other offences received brief sentences and were pardoned, and Yellin-Mor was elected to the first Knesset while in custody. The government’s determination to eliminate the dissident organizations was one result. Another was a lasting international suspicion of those who had planned the attack. For the United Nations, the episode underscored the dangers of the mediation effort, and the Security Council demanded an inquiry.

After the assassination, UN officials faced the practical problem of continuing field supervision while preserving the credibility of a mission whose senior representative had been murdered. The safety of observers, channels to Israeli and Arab military commands, and the timing of truce-violation investigations all mattered to the prospects for a negotiated settlement. Bunche worked with the existing UN field apparatus rather than constructing an entirely new one. His eventual success in bringing military delegations to agreements came partly from narrowing the immediate questions to troop withdrawals, ceasefire supervision, and demarcation lines. That method created a durable cessation of major interstate operations but also deferred questions about refugee return, civilian property, recognition, and sovereignty. Understanding the outcome requires distinguishing the mediator’s personal skill from the structural limits of the agreement he could secure. The armistices were not a failed version of peace treaties; they were deliberately interim instruments negotiated in circumstances where a final political settlement remained beyond reach.

Bernadotte’s deputy, the American diplomat Ralph Bunche, took over as acting mediator and continued the work. Bunche, a professor of political science and an experienced official, proved to be skillful and persistent, and in 1949 he mediated the armistice negotiations on Rhodes, for which he received the Nobel Peace Prize in 1950. The shift in mediation, from a plan for a comprehensive political settlement to a step-by-step effort to end the fighting through military armistices, reflected the changing balance of forces. It succeeded in ending the war but not in solving the issues Bernadotte had raised.

Section 61.41

United Nations General Assembly Resolution 194 and the Refugee Question

On December 11, 1948, meeting in Paris, the General Assembly adopted Resolution 194 (III), which dealt with a range of questions. It established a Conciliation Commission for Palestine, with representatives of the United States, France, and Turkey, to assist the parties in reaching a final settlement, and it resolved that Jerusalem should be placed under effective United Nations control and that the holy places should be protected, with access for all. It also contained, in paragraph 11, the passage on the refugees that has since been a central text. It resolved that refugees wishing to return to their homes and live at peace with their neighbors should be permitted to do so at the earliest practicable date, and that compensation should be paid for the property of those choosing not to return and for loss or damage to property under principles of international law or equity. It instructed the Conciliation Commission to facilitate repatriation, resettlement, economic and social rehabilitation, and the payment of compensation.

The resolution was adopted by a large majority, with the Arab states voting against it, in part because it implied negotiation with Israel. Its terms have been interpreted in contrasting ways. Palestinians and many other observers have read paragraph 11 as recognition of a right of return that Israel has been obliged to honor. Israel, and some legal commentators, have noted that the General Assembly’s resolutions are recommendations, that the wording says that refugees “wishing to return” should be permitted to do so, but only if they are willing to live at peace with their neighbors, and that it also provides for compensation and resettlement; Israeli officials argued from the beginning that return should be considered only as part of a general peace. Both readings use the same text, which shows how the question became a political and legal dispute from its first articulation.

Resolution 194 should be read in its institutional and conditional wording rather than reduced to a slogan. Its paragraph 11 addressed refugees who wished to return to their homes and live at peace with their neighbors and contemplated compensation for those choosing not to return, as well as loss or damage to property. Other paragraphs dealt with Jerusalem, holy places, and the mandate of the Conciliation Commission. The resolution expressed the General Assembly's position, but the Assembly had not created a mechanism able to compel military authorities to open roads or restore possession of individual houses. The Israeli and Arab governments subsequently disagreed about security conditions, return, compensation, territorial bargaining, and whether a settlement could be made without mutual recognition. Those differences did not erase the existence of the resolution or the claims that Palestinians continued to assert; they help explain why a widely cited document remained largely unimplemented. Students must distinguish the resolution's text, its legal and political interpretations, and later policies pursued in its name.

In practical terms, the resolution established three things: a principle that the refugees’ claims should be addressed, a body charged with addressing them, and a link between the refugee question and a general settlement. It did not specify how many refugees should return or in what time, and it contained no mechanism of enforcement. Subsequent General Assemblies reaffirmed it repeatedly. It is a primary source for students of this chapter, and the Primary Source Window gives it fuller treatment.

Section 61.42

Refugee Relief, Lausanne, and the Failure of Comprehensive Peace Negotiations

The relief of refugees and the search for peace proceeded on different tracks, which the United Nations tried to keep separate and which kept colliding. In November 1948 the General Assembly established United Nations Relief for Palestine Refugees, a temporary body that coordinated food, shelter, and medical aid delivered largely through voluntary agencies. The political task belonged to the Conciliation Commission for Palestine, composed of France, Turkey, and the United States, whose members opened a conference at Lausanne on April 27, 1949, meeting the Israeli and Arab delegations separately and, in the early weeks, representatives of the refugees as well.

The sequence of events in May is easily misread and must be stated precisely. On May 11, 1949, the General Assembly voted to admit Israel to the United Nations, in Resolution 273 (III), after hearing Israeli statements about the state’s intention to honor Resolutions 181 and 194. The next day, May 12, the Commission asked each side to sign a protocol, and the Israeli delegation, headed by Walter Eytan, signed it in the morning, with the Arab delegations of Egypt, Jordan, Lebanon, and Syria signing an identical text later the same day; the records of the two meetings are documents A/AC.25/SR/LM/8 and SR/LM/9. The protocol stated that the Commission was anxious to achieve the objectives of Resolution 194 and proposed that the working document attached, a map of the boundaries in Resolution 181, be taken as a basis for discussion with the Commission. Israel’s admission thus preceded its signature of the protocol; the admission did not depend on the signature. The Commission and its American member nonetheless pressed Israel to sign, and the United States warned the Israeli government in late May that its attitude toward the refugees and the territorial question was inconsistent with its undertakings.

The parties interpreted the protocol differently from the outset. Arab delegations treated it as a recognition that the partition map was the starting point for territory and that return of refugees was the first question. The Israeli delegation had made clear before signing that its position was reserved, and on June 11 Eytan wrote to the Commission that the protocol was a basis for discussion, not the only one, and could not displace the provisions of the resolution under which the Commission acted. Israel argued that the lines of the armistice should be the basis for territory, with some adjustments, and that the refugee question could only be addressed in the context of a general peace; in the summer, it proposed to accept up to 100,000 refugees, a number that included those who had already returned or been reunited with their families, as part of a comprehensive settlement. The Arab states demanded that Israel accept the principle of return first. The Commission left Lausanne in September having produced no comprehensive result.

The chronology of the Economic Survey Mission is especially important. The Mission's first interim report was communicated to the General Assembly in November 1949, when refugee relief was already an urgent issue; that report informed the Assembly's December 8 decision to establish UNRWA under Resolution 302. The separately published final report, dated December 28, cannot have been the initiating document for a decision taken twenty days earlier, even though it included analysis consistent with the relief-and-works approach. The Mission's estimate of approximately 726,000 displaced people unable to return to Israel should be separated from the roughly 25,000 borderline cases it identified. Its recommendations for public works and economic stabilization were not a political settlement, and the Mission itself recognized that repatriation required decisions beyond an economic survey's competence. Placing the correct document dates alongside each other reveals both the urgency of the humanitarian response and the political deadlock that made an emergency institution necessary.

The failure of Lausanne turned attention to relief and economic measures. The Economic Survey Mission, led by the American Gordon Clapp, which was established by the Commission and reported on December 28, 1949, estimated the number of refugees at 726,000 and recommended a program of relief and public works to employ them. On December 8, 1949, the General Assembly adopted Resolution 302 (IV), creating UNRWA, which began operations on May 1, 1950, with a mandate that combined direct relief and works programs and was initially expected to last a short time. The Arab states and the refugees feared that an emphasis on resettlement and work was meant to replace the political claim with an economic solution, while Western governments considered it the best available means of preventing a humanitarian disaster.

This division shows why the three tracks should be distinguished. Humanitarian relief, carried out by UNRWA and other agencies, addressed immediate needs but did not resolve legal status. Political mediation, carried out by Bernadotte, Bunche, and the Conciliation Commission, sought a territorial and political settlement but could not bridge the parties’ differences. The legal claims, based on Resolution 194, were widely acknowledged but never implemented. The persistence of the refugee question, and of the agency created to help the refugees, is one of the most important long-term consequences of 1948.

Division VIII

The Armistice Agreements and the Regional Settlement

Four bilateral military agreements that ended the principal fighting and fixed lines that were not peace borders.

Section 61.43

The Egyptian–Israeli Armistice and the Formation of the Gaza Strip

The armistice negotiations began on the island of Rhodes on January 13, 1949, under the chairmanship of Ralph Bunche, the acting mediator, who set the delegations up in a hotel and separated them for most of the process, meeting each in turn. Security Council Resolution 62 of November 16, 1948, had called for armistices, and Egypt’s acceptance of a ceasefire after Operation Horev made it the first Arab state to negotiate. The Egyptian delegation consisted of army officers and diplomats, and the Israeli delegation included Walter Eytan of the Foreign Ministry, Reuven Shiloah, and senior army officers such as Yigael Yadin and Yitzhak Rabin. The talks were difficult, particularly over the Egyptian troops besieged at Faluja, the status of the Negev, and the demilitarization of the border area at al-Auja (Nitzana).

The agreement, signed on February 24, 1949, drew an armistice demarcation line that followed the former international boundary between Egypt and Palestine in the south and then ran along the line of the Egyptian positions north of Rafah. Its terms provided for the limitation of forces near the line, for a demilitarized zone around al-Auja, for the evacuation of the Egyptian troops from Faluja with their arms, and for a Mixed Armistice Commission with a United Nations chairman. Egypt’s negotiators insisted, as did the other Arab delegations, that the agreement was military in character and did not imply recognition of Israel or of any border, and the text reflects this: it states that its provisions are dictated by military considerations and that it does not prejudice the rights, claims, and positions of either party in the final settlement of the Palestine question.

Gaza's position illustrates how an armistice could produce an administrative unit without creating a sovereign state. Egyptian troops and authorities controlled the remaining coastal strip, while Palestinians displaced from elsewhere in southern Palestine crowded into places whose prewar infrastructure had supported far fewer inhabitants. The demarcation was negotiated primarily as a military line: it did not define an independent Palestinian citizenship, create a final border, or settle the right of displaced landowners to farms outside the strip. Palestinian laborers and traders who had previously moved between Gaza, the southern villages, and the larger coastal markets found those routes interrupted. On maps, the small size of the strip can conceal the scale of the population change. A geographical exercise should therefore pair territorial area with changes in the number of people needing housing, water, sanitation, and work, while labeling the population estimates by source and date. Egypt's decision to administer rather than annex Gaza also differed fundamentally from Jordan's policy in the West Bank.

The agreement left Egypt in control of a coastal territory about 40 kilometers long and some 6 to 12 kilometers wide, the Gaza Strip, of about 360 square kilometers. Before the war it held an Arab population of perhaps 60,000 to 80,000; the arrival of refugees from the southern coastal plain, the Negev, and the Hebron foothills raised the number to some 250,000 to 280,000 within months. Egypt did not annex the Strip. It administered it through a military governor and treated it as Palestinian territory held in trust, while maintaining controls on movement that kept the refugees in place. The Strip became a place of dense, poor camps with limited agricultural land and few opportunities for work, dependent on relief, first from the American Friends Service Committee and then from UNRWA. Its demographic and political character, as one of the most concentrated refugee populations in the world, was set in these months.

Section 61.44

The Lebanese–Israeli Armistice, March 1949

Lebanon’s armistice talks began on March 1 at Ras al-Naqoura, on the coast at the frontier, and led to an agreement signed on March 23, 1949. Lebanon was the weakest of the Arab combatants, had played the smallest part in the fighting, and was anxious to leave the war as soon as it could. The government, in which the president was a Maronite and the prime minister a Sunni, feared that prolonged involvement would aggravate internal divisions, and it had borne the arrival of about 100,000 Palestinian refugees, most of them from Galilee.

The agreement was among the simplest. The armistice line followed the international frontier that France and Britain had drawn between Lebanon and Palestine in 1923, so that no territorial adjustment was required, and Israel agreed to withdraw from the Lebanese villages it had occupied during Operation Hiram, which it did in the following weeks. The two parties undertook not to engage in military action, set up a Mixed Armistice Commission, and agreed on limits to forces near the line. Lebanon’s northern frontier with Israel became the most tranquil of Israel’s borders for some years.

The apparent simplicity of the Lebanese agreement rested on an important circumstance: the parties could largely use the preexisting international boundary as a reference, unlike the more complicated lines in the Jerusalem corridor or the Syrian sector. Withdrawal from Lebanese villages held by Israeli forces after Operation Hiram was therefore a practical matter of implementing a defined frontier, although local security arrangements still required supervision. The document created procedures for a Mixed Armistice Commission, but these could investigate incidents and manage compliance, not determine the political fate of Palestinians who had fled from Galilee into Lebanon. The distinction is particularly clear for families who could see their former villages across the boundary but could not cross it. A political history of the agreement needs both the text negotiated by the states and the civilian experience that the text left outside its terms. A relatively quiet frontier in the immediate postwar years did not amount to a comprehensive resolution of territorial, refugee, or recognition disputes.

The circumstances were nonetheless consequential. The agreement ended the fighting but did not address the refugees, who remained in Lebanon without citizenship rights and in camps that grew and settled over time, and the status of these communities became one of the most important elements of Lebanese politics, as Chapter 68 shows. The Israeli government regarded the agreement as a model for a possible peace, and Lebanon’s officials treated it as a purely military arrangement; the difference illustrates the gap between the parties’ understandings of what an armistice meant.

Section 61.45

The Jordanian–Israeli Armistice and the Division of Jerusalem

The Jordanian agreement, signed on April 3, 1949, had the greatest territorial consequences. It followed a series of direct and secret contacts between Israeli officials and King Abdullah’s representatives, among them a local ceasefire in Jerusalem negotiated in the autumn of 1948 by the military commanders Moshe Dayan and Abdullah al-Tall, and formal negotiations at Rhodes that started on March 1 under Bunche. The delegations bargained over the Little Triangle, the western edge of the West Bank, which contained some twenty Arab villages and lay within a few kilometers of the Mediterranean, and Israel, whose forces had just reached the Gulf of Aqaba in Operation Uvda, made clear that it might take the area by force if it were not conceded. Jordan agreed to adjust the line, with the Iraqi withdrawal and the transfer of the Iraqi sector to the Legion forming part of the arrangement, and the agreement provided for the protection of the inhabitants of the villages transferred to Israel. About 30,000 to 40,000 Palestinians in the Triangle thus came under Israeli administration.

In Jerusalem, the agreement confirmed a division that already existed. Israel held the new city and the western and southern suburbs, and Jordan the Old City and the northern and eastern districts, with a no-man’s-land between them. Mount Scopus, with the Hebrew University and Hadassah Hospital, remained an Israeli enclave surrounded by Jordanian positions, to be supplied under United Nations supervision. The line was marked by walls and barbed wire, and could be crossed by a few diplomats and officials at the Mandelbaum Gate. Jews were barred from the Old City, including the Western Wall, in spite of a provision on access to the holy places, and Muslims and Christians who lived in West Jerusalem, tens of thousands of whom had left or been expelled, were not allowed to return to their homes. The agreement also provided for a special committee to arrange access to the holy places and the use of the Mount Scopus road, which was not carried out in practice.

The territorial exchanges associated with the so-called Triangle reveal the limits of treating armistice lines as simply the locations where two armies stopped shooting. Negotiators altered positions to secure communications and reduce immediate military vulnerability, but those alterations placed Palestinian villages under new jurisdiction and raised difficult questions about residence and property. Administrative authority, military responsibility, and political allegiance were not identical. In Jerusalem, similar distinctions arose around Mount Scopus, the demilitarized areas, and roads whose practical use depended on agreement even when the general line was drawn. Jewish residents' exclusion from the Old City and the inability of former Arab residents of West Jerusalem to return were different human consequences of one divided urban space. The agreement's special arrangements for access to sites and institutions were only partially realized. Mapping these exceptions alongside the main demarcation makes clear why the 1949 line was an unstable political compromise despite becoming an enduring geographical reference.

Neither party regarded the arrangement as final. The General Assembly in Resolution 303 of December 9, 1949, reaffirmed its support for an international regime for Jerusalem; Israel replied by declaring Jerusalem its capital and moving government offices there later that month, and Jordan, in April 1950, annexed the West Bank and East Jerusalem, an act recognized by very few states. Three positions thus coexisted: the international status envisaged by the United Nations, Israel’s claim to the whole city, and Jordan’s and the Palestinians’ claims to its eastern part and holy places. Jerusalem was the city in which all the contested questions of 1948 converged.

Section 61.46

The Syrian–Israeli Armistice and the Limits of the 1949 Agreements

Syria was the last to sign. Talks began on April 5, 1949, near the Gesher Benot Ya’akov bridge on the Jordan, after the other agreements were done, and continued at Mahanayim until the agreement was signed on July 20. In the interval, the Syrian government had changed: Colonel Husni al-Za’im, the army’s chief of staff, seized power in March, and the new regime was more willing to negotiate. Za’im is reported to have proposed a broader settlement involving the resettlement of a large number of refugees in Syria in return for territorial adjustments, but Israel rejected it, and the proposals, which are known mainly through Israeli and American accounts, remain a matter of debate.

The agreement set the armistice line on the international boundary between Syria and Palestine as drawn in 1923, required Syria to withdraw from Mishmar HaYarden, and established demilitarized zones in the areas that Syria evacuated and some adjacent land, whose sovereignty was deliberately left unsettled. The ambiguity was the source of later trouble. The parties disagreed about the status of the zones, the rights of the Arab farmers who lived in them, and the use of land and water, particularly the draining of the Huleh marshes and the diversion of Jordan waters. These disputes provoked frequent incidents in the 1950s, which later chapters trace to their consequences.

The demilitarized zones in the Syrian agreement created a particularly revealing ambiguity. Syria's withdrawal from positions west of the former international frontier did not by itself determine which civilian authority could tax, cultivate, or police every tract inside the newly demilitarized areas. The armistice text gave practical rules for the absence of military forces, but sovereignty remained a contested question. Farmers whose land lay within a zone confronted restrictions that could not be settled by examining a battlefield map alone; water use and cultivation became recurring sources of friction. Israel and Syria interpreted subsequent administrative steps differently, and UN observers were frequently asked to assess incidents that had political consequences beyond their mandate to supervise military compliance. A clear textbook map should show the former international frontier, the actual armistice lines, and demilitarized areas as separate elements. Otherwise students may mistake a military buffer arrangement for a treaty transfer of sovereignty.

Iraq did not sign an armistice. It had withdrawn its troops and handed their sector to the Legion, and it declined to negotiate with Israel, so that it technically remained in a state of war. Saudi Arabia and Yemen, which had sent token forces, also did not sign. The four agreements ended the main fighting, but they were not treaties of peace. Their texts describe them as military arrangements intended to facilitate the transition to permanent peace, and they state that the armistice lines are without prejudice to the rights, claims, and positions of the parties in a final settlement. The agreements did not recognize Israel, did not address the refugees, and left Jerusalem and the final borders open. The distinction between an armistice and a peace is one that students must keep in view whenever the 1949 lines are mentioned.

Section 61.47

The Green Line, Territorial Control, and the Unestablished Palestinian State

The territorial outcome differed from the partition recommendation in several ways. Resolution 181 had assigned about 56 percent of Mandate Palestine to the Jewish state, about 43 percent to the Arab state, and about 1 percent to an international enclave around Jerusalem. At the end of the war Israel controlled about 78 percent of the territory. It held western Galilee, much of which the partition plan had assigned to the proposed Arab state, as well as eastern Galilee, which the plan had allocated to the proposed Jewish state; it also held the Jaffa area, Lydda, Ramle, Beersheba, and western Jerusalem. The remaining 22 percent was divided between Jordan, which held the West Bank, about 5,800 square kilometers including East Jerusalem and the Old City, and Egypt, which administered the Gaza Strip, about 1 percent of the whole. The armistice demarcation line between Israel and these areas is called the Green Line, from the color used to draw it on the maps at Rhodes.

It is important to describe the Green Line accurately. It was an armistice demarcation line, a provisional military line, and not a mutually agreed permanent boundary. The agreements with Egypt and Jordan state that the line is not a political or territorial boundary and is drawn without prejudice to future territorial settlements or claims, and the Arab delegations insisted on this wording. For nearly two decades the line functioned as a de facto frontier, and Israel, Jordan, and the international community usually treated it as such, but its legal status was that of a provisional arrangement pending a final settlement, which never came. Later chapters will show how this ambiguity affected subsequent wars and negotiations.

Comparison of the partition plan and the armistice outcome requires three map layers. The first is the 1947 proposed division, including a Jewish state with eastern Galilee, an Arab state with much of western Galilee, and a separately administered Jerusalem area. The second is the military control achieved during 1948, when front lines changed repeatedly and enclaves could be surrounded without being captured. The third is the 1949 armistice demarcation, which left Israel in control of far more territory than the General Assembly had proposed and left the remaining Palestinian territories under Jordanian and Egyptian administration. Collapsing these layers makes an elementary error appear plausible: Galilee was never assigned entirely to the proposed Arab state. More importantly, the three-layer method prevents a reader from treating a proposal, a battlefield situation, and a military demarcation as equivalent legal borders. It also reveals how the geographical separation of Gaza and the West Bank constrained later efforts to establish a single Palestinian political administration.

The Palestinian Arab state recommended by the General Assembly was not established. Several causes, which students should consider together, explain why. The Palestinian leadership and the Arab states rejected partition and did not prepare for a state on its terms. Palestinian society, weakened by the revolt of 1936–1939 and the departures of early 1948, lacked the military and administrative strength to defend itself, and its institutions collapsed in the war. The Arab governments that intervened had their own aims: Transjordan sought to annex the territory it held, Egypt sought to control Gaza but not to incorporate it, and none supported independent Palestinian rule, which would have strengthened the Mufti. Israel did not wish to see a Palestinian state on the territory it did not control and preferred an understanding with Abdullah, and the great powers did not impose the plan.

The Palestinian efforts to create institutions were brief. In September 1948, the Arab Higher Committee proclaimed an All-Palestine Government in Gaza, under Egyptian protection, headed by Ahmad Hilmi Abd al-Baqi, with Hajj Amin al-Husayni presiding over a Palestine National Council; it was recognized by most Arab League members, but not by Transjordan, and it had no army, revenue, or territory of its own, and it faded in the following years. In December 1948, a congress of Palestinian notables at Jericho, convened under Abdullah’s patronage, called for the union of Arab Palestine with Transjordan and acclaimed him as king, and in April 1950 the West Bank was formally annexed. The result was that, by 1950, the Green Line divided the Israeli state from two Arab administrations, Jordanian and Egyptian, that had absorbed what remained of the territory that the United Nations had intended for an Arab state.

Division IX

State Formation, Historical Memory, and the Unresolved Conflict

The institutional, political, and interpretive consequences of 1948–1949.

Section 61.48

Israel, Palestinian Political Dispossession, and the Transformation of the Arab States

In the year after the armistices, Israel consolidated its institutions. It held elections for a Constituent Assembly on January 25, 1949, which became the first Knesset; Chaim Weizmann was elected president and Ben-Gurion’s party, Mapai, formed the government. On May 11, 1949, the General Assembly admitted Israel as a member of the United Nations by Resolution 273, with Israel declaring its acceptance of the obligations of the Charter. The new state absorbed hundreds of thousands of Jewish immigrants, from the displaced persons camps of Europe and, increasingly, from Arab countries, so that the Jewish population roughly doubled within three years; many were housed in properties abandoned by Palestinians. The state had a unified army, a functioning civil administration, and a growing economy, though it faced severe shortages and a heavy defense burden. It also had a substantial Arab minority governed by military administration.

The Palestinians were politically dispossessed. They had no state, their leadership was divided and discredited, their population was dispersed in the West Bank, Gaza, and neighboring countries, and the territory under their control had been absorbed by Jordan and Egypt. The Mufti’s authority declined; the All-Palestine Government had little power. For the next decade Palestinian political activity occurred mainly through broader Arab movements, such as Arab nationalism and communism, until the emergence of independent organizations in the 1950s and 1960s, the subject of later chapters.

For the Arab states, the defeat was an enduring shock. In Egypt, the prime minister Nuqrashi was assassinated in December 1948 and the Muslim Brotherhood’s founder Hasan al-Banna was killed in February 1949, in a sequence of violence that was partly related to the war; officers who fought in Palestine, with the experience of Faluja, began forming the group that later carried out the 1952 revolution, a subject of Chapter 62. In Syria, the coup of March 1949 opened a period of military intervention. In Jordan, Abdullah’s absorption of the West Bank gave the state a large Palestinian population, and he was assassinated in Jerusalem in 1951 by a Palestinian. In Iraq and Lebanon, too, the war and the refugees affected politics. The conventional view that defeat in Palestine discredited the old regimes and prepared the way for the coups and revolutions of the 1950s is broadly accepted, though historians emphasize that other pressures, including social inequality, the British presence, and generational change, were also at work.

Section 61.49

Independence, Nakba, and the Historiography of 1948

The historiography of 1948 is a field in which the interpretation of events has been inseparable from national identity. For decades, the standard Israeli account portrayed the war as a defensive struggle by a small and vulnerable community against overwhelming Arab forces, from which the new state emerged by a combination of heroism and necessity, and it explained the Palestinian exodus as the result of Arab leaders’ orders and fear of the war. The standard Palestinian and Arab account portrayed 1948 as a catastrophe imposed on the Palestinians by Zionism and imperialism, involving a planned expulsion and the theft of their country. Each version contained documented elements, and each omitted or minimized evidence that did not fit.

The opening of archives in the 1980s changed the debate. A group of Israeli scholars, often called the “new historians,” among them Benny Morris, Avi Shlaim, Simha Flapan, and Ilan Pappé, used Israeli documents to question parts of the traditional narrative. They argued that the Palestinian exodus was not mainly the result of Arab orders, that expulsions had occurred, that Israeli policy had prevented the refugees’ return, and that the balance of forces and Arab aims were different from the traditional picture; Shlaim, in particular, argued that Abdullah and the Zionist leadership had reached a tacit understanding. These conclusions have been disputed, and they have been developed in different directions. Morris, who emphasized the war’s contingencies and located the origins of the refugee problem in the war and in the dynamics of fear, later argued that a policy of transfer was widely accepted among Zionist leaders; Pappé characterized the events as ethnic cleansing carried out according to a plan; critics, such as Efraim Karsh, argued that the new historians overstated Israel’s responsibility. Palestinian historians, including Walid Khalidi, Nur Masalha, and Salman Abu Sitta, have emphasized the evidence of planning and the reconstruction of destroyed villages through maps, memorial books, and oral histories. Arab and other scholars have also examined Arab states’ politics, with limited access to their archives.

A responsible reading distinguishes the areas of substantial agreement from those of continuing dispute. There is broad agreement on the main sequence of events; on the scale of Palestinian displacement, of the order of 700,000 people; on the fact that expulsions occurred in a number of places and that other people fled from fighting and fear; on the Israeli decision not to allow return and on the destruction of many villages; on the occurrence of massacres of civilians in several places, and of Jewish civilians and prisoners in others, such as at Kfar Etzion and the Hadassah convoy; on the lack of a unified Arab command; and on the superior organization of Israeli forces after the first weeks. There is continuing disagreement about whether Plan Dalet was a plan of expulsion, about the intentions of Ben-Gurion and other leaders, about the weight of different causes in different places, about the nature of Abdullah’s contacts with the Zionists, and about the number of victims in particular incidents, such as the killings at Tantura on May 22–23, 1948, whose scale and character remain contested in research and in public debate. These disagreements should not be treated as symmetrical in every case, since the evidence for some claims is far stronger than for others. The Israeli state archives, moreover, have not been uniformly open, and some documents opened earlier have been reclassified, while Arab archives are mostly closed, so that the evidence from each side remains incomplete.

Section 61.50

The Legacy of 1948–1949 and the Unresolved Problems of Peace

The events of 1948 and 1949 produced a new political order in Palestine and in the Arab world. A Jewish state, Israel, was established and survived the war; its society was transformed by war, mass immigration, and the absence of most of the Arab population that had lived in the territory. Palestinian Arab society was shattered. Most of its urban and much of its rural population had been displaced, hundreds of communities had been destroyed or emptied, and a population of refugees and a minority of Palestinians within Israel had come into being. The territory that the United Nations had proposed for an Arab state was divided between Jordan and Egypt, and the Arab state itself was never established. The new boundaries, the Green Line, were military armistice lines, not agreed political borders.

The reasons for the outcome in 1949, an armistice but not a peace, can now be summarized. Israel, having achieved victory and territory, saw no reason to make concessions on borders or refugees without peace and recognition. The Arab states, having lost the war, were unwilling to recognize Israel or to concede what they saw as the Palestinians’ rights, and their governments feared domestic reactions; they also did not agree among themselves. The Palestinians lacked a state or a unified leadership able to negotiate. The United Nations had recommended partition but could not enforce it, and its mediators could do no more than end the fighting. The great powers had interests in the region but did not impose a settlement. The armistice agreements, by design, left open the questions of borders, refugees, and Jerusalem, and those questions remained the substance of the conflict.

The consequences extended beyond 1949. The refugee population and its institutions, such as UNRWA, became a lasting feature of the region. The question of the return, the status of Jerusalem, the borders, and the recognition of Israel and Palestinian rights remained subjects of diplomacy and war in the decades that followed, as Chapters 62 through 72 show. In the Arab world, the defeat became a reference point for debates about the failure of the old order, which shaped political movements across the region. In Israel and in Palestinian communities, 1948 became a foundational event with different meanings. That these meanings coexist, and that the historical evidence supports a more complex account than either alone, is one of the central lessons of the chapter.

Historical Vocabulary
Nakba (النكبة)

The Arabic word nakba means “catastrophe” or “disaster.” In 1948 the Syrian historian Constantin Zurayk used it in a pamphlet that sought to explain the Arab defeat and to call for self-examination in Arab societies. Over the following decades the term came to designate the specifically Palestinian experience of 1948: the displacement and dispossession of most of the Arab population of the territory that became Israel, the destruction of communities, and the loss of a political future in the country.

The word matters because it names a historical process, not only a military outcome. Its use requires care. In Palestinian and much international usage it refers to events of 1947–1949 and, in some uses, to a continuing condition; in Israeli public discourse it has been contested, and many Israelis prefer “War of Independence.” This chapter uses Nakba as a historical term for the Palestinian experience and “War of Independence” and “1948 war” as terms for the broader conflict, while distinguishing documented events from interpretations about causes and intentions.

Historical Vocabulary
Yishuv (היישוב)

The Hebrew word yishuv means “settlement” or “community.” In Zionist and historical usage, “the Yishuv” denotes the organized Jewish community of Palestine before the creation of Israel in 1948, and it is sometimes qualified as the “new Yishuv” to distinguish it from older Jewish communities that existed before modern Zionist immigration. By 1947 it numbered about 630,000 people, with its own political bodies, schools, labor federation, economy, and military organization.

The term matters for this chapter because many of the institutions that became the State of Israel, including the provisional government, the civil service, and the core of the army, were already in existence as institutions of the Yishuv. Students should distinguish the Yishuv before May 14, 1948, from the Israeli state and its citizens afterward, a body that included, from that date, a substantial Arab minority.

Historical Vocabulary
Armistice and Green Line

An armistice is an agreement to cease hostilities, usually made by military commanders or on their behalf, that does not by itself end a state of war or settle the political questions at issue. The four General Armistice Agreements of 1949 each established an armistice demarcation line, which is commonly called the Green Line after the color used to draw it on the maps at Rhodes.

The agreements stated that the lines were dictated by military considerations and that they did not prejudice the rights, claims, or positions of the parties in a later political settlement. They were therefore not recognized international borders in the full sense. The distinction matters because later political discussion often treats the 1949 lines as if they were agreed borders, while in legal and historical terms they were provisional military lines that became, in practice, the de facto boundaries of Israel, Jordan, and Egypt’s administration of Gaza for nearly two decades.

General Assembly Resolution 181 (II), November 29, 1947

Source type: Resolution of an international organization, with annexed boundary description and map.

Context: Adopted at Flushing Meadow, New York, by 33 votes to 13 with 10 abstentions, on the recommendation of the majority of UNSCOP. Every Arab and several Muslim-majority member states voted against; the United States and the Soviet Union both voted in favor, and Britain abstained.

Paraphrase of content: The resolution recommends termination of the mandate and the partition of Palestine into an independent Arab state and an independent Jewish state linked by an economic union, with Jerusalem under a special international regime administered by the United Nations. It sets out boundaries in technical detail, assigns a commission to supervise the transition, calls for British withdrawal by August 1, 1948, and includes provisions on minority and religious rights.

Analysis: The text shows what was recommended and what was not provided: any means of enforcement. The boundary descriptions also reveal the demographic problem on which the whole plan rested: the proposed Jewish state would have had a very large Arab minority, roughly 40 percent of its population, while the Arab state would have contained a small Jewish minority. As a recommendation it was not legally binding. The document says nothing about how the parties would respond, and the map annexed to it is the document the Lausanne Protocol of 1949 later took as a working basis.

Plan Dalet (Plan D), Haganah General Staff, March 1948

Source type: Military planning document; classified at the time.

Context: A general operational plan completed in early March 1948 under Yigael Yadin of the Haganah’s operations staff, with orders to brigades issued over the following weeks. It built on earlier plans and was designed for the end of the mandate and the expected Arab invasion. Translations have been published by Walid Khalidi (Journal of Palestine Studies, 1988) and are discussed by Benny Morris.

Paraphrase of content: The plan states its aim as securing the territory of the Jewish state and Jewish settlements outside it, defeating the expected invasion, and keeping control of roads and communications. It directs the occupation of installations vacated by the British. Among its provisions are operations against enemy population centers located within or near the defensive system, which may include destroying villages, and, in the event of resistance, expelling the population beyond the borders of the state, while allowing villages that do not resist to be occupied and left in place under military control.

Analysis: The plan is central evidence about the relation of military objectives and treatment of civilians. Its wording is general and leaves to local commanders the decision about which villages resist, which is why historians read it differently. It does not itself prove what orders were given in a particular operation, and it must be read with brigade orders, operational reports, and records of events at specific places. Source type: Diplomatic communication and legal argument by governments.

General Assembly Resolution 194 (III), December 11, 1948

Source type: Resolution of the General Assembly.

Context: Adopted in Paris at the third session on a proposal that built on Bernadotte’s report. It created the Conciliation Commission and stated principles for a final settlement. The Arab states voted against it, and Israel, not yet a member, objected to parts of it.

Paraphrase of content: Paragraph 11 resolves that refugees wishing to return to their homes and to live at peace with their neighbors should be permitted to do so at the earliest practicable date, that compensation should be paid for the property of those choosing not to return and for loss or damage under principles of international law or equity, and that the Conciliation Commission should facilitate repatriation, resettlement, and rehabilitation. It shows what the international community recommended, not what the parties did.

Historical Debate

Why Did the Palestinians Leave?

The question: What caused the departure of some 700,000 Palestinians from the territory that became Israel, and who bore responsibility for it? The question is both historical and politically charged, since it bears on the claims of both sides.

Interpretation A — Flight mainly caused by war, with Arab leaders’ role emphasized: The older Israeli account held that the Palestinians left mainly at the urging of their own leaders and of Arab radio broadcasts, or because of the general conditions of war, and that Israel was not responsible for creating the refugee problem. In a later and more qualified form, some historians continue to emphasize the role of the war, of fear, and of the weakness of Palestinian leadership, and that the Arab rejection of partition and invasion started the war.
Interpretation B — Displacement caused substantially by Jewish military operations, expulsion, and policy: Palestinian historians, and since the 1980s many Israeli and other historians, argue that a large part of the departure resulted from military operations, expulsions, fear following massacres, psychological warfare, and the destruction of villages, and that the policy of preventing return made it permanent. Some within this view emphasize a plan or an intention to remove the Arab population; others emphasize the effect of military operations conducted with the expectation that the population would leave.

Evidence: The debate depends on Israeli military and government records, including the June 1948 intelligence assessment; British and UN reports; monitored Arab broadcasts, which historians have examined and which do not show a general call to leave; Arab and Palestinian accounts and oral histories; and records of particular operations. The documents support different explanations in different places.

Why it matters: Under the weight of the evidence, no single cause explains all the departures, and the claim that Palestinians left chiefly because Arab leaders told them to is not supported by the documents. Disagreement continues over how much weight to give to intention and policy as against the dynamics of war, and over how to describe the events. How the question is answered affects the interpretation of Israeli responsibility, Palestinian claims, and the refugee question.

Historical Debate

Plan Dalet: Defensive Military Plan or Blueprint for Expulsion?

The question: Was Plan Dalet an operational plan to secure the Jewish state against an expected invasion, or a plan to remove the Arab population?

Interpretation A — A military plan, not a master plan for expulsion: Historians such as Benny Morris and Israeli military historians argue that the plan was mainly designed for defense against the Arab armies, that its text does not call for a general expulsion, and that the expulsions resulted from the way commanders applied its provisions in the course of fighting, though it contained provisions for destroying villages and expelling inhabitants that did allow this.
Interpretation B — A plan with expulsion as an aim or foreseeable method: Walid Khalidi, Ilan Pappé, and others argue that the plan’s provisions, combined with the ideas of transfer among the leadership and the pattern of operations, show that it was a blueprint for clearing Arab areas; they point to the correspondence between the plan’s provisions and the villages emptied.

Evidence: The text of the plan, its accompanying orders to brigades, the operational records of particular battles, and the statements of leaders. The text is general and the orders for individual operations vary.

Why it matters: If the plan was a blueprint for expulsion, the exodus appears as a result of policy; if it was a military plan that allowed expulsions by local decision, it appears as a result of the dynamics of war and permissive orders. Both views accept that the plan’s provisions allowed the removal of populations.

Multiple Perspectives

The Spring of 1948 from Different Positions

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns identified by historians across many sources. They are not quotations from named individuals. The groups did not have equal power, equal freedom of action, or equal responsibility for events.

A Palestinian villager or townsperson in the area later held by Israel: Life was increasingly dominated by insecurity, the loss of income, and uncertainty about whether leaders or Arab armies could provide protection. News of massacres, the approach of troops, and the collapse of neighboring towns forced decisions about whether to stay, send women and children away, or leave altogether, often with the expectation of a short absence. For many, the result was permanent loss of home, land, and community.
A member of the Yishuv, a settler, soldier, or resident of besieged Jerusalem: The prospect of Arab invasion, the blockade of roads, and the memory of the Holocaust produced a widespread sense of existential danger. Many saw the war as a struggle for survival, and many soldiers were new immigrants. Shortages and casualties were real, and the possibility of defeat was taken seriously. Participants differed in how they viewed the treatment of Arab civilians, and some objected to expulsions and killings.
An Arab government or army officer: Governments faced public demands to act for Palestine, as well as rivalry among themselves, limited military capacity, and divergent political aims. Officers often found that their forces lacked supplies and a coordinated plan. Defeat brought domestic criticism of rulers and armies and contributed to political change.
A British official or soldier preparing to leave: The main concerns were the safe withdrawal of troops, relations with Arab governments, and avoiding further commitments. British forces sometimes intervened to restrain one side or another and sometimes stood aside, and their choices about timing and positions affected the outcome in places such as Haifa and Jaffa.
A United Nations official or observer: The mediators and observers saw the war as an international problem to be moderated by negotiation. They had little power and depended on the cooperation of the parties. Their reports are among the most detailed contemporary evidence of the truces and the refugee crisis, and their efforts were shaped by the interests of the great powers.
Continuity and Change Over Time

Palestine Before and After 1948

What Changed

  • British rule ended and a Jewish state was established with its own army, parliament, and government
  • Most of the Arab population of the territory that became Israel was displaced, and hundreds of villages and many urban quarters were emptied
  • The territory was divided among Israel, Jordan (West Bank), and Egypt (Gaza Strip), and Jerusalem was divided
  • The Palestinian national movement lost its leadership structure, its institutions, and its territorial base
  • A Palestinian refugee population and an international relief system appeared
  • Several Arab governments faced defeat and political crisis

What Stayed the Same

  • The land remained the object of competing national claims, now with different institutions and populations
  • Jerusalem remained a contested holy city
  • The Arab states’ dependence on external powers and their rivalries continued
  • The international community remained involved but without a means to impose a settlement
  • Arabic-speaking Palestinians continued to live in the region, in refugee communities, in the West Bank and Gaza, and as a minority in Israel

Who experienced change most directly: Palestinians who lost homes and Jewish immigrants who found new homes in the same period; those who stayed in place experienced more continuity, though under a new political order. Why the pattern matters: The year 1948 was a radical break for the demography and politics of the country, but many underlying conflicts continued, which helps explain the persistence of the dispute.

Cause and Effect

The Outcome of 1948: Israel, Nakba, and an Unresolved Conflict

Long-term causes

The growth of Zionism and Palestinian nationalism under the mandate; the unresolved commitments of British policy; the Holocaust and Jewish displacement; the Arab revolt and the weakening of Palestinian institutions; the rivalries and limited capacities of the Arab states.

Short-term causes

Resolution 181 and its rejection by Arab leaders; British withdrawal without a transfer of authority; lack of an enforcement mechanism; the road war and breakdown of order; Haganah offensives after March 1948; the declaration of the state; the Arab invasion.

Immediate effects

The establishment and survival of Israel; displacement and dispossession of Palestinians; the territorial division among Israel, Jordan, and Egypt; the armistice agreements; the creation of UN bodies for mediation and relief.

Long-term consequences

A durable refugee question; unresolved borders and Jerusalem; Arab political upheaval; the transformation of Israeli and Palestinian societies; the central place of 1948 in national memory on both sides.

Historical complexity: The outcome cannot be reduced to a single cause, whether the Arab rejection of partition, the Zionist leadership’s aims, British policy, the weakness of the Arab armies, or great-power choices. It resulted from their interaction with the choices of each community, and none of it was inevitable at the time of the vote in November 1947.

Everyday Life and Social History

Households in War and Displacement

For most inhabitants of Palestine, 1948 was experienced in the daily routines of households rather than in headlines. In Arab villages, the agricultural year, including the olive harvest and the grain and fruit seasons, was interrupted by fighting, curfews, and the closure of roads and markets, and families faced decisions about whether to keep working their land, send members to safer places, or leave. In coastal towns such as Jaffa, whose citrus groves had been a major export industry, the economy collapsed as shipping and trade stopped. In Jewish Jerusalem, residents lived through a siege that brought rationing of food and water, cooking in improvised ways, and the use of shelters. In many cases, children and the elderly suffered most from shortages and disease.

Women’s experiences were often central and are less well recorded in official documents. In the exodus, women often carried children and belongings on foot, kept households together, and organized shelter and food in the camps; in the Yishuv, women served in the Palmach and the army, worked in kibbutz and city economies, and cared for families under strain. Historians rely on oral histories, diaries, letters, and photographs to reconstruct these experiences, and note the limits of sources produced mainly by officials and military men.

After the war, Jewish immigrants from camps in Europe and from Arab countries were housed in transit camps and often in abandoned Arab houses, while Palestinian refugees lived in tents and rented rooms. These contrasting experiences of arrival and loss in the same places are a central part of the social history of 1948.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced the history of the war of 1947–1949 and its consequences, distinguishing the civil war that began after the partition vote of November 29, 1947, from the interstate war that began when Arab armies entered Palestine on May 15, 1948. The collapse of the mandate, the failure of the United Nations to implement partition, and the unequal capacities of the two communities’ institutions shaped the first phase, and by the time the State of Israel was proclaimed, Haganah operations had secured most of the territory allotted to the Jewish state and parts beyond it.

The Yishuv entered the conflict with an embryonic government, a unified though not monolithic military organization, a modern economic base, and an effective system of mobilization. The Palestinian Arab community, by contrast, had lost much of its leadership in the revolt of 1936–1939, was divided politically, depended on local militias and volunteers, and lacked a regular army or central administration. The Arab states, which did not intervene until May, had divergent aims, limited preparations, and no joint command. These differences help explain the course of the war without making any outcome inevitable.

The military narrative shows a progression. From December to March, communities fought over roads, mixed neighborhoods, and convoys. In April the Haganah, adopting Plan Dalet and launching operations such as Nachshon, shifted to territorial control, and the fall of Haifa, Tiberias, Jaffa, Safed, and Beisan removed the Palestinian urban centers. After May 15, the Arab armies were stopped on most fronts, the truces allowed Israel to rearm and reorganize more effectively than its opponents, and the offensives of July, October, and December 1948 extended Israeli control to the Negev, central Galilee, and the southern coast, with a final operation reaching the Gulf of Aqaba in March 1949.

The Palestinian Nakba was not a side effect of the military story but a central part of it. Some 700,000 to 750,000 Palestinians left or were driven from their homes, most before or independently of the Arab invasion. The documents show a variety of causes: direct expulsion in places such as Lydda and Ramle, flight from combat as at Haifa, fear following massacres such as Deir Yassin and, later, those in the Galilee, psychological warfare, and the collapse of daily life. The claim that most Palestinians left on the orders of their leaders is not supported by the evidence. Hundreds of villages were emptied, property was transferred to the state and to new immigrants, and return was prevented by government decision and military practice.

Refugees found shelter in the West Bank, Gaza, Lebanon, Syria, and Transjordan, where relief organizations and then UNRWA provided assistance in conditions of great hardship. A minority of about 150,000 Palestinians remained in Israel under military government, some of them displaced from their original villages. The experiences of refugees and of those who remained differed by class, gender, and place, and their memories became a central part of Palestinian identity.

International diplomacy ran on three tracks that should be distinguished. The United Nations, lacking enforcement power, could recommend and mediate but not impose. Count Bernadotte’s plans, which recognized the refugees’ claim to return, were overtaken by events and by his assassination. Resolution 194 articulated principles on refugees and Jerusalem that were interpreted differently by the parties, the Lausanne conference failed to produce a comprehensive peace, and relief was institutionalized in UNRWA while the political questions remained unresolved.

The four armistice agreements of 1949 ended the main fighting but were not peace treaties. They created lines, the Green Line among them, that differed markedly from the partition map: Israel held about 78 percent of Mandate Palestine, Jordan held the West Bank and East Jerusalem, Egypt administered Gaza, and Jerusalem was divided. The Palestinian Arab state recommended in 1947 was not established, because of the choices of the Palestinian and Arab leaderships, Israeli and Transjordanian interests, the collapse of Palestinian institutions, and the absence of international enforcement.

The consequences were far-reaching. Israel became a member of the United Nations, consolidated its institutions, and absorbed large-scale immigration, while the Palestinians were left without sovereignty or unified leadership. In the Arab states the defeat contributed to crises that produced assassinations, coups, and revolutions, themes of Chapters 62 and 64. The historiography of 1948 has been transformed by archival access, yet it remains shaped by national narratives, and students must distinguish well-documented events from disputed claims about intentions and responsibility.

The chapter has emphasized that 1948 is both the foundation of the State of Israel, remembered by most Israelis as a war of independence, and the Nakba, remembered by Palestinians as the loss of their homeland. Historical analysis does not require choosing between these memories as experiences, but it does require careful attention to the evidence, to differences among places and phases, and to the difference between an armistice and a peace.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Nakba
Arabic for “catastrophe”; the Palestinian term for the displacement and dispossession of 1947–1949 and its consequences.
Yishuv
The organized Jewish community of Palestine before May 1948, whose institutions became the basis of the Israeli state.
Partition
The division of a territory into separate states; here, the proposal of Resolution 181 to divide Mandate Palestine into Jewish and Arab states with an international Jerusalem.
Resolution 181
The General Assembly recommendation of November 29, 1947, adopted 33–13 with 10 abstentions, which was not implemented as proposed.
Civil war phase
The period from November 30, 1947, to May 14, 1948, in which the Jewish and Arab communities of Palestine fought before neighboring armies entered.
Plan Dalet
The Haganah’s operational plan of March 1948, whose provisions on Arab villages are the subject of historical debate.
Haganah
The main military organization of the Yishuv, subordinate to the Jewish Agency, and the basis of the Israel Defense Forces.
Palmach
The elite striking force of the Haganah, disbanded as a separate command in November 1948.
Irgun and Lehi
The dissident Jewish underground organizations; Irgun and Lehi carried out the attack on Deir Yassin, and Lehi assassinated Bernadotte.
Arab Liberation Army
A volunteer force raised under Arab League auspices and commanded by Fawzi al-Qawuqji; defeated in Galilee in October 1948.
Arab Legion
The army of Transjordan, commanded by British officers, the strongest Arab force in Palestine.
Arab Higher Committee
The central Palestinian Arab political body, headed by Hajj Amin al-Husayni, reconstituted in 1945.
Deir Yassin
A village west of Jerusalem where Irgun and Lehi forces killed a large number of civilians on April 9, 1948.
Declaration of Independence
The proclamation of the State of Israel on May 14, 1948, read by David Ben-Gurion.
Israel Defense Forces
The national army created on May 26, 1948, from the Haganah and absorbing other forces.
Truce
A temporary cessation of fighting; two truces (June 11–July 8 and from July 18, 1948) were supervised by United Nations observers.
Operation Dani
The July 1948 offensive that captured Lydda and Ramle and led to the expulsion of their inhabitants.
Expulsion
Compulsion of people to leave a place by military or official action, distinguished in this chapter from flight in response to combat or fear.
Depopulation
The emptying of a village or town of its inhabitants, by whatever cause.
Absentee property
Property of Palestinians who had left, placed under state custody by Israeli emergency regulations and the 1950 law.
Internally displaced Palestinians
Palestinians who remained in Israel but were removed from their homes or villages, sometimes called “present absentees.”
Conciliation Commission
The body (United States, France, Turkey) established by Resolution 194 to assist in a final settlement.
Resolution 194
The General Assembly resolution of December 11, 1948, whose paragraph 11 addresses refugee return and compensation.
UNRWA
The United Nations Relief and Works Agency for Palestine Refugees in the Near East, established by Resolution 302 of December 8, 1949.
Armistice agreement
A military agreement to end hostilities that does not settle the political questions; four were signed in 1949.
Green Line
The 1949 armistice demarcation line between Israel and its neighbors, not a final border.
Demilitarized zone
An area in which neither side may station military forces, as along the Syrian and Egyptian lines.
Corpus separatum
The special international regime proposed for Jerusalem in Resolution 181.
All-Palestine Government
A government proclaimed in Gaza in September 1948 under Egyptian protection, with little real authority.
New historians
Scholars, mainly Israeli, who from the 1980s used archives to revise the traditional account of 1948.

Chapter Reflection

Chapter 61 matters because the events of 1947–1949 created conditions that shaped the history of the Arab world for the rest of the century and beyond. They produced a Jewish state in the heart of the region, a Palestinian refugee population and a stateless nation, a new set of borders and administrations, and a lasting dispute over land, return, and Jerusalem. They also changed the politics of every Arab state that took part.

The chapter reveals how large historical outcomes emerge from the interaction of institutions, decisions, and contingency. The weakness of the mandate’s end, the unequal organization of the two communities, the aims of the Arab governments, and the choices of military commanders each contributed. It also shows how a population can be changed by war, and why historians need to distinguish between events, causes, and later interpretations, and to treat the displacement of civilians as a central subject of history rather than a footnote.

Later chapters build on this foundation. Chapter 62 will examine how the defeat in Palestine contributed to the Egyptian revolution and to Nasser’s pan-Arabism; Chapter 63 will show how the Arab world’s decolonization movements unfolded in the same years; Chapters 64 through 66 will follow the revolutionary regimes, the oil economies, and the wars of 1967 and 1973. The refugee question, the status of Jerusalem, and the borders of 1949 recur across Chapters 66 through 72.

Students who understand the distinctions made in this chapter, between the civil war and the interstate war, between expulsion and flight, between armistice and peace, and between documented events and contested interpretations, will be better equipped to read the later history of the region critically. Understanding 1948 requires holding in view the experiences of those who built a state and those who lost a homeland, and the evidence that allows historians to reconstruct both.
