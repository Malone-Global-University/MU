Part XI — Independence, Revolution, and the Arab Cold War
61

1948: War, Israel, and the Palestinian Nakba

A History of the Arab World: From Ancient Crossroads to the Modern Arab World

Chapter Opening

Chapter 59 ended on November 29, 1947, with a vote in the General Assembly of the United Nations recommending that Mandate Palestine be divided into a Jewish state, an Arab state, and an internationally administered Jerusalem. Within eighteen months the recommendation had been overtaken by civil war, the end of British rule, the proclamation of the State of Israel, the intervention of five neighboring Arab states, the flight and expulsion of a majority of Palestine’s Arab population from the territory that became Israel, and a set of armistice lines that matched the partition map in almost no respect. This chapter explains how that happened, and why a war that ended in armistice rather than peace left behind a conflict that remained unresolved.

Begin with a single day, April 9, 1948. In the morning, near the village of al-Qastal on the road to Jerusalem, Palestinian fighters were still absorbing the death of Abd al-Qadir al-Husayni, the most prominent field commander of the Palestinian Arab forces around Jerusalem, who had been killed the previous night while trying to retake the hill. At dawn, a short distance away, Irgun and Lehi fighters, working with the knowledge of the Haganah, attacked the village of Deir Yassin on the western edge of Jerusalem. By the time the fighting was over, a substantial number of the village’s inhabitants were dead, many of them noncombatants. Within days, news of the attack, in versions that exaggerated and disputed its details, was circulating through Palestinian towns and villages. On that same day, in other parts of the country, convoys were being ambushed, Jewish and Arab neighborhoods were exchanging fire, and British soldiers who still formally governed the country were preparing to leave. No single event of that day determined what followed, but the day is a useful reminder that the war of 1948 was not one conflict but several overlapping ones: a struggle over roads and villages, a contest between two national movements, a collapse of civil government, a series of local tragedies, and, later, a war between states.

The chapter’s organizing problem is therefore one of explanation and distinction. The civil war between Palestine’s Jewish and Arab communities that began after the partition vote (November 30, 1947, to May 14, 1948) was related to, but distinct from, the interstate war that began when Arab armies entered Palestine after the British departure (May 15, 1948, onward). The military history of each phase must be told, but not alone. A second strand concerns political and state institutions: the Jewish Agency and the Yishuv’s institutions that became the government of Israel, the Palestinian leadership organized around the Arab Higher Committee and local committees, and the divergent political aims of Egypt, Transjordan, Iraq, Syria, and Lebanon. A third strand, which this chapter treats as equal in weight to the military narrative, is the social history of the Palestinian Nakba (Arabic for “catastrophe”): the displacement, expulsion, and dispossession of roughly three-quarters of a million people, the destruction or emptying of hundreds of communities, and the creation of a refugee population whose status has remained a central political question. A fourth strand is diplomacy: the United Nations, mediation by Count Folke Bernadotte and Ralph Bunche, General Assembly Resolution 194, and the armistice negotiations of 1949.

The chapter’s principal chronology runs from November 30, 1947, to July 20, 1949, the date of the last of the four armistice agreements, with limited discussion of refugee relief and diplomacy through December 1949. Its subject matter is bounded by its neighbors in the textbook. The origins of the conflict, the mandate, and the partition resolution itself belong to Chapter 59. The consequences of the defeat for Egypt’s politics, including the revolution of 1952, belong to Chapter 62, and the later history of Palestinian political organization, later wars, and regional interventions belongs to later chapters. Chapter 61 explains the war itself, the Nakba, the armistice settlement, and the immediate shape of the unresolved conflict.

The chapter also asks students to confront a problem of terminology and narrative. The same events are called the “War of Independence” (Milhemet ha-Atzma’ut) or “War of Liberation” in Israeli usage and the Nakba in Palestinian and wider Arab usage; in English-language scholarship they are also called the 1948 Arab–Israeli War, the First Arab–Israeli War, or the Palestine War. These names are not neutral labels. They reflect distinct experiences: for most Israeli Jews, 1948 was a war of survival that produced a state; for most Palestinians, it was the moment of national dispossession. Serious history can hold both facts. It need not treat every claim made about 1948 as equally well supported, and this chapter distinguishes carefully among documented events, interpretations of causes and intentions, and later national narratives.

The evidence available to historians has changed greatly since the 1980s. Older accounts depended heavily on memoirs, official statements, and the records of the United Nations and the British government. From the late 1970s and 1980s, the opening of Israeli state and military archives, the British Public Record Office, and the archives of the United Nations and the American government permitted new scholarship, often associated with the “new historians,” as well as continuing work by Palestinian historians relying on oral histories, village memorial books, captured and Arab-language records, and local documentation. Some Israeli files have since been reclassified or restricted. Arab state archives, by contrast, remain much less accessible, a limitation that affects what can be said with confidence about Arab decision-making. Students should read every statement in this chapter with these limits in mind.

By the end of the chapter, students should understand why the outcome in 1949 differed so sharply from the partition recommendation of 1947: Israel was established and, by the end of the fighting, controlled roughly three-quarters of the territory of Mandate Palestine, a considerably larger share than the partition resolution had allotted; the Palestinian Arab state the resolution envisaged was not created; the West Bank came under Jordanian rule and the Gaza Strip under Egyptian administration; Jerusalem was divided; and the question of the refugees, along with the status of Jerusalem and the borders, remained unresolved. The 1949 armistice agreements ended the main interstate fighting without producing a political peace, and that distinction is among the most important facts in the chapter.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Distinguish the civil war of November 1947–May 1948 from the interstate war that followed, and explain how the two phases were connected.
  2. Trace the collapse of British authority and the failure of the United Nations transition plan, explaining why partition was not implemented as recommended.
  3. Compare the political organization, military resources, and strategic decisions of the Yishuv and the Palestinian Arab leadership between November 1947 and May 1948.
  4. Explain the proclamation of the State of Israel, the formation of the Israel Defense Forces, and the Arab League’s decision to intervene, including the different aims of the Arab governments.
  5. Reconstruct the principal military operations and truces from April 1948 to March 1949, using maps to explain changes in territorial control.
  6. Analyze the causes of Palestinian displacement, distinguishing expulsions, flight from combat, fear after massacres, local evacuation orders, and the collapse of civil life, and explain why no single cause accounts for every case.
  7. Describe the experiences of refugees, of Palestinians who remained in Israel, and of internally displaced people, including the loss of property and the prevention of return.
  8. Evaluate the roles of the United Nations, Count Folke Bernadotte, and Ralph Bunche, and distinguish humanitarian relief, political mediation, and legal claims in Resolutions 194 and 302.
  9. Explain the terms and limits of the four 1949 armistice agreements and distinguish armistice demarcation lines from final political borders.
  10. Assess how Israeli, Palestinian, British, Arab, and United Nations sources shape historical interpretation, and distinguish well-documented facts from contested questions of intention and responsibility.
  11. Connect the outcomes of 1948–1949 to later developments in Palestinian society, Israeli state formation, and Arab politics.

Historical Context

By late 1947 Palestine had been governed by Britain for a quarter-century under a League of Nations mandate, whose terms, examined in Chapters 56 and 59, committed Britain both to facilitating a Jewish national home and to safeguarding the civil and religious rights of the non-Jewish communities. The mandate period transformed both communities. The Jewish population, the Yishuv, grew through immigration from perhaps 10 percent of the population in 1918 to roughly one-third by 1947, building national institutions under the Jewish Agency, the Histadrut labor federation, and a clandestine army, the Haganah. The Arab population, about two-thirds of the total, remained overwhelmingly rural and was politically organized around notable families, parties, and the Arab Higher Committee headed by Hajj Amin al-Husayni. The Arab Revolt of 1936–1939 had been suppressed by Britain with the participation of Zionist auxiliaries, and the Palestinian leadership emerged from it exiled, divided, and without a unified armed force. Chapter 59 treats this history in detail; the present chapter relies on it without retelling it.

The Second World War and the Holocaust transformed the diplomatic setting. Jewish displaced persons in Europe, the British restriction on immigration in the 1939 White Paper, the illegal immigration campaign, and the Zionist insurgency against British rule in 1945–1947 pushed Britain, in February 1947, to refer the Palestine question to the United Nations. A special committee, UNSCOP, recommended partition, and on November 29, 1947, the General Assembly adopted Resolution 181 by a vote of 33 to 13, with 10 abstentions. The plan proposed a Jewish state and an Arab state linked by an economic union, with Jerusalem and its surroundings governed by a special international regime. The Jewish state would have received a little over half of the land, including the Negev, in a territory in which the Jewish population was a bare majority and the Arab population very large. The Zionist leadership accepted the plan, while the Arab Higher Committee and the Arab League rejected it as a violation of the rights of the majority population.

Resolution 181 was a recommendation. It specified the end of the mandate, the withdrawal of British forces by August 1, 1948, and the establishment of a United Nations Palestine Commission to oversee a transition and the creation of provisional governments. Britain, however, declined to share authority with the Commission, announced that it would end the mandate at midnight on May 14–15, 1948, and refused to facilitate a gradual handover. The United Nations had no armed force of its own, and the great powers that had endorsed partition, chiefly the United States and the Soviet Union, were not prepared to enforce it. That gap between a recommendation and the capacity to carry it out is the context in which the war began.

The regional setting also mattered. The Arab states had won formal independence only recently, most within the previous decade, and several, including Egypt, Iraq, and Transjordan, remained tied to Britain by treaty or by military dependence. Their governments faced domestic pressure over Palestine, rivalries among one another, and a reputation for rhetorical commitment greater than their capacity. Abdullah of Transjordan, who had long sought a role in Palestine and had held contacts with the Zionist leadership, pursued aims different from those of Egypt, Syria, or the Palestinian leadership. Chapters 56 through 60 describe the construction of this state system, and the present chapter shows its first great test.

Evidence and terminology. This chapter uses “Palestinian Arabs” and “Palestinians” for the Arab population of Mandate Palestine and its descendants, and “the Yishuv” for the Jewish community before May 1948 and “Israeli” for the state’s population after its establishment. It uses “Nakba” for the Palestinian experience of 1948, “expulsion” where documents show people were compelled to leave by military or official action, and “flight” where people left in response to combat, fear, or the collapse of daily life. Figures for casualties and refugees are given as ranges with their sources, because the numbers are contested and some are uncertain. “Arab Legion” refers to the army of Transjordan, which was commanded by British officers, a fact that matters for interpretation. Place names are given in the forms most commonly used in English-language scholarship; Palestinian villages are generally named in their Arabic forms and Israeli towns in their Hebrew forms.

Division I

From Partition to Civil War, November 1947–March 1948

How fighting developed inside Palestine before any neighboring Arab army crossed the border.

Section 61.1

The Partition Vote and the Beginning of Armed Conflict

The General Assembly vote of November 29, 1947, was followed by celebrations in Jewish neighborhoods and a general strike and demonstrations in Arab ones. Violence began almost at once. On November 30, Arab gunmen attacked a bus on the road near Petah Tikvah and a second vehicle near Lydda, killing several Jewish passengers, and in the following days crowds attacked the commercial center of Jerusalem, where Arab shops near the Jaffa Gate were looted and burned. Jewish militias, in particular the Irgun, responded with bombings in Arab crowds and neighborhoods in Jerusalem, Jaffa, and Haifa. By the end of December, perhaps a few hundred people had died. The numbers are uncertain, but the pattern was clear: roads, mixed neighborhoods, and the borders between Jewish and Arab quarters of towns became zones of fear.

The two national movements reacted to the vote in sharply different ways. The Jewish Agency accepted the resolution, while privately regarding its borders, and especially the position of Jerusalem and the large Arab population inside the Jewish state, as a difficulty to be managed rather than a final settlement. The Arab Higher Committee, the Arab League, and the Arab governments rejected partition as illegitimate, arguing that the United Nations had no right to divide a country against the will of its majority population. Many Palestinians also believed, that Arab strength and British indifference would prevent the plan from taking effect. The refusal to accept the plan did not, in itself, produce an organized plan of Arab military action, and the early fighting was largely improvised.

The immediate political consequence was the disintegration of the framework within which the two communities had lived together under British rule. The mandate government retained formal authority but not the will or the capacity to impose order on a country divided by a decision it did not support. Police and officials left their posts, and local authority passed gradually to community organizations. In the vacuum, each community mobilized: the Yishuv through its established national institutions and the Haganah, and the Palestinian Arab population through local committees, volunteers, and the political leadership around the Husayni family. From the outset, the conflict therefore had two dimensions that must be kept distinct in the narrative: a communal war on the ground, with its ambushes, reprisals, and civilian casualties, and a struggle over who would inherit state power when Britain left.

Section 61.2

British Withdrawal and the Failure of the Transition

Britain’s policy in the winter of 1947–1948 was governed by a determination to leave Palestine without becoming entangled in implementing a plan it had not endorsed. It announced that the mandate would end on May 15, 1948, and that it would withdraw its forces in stages, retaining control of the ports and the Haifa area for as long as possible to preserve an orderly departure. It refused to let the United Nations Palestine Commission enter the country before the end of the mandate, to transfer administration to provisional Arab and Jewish authorities early, or to establish the joint arrangements for transport, communications, and security that the partition plan assumed. British officials also had concerns that were mainly imperial: relations with Arab governments, the security of their own troops, and the need to avoid an open commitment to either side.

The practical result was a progressive disintegration of mandate government. British forces continued to intervene occasionally, in some cases to stop attacks, in others to disarm one side or another, and in Haifa and elsewhere to protect their own withdrawal routes. But the rule of law eroded steadily. Postal services, railways, courts, and local administration weakened or ceased; the economy of the towns was disrupted by strikes and fighting; and the Palestine Commission, which Britain never permitted to take up administration inside the country and which worked from Lake Success, reported to the Security Council that it could not carry out its task without an armed international force that no state was willing to provide. Britain’s policy has been interpreted in contrasting ways. Some historians emphasize a deliberate withdrawal that allowed the conflict to be resolved by arms; others emphasize improvisation, exhaustion, and the pressures of a larger imperial retreat. The records show a government more interested in extrication than in any particular outcome, and the result of that stance was a vacuum that the better organized side was best prepared to fill.

Section 61.3

Palestinian Arab Leadership, Local Committees, and Volunteer Forces

The Palestinian Arab leadership entered 1948 organizationally weaker than its Jewish counterpart. Its central body, the Arab Higher Committee, had been reconstituted in 1945 under the auspices of the Arab League and was dominated by Hajj Amin al-Husayni, who lived in exile in Cairo and had a reputation for rivalry with other Arab leaders, in particular with King Abdullah of Transjordan. His wartime association with Nazi Germany and the fact that Britain had banned him from Palestine since 1937 further limited his role. The leadership was divided between the Husayni camp and the opposition associated with the Nashashibi family and other notables, and it lacked a unified administration, a regular army, a reliable system of taxation, or a stable source of arms.

Local organization partly compensated for these weaknesses. In towns and villages, national committees collected funds, organized guards, and tried to maintain services and order. In the Jerusalem region, Abd al-Qadir al-Husayni formed the Holy War Army (Jaysh al-Jihad al-Muqaddas), and in the south and elsewhere other commanders organized local forces. The Palestinian fighters were largely part-time, short of weapons, and tied to their home villages, a pattern that made village defense effective in some places and mobile offensive operations difficult. Many men had served in the 1936–1939 revolt or in British forces during the Second World War, but there was no systematic training, nor a general staff.

In January 1948 the Arab Liberation Army, a volunteer force recruited under the Arab League’s Military Committee, began entering Palestine from Syria. Commanded by Fawzi al-Qawuqji, a veteran of the earlier revolt, it comprised some thousands of men from Syria, Iraq, Lebanon, and other countries, along with some Palestinians. The Army took positions in the north and center of the country, but its relations with the Palestinian leadership were uneasy, and its operations, such as the attack on Kibbutz Tirat Zvi and the Battle of Mishmar HaEmek in April, achieved little. It was a symptom of the Arab states’ approach: they wished to support the Palestinians without committing their regular armies, and they preferred volunteers they could control to a Palestinian force under the Husaynis. The gap between rhetoric and capacity that characterized Arab policy, and to which Chapter 59 pointed, is clear in this period.

Section 61.4

The Yishuv’s Military Mobilization: Haganah, Irgun, and Lehi

The Yishuv was better prepared. It possessed a government in embryo, with a Jewish Agency and National Council (Va’ad Leumi) that could raise taxes, mobilize labor, and coordinate defense; a unified, though not monolithic, military organization in the Haganah, with its elite striking force, the Palmach; a modern industrial base capable of producing light weapons; and a population that included many veterans of the British army and of the Second World War, together with refugees and survivors who saw the conflict as existential. David Ben-Gurion, who as head of the Jewish Agency’s defense portfolio took charge of military organization from late 1946, expanded conscription, reorganized the Haganah into brigades, and sought arms abroad.

The Haganah in December 1947 had on the order of tens of thousands of trained or partly trained personnel, but few heavy weapons, little artillery, and almost no armor or aircraft. Its main strategic problem was that Jewish settlements were scattered across the country and dependent on roads that passed through Arab territory. Ben-Gurion’s decision to hold every settlement, including isolated ones in the Negev and the Etzion bloc south of Jerusalem, imposed serious burdens on the army but preserved a political presence across the territory. From early 1948, the Yishuv also pursued arms purchases, in particular from Czechoslovakia, through agents in Europe and the United States; the arrival of Czech rifles, machine guns, and later aircraft in the spring and summer was to be decisive for the capacity of the Israeli forces.

The Irgun Zvai Leumi and Lehi (the Stern Group) represented a different political tradition. Both were products of the right-wing Revisionist movement and of the underground struggle against Britain, which they conducted with more violence and less restraint than the mainstream Zionist leadership. The Irgun, under Menachem Begin, and Lehi, a smaller and more radical group, had conducted attacks on British personnel and on Arab civilians, and the Jewish Agency had at times cooperated with the British in suppressing them. In 1948 they operated alongside, and sometimes against, the Haganah. The tension was not resolved until the new state’s government demanded their integration into the Israel Defense Forces and, in June, forcibly ended the Irgun’s independent arms shipment on the vessel Altalena. Understanding the distinctions among these organizations matters because many of the events discussed in later sections, including Deir Yassin and the assassination of Bernadotte, involved the dissident groups, and because the national narratives of Israeli and Palestinian historians treat their role very differently.

Section 61.5

Urban Fighting, Roads, Convoys, and the Fragmentation of Palestine

The characteristic form of the civil war was the struggle for roads. Jewish settlements and neighborhoods needed supplies, and Arab forces were able to block movement on roads that ran through Arab-populated territory. Jerusalem, whose population was roughly 100,000 Jews and some 65,000 Arabs, depended on the road to the coast, and convoys were ambushed repeatedly on the road through Bab al-Wad. Convoys to the Etzion bloc and to the Galilee were attacked, and in March the convoy known as the Nebi Daniel convoy was lost. By the end of March, Jerusalem’s Jewish population faced shortages of food, water, and fuel, and the road was effectively closed.

In the towns, fighting was less systematic and more terrible. In Jerusalem, bomb attacks and counterattacks between Jewish and Arab neighborhoods killed civilians; in January, a Jewish attack on the Semiramis Hotel killed residents, and on February 22, Arab-led bombings on Ben Yehuda Street killed dozens of Jews, with the involvement of British deserters, according to later accounts. In Haifa, the oil refinery incident in December, the Balad al-Shaykh raid by the Haganah, and other attacks and reprisals escalated tension. In Jaffa and Tel Aviv, snipers fired across the boundary between the two cities, and the border neighborhoods emptied of residents. The effects on commerce were serious: Arab commercial districts lost their Jewish customers, and the economic interdependence that had marked the mandate’s mixed towns dissolved into mutual boycott and siege.

Fragmentation had a political meaning. A country in which each community could not move safely beyond its own area could not be governed from the center. The Palestinian leadership could not prevent local deals, such as those between some villages and Jewish neighbors, nor could it impose discipline on local commanders. The Yishuv, by contrast, could treat the roads as a single strategic problem, with the Haganah’s command free to decide which operations to launch. That asymmetry became decisive in April.

Section 61.6

The Military and Political Crisis of Early 1948

By March 1948, both communities were in a crisis. For the Yishuv, the crisis was military and psychological: convoys were being destroyed, Jerusalem was under siege, the Etzion bloc and parts of the Galilee were isolated, and the expectation of an invasion by the Arab armies after the British left made the prospect of survival uncertain. Many in the Haganah leadership feared that the Jewish state, once declared, would not survive the first weeks. At the same time, the political situation of the Yishuv worsened when, on March 19, the American representative at the Security Council proposed that partition be suspended and replaced by a temporary United Nations trusteeship. The proposal shook the Zionist leadership, who feared the loss of American support for a state.

For the Palestinians, the crisis was social and political. Civilians who could afford to leave the towns had begun to do so, and the process of departure accelerated as the economy deteriorated and the sense of order disappeared. By the end of March, tens of thousands of Palestinians, according to the estimates historians have offered, and which depend on the sources they use, had left Haifa, Jaffa, Jerusalem, and other mixed towns, often to nearby Arab areas or to neighboring countries, expecting to return after the fighting ended. Many of those who left were from wealthier and more mobile classes, including families with relatives abroad, and the emigration of the urban elite deprived the Palestinian leadership of the resources and social cohesion necessary to organize resistance. The numbers and the dynamics of these early departures are discussed in Division VI.

Both national movements changed strategy. For the Arab side, the failure of volunteers to win the war and the weakness of local forces led to a gradual shift of responsibility to the Arab states, a process that culminated in the Arab League’s decision to authorize intervention. For the Zionist side, the combination of the road blockade, the arrival of arms from Czechoslovakia, and the failure of the Arab forces to exploit their early advantage led to a decision to take the offensive. These changes, taken in March and April, mark the passage from the first phase of the civil war to the second, the subject of Division II.

Division II

Military Transformation and the Collapse of the Mandate, March–May 1948

Section 61.7

Plan Dalet and the Strategic Reorganization of the Haganah

Plan Dalet (Plan D), completed in March 1948 under the direction of Yigael Yadin of the Haganah’s operations staff, was a general operational plan intended to prepare the Haganah for the anticipated invasion by Arab armies after the British left. It replaced earlier plans that had been defensive in character. Its immediate purpose was to secure the territory allotted to the Jewish state by Resolution 181 and Jewish settlements outside it, along with the roads between them, to establish a defensible territorial base before the Arab armies entered. To do so, it instructed commanders to seize strategic points, including police stations and British installations as the British withdrew, and to establish control over the main routes.

The plan’s language on Arab villages and towns is the part that matters most for the history of the Nakba. It directed that enemy population centers located within or near the defensive system be dealt with so that they could not serve as bases for attack. In the case of villages that offered resistance, the plan envisaged the destruction of the village and, if they resisted, the expulsion of the population outside the borders of the state. Where there was no resistance, the plan allowed garrisons to be installed and the population to remain under military administration. The text is general and leaves the decision to local commanders to determine what constituted resistance.

How the plan was understood and carried out is the subject of one of the central historiographical debates of the period, presented in the Historical Debate feature below. What is not disputed is the plan’s organizational importance. It marked the transition from a force whose commanders thought in terms of defending settlements to one that thought in terms of controlling territory, and it supplied the framework for brigade-level offensives in April and May. Operations conducted in its spirit, including those in the Jerusalem corridor, at Haifa, and in eastern Galilee, brought about the capture of Arab towns and villages in the weeks before the Arab armies entered. Whether the plan’s authors intended depopulation, or whether depopulation was a consequence of its military logic, is a different question, which scholars answer differently.

Section 61.8

Operation Nachshon and the Battle for the Jerusalem Corridor

Operation Nachshon, launched on April 2, 1948, was the first large Haganah offensive of the war. Its purpose was to open the road to Jerusalem, and in particular to bring a large convoy of supplies, arms, and personnel to the besieged Jewish city. About 1,500 troops were assembled, drawn from the Haganah’s brigades, including a Palmach element, and the force was supplied, in part, with weapons that had recently arrived from Czechoslovakia. The operation involved the capture of positions along the road, including the hill of al-Qastal, a village on the road to Jerusalem.

In the first days of April the Palmach took al-Qastal; the Palestinian forces under Abd al-Qadir al-Husayni, who had come back from Cairo to command the Jerusalem front, attempted to retake it, and on the night of April 7–8 he was killed in the fighting. His death, and the funeral at which large crowds gathered in Jerusalem on April 9, deprived the Palestinian side of its most capable local commander at a decisive moment, and the Arab force that had held the hill dispersed. The Haganah, in turn, recaptured the position on April 9, and by April 20 it had cleared much of the corridor and sent through several convoys.

The operation had consequences for the Arab villages along the road, among them Qastal, Saris, Beit Mahsir, Qaluniya (Colonia), and other communities in the hills west of Jerusalem. Many were depopulated during the operation, some as a direct result of attacks and some as people fled in fear, and their homes were in a number of cases destroyed or occupied. The corridor remained open only briefly, because the Arab forces reorganized and again closed the road in May, and the road to Jerusalem was not secure until the later Burma Road alternative route was constructed in June. The relief of Jerusalem and the depopulation of its approaches were thus linked, and later sections return to both.

Section 61.9

Deir Yassin: Violence, Massacre, and Historical Controversy

Deir Yassin, a village of some 600 to 750 inhabitants on a hill west of Jerusalem, lay near the road and the Jewish suburb of Givat Shaul. Its leaders had reportedly made an agreement of non-aggression with their Jewish neighbors, and it had not been a major center of Arab fighters, though it had been included in the sphere of operations of the Haganah in the Nachshon plan. In early April, the Irgun and Lehi, with the permission of the Haganah’s Jerusalem command, which attached conditions to it, planned an attack on the village. The operation, on April 9, involved about 120 men from the two organizations. The village’s defenders were few and poorly armed, and they resisted, and the Jewish fighters, who also took casualties, fought house to house.

What happened in and after the fighting is the center of the controversy. It is established beyond serious dispute that a considerable number of villagers, including women, children, and elderly men, were killed, that some were killed after the fighting had effectively ended, that survivors, including prisoners, were paraded through Jerusalem before being released, and that the attackers’ own leaders, as well as the Jewish Agency and the Haganah, quickly acknowledged that atrocities had occurred. The Jewish Agency sent a letter of condemnation to King Abdullah. The Haganah and the Jewish Agency denounced the killings, and the Irgun’s leader, Menachem Begin, later wrote that the attack contributed to the panic that helped the Jewish side.

The casualty figures are contested. Early reports by the Irgun and by Jewish and Arab spokesmen gave a figure of about 250, and that number was widely repeated for decades. A Red Cross delegate, Jacques de Reynier, who visited on April 10, reported on the bodies he found. A later Palestinian study of the village, carried out by researchers at Bir Zeit University in the 1980s, identified fewer than 120 victims by name, and many scholars, Israeli and Palestinian, now accept a figure in the range of 100 to 120, which is lower than the early claim but still an unambiguous massacre of civilians. The disagreement about the number should not obscure the fact that a massacre took place. Nor does the revision of the casualty figure reduce the significance of the event in the minds of the Palestinians who heard of it. Some Palestinian leaders, according to later accounts, had also exaggerated the events in the hope of rallying Arab opinion, with the unintended effect of increasing fear.

The wider consequences were political and psychological. The news of Deir Yassin spread through Palestine, and was repeated in broadcasts and leaflets, sometimes with added atrocity details, and later memoirs by Palestinians repeatedly invoke it as one of the causes of flight. Four days later, on April 13, a convoy of doctors, nurses, and staff traveling to Hadassah Hospital on Mount Scopus was ambushed in what is generally understood as a reprisal, and some seventy-nine people were killed. The two events demonstrate how reprisal and fear drove one another. Chapter 61’s later treatment of the causes of flight (Section 61.33) shows that Deir Yassin was important but not the sole or even the most common cause of departure.

Section 61.10

The Battle for Haifa and the Displacement of Its Arab Population

Haifa, in April 1948, was a port city of roughly equal Arab and Jewish populations, each in the tens of thousands, divided by topography: Jewish residents lived on Mount Carmel and Arab residents in the lower town and in neighborhoods near the harbor. The British, who controlled the port and used it for their withdrawal, retained a garrison there. In the weeks before April 21, incidents between the communities escalated, and Arab residents who could afford to had been leaving.

On April 20–21, the British commander, General Hugh Stockwell, informed the Haganah that British forces were withdrawing from positions between the Arab and Jewish quarters. The Haganah’s Carmeli Brigade, under Moshe Carmel, which had prepared for the moment, launched an attack, sometimes called Operation Bi’ur Hametz, on April 21–22. Using mortars and infantry attacks from the heights, it overran the Arab neighborhoods in a day and a half. Resistance was poorly coordinated; some Arab fighters from the Arab Liberation Army had withdrawn, and the local leaders were unable to hold the lower city. A delegation of Arab notables requested a truce through the British; they were presented with terms by the Haganah that included the surrender of arms, and the Arab leadership declined to sign them and left the city.

The population then left in large numbers. The largest part moved by sea, in boats supplied or permitted by the British, toward Acre and Lebanon, and others left by road. In the following days and weeks, only a few thousand Arabs remained in the city. The reasons are documented in some detail and show the complexity discussed in Division VI: fear of the fighting, the collapse of the Arab leadership, the departure of the local committee and of wealthier residents, the British decision to facilitate evacuation, an appeal from Jewish municipal leaders for Arab residents to remain, and the effect of Haganah propaganda and actions. Some Haganah leaders encouraged departure and some opposed it. Whatever the mixture, the outcome is clear: the Arab population of Haifa was reduced from tens of thousands to a small remnant, and the city was transformed into a predominantly Jewish city within days.

Section 61.11

Jaffa, the Coastal Plain, and the Collapse of Palestinian Urban Centers

Jaffa, the largest Arab city in Palestine, with a population of some 70,000 before the war, lay alongside Tel Aviv, and the two cities’ border had been a front since December. The partition plan had assigned Jaffa to the Arab state as an enclave surrounded by the Jewish state, and the Jewish leadership regarded the enclave as a strategic problem. In late April the Irgun, with Haganah knowledge, launched a mortar bombardment and attack on Manshiya, the quarter between the two cities, beginning on April 25. British forces intervened to stop the attack and then the Haganah, in a series of operations, took over in the surrounding villages and closed the city’s links with the interior.

The city’s defense, by this point, was fragmented. The local committee had little authority, discipline among the fighters was poor, and some fighters left or demanded pay. As in Haifa, those who could leave the city did so, many by sea. By May 13, the day before the British left, Jaffa’s leaders signed a surrender to the Haganah, which entered the city, and only several thousand of the original residents remained. The loss of Jaffa had an enormous economic and symbolic importance: it was the commercial center of Arab Palestine, home to a number of newspapers and business families, and its fall demonstrated that the Palestinian political and economic infrastructure could not survive without protection.

The collapse of the urban centers is among the central facts of the war. Palestinian society had been undergoing a major social transformation in the mandate period, in which towns such as Jaffa, Haifa, Acre, and Jerusalem were the centers of commerce, education, press, and political organization. Their fall, and the departure of much of their leadership, disrupted the networks that connected rural and urban communities. When Arab armies entered in May, they were not entering a country with functioning Palestinian institutions to support, but one in which these institutions were largely in ruins, a fact that shaped both the political aims and the military potential of the Arab intervention.

Section 61.12

Galilee, Rural Palestine, and the Eve of British Withdrawal

In the north, Haganah and Palmach operations between April and May extended Jewish control across eastern and western Galilee. Tiberias, whose Arab population was evacuated with British help after the Haganah captured it on April 18, was the first Arab town to fall. Operation Yiftach, from late April, secured the area around the Hula Valley and Safed. Safed, a town of mixed population in which Jews were outnumbered, fell on May 10–11 after a battle in which Palmach forces defeated the Arab defenders, and most Arab residents left. Beisan (Beit She’an) was taken on May 12–13, and its inhabitants were largely expelled or left. In the west, Operation Ben-Ami, from May 13, relieved the isolated Jewish settlements and led to the fall of Acre on May 17–18, after the state was already declared. In these operations, nearby villages were captured, with varying consequences for their inhabitants.

In the center, the Haganah’s battle at Mishmar HaEmek in April defeated Qawuqji’s Arab Liberation Army and led to the displacement of several Arab villages around the kibbutz. Along the coastal plain, Operation Hametz in late April and May captured villages near Jaffa, and Jewish forces consolidated the corridor between Tel Aviv and Jerusalem. The pattern was not uniform. In some regions, villages negotiated non-aggression agreements and were left alone; in others, Haganah units attacked and cleared them. Villages were frequently emptied of civilians when fighting approached, and evidence from Israeli military records and from Palestinian accounts shows many individual circumstances.

Not all the fighting went the Haganah’s way. In the Etzion bloc south of Jerusalem, a group of four Jewish settlements, defenders had held out since the start of the war, but on May 12–13 the bloc fell to a combined attack by the Arab Legion and local irregulars. A number of the defenders who had surrendered, as well as some who had not, were killed by irregular forces, and the Jewish survivors were taken prisoner; the memory of the massacre at Kfar Etzion became part of Israeli national memory, much as Deir Yassin did of Palestinian. By May 14, the Haganah controlled the areas of the Jewish state allotted by Resolution 181, apart from certain pockets, and also a number of areas beyond it. Historians offer estimates, which differ by method, of 250,000 to 300,000 Palestinian Arabs who had left or been expelled from their homes by the time the state was declared, before any Arab army had entered Palestine. That fact, more than any other, distinguishes the civil war from the interstate war that followed and demonstrates the importance of separating them.

Division III

The Establishment of Israel and the Regionalization of the War

Statehood as an act of institutional construction under fire, and the different purposes of the Arab governments that intervened.

Section 61.13

The Declaration of the State of Israel, May 14, 1948

On the afternoon of Friday, May 14, 1948, in the Tel Aviv Museum, David Ben-Gurion read the Declaration of the Establishment of the State of Israel before the People’s Council, the provisional legislative body of the Yishuv, and a gathering of invited guests. The decision had not been unanimous or easy. On May 12, the small executive known as the People’s Administration had voted six to four to proceed with a declaration after hearing military advisers who offered gloomy assessments of the chances of survival and after the American Secretary of State, George Marshall, had warned the Zionist leadership, through Moshe Shertok (Sharett) and other emissaries, against declaring a state and risking war. Ben-Gurion, who argued that the Yishuv could not afford to wait, prevailed.

The Declaration itself is a carefully balanced political document. It grounded the state’s legitimacy in several sources: the historic connection of the Jewish people to the land, the international recognition embodied in the Balfour Declaration and the League of Nations mandate, the Holocaust and the need for refuge, and Resolution 181 of the General Assembly. It declared the establishment of a Jewish state to be called Israel, to take effect upon the end of the mandate, and it promised equality of social and political rights for all inhabitants regardless of religion, race, or sex. It appealed to the Arab inhabitants to keep the peace and take part in the building of the state, and it offered peace to the neighboring states. It did not define the borders of the state; the drafters, after debate, left out any reference to boundaries, in part because the war was about to determine them. The Declaration is a primary source examined in the feature below.

The meaning of the Declaration has been interpreted in different ways. Israeli historians have emphasized that it enacted a right to self-determination recognized by the United Nations and carried out under threat of invasion. Palestinian historians have emphasized that it was issued without Palestinian consent and effected a proclamation of sovereignty over territory in which a large Arab population lived, some of it outside the partition borders. The Declaration’s promise of equality and the subsequent flight and expulsion of most Arab inhabitants are an important contrast to keep in view. The immediate effect of the Declaration was the creation of a provisional government, with Ben-Gurion as prime minister and defense minister, which assumed control of the institutions of the Yishuv and set out to turn them into the machinery of a state in the middle of a war.

Section 61.14

The End of the British Mandate and International Recognition

At midnight on May 14–15 the mandate ended. The High Commissioner, Sir Alan Cunningham, left Haifa by sea on May 14, and the last British forces in the country were withdrawn in stages, with the final contingents remaining in Haifa until the end of June. Britain transferred no authority to a successor and did not recognize either an Arab or a Jewish state at the time, though Jewish forces had been preparing to take over British installations as they were evacuated. The British position was that it was ending the mandate, not partitioning the country; its government later extended de facto recognition to Israel in January 1949 and de jure recognition in April 1950.

International recognition came quickly. President Harry Truman, who had been under pressure from both domestic Jewish organizations and some of his own advisers, extended de facto recognition to the provisional government within minutes of the proclamation, a decision against the advice of the State Department. The Soviet Union recognized Israel de jure on May 17, and a number of other states, including Poland, Czechoslovakia, and Guatemala, followed in the next days. The motives of the great powers were different: American recognition reflected domestic politics, humanitarian sympathy, and the desire to preempt a Soviet advantage, while Soviet policy sought to weaken British influence in the Middle East and appeared sympathetic to the Zionist cause at that moment. Both powers, though, imposed arms restrictions on the combatants during the war, and the principal supply of weapons to Israel came from Czechoslovakia, which acted with Soviet acquiescence.

The Arab states refused to recognize the legality of the new state. In their view, the General Assembly had no authority to partition a country, the mandate had been intended to lead to the independence of a unified Palestine, and the declaration of a Jewish state on part of the territory was a violation of the rights of its inhabitants. The legal arguments, which were expressed in the Arab states’ communication to the Secretary-General of May 15, are discussed in the primary source below. This difference over legitimacy was the diplomatic counterpart of the fighting, and it persisted long after the armistice agreements.

Section 61.15

The Israel Defense Forces and the Machinery of Statehood

A state at war needs a single army. On May 26, 1948, the provisional government issued an ordinance establishing the Israel Defense Forces (IDF), and the Haganah ceased to exist as an independent organization. The act was both administrative and political. It affirmed the principle that the armed forces would be subordinate to the civilian government and that no other force could operate. Ben-Gurion, who regarded the private armies of the Yishuv as a danger to national authority, pursued the principle consistently, including against his own political allies in the Palmach and its parent party.

Integrating the dissident groups was the first test. The Irgun in most areas agreed to merge its units into the IDF, but it retained a separate command in Jerusalem for a time and attempted to land a large shipment of arms from France on the ship Altalena on June 20–22, in violation of the terms of the truce and the integration agreement. When negotiations over the distribution of the weapons failed, the army fired on the ship off Tel Aviv, and it was destroyed with some loss of life on both sides. Begin ordered his men not to retaliate and a civil war among Jews was avoided. After Bernadotte’s assassination in September the government moved decisively against Lehi, and the Palmach, whose leaders had been affiliated with the left-wing party Mapam, was disbanded as a separate command in November 1948. By that point the army had become a single national force.

The army’s growth was rapid. At the time of the declaration, the Haganah and its associated forces had perhaps 30,000 troops at their disposal; conscription of men and women, and the arrival of immigrants, including Holocaust survivors who were sometimes sent into combat with minimal training, as well as foreign volunteers (Mahal), including World War II veterans from Britain, the United States, and other countries, swelled its strength. By the winter of 1948–1949, estimates put the number of personnel under arms at roughly 100,000. The provisional government also created ministries, a civil service, courts, and a system of military government over Arab-populated areas, and held elections for a constituent assembly in January 1949. State formation was therefore not a single act but a process conducted during war, and it relied on the institutions the Yishuv had built during the mandate.

Section 61.16

The Arab League and the Decision to Intervene

The Arab League’s decision to intervene emerged from months of debate. At the end of 1947, the League had approved the supply of arms and volunteers to the Palestinians, a policy that suited most governments, which wished to avoid direct commitment. By April 1948, with the Palestinian side losing ground, the League’s political committee, meeting in Cairo, concluded that intervention by regular armies was necessary when the mandate ended. Some governments hesitated. The Egyptian prime minister, Mahmud Fahmi al-Nuqrashi, was reluctant, and his army’s leaders warned of its lack of preparedness; it was only in the second week of May, under pressure from King Farouk and from public opinion, that the cabinet and parliament approved the entry of troops.

The governments entering Palestine did not share an aim. Transjordan’s King Abdullah, who had long sought to extend his authority over the Arab parts of Palestine, regarded the intervention as a chance to secure them, and he had discussed with Jewish Agency representatives, including a meeting with Golda Meir on May 10, the possibility of an arrangement that would avoid a war with the Yishuv. He did not succeed in that attempt, but his army’s later operations indicate that its priorities were the Arab areas assigned to the Arab state by the partition plan and Jerusalem, rather than an attack on the Jewish state proper. Egypt’s king and government wanted to demonstrate Egypt’s leadership of the Arab world, to prevent Abdullah from taking Palestine, and to establish an Egyptian presence in the south. Syria, whose government under President Shukri al-Quwatli feared that Abdullah’s dream of a Greater Syria endangered its own sovereignty, acted partly to forestall Hashemite expansion. Iraq, a Hashemite monarchy allied with Britain, sent an expeditionary force whose political direction was not clearly subordinated to the others. Lebanon, divided by confessional politics and militarily weak, sent a small force and was reluctant to take part. Saudi Arabia and Yemen sent token contingents.

The Arab League named Abdullah the nominal commander of the combined forces but did not establish a real joint command, and each army acted according to the orders of its own government. This division mattered. The Palestinian leadership was not given a decisive role, and the Arab governments distrusted both the Mufti’s ambitions and each other’s. The Arab states entered a war for which they had not prepared a common plan and for which their military and political preparations were poor. The gap between public pledges, which spoke of saving Palestine, and the actual capabilities and priorities of the governments is a theme to which the chapter returns. The Arab states’ own explanation of their intervention, in a communication to the United Nations on May 15, is analyzed in the Primary Source Window below.

Section 61.17

Palestinian Armed Organizations and the Limits of Independent Command

The Palestinian armed organizations had suffered a severe blow in April: the death of Abd al-Qadir al-Husayni and the defeats of other local forces. By May the Holy War Army was fragmented, and the Arab Higher Committee had limited capacity to coordinate the remaining irregulars. The Arab League, which had in the winter refused to give the Mufti’s forces a central role, chose in May to let the regular armies take command of operations. In practice, Palestinian fighters were either absorbed into the Arab regular armies, fought as local militias beside them, or continued as semi-independent bands. In the Jerusalem area and around Hebron, Palestinians fought alongside the Arab Legion and Egyptian forces, among them volunteers from the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood; in Galilee, they were supplemented by the Arab Liberation Army under Qawuqji.

The limits on Palestinian independence also reflected the political goals of the states that intervened. The Arab Legion in the areas it controlled disarmed some Palestinian irregulars and discouraged the formation of separate Palestinian administrations. Egypt, for its part, retained control over its occupied areas of the south, though it later accepted the formation of an All-Palestine Government in Gaza in September 1948, a body whose real authority was slight. The result was that the Palestinians, who had been the principal actors in the civil war, became secondary actors in the interstate war. Their leadership was divided, their forces lacked heavy weapons and command structures, and key decisions about the course of the war were taken in Amman, Cairo, Damascus, and Baghdad. The consequence, a Palestinian national movement without sovereignty or territory under its control at the end of the war, is one of the central results of 1948.

Section 61.18

Military Resources, Strategic Objectives, and the Balance of Forces in May 1948

Estimates of the balance of forces in May 1948 vary, and historians depend on different sets of sources and definitions of who counts as a soldier, so figures should be treated as approximate. A widely used estimate holds that the Israeli forces numbered about 30,000 mobilized personnel at the start of the interstate war, with the number rising rapidly through the summer, while the Arab regular armies that entered Palestine in May numbered in total in the low tens of thousands, with Egypt and Transjordan fielding the largest contingents, Iraq and Syria smaller ones, and Lebanon very few. Egypt’s expeditionary force was initially several thousand troops, expanded later; the Arab Legion had perhaps 4,500 to 6,000 men in Palestine; Iraq, Syria, and Lebanon each committed forces numbered in the low thousands or below. The widespread image of a small Jewish force confronting overwhelming Arab numbers is not supported by the figures on the ground at the start of the invasion, though in total population and potential manpower the Arab states were vastly larger than the Yishuv, and Israeli commanders at the time feared the outcome.

Over the course of the war the balance changed. Israel’s forces grew rapidly, as the truce allowed mobilization and rearmament, and weapons acquired in Czechoslovakia, and later in other places, gave them advantages in artillery, aircraft, and armor that they did not possess in May. The Arab armies, by contrast, were hampered by supply problems, limited manpower, restrictions on British arms deliveries resulting from the United Nations embargo, and the lack of a joint command. By the end of 1948, estimates suggest that Israel’s forces outnumbered the Arab forces in the field, a pattern which scholars have used to argue that Israel’s victory was due not to a sudden advantage but to better organization, more effective mobilization, and unity of command.

The strategic objectives of each side mattered as much as the number of troops. The Israeli command’s objectives were to secure the territory of the state and its settlements and to prevent a coordinated Arab attack; its commanders understood that the war would be decided by the first weeks. The Arab commanders’ objectives were more varied, as noted, and often limited: Egypt’s was to occupy southern Palestine and Gaza, Transjordan’s to secure the Arab areas, Syria’s and Iraq’s to take positions in the north and center. Few were prepared for a decisive campaign against the Jewish state, and such coordinated plans as existed were set aside in the confusion of the first days. These differences help explain why the Arab armies, individually, were stopped in battles around the borders of the Jewish state, as the next division shows.

Division IV

The First Interstate Campaigns, May–June 1948

Five armies enter Palestine with different objectives; the front lines that formed by early June shaped the rest of the war.

Section 61.19

The Egyptian Advance through Gaza and Southern Palestine

On May 15, 1948, Egyptian forces crossed the border and advanced in two main columns. The first, moving along the coastal road through Rafah, Gaza, and the town of al-Majdal, aimed to take southern Palestine and approach Tel Aviv; the second moved through Beersheba and Hebron toward Bethlehem and the southern outskirts of Jerusalem. The Egyptian army, which had been prepared hurriedly and with poor intelligence, encountered Jewish settlements on the way, such as Kfar Darom, Nirim, Yad Mordechai, and Negba, whose defenders delayed the advance and in some cases held out with few weapons. The battle at Yad Mordechai, from May 19 to 24, slowed the Egyptian advance by several days, giving the Israeli command time to organize its southern defenses, though the kibbutz itself was eventually abandoned.

The Egyptians reached the vicinity of Isdud (Ashdod), about 30 kilometers from Tel Aviv, by the end of May, where on June 2–3 they were stopped by the Israeli Givati Brigade at a bridge at Ad Halom, near the coast. Egyptian aircraft bombed Tel Aviv in the opening weeks, causing casualties, until the arrival of Israeli fighters. The southern column reached the area of Bethlehem and the outskirts of Jerusalem, and the Egyptians established a front in the Negev and the Hebron hills. These advances gave Egypt control over the area that would become the Gaza Strip and over parts of the Negev, as well as contact with the Arab Legion near Hebron. But the advance had stopped, and the Egyptian forces were stretched along long supply lines in the desert.

Egypt’s war aims were limited and in part defined by domestic and inter-Arab politics. King Farouk and his government wanted to show Egyptian leadership and prevent the extension of Transjordanian power, and they aimed to occupy the southern part of Palestine rather than to destroy Israel. The army’s officer corps, many of whom served in the war and returned embittered by what they saw as inadequate equipment, political interference, and corruption, became important in the postwar politics examined in Chapter 62, though that history belongs to a later chapter.

Section 61.20

The Arab Legion, Jerusalem, and the Central Highlands

The Arab Legion was the best-trained Arab army, and its officers, notably the commander John Bagot Glubb (Glubb Pasha), were British. It crossed the Jordan River on May 15 and moved to take positions in the Arab areas assigned to the Arab state, including Ramallah, Nablus, and Hebron. In Jerusalem, a city that the partition plan had placed under an international regime, the Legion entered on May 17–18 and began a battle for the Old City and its Jewish Quarter. The Quarter, defended by a small force of the Haganah and Irgun and sheltering about 1,500 civilians, resisted for ten days before it surrendered on May 28. Most of its residents were expelled to the Jewish part of the city, and a few hundred men were taken as prisoners; the quarter’s buildings were afterward damaged or looted, and Jews were barred from the Old City for the next nineteen years.

For King Abdullah, the capture of the Old City and the Haram al-Sharif had immense political importance. He styled himself the protector of the Muslim and Christian holy places and tied his claim over Palestine to his role as a Hashemite ruler. The Legion avoided attacks on territory that the partition plan allotted to the Jewish state, and in Jerusalem it fought for the Old City and neighboring areas while Israeli forces took the western part of the city and positions such as Mount Zion. The role of British officers in the Legion, and British policy toward Transjordan, was a point of controversy at the time, and it remains one: Britain had a subsidy agreement and military mission in Transjordan, and the British government wished to avoid confrontation with the Legion while preventing it from clashing with Israeli forces. Historians have debated how closely British policy shaped Abdullah’s moderation, and the available documents suggest that Abdullah’s own calculations, including his contacts with the Yishuv leadership, were primary.

Section 61.21

Latrun, the Jerusalem Corridor, and the Struggle over Supply

The most severe contest between Israeli forces and the Legion was at Latrun, a hill with a police fort and a monastery that controlled the road between Tel Aviv and Jerusalem. Jerusalem’s Jewish population, around 100,000, was running short of food and water, and the capture of Latrun was seen as vital. The Legion occupied it in mid-May. The Israeli army launched a succession of attacks between May 25 and June 10, some of which were poorly coordinated, used units that had just been formed from newly arrived immigrants with little training, and encountered a determined defense. They failed, with heavy Israeli casualties, an episode that remained one of the most painful in Israeli military memory and the subject of recriminations among commanders.

The failure of the Latrun attacks led the Israelis to build an alternative route through the hills south of Latrun, known as the Burma Road, which was cut and widened by work crews, largely by hand and with improvised equipment, until jeeps and then trucks could pass in early June. It was opened shortly before the first truce took effect and allowed supplies, and later a water pipeline, to reach Jerusalem. The importance of the new route was that it enabled the Israeli forces to hold Jerusalem’s western part and ensured that the city would not fall by siege. The Latrun salient remained in Jordanian hands until 1967. The struggle for the corridor illustrates how, in the first phase of the interstate war, control over a few kilometers of road could outweigh a larger territorial gain elsewhere.

Section 61.22

Syrian Operations on the Northern Front

Syria’s army, small and recently formed, attacked in the Jordan Valley on May 15–16 and after some initial advances captured the village of Samakh on May 18. It then attacked the kibbutzim of Degania Alef and Degania Bet, south of the Sea of Galilee, on May 20–21 with armored vehicles and artillery. The defenders, from the settlers themselves and reinforcements, stopped the tanks with Molotov cocktails and with a small artillery piece that had just arrived, and the Syrians withdrew. The Syrian advance in the valley was then contained. In June the Syrians took a bridgehead at Mishmar HaYarden, west of the Jordan River in the Galilee Panhandle, which they held until the armistice.

Syria’s army was limited in size and equipment, and the Syrian government, whose political circles were divided over the war and which was worried about Abdullah’s aims, gave the army limited objectives. The operation also shows the extent to which Syrian forces coordinated with other Arab armies: poorly. The Syrian army’s experience in 1948 contributed to unrest within the army and, in March 1949, to the first of a succession of military coups in Syria, a development outside this chapter’s scope that Chapter 64 examines.

Section 61.23

Iraqi and Lebanese Forces and the Wider Northern and Central Fronts

Iraq, which did not share a border with Palestine, sent an expeditionary force through Transjordan whose total strength was initially a few thousand men and later more. After a failed attempt to cross the Jordan River at Gesher on May 15, the Iraqi forces were redirected southward and took up positions in the area around Jenin, Nablus, and Tulkarm, in the northern West Bank, from which they threatened the Israeli coastal plain. Israeli attacks, including an attempt to capture Jenin in early June, failed, and the Iraqi army held the triangle until the end of the war. Iraq’s presence there is notable because Iraq, unlike the other Arab states, did not sign an armistice agreement with Israel. In 1949, after the agreement with Jordan, Iraqi troops withdrew and handed their positions over to the Arab Legion.

Lebanon, a country with a small army and a delicate confessional balance, sent a force of about 1,000 men to the northern border region. It took part in limited fighting along the frontier around Malkiya and neighboring positions, but its military role was small and its participation was mostly symbolic. The Lebanese government, headed by President Bishara al-Khuri, had been reluctant to join and feared the effects of war on the country’s fragile internal politics. The Arab Liberation Army, meanwhile, kept its positions in central Galilee until defeated in October. The contrast between the national contingents shows why it is misleading to speak of a single Arab army: they differed in size, training, objectives, supply, and relations with their governments.

Section 61.24

The Battlefield by June 1948: Advances, Defenses, and Strategic Limitations

By the first week of June, the war’s first phase had produced a set of front lines that no side had expected. The Egyptians held southern Palestine and the Gaza area but had been stopped north of Isdud. The Arab Legion held the Old City, Latrun, and the central West Bank. The Iraqis held the northern West Bank triangle. The Syrians held a salient on the Jordan. The Israeli forces held the territory allocated to the Jewish state, minus a few exposed settlements and the Negev settlements that were cut off, and some areas in western Galilee and the central corridor that had been assigned to the Arab state or to Jerusalem. The Israeli forces had survived the initial attack and, in a number of battles, stopped the Arab columns, but had not been able to expel them, and had not yet recovered the initiative.

The costs were high. Israeli losses by June were severe in proportion to the population, and by the end of the war roughly 6,000 Israelis, about one percent of the Jewish population, had died, about two-thirds of them soldiers; Arab military and civilian casualties are much less certain, and estimates vary widely depending on the sources and the definitions used. The Israeli army was short of weapons, ammunition, and trained commanders, and the Arab armies were short of supplies, in particular because of Britain’s withdrawal of its officers and supplies in some cases and the embargo. Both sides, in the circumstances, were open to a truce. Security Council Resolution 50, of May 29, called for a cessation of hostilities and a four-week truce under the supervision of the UN mediator, a development that, as Division V shows, favored the side better able to use the pause. The military lessons of the first campaigns, including the importance of unity of command, supply, and the integration of air power and armor, shaped the Israeli plans for July.

Division V

The Truces and the Final Campaigns, June 1948–March 1949

Pauses that reshaped the balance of forces, and the offensives that determined the territory Israel held at the end of the war.

Section 61.25

The First United Nations Truce, June 11–July 8, 1948

The Security Council’s call for a truce was linked to the appointment of a United Nations Mediator, Count Folke Bernadotte of Sweden, whom the General Assembly had named on May 20, 1948. The truce took effect on June 11 and was to last four weeks. Its terms forbade the introduction of fighting men and war material into Palestine and the Arab countries and called for observers to supervise the arrangements. A small group of military observers, drawn from officers of Belgium, France, Sweden, and the United States, began to patrol, and this was the beginning of what became the United Nations Truce Supervision Organization (UNTSO). The mediator also began to explore proposals for a political settlement, discussed in Division VII.

The truce was not enforced. The observers were few, had little power, and depended on the cooperation of the parties, who accused each other of violations. In the first days Israel received weapons and aircraft that had been purchased abroad, and its forces expanded through conscription and the integration of immigrants. The Arab states also strengthened their forces. Egypt, in particular, blocked Israeli convoys to the besieged Negev settlements, a restriction that Israel used afterward as justification for later operations. The Altalena incident, on June 20–22, took place during the truce. Both sides used the pause, but historians generally conclude that the truce favored Israel, whose better organization and access to outside supply allowed it to expand its army from perhaps 30,000 in May to roughly 60,000 by July, while Arab forces grew more slowly and suffered shortages, especially of ammunition. On June 16 the Israeli cabinet decided that Palestinian refugees would not be allowed to return while the war lasted, a decision whose consequences are discussed in Division VI.

Section 61.26

Rearmament, Military Reorganization, and the Second Truce

During the truce Israel reorganized its forces into brigades with standard command structures and appointed new commanders, and it acquired the first of the aircraft that would give it local air superiority. Czechoslovakia supplied rifles, machine guns, ammunition, and Avia fighters, along with later shipments from other sources, and the IDF added artillery, armored vehicles, and a small navy. Training was improved, and the Palmach and other formations were absorbed in a common structure. These developments transformed the army from a force mainly equipped with light weapons to one capable of mounting multi-brigade offensives.

On the Arab side, the principal problem was not recruitment but coordination and logistics. The Arab Legion’s British officers faced restrictions on supplies of ammunition from Britain, and Egyptian forces faced extended supply lines and a lack of reliable equipment. No joint command was created, and the armies’ political leaders could not agree on a coordinated offensive. When the truce expired on July 8, the Arab governments, in particular Egypt, did not agree to extend it, and fighting resumed the next day. Israel launched the offensives known as the “Ten Days.” On July 15, the Security Council, in Resolution 54, declared that the situation in Palestine constituted a threat to the peace under Chapter VII of the Charter and ordered a cease-fire. The second truce, which took effect on July 18, was open-ended rather than fixed in duration, and it remained in effect, with many violations, through the autumn. It left the Israeli forces in control of more territory than before July 8 and with the initiative in the coming campaigns.

Section 61.27

The Ten Days and Operation Dani: Lydda, Ramle, and Central Palestine

The Ten Days (July 8–18) were a series of Israeli operations on several fronts. In the north, Israeli forces captured Nazareth on July 16 and cleared parts of Lower Galilee; in the south, they attacked Egyptian positions and tried to relieve the Negev; in Jerusalem, an attempt to take the Old City failed. The most important operation, however, was Operation Dani in the center of the country, which aimed to eliminate the Arab-held salient that threatened Tel Aviv and to widen the corridor to Jerusalem. The target was the area around the towns of Lydda and Ramle, with the country’s main airport at Lydda, and the Latrun salient beyond.

The Israeli attack on Lydda began on July 10–11, with units of the Yiftach and Eighth Brigades under the overall command of Yigal Allon. On July 11 Israeli forces entered the town, whose population, swollen with refugees from other places to perhaps 30,000 or more, was defended by local militia and a small Arab Legion force. On July 12, an incident occurred at the Dahmash mosque and nearby buildings, where Israeli soldiers were fired on or believed they were, and where a large number of men who had taken refuge there were killed; Israeli estimates run to several hundred, and others suggest lower numbers, with the exact number still disputed. Ramle surrendered on July 12. The Arab Legion, whose commanders had decided not to defend the towns in strength and had withdrawn, was criticized by Palestinians and other Arabs for abandoning them.

What followed is among the best-documented episodes of expulsion in the war. Ben-Gurion approved, and Allon and Yitzhak Rabin, then a senior operations officer, ordered, the removal of the population of both towns. Over the following days, tens of thousands of inhabitants, usually estimated at 50,000 to 70,000 across the two towns, were forced out and made to march in the heat toward Arab lines at Ramallah, in a march on which some, particularly the elderly and young children, died of exhaustion, thirst, and illness; the number of those who died is uncertain. Rabin’s account of the episode appeared in his memoirs, from which Israeli censors cut the passage in 1979; the text was published in the United States. The events at Lydda and Ramle also showed that the army’s treatment of Arab populations had changed since April: by July, the population of towns that were captured was expelled in many cases, not merely allowed to leave. Only a few thousand people remained in the two towns, and they were afterward concentrated in a small part of the towns under military administration, while Jewish immigrants were settled in the emptied houses. Division VI returns to the episode.

Section 61.28

Operation Yoav and the Struggle for the Negev

The southern front was quiet in the late summer, but the Israeli leadership considered the Negev a vital objective. It was allocated to the Jewish state in the partition plan, had a small Jewish population, and was held in part by the Egyptians, and it had been designated for Arab control in Bernadotte’s proposals. On October 15, 1948, after an Israeli convoy was fired upon and the Egyptian army was accused of violating the truce, Israeli forces launched Operation Yoav (also called Operation Ten Plagues). The offensive, supported by air power, broke the Egyptian lines near the coastal plain and the road to Beersheba and cut the Egyptian forces into separate groups.

Within a week, the Israeli forces had taken Beersheba (October 21), cleared much of the northern Negev, and surrounded a pocket of Egyptian troops at Faluja (al-Faluja), among whom were several young officers who later played a role in Egyptian politics; that story belongs to Chapter 62. The Egyptians, who found it difficult to resupply, withdrew toward Gaza, and the Israeli forces moved onward to Beit Hanoun and Hebron’s approaches. The Security Council, in Resolution 61 of November 4, demanded the withdrawal of forces to the positions held before October 14, and the acting mediator, Ralph Bunche, sought to restore the truce. Israel complied only partially, and by the end of the operation it had taken most of the northern Negev, opened the road to the besieged southern settlements, and left the Egyptian army confined to the Gaza area and Faluja.

The transformation of Israel’s military position was far-reaching. Israel now controlled the territory that connected its core to the Negev and the southern coast. For Palestinian communities in the region, such as those around Beersheba, Isdud, and al-Majdal, the consequences were severe: most inhabitants left or were expelled during and after the operation, and the Arab population of the Negev was drastically reduced, with part of it dispersed to Gaza and Hebron. For the Egyptians, it showed that their army could not be supplied or reinforced for a prolonged war.

Section 61.29

Operation Hiram and the Conquest of Northern Galilee

On October 29–31, 1948, Israeli forces under Northern Command, led by Moshe Carmel, launched Operation Hiram against the Arab Liberation Army and the Arab areas of central and upper Galilee. The operation was swift. In about sixty hours, Israeli brigades overran the ALA positions, which were poorly supplied and without clear command, and captured Safsaf, Jish, Sasa, Meron, and many other villages, up to the Lebanese border; Israeli units also crossed into southern Lebanon and occupied a number of villages, from which they withdrew in the following months. The ALA, which had been the main Arab force in the north, ceased to be an effective formation.

Hiram was also the occasion of a number of documented atrocities. In several villages, among them Safsaf, Saliha, Hula (in Lebanon), Jish, and Eilabun, Israeli troops killed civilians and prisoners, in some cases in groups, and in others forced inhabitants to leave. The evidence for these events comes from Israeli military records, contemporary letters, and testimonies of survivors, as well as from the inquiries that Israeli authorities opened. Estimates of the number killed at each site vary; at Safsaf, for example, evidence has pointed to dozens of victims, and at Saliha to a high number killed when a house was blown up. Some of those responsible were punished, if lightly, and Ben-Gurion expressed concern about the impact of the events, while some officers, according to the records, had given instructions to expel. The treatment of villagers was not uniform. Many Christian and Druze communities, including Rama and Peqi’in, were allowed to remain, and in some cases communities were not harmed, while in others the population was forced out.

The result was that the population of the northern Galilee, which had held, in the partition plan, the area assigned to the Arab state, was reduced; tens of thousands of Palestinians left for Lebanon, and many others were displaced within the area. The campaign demonstrated the practical difference between the Yishuv’s position in April, when it was fighting for survival, and in October, when it had dominant military power and a decisive political advantage. The capture of the Galilee extended Israeli control well beyond the borders of the partition plan.

Section 61.30

Operation Horev and the Final Southern Operations, 1948–1949

The last major operations of the war took place in the south. In late December 1948, Israel launched Operation Horev (also called Operation Lot), aimed at destroying the Egyptian forces in the Gaza area and forcing Egypt to negotiate. The offensive began on December 22 and its forces broke through the Egyptian defenses, crossed into the Sinai Peninsula and approached the town of al-Arish and the Egyptian border, capturing Abu Ageila on January 1–2. The Israeli advance into Egyptian territory attracted British and American diplomatic pressure, and Israel withdrew from Sinai. A cease-fire took effect on January 7, 1949, the day on which Israeli aircraft shot down several British reconnaissance planes, an episode that heightened tension with Britain, and Egypt agreed to armistice negotiations, which opened on Rhodes on January 13.

The final operation, Uvda (March 5–10, 1949), was conducted after the armistice with Egypt had been signed and in the absence of effective opposition. Israeli forces advanced through the southern Negev to the Gulf of Aqaba and raised the flag at Umm Rashrash (Eilat) on March 10, thereby extending Israel’s territory to the Red Sea. The operation, conducted at a time when negotiations with Jordan were under way, had diplomatic and strategic significance: it established an Israeli presence on the Gulf of Aqaba and gave the state a southern outlet. The operation also shows that the military campaigns continued to the very end to be used to establish positions that could affect the diplomatic settlement. The campaign dates and boundaries given in this division are those most commonly used in the scholarly literature, which draws on Israeli military records, UN truce documentation, and Arab accounts; where these sources differ on operational details, the differences are noted in the text.

Division VI

The Palestinian Nakba: Displacement, Dispossession, and the Destruction of Communities

What happened to Palestinian towns, villages, and families, reconstructed place by place rather than by a single formula.

Section 61.31

The First Waves of Palestinian Displacement, 1947–Early 1948

The Nakba did not begin with the arrival of Arab armies or even with the fall of Haifa. It began in the winter of 1947–1948 with a quieter exodus of people who left their homes because life had become unsafe and unworkable. The first to go were usually those with the means and connections to do so: families of wealthy landowners, merchants, professionals, and officials, many of them in Jaffa, Haifa, and Jerusalem, who moved to Beirut, Cairo, Amman, Damascus, or to the Arab-controlled hill country, in most cases expecting to return after the fighting. Banks closed or restricted withdrawals, public services deteriorated, shops lost customers, and the mandate police withdrew. The effects of the economic collapse were felt especially in towns on the boundary between Jewish and Arab areas, where snipers and bombs made residence perilous.

Historians estimate that by the end of March 1948, some 75,000 to 100,000 Palestinian Arabs, depending on the study and the sources, had left their homes. The Arab Higher Committee and local committees at times tried to discourage departure, and at other times arranged for the evacuation of women, children, and elderly people from villages close to the fighting; the Arab Liberation Army, similarly, sometimes asked for the evacuation of a village in order to prepare defenses. Some villagers in the coastal plain and near Jewish settlements were pressed to leave by Haganah forces or by Jewish neighbors’ threats: the people of Caesarea (Qisarya), for example, left in February in circumstances in which pressure by Haganah forces is documented. In other areas, villages made local arrangements with Jewish neighbors and were left alone for a time.

Two features of this first phase need emphasis. The first is that departure was not generally understood by those who left as permanent, and many left household goods, livestock, or crops behind, believing that they would be back. The second is that the early departures weakened Palestinian society as the war progressed: the loss of leadership, financial capital, and the urban middle class undermined the ability of the community to organize resistance or to maintain public services. Many Palestinians later said that by the time the major offensives began, the sense of a collapsing order had already convinced many neighbors to leave, and the rural population began to follow.

Section 61.32

The Mass Displacement of Spring and Summer 1948

The scale of displacement changed in April. The Haganah’s offensives, Operation Nachshon in the corridor to Jerusalem, the attacks on Tiberias, Haifa, Jaffa, Safed, Beisan, and Acre, and the operations in the Galilee and the coastal plain caused the fall of nearly all of Palestine’s major towns within the area that became Israel and the departure of most of their inhabitants. Historians estimate that between 250,000 and 300,000 Palestinians had left or been driven out by the time of the Declaration of May 14, and the departure of the urban population accounts for much of the figure. Most had gone to territory still controlled by Arab forces or to neighboring countries, in the belief, again, that the departure was temporary.

The interstate war did not stop the displacement; it altered its character. The July offensives, with the expulsion of the inhabitants of Lydda and Ramle, added tens of thousands, and the October and later operations in the Negev, the southern coast, and Galilee displaced further groups, among them the populations of Beersheba, Isdud, many villages in the south and in the Galilee, and the people of the northern Galilee who went to Lebanon. A final wave of displacement extended into 1949 and even later, as Israeli forces removed some remaining villagers from border areas and from places such as al-Majdal, whose remaining inhabitants were expelled in 1950.

The total number of Palestinians displaced from the territory that became Israel is a matter of continuing discussion, as Section 61.37 explains. Most careful estimates fall in the range of about 700,000 to 750,000, and the United Nations figure of 726,000 for 1949 has been widely used. Some of these people moved several times, from a village to a nearby town and then to a camp, or from one country to another. It is worth stressing that the collapse of the Palestinian towns was almost wholly complete before the intervention of Arab armies, an important qualification of narratives that explain the refugee problem as a product of the Arab invasion or of Arab orders to leave.

Section 61.33

Expulsions, Massacres, Fear, and the Causes of Flight

No single explanation fits every case, and the best historical work treats the causes of Palestinian displacement as plural. Historians identify several categories, which operated together in many places, and the chapter’s task is to distinguish them and to show where the documents support each.

The first is direct expulsion: instances in which Israeli military commanders ordered inhabitants to leave or removed them by force. The clearest is Lydda and Ramle in July 1948, where written orders, military reports, and the recollections of participants document the decision. Other cases include a number of villages in Galilee, the Negev, and the coastal plain, such as Eilabun and al-Majdal, and in Jaffa’s surrounding villages. The second is flight from combat: Palestinians who fled from artillery or mortar fire, or from the approach of troops, as at Haifa and in many villages. The third is fear following atrocities: the effect of reports of killings at Deir Yassin and, later, at places such as Safsaf, Saliha, al-Dawayima, and Hula. Estimates of the number killed in these incidents differ, and some remain uncertain, but contemporary documents, including Israeli military reports and inquiries, confirm that civilians and prisoners were killed in a number of them. The fourth is psychological warfare: leaflets, loudspeaker broadcasts, and rumors, which the Haganah used, for example, in the operations in the Safed area. The fifth is the collapse of the civil order, which left populations without leadership, food, or security.

A sixth category, which Israeli accounts long stressed, is that Arab leaders or radio broadcasts instructed Palestinians to leave. Historians who have examined broadcasts and documents, Palestinian and Israeli alike, have not found evidence of a general order to evacuate, and some documents show Arab leaders trying to prevent departure. There were, however, local instances in which Arab commanders and committees ordered or advised evacuation of women and children, and these are documented, as in some villages near Tiberias and in parts of Haifa; the Arab Higher Committee’s position was generally to discourage flight. A Haganah intelligence report of June 1948 examining the departures of the preceding months attributed the majority to Jewish military operations and related psychological effects, and only a small portion to Arab instructions.

The question of intention is related, but distinct. Some Zionist leaders before and during 1948, among them Ben-Gurion, had discussed the idea of “transfer” of Arab populations, and the idea was part of earlier Zionist debate. Historians disagree about whether the expulsions of 1948 resulted from a systematic plan or from decisions made locally in the course of war, with the encouragement or acquiescence of political leaders. A committee headed by Yosef Weitz, which later advised on the settlement of the abandoned areas, was an important example of official thinking about the new demographic reality. What the record shows with certainty is that, after the exodus began, the Israeli government decided not to permit the return of the refugees, and that military commanders in many places destroyed or occupied abandoned villages to prevent it. Historians who emphasize planning and those who emphasize the contingencies of war agree on the outcome and on much of the documentary record, while differing on how to interpret motive; the Historical Debate feature examines this issue.

Section 61.34

Depopulated Villages, Property Loss, and the Prevention of Return

By the end of the war, several hundred Palestinian communities in the territory that became Israel had been emptied of their inhabitants. The exact number depends on the definition and the source. A detailed survey by Walid Khalidi, All That Remains, lists 418 villages that were depopulated and occupied or destroyed; other Palestinian counts, which include small settlements and urban quarters, reach more than 500. Israeli researchers using military and governmental records have counted somewhat fewer villages but acknowledged the same general scale. Many of the depopulated villages were demolished, in some cases deliberately as part of military policy to prevent return and in others for reasons of settlement, farming, or construction, while others were occupied by new Jewish immigrants or kibbutzim, or had their buildings partly preserved; a few remain visible today as ruins among forests and fields.

The emptying of the towns was accompanied by a transfer of property. Homes, shops, workshops, orchards, and agricultural lands in cities such as Jaffa, Haifa, Tiberias, and Acre were occupied by Jewish immigrants and by state institutions; the contents of houses were often looted by soldiers and civilians, a phenomenon documented in Israeli sources and condemned by some officials. The government regulated the process through emergency regulations in 1948 and 1949 that provided for the custody of “abandoned” and “absentee” property, and legislated it more fully in 1950 in the Absentee Property Law, which placed the property of absent owners in the hands of a state custodian. The legislation covered not only refugees outside the country but also Palestinians who remained in Israel but had been displaced within it, and the effect was that large amounts of Arab-owned land passed into state ownership. The legal category of “absentee” thus joined the political decision on return in making the departure irreversible.

The prevention of return was a policy as well as a consequence. On June 16, 1948, as noted, the Israeli cabinet decided against permitting refugees to come back during the war, and later decisions, as well as military practices of firing on returning villagers and demolishing villages, made return difficult. In 1949, thousands of refugees, many of them farmers who wanted to harvest crops or bring out family members or goods, tried to cross the armistice lines, and the Israeli military treated them as infiltrators; the policy of shooting those who crossed became a main feature of the border region in the following years. The position of the Israeli government that the refugee question should be resolved as part of a general peace, and that Israel was not responsible for the exodus, is examined in Division VII.

Section 61.35

Refugee Routes, Camps, Families, and the Humanitarian Crisis

The refugees’ journeys followed the geography of the war. From Haifa and Acre, many went by sea or road to Lebanon; from the Galilee, across the northern frontier to Lebanon and Syria; from the central coast and the interior, to the hill country of the West Bank, where the Arab Legion and Iraqi forces held the ground; from Jaffa and the south, to Gaza and, to a lesser degree, to Egypt; and from the Jordan Valley, across the river into Transjordan. Estimates of where the refugees ended up in 1949 and 1950, which are rough and based on United Nations relief registrations, give about 280,000 in the West Bank, perhaps 200,000 in the Gaza Strip, where the population of the area approximately tripled, roughly 100,000 in Lebanon, perhaps 75,000 to 90,000 in Syria, and some tens of thousands in Transjordan, along with smaller numbers elsewhere.

The humanitarian emergency was immediate. In many places, families lived in caves, schools, mosques, orchards, or open fields; they ran short of food and water, and the cold, wet winter of 1948–1949 caused severe suffering. Disease and malnutrition struck children and elderly refugees. Family members were separated in the confusion, and many people lost contact for years. The hosting communities, themselves poor, were overwhelmed, and the governments of the host states had limited means. Relief was first provided by local charities, the Red Cross, and religious groups, with the American Friends Service Committee, the Quakers, playing an important role in Gaza, and then by the United Nations Relief for Palestine Refugees, established late in 1948, and, from 1950, by UNRWA. Camps, first of tents and then of more permanent shelters, were set up in the West Bank, Gaza, Lebanon, Syria, and Jordan.

The experiences of the refugees differed by class, gender, and place. Peasant families, who had lost their land, their principal asset, found it hardest to rebuild, while wealthier urban families could rent housing, move money abroad, or use professional skills to find work in Beirut, Amman, Kuwait, and elsewhere. Women, who in rural households had been central to agricultural and domestic work, often bore the burdens of caring for children and households in the camps under the poorest conditions. Children’s schooling was interrupted, and one of the first institutional responses was the establishment of schools. The legal status of the refugees varied: Jordan eventually extended citizenship to many, while Lebanon and Syria did not, and Egypt limited the rights of Palestinians in Gaza. The refugees’ status, as stateless people whose claims to return were recognized in principle by the United Nations but not implemented, became a lasting feature of the region’s politics.

Section 61.36

Palestinians Who Remained: Minority Communities and Internal Displacement

Not all Palestinians left. At the end of the war some 150,000 to 160,000 Palestinian Arabs remained in the territory under Israeli control, a minority of about one-eighth of the population of the new state. The largest groups lived in Galilee, including the town of Nazareth, which surrendered in July and whose population largely remained in part because of the conduct of the officer who took it; in the “Little Triangle” of villages along the armistice line, which Israel obtained in the April 1949 agreement with Transjordan; and in the Negev, where, among the Bedouin tribes, a large part of the population was displaced. Many of those who stayed lived in villages, and some in the mixed towns of Haifa, Jaffa, Acre, Lydda, and Ramle.

A substantial number of Palestinians who remained within the state were themselves displaced from their homes. Estimates of internally displaced persons, sometimes called “present absentees” in Israeli law because they were physically present in Israel but legally classified as absent from their property, range from about 30,000 to 46,000. Among them were the inhabitants of villages such as Ikrit and Bir’im in the Galilee, whose Christian residents were asked to leave in 1948 on the promise of an early return that was never fulfilled, in spite of a decision by Israel’s High Court of Justice in 1951 in favor of their return. The land and property of such persons were treated as absentee property.

Those who remained came under military government, which restricted movement and political activity and which was in place from 1948 to 1966, and their relationship with the state was shaped by the fact that they were a minority within a state whose defining institutions were Jewish, while having been separated from the larger Palestinian people. Their citizenship status was settled by law in 1952. These communities experienced the Nakba too, though in a different way: they lost relatives, land, and political leadership, and they lived under restrictions while becoming a part of the society of the new state. Their position as both Palestinian Arabs and citizens of Israel is a theme in the history of the state that this chapter introduces and later chapters develop.

Section 61.37

Counting the Nakba: Demography, Evidence, Memory, and Historical Interpretation

Estimates of the number of Palestinian refugees vary, partly because of different definitions and partly because of the quality of the sources. The United Nations Economic Survey Mission for the Middle East put the number at about 726,000 in 1949, a figure accepted by many scholars as a reasonable approximation; the Conciliation Commission later revised that to 711,000, and the UNRWA registered 914,000 or more by 1950, a figure that includes needy local residents and children born after the exodus. Historians such as Benny Morris, using Israeli and other documents, estimated between 600,000 and 760,000. Israeli government estimates at the time were lower, near 520,000, while Palestinian estimates were higher, 800,000 or more. The differences are due to the absence of a complete census for 1948, the difficulty of knowing how many people moved, and political motives. The British mandate’s village statistics of 1945 and its land records remain the best baseline for population by locality.

The sources for understanding what happened are diverse. They include the records of the Israeli army and government, which since the 1980s have been opened in part; the British administration’s archives; United Nations records, including the reports of observers and mediators; Arab governmental and military records, which are largely closed or incomplete; the records of relief agencies; newspapers and radio monitoring; photographs; memoirs of participants; and the oral histories collected from refugees. The last are especially important for reconstructing village life, the day of departure, and the experiences of women and children, which official documents rarely describe. Oral history, like any source, has limits: memories change, and accounts must be checked against other evidence, but historians have found that, where tested, many oral accounts correspond closely to documents. Palestinian memorial books about destroyed villages, the surveys produced by Khalidi and others, and community archives preserve information about the places themselves.

The Nakba became central to Palestinian identity. The term was given currency in 1948 by the Syrian historian Constantin Zurayk, whose pamphlet on “the meaning of the disaster” argued that Arab societies had to understand and reform themselves after the defeat. For Palestinians, it came to designate the loss of homeland, society, and political existence, and the symbols of the memory, such as house keys and village names, became part of national culture. The claim to return, based on Resolution 194, is a principal theme of Palestinian politics. For many Israelis, 1948 was the War of Independence, and for many years the history of the displacement of Palestinians was marginal in Israeli public memory, and the term Nakba itself is contested. This divergence of memory is a historical fact that students must understand, while evaluating the evidence on which each account rests.

Division VII

International Diplomacy, Mediation, and the Refugee Question

Three tracks that must be kept apart: political mediation, legal claims, and humanitarian relief.

Section 61.38

The United Nations and the Failure to Implement Partition

Resolution 181 gave the United Nations Palestine Commission, composed of representatives of five states (Bolivia, Czechoslovakia, Denmark, Panama, and the Philippines), the task of supervising the transition. In practice the Commission could do almost nothing. Britain refused to allow it to take over the administration of any area before the end of the mandate, the Arab states and the Arab Higher Committee refused to cooperate, and the Commission lacked armed force. In February 1948 it reported to the Security Council that the plan could not be implemented peacefully unless armed forces were provided, and it asked the Council to supply them. The great powers, which had voted for partition, were not prepared to do so. The American government in particular, which had worried about the strain on its resources and its relations with Arab oil-producing states, concluded that partition could not be enforced without military commitments it was unwilling to make.

The American proposal of March 19 to place Palestine under a temporary trusteeship reflected this reversal. It was not adopted, but it temporarily undermined the Jewish Agency’s confidence and strengthened the sense among Arab leaders that partition might be abandoned. The General Assembly met in special session in April and May, and on May 14, the day the State of Israel was proclaimed, it voted to appoint a United Nations Mediator and discharge the Palestine Commission. The Security Council, in the meantime, set up a Truce Commission composed of the consuls in Jerusalem of the United States, France, and Belgium. These arrangements show the limits of United Nations authority at that time. The organization was only three years old, it depended on the great powers, and the Charter’s enforcement powers under Chapter VII were not used to impose partition. They were first invoked on July 15, 1948, in Resolution 54, and then only to order a cease-fire.

The failure to implement partition, then, was not primarily a failure of a UN body but a failure of the states that had voted for the plan to commit the means to carry it out, compounded by the refusal of the Arab leadership and states to accept it and by the military decisions of the two sides. The Assembly’s recommendation retained a legal and moral status that the parties continued to invoke, the Israelis citing it for the legitimacy of their state, and the Arabs citing it for the creation of an Arab state, but it did not itself determine the outcome.

Section 61.39

Count Folke Bernadotte and the Search for a Political Settlement

The man appointed as mediator, Count Folke Bernadotte, was a Swedish nobleman, president of the Swedish Red Cross, and a nephew of the Swedish king, who was known for his negotiations in 1945 to rescue prisoners from German concentration camps. He arrived in the region in late May 1948, helped to arrange the first truce, and then worked to produce a plan for a political settlement. His first proposal, delivered at the end of June, suggested a union of Palestine and Transjordan, under Abdullah’s crown, with the Jewish and Arab parts retaining autonomy; it proposed to assign the Negev to the Arabs and Galilee to the Jews, to place Jerusalem under Arab control with autonomy for the Jewish inhabitants, and to make Haifa a free port and Lydda a free airport. Both sides rejected it. The Israelis objected to the loss of the Negev and Jerusalem and the union with Transjordan, and the Arab governments objected to any recognition of a Jewish state.

His second set of recommendations, completed on September 16 as part of a progress report, revised the earlier ideas. It dropped the proposed union between the Jewish state and Transjordan, though it still recommended that the Arab part of Palestine be merged with Transjordan, proposed that Galilee be allocated to Israel and the Negev to the Arab side, and called for Jerusalem to be placed under United Nations control with municipal autonomy for Jewish and Arab communities, and for Haifa and the Lydda airport to be free zones. It proposed that the final boundaries be determined by the United Nations. Bernadotte also gave strong attention to the refugees. He argued that Palestinians who had fled from their homes should be given the right to return at the earliest possible date and to receive compensation for the property of those who chose not to return, and he wrote that it would be an offense against the principles of elemental justice to deny the right of return to these victims of the conflict while Jewish immigrants were flowing into Palestine. His observations, which became a basis for the General Assembly’s resolution three months later, are analyzed in the Primary Source Window.

Bernadotte’s approach has been judged in different ways. Some historians emphasize his moral seriousness and the realism of his proposals, in particular on the refugee issue. Others point out that his proposals ignored the facts on the ground: Israel had by then established its government and army and was unwilling to give up territory it controlled or to accept the loss of the Negev. His plans also were opposed by hard-line groups and, by some accounts, by Ben-Gurion and his colleagues. They illustrate the difficulty of any plan that required one of the parties to give up what it had won in battle.

Section 61.40

Bernadotte’s Assassination and the Continuation of UN Mediation

On September 17, 1948, the day after he completed his report, Bernadotte was shot dead in Jerusalem as his convoy was driving through the Katamon neighborhood in the Israeli-held part of the city. The French officer traveling with him, Colonel André Serot, was killed as well. The attack was carried out by a group of Lehi members who acted under the name “Front of the Homeland,” and its perpetrators believed that his plan, particularly on Jerusalem and the Negev, threatened Israel’s interests. Lehi’s leaders, among them Nathan Yellin-Mor and Yitzhak Shamir, were not tried for the killing during the war, and the assassins were not publicly identified until later.

The international reaction was one of shock and condemnation. The government of Israel, under Ben-Gurion, denounced the murder, declared Lehi illegal, and arrested hundreds of its members, but it did not prosecute the killers. The Lehi leaders who were convicted of other offences received brief sentences and were pardoned, and Yellin-Mor was elected to the first Knesset while in custody. The government’s determination to eliminate the dissident organizations was one result. Another was a lasting international suspicion of those who had planned the attack. For the United Nations, the episode underscored the dangers of the mediation effort, and the Security Council demanded an inquiry.

Bernadotte’s deputy, the American diplomat Ralph Bunche, took over as acting mediator and continued the work. Bunche, a professor of political science and an experienced official, proved to be skillful and persistent, and in 1949 he mediated the armistice negotiations on Rhodes, for which he received the Nobel Peace Prize in 1950. The shift in mediation, from a plan for a comprehensive political settlement to a step-by-step effort to end the fighting through military armistices, reflected the changing balance of forces. It succeeded in ending the war but not in solving the issues Bernadotte had raised.

Section 61.41

United Nations General Assembly Resolution 194 and the Refugee Question

On December 11, 1948, meeting in Paris, the General Assembly adopted Resolution 194 (III), which dealt with a range of questions. It established a Conciliation Commission for Palestine, with representatives of the United States, France, and Turkey, to assist the parties in reaching a final settlement, and it resolved that Jerusalem should be placed under effective United Nations control and that the holy places should be protected, with access for all. It also contained, in paragraph 11, the passage on the refugees that has since been a central text. It resolved that refugees wishing to return to their homes and live at peace with their neighbors should be permitted to do so at the earliest practicable date, and that compensation should be paid for the property of those choosing not to return and for loss or damage to property under principles of international law or equity. It instructed the Conciliation Commission to facilitate repatriation, resettlement, economic and social rehabilitation, and the payment of compensation.

The resolution was adopted by a large majority, with the Arab states voting against it, in part because it implied negotiation with Israel. Its terms have been interpreted in contrasting ways. Palestinians and many other observers have read paragraph 11 as recognition of a right of return that Israel has been obliged to honor. Israel, and some legal commentators, have noted that the General Assembly’s resolutions are recommendations, that the wording says that refugees “wishing to return” should be permitted to do so, but only if they are willing to live at peace with their neighbors, and that it also provides for compensation and resettlement; Israeli officials argued from the beginning that return should be considered only as part of a general peace. Both readings use the same text, which shows how the question became a political and legal dispute from its first articulation.

In practical terms, the resolution established three things: a principle that the refugees’ claims should be addressed, a body charged with addressing them, and a link between the refugee question and a general settlement. It did not specify how many refugees should return or in what time, and it contained no mechanism of enforcement. Subsequent General Assemblies reaffirmed it repeatedly. It is a primary source for students of this chapter, and the Primary Source Window gives it fuller treatment.

Section 61.42

Refugee Relief, Lausanne, and the Failure of Comprehensive Peace Negotiations

The relief of refugees and the search for peace proceeded on different tracks. In November 1948, the General Assembly established the United Nations Relief for Palestine Refugees, a temporary body that provided food and shelter with the help of voluntary agencies. The Conciliation Commission opened a conference in Lausanne, Switzerland, in the spring of 1949, with Israel and the Arab states participating separately. On May 12, the parties signed a protocol agreeing to use the partition map of 1947 as a basis for discussion, a document that Israel signed in part to obtain admission to the United Nations a day earlier, on May 11, 1949. But the substantive discussions failed. Israel insisted that the refugee problem could be addressed only in a general peace, that the armistice lines should be the basis of future borders, and that most of the refugees should be settled in the Arab states; in the summer, it offered to accept a limited number, about 100,000 by some accounts including those who had already returned or were reunited with their families, as part of a general settlement. The Arab states demanded that Israel accept the principle of return first and that it withdraw to the partition lines, and they were unwilling to negotiate peace separately from the refugees’ rights. The Commission left in September having achieved no comprehensive result.

The failure of Lausanne turned attention to relief and economic measures. The Economic Survey Mission, sent by the Conciliation Commission, recommended public works programs to employ refugees and help integrate them in the economies of their host countries. On December 8, 1949, in Resolution 302 (IV), the General Assembly created the United Nations Relief and Works Agency for Palestine Refugees in the Near East, UNRWA, which began operations in May 1950. Its mandate combined direct relief and works programs, and it was originally conceived as a temporary organization. The Palestinians and the Arab states saw the emphasis on resettlement and public works with suspicion, fearing that it was meant to replace the political claim to return with an economic solution, while Western governments regarded the arrangement as the best available way to prevent a humanitarian disaster.

This division shows why the three tracks should be distinguished. Humanitarian relief, undertaken by UNRWA and other agencies, addressed immediate needs but did not resolve legal status. Political mediation, undertaken by Bernadotte, Bunche, and the Conciliation Commission, sought a territorial and political settlement but could not overcome the parties’ differences. The legal claims, based on Resolution 194, were widely recognized but never implemented. The persistence of the refugee question, and of the institution created to help the refugees, is among the most important long-term consequences of 1948.

Division VIII

The Armistice Agreements and the Regional Political Settlement

Four bilateral military agreements that ended the principal fighting and fixed lines that were not peace borders.

Section 61.43

The Egyptian–Israeli Armistice and the Formation of the Gaza Strip

Armistice negotiations between Israel and Egypt opened on the island of Rhodes in January 1949, under the chairmanship of Ralph Bunche, after Egypt accepted a cease-fire in the wake of Operation Horev. Egypt, which had been the strongest Arab participant, was the first to negotiate, and its agreement set a pattern for the others. The Egyptian delegates accepted the principle that the agreement would be a military one and not a recognition of Israel. The agreement, signed on February 24, 1949, established a demarcation line approximately along the international boundary between Egypt and Palestine in the south, with the Gaza area to the north remaining in Egyptian hands, and provided for a demilitarized zone around al-Auja (Nitzana) on the Sinai border, for the withdrawal of Egyptian troops from the besieged Faluja pocket, and for a Mixed Armistice Commission under United Nations chairmanship to supervise the agreement.

The result was the creation of the Gaza Strip, a narrow coastal territory of about 360 square kilometers around the city of Gaza, running some 40 kilometers along the Mediterranean. It had had a population of about 70,000 to 80,000 before the war, and by the end of 1949 it contained perhaps 200,000 refugees in addition, so that its population approximately tripled in the space of a few months. Egypt did not annex the Strip; it administered it through a military governor, treating it as territory of Palestine under Egyptian control, and it maintained restrictions on the movement of its Palestinian inhabitants. For the refugees, the Strip was a place of dense and poor camps, with little arable land and few employment opportunities, dependent upon relief. The Gaza Strip became one of the most densely settled and most politically significant areas of the Palestinian refugee population, a status it has retained.

Section 61.44

The Lebanese–Israeli Armistice, March 1949

Negotiations between Israel and Lebanon took place at Ras al-Naqoura on the coast, on the border, and the agreement was signed on March 23, 1949. Lebanon was the weakest of the Arab combatants and had played the smallest military role, and its government wanted to end its involvement quickly, in part because of the country’s internal confessional balance and the fears of the Maronite leadership of a conflict that might draw Lebanon into broader regional issues. The agreement set the armistice line on the international frontier, which had been drawn in 1923 between the French and British mandates, and it required Israel to withdraw from the Lebanese villages it had taken in Operation Hiram. Both parties committed themselves to avoid military action, and a Mixed Armistice Commission was set up.

The agreement was among the simplest, since it did not involve territorial changes, and the Lebanese border was, for many years, the quietest of Israel’s frontiers. Its political context, however, was complicated. Lebanon received about 100,000 Palestinian refugees, a substantial burden for a country with a population of about 1.2 million and a delicately balanced confessional system, and the government did not grant most of them citizenship. This early decision had lasting effects, which Chapter 68 describes, on the country’s politics and the status of the Palestinians there.

Section 61.45

The Jordanian–Israeli Armistice and the Division of Jerusalem

The agreement with Transjordan, signed on April 3, 1949, was the most politically consequential for the territorial outcome. It followed secret and direct contacts between Israeli officials and King Abdullah’s representatives, including a local cease-fire in Jerusalem negotiated in November 1948 by the military commanders Moshe Dayan and Abdullah al-Tall, as well as the formal discussions on Rhodes. The agreement drew the line between the two armies across the West Bank, and it provided that Israel would receive a strip of territory in the area known as the Little Triangle, containing about twenty Arab villages, in exchange for Iraqi troops’ withdrawal and the transfer of their sector to the Arab Legion. Thus Iraq was able to leave the war without signing a document of its own.

In Jerusalem, the agreement formalized the city’s division. The Old City and the eastern neighborhoods were held by Jordan, and the western and southern neighborhoods by Israel. A strip of no-man’s-land separated them, which was crossed at the Mandelbaum Gate by diplomats and some others, and Israel retained an enclave on Mount Scopus with the Hebrew University and Hadassah Hospital, which was to be supplied under United Nations supervision. Jews were excluded from the Old City and the Jewish Quarter, and Jerusalem’s Jewish holy sites, including the Western Wall, were inaccessible, in violation, according to Israelis, of the agreement’s provision for access to the holy places. The Arab inhabitants of West Jerusalem, who had numbered tens of thousands, were gone.

Neither side regarded the arrangement as final. The General Assembly, in Resolution 303 of December 9, 1949, reaffirmed its desire to place Jerusalem under an international regime, but Israel nevertheless declared Jerusalem its capital in December and transferred its government offices to it, and Jordan, in 1950, formally annexed the West Bank, including East Jerusalem, a step recognized by very few states. The status of Jerusalem therefore remained disputed among three positions: the international regime in the UN resolutions, Israel’s claim to the city, and the Jordanian and Palestinian claims. Division IX returns to the consequences.

Section 61.46

The Syrian–Israeli Armistice and the Limits of the 1949 Agreements

Talks between Israel and Syria began later, in April 1949, at a bridge on the Jordan River (the Gesher Benot Ya’akov), and concluded with an agreement signed on July 20 near Mahanayim. Syria’s government had changed in March, when Colonel Husni al-Za’im seized power in a military coup, and the new regime was more open to negotiation, though the discussions were fraught. The armistice line followed the 1923 international boundary between Palestine and Syria, with the Syrian army withdrawing from the area it held at Mishmar HaYarden. The territory evacuated by Syria and some adjacent land were designated demilitarized zones, in which neither party could station military forces but whose sovereignty was left undefined.

The ambiguity of the demilitarized zones was one of the principal weaknesses of the Syrian armistice. The two states disagreed about the status of the zones, the rights of the Arab cultivators who lived in them, the use of land and water resources, and the right of Israel to drain the Huleh swamps and use the waters of the Jordan River. These issues caused frequent incidents in the 1950s and led to the escalation discussed in later chapters. Iraq, as noted, did not conclude an armistice and technically remained in a state of war with Israel, as did Saudi Arabia and several other Arab states that had sent token forces or none at all.

The four agreements ended the main interstate fighting, but they were not peace treaties. Their texts stated that they were military agreements, that their purpose was to facilitate transition to permanent peace, and that the armistice lines were not to prejudice the rights, claims, or positions of either party in a final political settlement. The Arab delegates had insisted on this language and the Israeli delegates accepted it, in part because it also served their interests. The agreements did not address the refugees, they did not recognize Israel, and they left unresolved the status of Jerusalem and the final borders. The distinction between an armistice and a peace is one that students must keep in view whenever they encounter references to the 1949 lines.

Section 61.47

The Green Line, Territorial Control, and the Unestablished Palestinian State

The territorial outcome of the war differed from the partition recommendation in several ways. Resolution 181 had allotted about 56 percent of Mandate Palestine to the Jewish state, about 43 percent to the Arab state, and about 1 percent to an international zone around Jerusalem. By the end of the war, Israel controlled about 78 percent of the territory. It held the whole of the Galilee, which the partition plan had assigned to the Arab state, the coastal strip from Jaffa northward, the towns of Lydda and Ramle, the Beersheba region, and the western part of Jerusalem. The remaining 22 percent was divided between Jordan, which controlled the West Bank, about 5,800 square kilometers, including East Jerusalem and the Old City, and Egypt, which administered the Gaza Strip, about 1 percent. The armistice line that divided Israel from these areas is the Green Line, named after the color pencil used to draw it on the maps at Rhodes.

The Palestinian Arab state recommended by the General Assembly was not established. Several factors explain this outcome, and students should consider them together. The Palestinian leadership and the Arab states rejected partition and did not prepare for an Arab state on the plan’s terms. The Palestinian community lacked the military and administrative strength to defend itself, and its institutions collapsed during the war. The Arab states that intervened had their own aims: Transjordan sought to annex the territory it held, Egypt sought to keep control of the Gaza area but not to incorporate it, and none was ready to support independent Palestinian rule. Israel had no interest in a Palestinian state on the territory it did not control, and its leaders regarded an understanding with Abdullah as preferable. Great powers did not intervene to impose the plan.

The Palestinians’ attempts to establish political institutions were brief. In September 1948, the Arab Higher Committee proclaimed an All-Palestine Government in Gaza, under Egyptian protection, headed by Ahmad Hilmi Abd al-Baqi, and the Mufti presided over a Palestine National Council; it was recognized by most Arab League states but not by Transjordan, and it had no army, revenue, or territory of its own, and it faded in the following years. In December 1948, a conference of Palestinian notables meeting at Jericho, under Abdullah’s sponsorship, called for the union of Arab Palestine with Transjordan and acclaimed him as king; the move was opposed by many Palestinians and by other Arab governments but was carried out, and in April 1950 the West Bank was formally annexed. The Green Line was thus both the border of the Israeli state as it existed after the war, and the dividing line between two Arab administrations that had absorbed what remained of the Palestinian territory.

Division IX

State Formation, Historical Memory, and the Unresolved Conflict

The institutional, political, and interpretive consequences of 1948–1949.

Section 61.48

Israel, Palestinian Political Dispossession, and the Transformation of the Arab States

In the year after the armistices, Israel consolidated its institutions. It held elections for a Constituent Assembly on January 25, 1949, which became the first Knesset; Chaim Weizmann was elected president and Ben-Gurion’s party, Mapai, formed the government. On May 11, 1949, the General Assembly admitted Israel as a member of the United Nations by Resolution 273, with Israel declaring its acceptance of the obligations of the Charter. The new state absorbed hundreds of thousands of Jewish immigrants, from the displaced persons camps of Europe and, increasingly, from Arab countries, so that the Jewish population roughly doubled within three years; many were housed in properties abandoned by Palestinians. The state had a unified army, a functioning civil administration, and a growing economy, though it faced severe shortages and a heavy defense burden. It also had a substantial Arab minority governed by military administration.

The Palestinians were politically dispossessed. They had no state, their leadership was divided and discredited, their population was dispersed in the West Bank, Gaza, and neighboring countries, and the territory under their control had been absorbed by Jordan and Egypt. The Mufti’s authority declined; the All-Palestine Government had little power. For the next decade Palestinian political activity occurred mainly through broader Arab movements, such as Arab nationalism and communism, until the emergence of independent organizations in the 1950s and 1960s, the subject of later chapters.

For the Arab states, the defeat was an enduring shock. In Egypt, the prime minister Nuqrashi was assassinated in December 1948 and the Muslim Brotherhood’s founder Hasan al-Banna was killed in February 1949, in a sequence of violence that was partly related to the war; officers who fought in Palestine, with the experience of Faluja, began forming the group that later carried out the 1952 revolution, a subject of Chapter 62. In Syria, the coup of March 1949 opened a period of military intervention. In Jordan, Abdullah’s absorption of the West Bank gave the state a large Palestinian population, and he was assassinated in Jerusalem in 1951 by a Palestinian. In Iraq and Lebanon, too, the war and the refugees affected politics. The conventional view that defeat in Palestine discredited the old regimes and prepared the way for the coups and revolutions of the 1950s is broadly accepted, though historians emphasize that other pressures, including social inequality, the British presence, and generational change, were also at work.

Section 61.49

Independence, Nakba, and the Historiography of 1948

The historiography of 1948 is a field in which the interpretation of events has been inseparable from national identity. For decades, the standard Israeli account portrayed the war as a defensive struggle by a small and vulnerable community against overwhelming Arab forces, from which the new state emerged by a combination of heroism and necessity, and it explained the Palestinian exodus as the result of Arab leaders’ orders and fear of the war. The standard Palestinian and Arab account portrayed 1948 as a catastrophe imposed on the Palestinians by Zionism and imperialism, involving a planned expulsion and the theft of their country. Each version contained documented elements, and each omitted or minimized evidence that did not fit.

The opening of archives in the 1980s changed the debate. A group of Israeli scholars, often called the “new historians,” among them Benny Morris, Avi Shlaim, Simha Flapan, and Ilan Pappé, used Israeli documents to question parts of the traditional narrative. They argued that the Palestinian exodus was not mainly the result of Arab orders, that expulsions had occurred, that Israeli policy had prevented the refugees’ return, and that the balance of forces and Arab aims were different from the traditional picture; Shlaim, in particular, argued that Abdullah and the Zionist leadership had reached a tacit understanding. These conclusions have been disputed, and they have been developed in different directions. Morris, who emphasized the war’s contingencies and located the origins of the refugee problem in the war and in the dynamics of fear, later argued that a policy of transfer was widely accepted among Zionist leaders; Pappé characterized the events as ethnic cleansing carried out according to a plan; critics, such as Efraim Karsh, argued that the new historians overstated Israel’s responsibility. Palestinian historians, including Walid Khalidi, Nur Masalha, and Salman Abu Sitta, have emphasized the evidence of planning and the reconstruction of destroyed villages through maps, memorial books, and oral histories. Arab and other scholars have also examined Arab states’ politics, with limited access to their archives.

A responsible reading distinguishes the areas of substantial agreement from those of continuing dispute. There is broad agreement on the main sequence of events; on the scale of Palestinian displacement, of the order of 700,000 people; on the fact that expulsions occurred in a number of places and that other people fled from fighting and fear; on the Israeli decision not to allow return and on the destruction of many villages; on the occurrence of massacres of civilians in several places, and of Jewish civilians and prisoners in others, such as at Kfar Etzion and the Hadassah convoy; on the lack of a unified Arab command; and on the superior organization of Israeli forces after the first weeks. There is continuing disagreement about whether Plan Dalet was a plan of expulsion, about the intentions of Ben-Gurion and other leaders, about the weight of different causes in different places, about the nature of Abdullah’s contacts with the Zionists, and about the number of victims in particular incidents, such as the killings at Tantura on May 22–23, 1948, whose scale and character remain contested in research and in public debate. These disagreements should not be treated as symmetrical in every case, since the evidence for some claims is far stronger than for others. The Israeli state archives, moreover, have not been uniformly open, and some documents opened earlier have been reclassified, while Arab archives are mostly closed, so that the evidence from each side remains incomplete.

Section 61.50

The Legacy of 1948–1949 and the Unresolved Problems of Peace

The events of 1948 and 1949 produced a new political order in Palestine and in the Arab world. A Jewish state, Israel, was established and survived the war; its society was transformed by war, mass immigration, and the absence of most of the Arab population that had lived in the territory. Palestinian Arab society was shattered. Most of its urban and much of its rural population had been displaced, hundreds of communities had been destroyed or emptied, and a population of refugees and a minority of Palestinians within Israel had come into being. The territory that the United Nations had proposed for an Arab state was divided between Jordan and Egypt, and the Arab state itself was never established. The new boundaries, the Green Line, were military armistice lines, not agreed political borders.

The reasons for the outcome in 1949, an armistice but not a peace, can now be summarized. Israel, having achieved victory and territory, saw no reason to make concessions on borders or refugees without peace and recognition. The Arab states, having lost the war, were unwilling to recognize Israel or to concede what they saw as the Palestinians’ rights, and their governments feared domestic reactions; they also did not agree among themselves. The Palestinians lacked a state or a unified leadership able to negotiate. The United Nations had recommended partition but could not enforce it, and its mediators could do no more than end the fighting. The great powers had interests in the region but did not impose a settlement. The armistice agreements, by design, left open the questions of borders, refugees, and Jerusalem, and those questions remained the substance of the conflict.

The consequences extended beyond 1949. The refugee population and its institutions, such as UNRWA, became a lasting feature of the region. The question of the return, the status of Jerusalem, the borders, and the recognition of Israel and Palestinian rights remained subjects of diplomacy and war in the decades that followed, as Chapters 62 through 72 show. In the Arab world, the defeat became a reference point for debates about the failure of the old order, which shaped political movements across the region. In Israel and in Palestinian communities, 1948 became a foundational event with different meanings. That these meanings coexist, and that the historical evidence supports a more complex account than either alone, is one of the central lessons of the chapter.

Historical Vocabulary
Nakba (النكبة)

The Arabic word nakba means “catastrophe” or “disaster.” In 1948 the Syrian historian Constantin Zurayk used it in a pamphlet that sought to explain the Arab defeat and to call for self-examination in Arab societies. Over the following decades the term came to designate the specifically Palestinian experience of 1948: the displacement and dispossession of most of the Arab population of the territory that became Israel, the destruction of communities, and the loss of a political future in the country.

The word matters because it names a historical process, not only a military outcome. Its use requires care. In Palestinian and much international usage it refers to events of 1947–1949 and, in some uses, to a continuing condition; in Israeli public discourse it has been contested, and many Israelis prefer “War of Independence.” This chapter uses Nakba as a historical term for the Palestinian experience and “War of Independence” and “1948 war” as terms for the broader conflict, while distinguishing documented events from interpretations about causes and intentions.

Historical Vocabulary
Yishuv (היישוב)

The Hebrew word yishuv means “settlement” or “community.” In Zionist and historical usage, “the Yishuv” denotes the organized Jewish community of Palestine before the creation of Israel in 1948, and it is sometimes qualified as the “new Yishuv” to distinguish it from older Jewish communities that existed before modern Zionist immigration. By 1947 it numbered about 630,000 people, with its own political bodies, schools, labor federation, economy, and military organization.

The term matters for this chapter because many of the institutions that became the State of Israel, including the provisional government, the civil service, and the core of the army, were already in existence as institutions of the Yishuv. Students should distinguish the Yishuv before May 14, 1948, from the Israeli state and its citizens afterward, a body that included, from that date, a substantial Arab minority.

Historical Vocabulary
Armistice and Green Line

An armistice is an agreement to cease hostilities, usually made by military commanders or on their behalf, that does not by itself end a state of war or settle the political questions at issue. The four General Armistice Agreements of 1949 each established an armistice demarcation line, which is commonly called the Green Line after the color used to draw it on the maps at Rhodes.

The agreements stated that the lines were dictated by military considerations and that they did not prejudice the rights, claims, or positions of the parties in a later political settlement. They were therefore not recognized international borders in the full sense. The distinction matters because later political discussion often treats the 1949 lines as if they were agreed borders, while in legal and historical terms they were provisional military lines that became, in practice, the de facto boundaries of Israel, Jordan, and Egypt’s administration of Gaza for nearly two decades.

Primary Source Window

General Assembly Resolution 181 (II), November 29, 1947

Source type: Official resolution of an international organization, accompanied by maps and annexes.

Context: Adopted by the General Assembly of the United Nations at Lake Success, New York, by 33 votes to 13, with 10 abstentions, on the recommendation of UNSCOP, the Special Committee on Palestine, whose majority proposed partition with economic union. The vote followed heavy diplomatic lobbying by the United States, the Soviet Union, and the Zionist organizations, and was opposed by all the Arab and Muslim-majority states then members.

Paraphrase of content: The resolution recommends that Britain’s mandate end and that Palestine be partitioned into an independent Arab state and an independent Jewish state, linked by an economic union, with the city of Jerusalem and its surroundings governed by a special international regime administered by the United Nations. It describes the boundaries in technical detail, provides for a United Nations commission to supervise the transition, calls for the withdrawal of British forces by August 1, 1948, and includes provisions guaranteeing minority and religious rights in both states.

Analysis: The resolution shows what the General Assembly proposed and what it did not provide: a method of enforcement. It also shows how much territory and population were involved: the Jewish state as designed would have contained a large Arab minority, a very large Arab minority, roughly 40 percent of its population. As a recommendation, it was not legally binding, and its significance was political and moral. The source does not tell us how the parties would have behaved had it been enforced, nor does it reveal the private views of those who voted for or against it.

Declaration of the Establishment of the State of Israel, May 14, 1948

Source type: Proclamation of statehood; a founding political document.

Context: Read by David Ben-Gurion on the afternoon of May 14, 1948 (5 Iyar 5708) at the Tel Aviv Museum, before members of the People’s Council. It was drafted by a committee and revised in the days before, with disagreements over the inclusion of a reference to God and over the question of borders. It was a political and legal act of the Jewish leadership, made on the eve of the end of the mandate.

Paraphrase of content: The Declaration recalls the historic connection of the Jewish people to the land of Israel and the rise of the Zionist movement; it invokes the Balfour Declaration, the League of Nations mandate, and the catastrophe of the Holocaust; it cites Resolution 181; it proclaims the establishment of a Jewish state to be called Israel; and it promises social and political equality to all inhabitants regardless of religion, race, or sex, and freedom of religion and conscience. It appeals to the Arab inhabitants to preserve peace and participate in the state and offers peace and cooperation to the neighboring states. It does not define borders.

Analysis: The Declaration is valuable evidence of how the Jewish leadership justified its action, of the principles the new state claimed, and of the circumstances of its creation. It is a statement of aspiration and legitimacy and not a description of what happened; its promises of equality can be compared with the actual treatment of the Arab population, including the expulsions, the property laws, and military government. It does not show how Palestinians or the Arab states regarded the proclamation, or how its drafters expected the war to unfold.

Primary Source Window

Plan Dalet (Plan D), March 1948

Source type: Military planning document; internal and classified at the time.

Context: A general operational plan of the Haganah staff, prepared under Yigael Yadin and completed in early March 1948, with orders to brigade commanders issued in the following weeks. It built on earlier plans of the Haganah and was designed for the moment when British forces left and Arab armies were expected to enter. Its Hebrew original is preserved in Israeli military archives and has been published in translation.

Paraphrase of content: The plan sets out its purpose as seizing control of the territory of the Jewish state and of Jewish settlements outside it, and of defeating the expected invasion. It lists tasks such as taking over police stations and installations as the British left, securing roads and communications, and dealing with Arab villages and population centers in the area of operations. In particular, it provides that villages that resist may be destroyed and their population expelled beyond the borders of the state, and that villages that do not resist may be occupied and left under military administration.

Analysis: The plan is a key piece of evidence about Haganah thinking and about the relation between military objectives and the treatment of civilians. Its wording is general and leaves the decision about which villages are resisting to local commanders; this is why historians disagree about how to read it, as the Historical Debate on this plan explains. The document alone does not prove what orders were given in particular operations, and it must be read together with brigade orders, operational reports, and records of the events at specific places.

General Assembly Resolution 194 (III), December 11, 1948

Source type: Resolution of the General Assembly; a statement of international principle.

Context: Adopted in Paris at the third session of the General Assembly, after the death of Bernadotte, on a proposal that built on his report. Egyptian–Israeli General Armistice Agreement, February 24, 1949

Historical Debate

Why Did the Palestinians Leave?

The question: What caused the departure of some 700,000 Palestinians from the territory that became Israel, and who bore responsibility for it? The question is both historical and politically charged, since it bears on the claims of both sides.

Interpretation A — Flight mainly caused by war, with Arab leaders’ role emphasized: The older Israeli account held that the Palestinians left mainly at the urging of their own leaders and of Arab radio broadcasts, or because of the general conditions of war, and that Israel was not responsible for creating the refugee problem. In a later and more qualified form, some historians continue to emphasize the role of the war, of fear, and of the weakness of Palestinian leadership, and that the Arab rejection of partition and invasion started the war.
Interpretation B — Displacement caused substantially by Jewish military operations, expulsion, and policy: Palestinian historians, and since the 1980s many Israeli and other historians, argue that a large part of the departure resulted from military operations, expulsions, fear following massacres, psychological warfare, and the destruction of villages, and that the policy of preventing return made it permanent. Some within this view emphasize a plan or an intention to remove the Arab population; others emphasize the effect of military operations conducted with the expectation that the population would leave.

Evidence: The debate depends on Israeli military and government records, including the June 1948 intelligence assessment; British and UN reports; monitored Arab broadcasts, which historians have examined and which do not show a general call to leave; Arab and Palestinian accounts and oral histories; and records of particular operations. The documents support different explanations in different places.

Why it matters: Under the weight of the evidence, no single cause explains all the departures, and the claim that Palestinians left chiefly because Arab leaders told them to is not supported by the documents. Disagreement continues over how much weight to give to intention and policy as against the dynamics of war, and over how to describe the events. How the question is answered affects the interpretation of Israeli responsibility, Palestinian claims, and the refugee question.

Historical Debate

Plan Dalet: Defensive Military Plan or Blueprint for Expulsion?

The question: Was Plan Dalet an operational plan to secure the Jewish state against an expected invasion, or a plan to remove the Arab population?

Interpretation A — A military plan, not a master plan for expulsion: Historians such as Benny Morris and Israeli military historians argue that the plan was mainly designed for defense against the Arab armies, that its text does not call for a general expulsion, and that the expulsions resulted from the way commanders applied its provisions in the course of fighting, though it contained provisions for destroying villages and expelling inhabitants that did allow this.
Interpretation B — A plan with expulsion as an aim or foreseeable method: Walid Khalidi, Ilan Pappé, and others argue that the plan’s provisions, combined with the ideas of transfer among the leadership and the pattern of operations, show that it was a blueprint for clearing Arab areas; they point to the correspondence between the plan’s provisions and the villages emptied.

Evidence: The text of the plan, its accompanying orders to brigades, the operational records of particular battles, and the statements of leaders. The text is general and the orders for individual operations vary.

Why it matters: If the plan was a blueprint for expulsion, the exodus appears as a result of policy; if it was a military plan that allowed expulsions by local decision, it appears as a result of the dynamics of war and permissive orders. Both views accept that the plan’s provisions allowed the removal of populations.

Multiple Perspectives

The Spring of 1948 from Different Positions

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns identified by historians across many sources. They are not quotations from named individuals. The groups did not have equal power, equal freedom of action, or equal responsibility for events.

A Palestinian villager or townsperson in the area later held by Israel: Life was increasingly dominated by insecurity, the loss of income, and uncertainty about whether leaders or Arab armies could provide protection. News of massacres, the approach of troops, and the collapse of neighboring towns forced decisions about whether to stay, send women and children away, or leave altogether, often with the expectation of a short absence. For many, the result was permanent loss of home, land, and community.
A member of the Yishuv, a settler, soldier, or resident of besieged Jerusalem: The prospect of Arab invasion, the blockade of roads, and the memory of the Holocaust produced a widespread sense of existential danger. Many saw the war as a struggle for survival, and many soldiers were new immigrants. Shortages and casualties were real, and the possibility of defeat was taken seriously. Participants differed in how they viewed the treatment of Arab civilians, and some objected to expulsions and killings.
An Arab government or army officer: Governments faced public demands to act for Palestine, as well as rivalry among themselves, limited military capacity, and divergent political aims. Officers often found that their forces lacked supplies and a coordinated plan. Defeat brought domestic criticism of rulers and armies and contributed to political change.
A British official or soldier preparing to leave: The main concerns were the safe withdrawal of troops, relations with Arab governments, and avoiding further commitments. British forces sometimes intervened to restrain one side or another and sometimes stood aside, and their choices about timing and positions affected the outcome in places such as Haifa and Jaffa.
A United Nations official or observer: The mediators and observers saw the war as an international problem to be moderated by negotiation. They had little power and depended on the cooperation of the parties. Their reports are among the most detailed contemporary evidence of the truces and the refugee crisis, and their efforts were shaped by the interests of the great powers.
Continuity and Change Over Time

Palestine Before and After 1948

What Changed

  • British rule ended and a Jewish state was established with its own army, parliament, and government
  • Most of the Arab population of the territory that became Israel was displaced, and hundreds of villages and many urban quarters were emptied
  • The territory was divided among Israel, Jordan (West Bank), and Egypt (Gaza Strip), and Jerusalem was divided
  • The Palestinian national movement lost its leadership structure, its institutions, and its territorial base
  • A Palestinian refugee population and an international relief system appeared
  • Several Arab governments faced defeat and political crisis

What Stayed the Same

  • The land remained the object of competing national claims, now with different institutions and populations
  • Jerusalem remained a contested holy city
  • The Arab states’ dependence on external powers and their rivalries continued
  • The international community remained involved but without a means to impose a settlement
  • Arabic-speaking Palestinians continued to live in the region, in refugee communities, in the West Bank and Gaza, and as a minority in Israel

Who experienced change most directly: Palestinians who lost homes and Jewish immigrants who found new homes in the same period; those who stayed in place experienced more continuity, though under a new political order. Why the pattern matters: The year 1948 was a radical break for the demography and politics of the country, but many underlying conflicts continued, which helps explain the persistence of the dispute.

Cause and Effect

The Outcome of 1948: Israel, Nakba, and an Unresolved Conflict

Long-term causes

The growth of Zionism and Palestinian nationalism under the mandate; the unresolved commitments of British policy; the Holocaust and Jewish displacement; the Arab revolt and the weakening of Palestinian institutions; the rivalries and limited capacities of the Arab states.

Short-term causes

Resolution 181 and its rejection by Arab leaders; British withdrawal without a transfer of authority; lack of an enforcement mechanism; the road war and breakdown of order; Haganah offensives after March 1948; the declaration of the state; the Arab invasion.

Immediate effects

The establishment and survival of Israel; displacement and dispossession of Palestinians; the territorial division among Israel, Jordan, and Egypt; the armistice agreements; the creation of UN bodies for mediation and relief.

Long-term consequences

A durable refugee question; unresolved borders and Jerusalem; Arab political upheaval; the transformation of Israeli and Palestinian societies; the central place of 1948 in national memory on both sides.

Historical complexity: The outcome cannot be reduced to a single cause, whether the Arab rejection of partition, the Zionist leadership’s aims, British policy, the weakness of the Arab armies, or great-power choices. It resulted from their interaction with the choices of each community, and none of it was inevitable at the time of the vote in November 1947.

Everyday Life and Social History

Households in War and Displacement

For most inhabitants of Palestine, 1948 was experienced in the daily routines of households rather than in headlines. In Arab villages, the agricultural year, including the olive harvest and the grain and fruit seasons, was interrupted by fighting, curfews, and the closure of roads and markets, and families faced decisions about whether to keep working their land, send members to safer places, or leave. In coastal towns such as Jaffa, whose citrus groves had been a major export industry, the economy collapsed as shipping and trade stopped. In Jewish Jerusalem, residents lived through a siege that brought rationing of food and water, cooking in improvised ways, and the use of shelters. In many cases, children and the elderly suffered most from shortages and disease.

Women’s experiences were often central and are less well recorded in official documents. In the exodus, women often carried children and belongings on foot, kept households together, and organized shelter and food in the camps; in the Yishuv, women served in the Palmach and the army, worked in kibbutz and city economies, and cared for families under strain. Historians rely on oral histories, diaries, letters, and photographs to reconstruct these experiences, and note the limits of sources produced mainly by officials and military men.

After the war, Jewish immigrants from camps in Europe and from Arab countries were housed in transit camps and often in abandoned Arab houses, while Palestinian refugees lived in tents and rented rooms. These contrasting experiences of arrival and loss in the same places are a central part of the social history of 1948.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced the history of the war of 1947–1949 and its consequences, distinguishing the civil war that began after the partition vote of November 29, 1947, from the interstate war that began when Arab armies entered Palestine on May 15, 1948. The collapse of the mandate, the failure of the United Nations to implement partition, and the unequal capacities of the two communities’ institutions shaped the first phase, and by the time the State of Israel was proclaimed, Haganah operations had secured most of the territory allotted to the Jewish state and parts beyond it.

The Yishuv entered the conflict with an embryonic government, a unified though not monolithic military organization, a modern economic base, and an effective system of mobilization. The Palestinian Arab community, by contrast, had lost much of its leadership in the revolt of 1936–1939, was divided politically, depended on local militias and volunteers, and lacked a regular army or central administration. The Arab states, which did not intervene until May, had divergent aims, limited preparations, and no joint command. These differences help explain the course of the war without making any outcome inevitable.

The military narrative shows a progression. From December to March, communities fought over roads, mixed neighborhoods, and convoys. In April the Haganah, adopting Plan Dalet and launching operations such as Nachshon, shifted to territorial control, and the fall of Haifa, Tiberias, Jaffa, Safed, and Beisan removed the Palestinian urban centers. After May 15, the Arab armies were stopped on most fronts, the truces allowed Israel to rearm and reorganize more effectively than its opponents, and the offensives of July, October, and December 1948 extended Israeli control to the Negev, central Galilee, and the southern coast, with a final operation reaching the Gulf of Aqaba in March 1949.

The Palestinian Nakba was not a side effect of the military story but a central part of it. Some 700,000 to 750,000 Palestinians left or were driven from their homes, most before or independently of the Arab invasion. The documents show a variety of causes: direct expulsion in places such as Lydda and Ramle, flight from combat as at Haifa, fear following massacres such as Deir Yassin and, later, those in the Galilee, psychological warfare, and the collapse of daily life. The claim that most Palestinians left on the orders of their leaders is not supported by the evidence. Hundreds of villages were emptied, property was transferred to the state and to new immigrants, and return was prevented by government decision and military practice.

Refugees found shelter in the West Bank, Gaza, Lebanon, Syria, and Transjordan, where relief organizations and then UNRWA provided assistance in conditions of great hardship. A minority of about 150,000 Palestinians remained in Israel under military government, some of them displaced from their original villages. The experiences of refugees and of those who remained differed by class, gender, and place, and their memories became a central part of Palestinian identity.

International diplomacy ran on three tracks that should be distinguished. The United Nations, lacking enforcement power, could recommend and mediate but not impose. Count Bernadotte’s plans, which recognized the refugees’ claim to return, were overtaken by events and by his assassination. Resolution 194 articulated principles on refugees and Jerusalem that were interpreted differently by the parties, the Lausanne conference failed to produce a comprehensive peace, and relief was institutionalized in UNRWA while the political questions remained unresolved.

The four armistice agreements of 1949 ended the main fighting but were not peace treaties. They created lines, the Green Line among them, that differed markedly from the partition map: Israel held about 78 percent of Mandate Palestine, Jordan held the West Bank and East Jerusalem, Egypt administered Gaza, and Jerusalem was divided. The Palestinian Arab state recommended in 1947 was not established, because of the choices of the Palestinian and Arab leaderships, Israeli and Transjordanian interests, the collapse of Palestinian institutions, and the absence of international enforcement.

The consequences were far-reaching. Israel became a member of the United Nations, consolidated its institutions, and absorbed large-scale immigration, while the Palestinians were left without sovereignty or unified leadership. In the Arab states the defeat contributed to crises that produced assassinations, coups, and revolutions, themes of Chapters 62 and 64. The historiography of 1948 has been transformed by archival access, yet it remains shaped by national narratives, and students must distinguish well-documented events from disputed claims about intentions and responsibility.

The chapter has emphasized that 1948 is both the foundation of the State of Israel, remembered by most Israelis as a war of independence, and the Nakba, remembered by Palestinians as the loss of their homeland. Historical analysis does not require choosing between these memories as experiences, but it does require careful attention to the evidence, to differences among places and phases, and to the difference between an armistice and a peace.

Reconstructing the Fate of One Town or Village

This project asks you to study the history of a single Palestinian community in 1947–1949, using the library’s sources to reconstruct what happened and to compare different kinds of evidence.

  1. Choose a community. Select a town or village discussed in the chapter or listed in a village survey.
  2. Establish a baseline. Use mandate-era statistics and maps to describe its population, economy, and location.
  3. Reconstruct events. Use military histories, UN reports, and oral histories or memorial books to create a timeline of events and identify the date and circumstances of its depopulation or survival.
  4. Compare sources. Identify where Israeli, Palestinian, British, and international sources agree and differ, and what each can and cannot establish.
  5. Trace the aftermath. Describe what happened to the people, the land, and the buildings, and where the inhabitants went.
  6. Write your findings. Produce a 4–6 page essay or a map-based presentation with sources cited, noting uncertainties.

Library resources to use: Village surveys, military histories, UN documents, oral-history collections, and the MGU History Library’s reference materials on the Arab–Israeli war (see also Appendix A, Glossary of Regional and Historical Terminology).

Chapter Reflection

Chapter 61 matters because the events of 1947–1949 created conditions that shaped the history of the Arab world for the rest of the century and beyond. They produced a Jewish state in the heart of the region, a Palestinian refugee population and a stateless nation, a new set of borders and administrations, and a lasting dispute over land, return, and Jerusalem. They also changed the politics of every Arab state that took part.

The chapter reveals how large historical outcomes emerge from the interaction of institutions, decisions, and contingency. The weakness of the mandate’s end, the unequal organization of the two communities, the aims of the Arab governments, and the choices of military commanders each contributed. It also shows how a population can be changed by war, and why historians need to distinguish between events, causes, and later interpretations, and to treat the displacement of civilians as a central subject of history rather than a footnote.

Later chapters build on this foundation. Chapter 62 will examine how the defeat in Palestine contributed to the Egyptian revolution and to Nasser’s pan-Arabism; Chapter 63 will show how the Arab world’s decolonization movements unfolded in the same years; Chapters 64 through 66 will follow the revolutionary regimes, the oil economies, and the wars of 1967 and 1973. The refugee question, the status of Jerusalem, and the borders of 1949 recur across Chapters 66 through 72.

Students who understand the distinctions made in this chapter, between the civil war and the interstate war, between expulsion and flight, between armistice and peace, and between documented events and contested interpretations, will be better equipped to read the later history of the region critically. Understanding 1948 requires holding in view the experiences of those who built a state and those who lost a homeland, and the evidence that allows historians to reconstruct both.
