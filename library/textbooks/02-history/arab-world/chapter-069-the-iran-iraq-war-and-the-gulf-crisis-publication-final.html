The Iran–Iraq War and the Gulf Crisis

A History of the Arab World: From Ancient Crossroads to the Modern Arab World

Chapter Opening

Between September 1980 and March 2003, the Arab Gulf passed through three wars, a decade of sanctions, and a continuous foreign military presence. Each episode grew out of the one before it. The Iran–Iraq War left Iraq with a vast army and enormous debts; those debts and that army helped produce the invasion of Kuwait; and the defeat of Iraq in 1991 created the sanctions, inspections, and patrols that framed Gulf politics until the invasion of 2003.

This chapter begins not with a battlefield but with a river. The Shatt al-Arab, formed where the Tigris and Euphrates meet before reaching the head of the Persian Gulf, carried the oil exports of Iraq's southern port of Basra and the commerce of Iran's cities of Khorramshahr and Abadan. For decades the question of who controlled it, and on what terms, had been a source of friction between Baghdad and Tehran. In September 1980 that friction became a full-scale war. The conflict that followed lasted eight years and is one of the longest conventional wars of the twentieth century. It was fought with trenches, artillery, armored columns, ballistic missiles, naval mines, and chemical weapons, and it killed and displaced very large numbers of people on both sides of the frontier.

The war did not remain a bilateral contest. Iraq's Arab neighbors in the Gulf, fearful of revolutionary Iran, supplied Baghdad with loans and political support. Syria and Libya sided with Tehran. The Soviet Union, France, and later the United States each pursued their own interests in the conflict. By 1987 the conflict had spread into the waters of the Gulf itself, where attacks on oil tankers drew American warships into direct clashes with Iranian forces. The Iran–Iraq War is therefore a chapter in the history of the Arab world because it reorganized the strategic position of the Arab Gulf monarchies, strengthened and distorted the Iraqi state, and inaugurated the internationalization of Gulf security.

Less than two years after the 1988 ceasefire, Iraqi forces overran Kuwait, a fellow Arab state and Iraq's recent financial supporter. The invasion of August 1990 was the first time in the modern Arab state system that one member of the Arab League had attempted to erase another by force. It divided Arab governments and Arab publics, produced the largest military coalition assembled since the Second World War, and ended with the expulsion of Iraqi forces from Kuwait in February 1991. Yet the war's conclusion did not bring a settled peace. The survival of Saddam Hussein's government, the uprisings that followed Iraq's defeat, and the Security Council's detailed ceasefire terms combined to create a long confrontation that this chapter follows through 1998 and, more briefly, to the invasion of March 2003.

Historians reconstruct this period from an unusually varied body of evidence. Security Council resolutions and the reports of United Nations missions provide authoritative documentary anchors. Captured Iraqi government records, now held in several archives, have allowed scholars to examine the internal workings of the Ba'athist state, including the Anfal campaign against Kurdish civilians. Memoirs by participants, journalistic reporting, survivor testimony, military histories, and statistical studies by international agencies complement these sources. Each has limits. Wartime communiqués on both sides were propaganda instruments; casualty and economic figures remain contested; and some of the most heavily cited statistics about the sanctions era have been challenged by later research. The chapter therefore distinguishes carefully among documented events, estimates, and contested claims.

The chapter also insists on two points of method. First, neither war was inevitable. Each followed from decisions made by identifiable governments responding to particular pressures, and each could have been avoided or ended differently. Second, the Gulf monarchies, Egypt, Syria, and other Arab governments were not passive observers of strategies devised elsewhere. They financed, armed, mediated, hosted, joined, and opposed, according to interests that did not always coincide. The people who lived through these wars, including conscripts, displaced families, Kurdish villagers, Kuwaiti residents under occupation, and Iraqi households under sanctions, were not simply the objects of state decisions, and their experiences form part of the narrative.

The chapter is organized in five divisions. The first examines Saddam Hussein's Iraq and the outbreak of war with Iran in 1980. The second follows the war through attrition, chemical warfare, the Anfal campaign, and the internationalization of the Gulf to the ceasefire of 1988. The third explains the road from that ceasefire to the occupation of Kuwait. The fourth treats the 1991 war and its immediate consequences, including the uprisings in Iraq. The fifth examines sanctions, inspections, and military containment, and ends with the 2003 invasion as a concluding bridge to the later chapters of this textbook. Readers should consult Chapter 64 for the broader history of Ba'athist republics, Chapter 65 for oil and the Gulf monarchies, Chapter 67 for the Iranian Revolution as a movement of political Islam, and Chapter 70 for the post-Cold War peace processes that unfolded alongside these events.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how the Ba'athist state and Saddam Hussein's concentration of power shaped Iraqi decision-making before 1980.
  2. Analyze the competing explanations for Iraq's invasion of Iran in September 1980, distinguishing documented events from debated motives.
  3. Trace the course of the Iran–Iraq War from Iraqi offensive to Iranian counteroffensive, attrition, and the ceasefire of August 1988.
  4. Assess the war's social and economic costs for Iraq, Iran, and neighboring states using critically evaluated evidence.
  5. Describe Iraq's use of chemical weapons and the Anfal campaign, and explain how historians document them.
  6. Explain the roles of the Gulf monarchies and external powers in financing, sustaining, and internationalizing the war.
  7. Analyze the economic, political, and strategic causes of Iraq's invasion of Kuwait, distinguishing grievances from legal justification.
  8. Interpret the Security Council resolutions of 1990–1991 as historical documents, identifying what each authorized and what it did not.
  9. Evaluate the formation, composition, and political significance of the anti-Iraq coalition, including divisions among Arab governments.
  10. Compare the Iran–Iraq War and the 1991 Gulf War in causes, participants, methods, and settlements.
  11. Assess the historical debate over comprehensive sanctions, separating factual disputes from normative judgments.
  12. Connect the 1980–1998 history to the changing U.S. regional role and the 2003 invasion without treating the later war as predetermined.

Historical Context

By the late 1970s the Gulf was the most strategically valuable energy-producing region in the world. The oil price increases of 1973–1974, examined in Chapters 65 and 66, had transferred enormous revenue to the governments of Iraq, Saudi Arabia, Kuwait, the smaller Gulf states, and Iran. Those revenues financed armies, bureaucracies, infrastructure, and welfare programs. They also made each government a more consequential regional actor and a more attractive target for foreign arms sellers and diplomats. After Britain's withdrawal from the Gulf in 1971, regional security rested on an informal balance: Iran under Shah Muhammad Reza Pahlavi was the leading military power and a close partner of the United States, Saudi Arabia was the largest oil exporter, and Iraq was a Soviet-armed Arab republic with ambitions of leadership.

Iraq in this period was ruled by the Arab Socialist Ba'ath Party, which had taken power in a coup in July 1968 (see Chapter 64). Its leadership combined Arab nationalist ideology with a centralized, security-dominated state. Iraq's population was diverse: a Shi'a Arab majority concentrated in the south and Baghdad, a Sunni Arab population in the center and west, a large Kurdish minority in the northeast, and smaller communities including Turkmen, Assyrians and other Christians, Yazidis, and others. Governments in Baghdad had long struggled to integrate these communities into a single political order, and the Kurdish question in particular was tied to Iraq's relations with Iran.

The Iranian Revolution of 1979, treated in Chapter 67, removed the Shah and replaced a pro-Western monarchy with an Islamic republic led by Ayatollah Ruhollah Khomeini. The revolution disrupted Iran's military, alarmed the Gulf monarchies, and raised new questions about the loyalties of Shi'a communities across the region. It is essential to avoid a simple religious reading of what followed. Iraq's ruling party was secular in ideology and its rulers were drawn largely from Sunni Arab networks, but it governed a Shi'a-majority population, and most Iraqi Shi'a fought for Iraq in the war. The conflict was a contest between states with different ideologies and interests, in which sectarian identities were sometimes invoked, but did not determine the sides.

Finally, the international setting mattered. The Cold War was in its last decade. The Soviet invasion of Afghanistan in December 1979 had pushed Washington to strengthen its position in the Gulf, but the United States did not yet maintain large permanent forces in the region. By 1990 the Soviet Union was collapsing and cooperating with Washington in the Security Council, a condition that made the response to Kuwait's invasion possible. These large structural facts framed, but did not dictate, the decisions of Baghdad, Tehran, Riyadh, Kuwait City, and Washington.

Section 69.1

The Ba'athist State, Saddam Hussein, and Iraq's Regional Ambitions

The Iraq that went to war in 1980 was a state of considerable resources and extreme political concentration. The Ba'ath Party seized power in the coup of 17 July 1968, as Chapter 64 explains, and within two weeks its dominant faction had removed its non-Ba'athist partners. Ahmad Hasan al-Bakr became president, but the party's day-to-day security and organizational work fell increasingly to his cousin and deputy, Saddam Hussein, who from 1969 held the vice-presidency of the Revolutionary Command Council. Saddam built his authority through control of the party apparatus and the intelligence services. Alongside the party's own security bureaus, the state maintained several overlapping intelligence and military-intelligence agencies, a party militia known as the Popular Army, and a network of informants. Overlap was deliberate: each body watched the others. Many senior posts were held by men from Saddam's home region around Tikrit and from kin and patronage networks connected to him and to Bakr.

In July 1979 this concentration became formal. Bakr resigned, and Saddam became president. Within days he convened a party meeting at which senior Ba'athists were accused of conspiracy; dozens were arrested and about twenty were executed, according to commonly cited accounts. The purge removed the last leaders with an independent base in the party and sent a message to everyone else. Thereafter the highest decisions, including those on war and peace, rested with a very small circle around the president, and military officers learned that contradicting him was dangerous. This matters for understanding 1980: the invasion of Iran was not the product of a deliberative process that weighed competing assessments, but of the judgment of a leadership that had narrowed its own sources of advice.

Iraq's capacity to act rested on oil. The nationalization of the Iraq Petroleum Company in 1972, followed by the price rises of 1973–1974 and again in 1979, raised Iraq's oil revenues from under a billion dollars annually in the early 1970s to some twenty-six billion by 1980, according to widely cited figures. The government used this income to build roads, power stations, hospitals, and schools; to subsidize food and housing; to expand the state bureaucracy and the military; and to purchase large quantities of Soviet and, increasingly, French and other weapons. A national literacy campaign launched in 1978 earned international recognition. Many Iraqis, particularly in the expanding urban middle class and in the civil service, experienced the 1970s as a period of rising living standards. The same revenue gave the president patronage to distribute, and it freed the state from dependence on taxation, and therefore from any need to bargain with its citizens.

The government's ideology was Arab nationalist, and it presented Iraq as a leader of the Arab world, a claim sharpened by Egypt's separate peace with Israel in 1979 (see Chapter 66), which had removed Cairo from the center of Arab politics and expelled Egypt from the Arab League. Baghdad hosted the 1978 summit that criticized Camp David and positioned itself as a leading voice of Arab rejection. Ba'athist ideology had no easy place for religious or ethnic particularism, and the regime combined incentives with coercion in managing Iraq's communities. Kurdish autonomy, promised in a March 1970 agreement, was never implemented to Kurdish leaders' satisfaction, and fighting resumed in 1974. The state meanwhile pursued Arabization of the oil-rich Kirkuk region. Shi'a religious institutions in Najaf and Karbala, and the Islamic Dawa Party, which organized Shi'a political opposition, were targeted by security services. After disturbances during the 1977 Arba'in pilgrimage, repression intensified. In April 1980 the senior cleric Muhammad Baqir al-Sadr was executed, and Dawa membership was made punishable by death. Tens of thousands of Iraqis whom the state labeled as being of Iranian origin, most of them Shi'a, were deported around the same time, by various estimates.

Historians draw on several kinds of evidence for this period. Party and security records captured after 2003 now allow direct examination of the regime's internal documents and recorded meetings, though they must be read with an awareness that they reflect what officials chose to write down. Memoirs by former officials, accounts by exiles, and reports by human rights organizations add further perspectives. Together they show a state that was neither a mere façade nor a monolith: it combined real developmental achievements, an effective coercive apparatus, and a leader who increasingly regarded both foreign and domestic politics as a struggle against conspiracies.

Section 69.2

The Iranian Revolution, Border Disputes, and the Road to War

The relationship between Baghdad and Tehran had been unstable long before 1979. Its core was the Shatt al-Arab, the river that forms the outlet of Iraq's principal waterway to the Gulf. A 1937 treaty had placed the frontier at the low-water mark on the Iranian bank, giving Iraq control of nearly the whole river except at the Iranian ports of Abadan and Khorramshahr, where the line followed the main channel. Iran under the Shah came to regard this as unjust, and in 1969 it announced that it no longer considered the treaty binding, sending ships through the river under naval escort and backing the Kurdish insurgency in northern Iraq. In 1971 Iranian troops occupied the Greater and Lesser Tunbs without agreement from Ras al-Khaimah and took positions on Abu Musa under a contested arrangement with Sharjah that left competing sovereignty claims unresolved. Iraq broke diplomatic relations in protest, framing the move as an assault on Arab territory. The mutual hostility was tied to Cold War alignments as well: Iran was a U.S. ally, Iraq a Soviet client, though Iraq's ties to Moscow were never exclusive.

In March 1975, at an OPEC meeting in Algiers and with Algerian mediation, Saddam Hussein and the Shah concluded an agreement. Iraq accepted the thalweg, the line of the deepest channel, as the river boundary; Iran halted its support for the Kurdish rebellion, which collapsed within weeks. A treaty and protocols signed in Baghdad in June 1975 gave the arrangement legal form. For Iraq the price was significant, but the agreement ended the Kurdish war and brought a period of calm that lasted until 1979. It also showed how closely Iraq's frontier with Iran and its internal Kurdish question were linked, a connection that would recur.

Historical Vocabulary
Thalweg and the Shatt al-Arab

The thalweg (from German, "valley way") is the line of lowest elevation along a river channel, in practice the line of the deepest, most navigable water. International practice often places a boundary in a navigable river along the thalweg, so that each state has equal access to the main channel. The alternative is a median line equidistant from the banks, or, as in the 1937 treaty, a line on one bank.

The Shatt al-Arab (Persian: Arvand Rud) is the waterway formed where the Tigris and Euphrates meet, flowing roughly two hundred kilometers to the Gulf. Its importance lay in the ports and industrial facilities on or connected to its course, including Basra on the Iraqi side and Abadan and Khorramshahr on the Iranian side. Umm Qasr, by contrast, is accessed through the separate Khawr Abd Allah–Khor al-Zubayr approach, not from the Shatt al-Arab. Iraq had a short coastline but more than one navigable approach to the Gulf; that geography also shaped the later dispute with Kuwait.

Students should note that the thalweg is a physical feature that shifts with silt and dredging, so a thalweg boundary requires technical surveying and continuing cooperation. Disputes over the river were as much about sovereignty and status as about navigation, and both governments treated them as symbols of national dignity.

Primary Source Window

The Algiers Agreement and the 1975 Iraq–Iran Frontier Treaty

Creators: The governments of Iraq and Iran, with Algerian mediation (President Houari Boumédiène) · Date: Joint communiqué, 6 March 1975; Treaty on the State Frontier and Neighbourly Relations and related protocols, signed at Baghdad, 13 June 1975 · Type: Diplomatic agreement and treaty

Archival record: UN Treaty Collection, Iraq–Iran Treaty (1975).

Context: The agreement followed Iraqi concessions on the river boundary and Iranian withdrawal of support for the Kurdish insurgent movement under Mustafa Barzani. It was concluded between Saddam Hussein, then vice-president of Iraq, and Shah Muhammad Reza Pahlavi.

Editorial paraphrase (not a direct quotation): The two governments agreed to carry out a definitive delimitation of their land frontier in accordance with the 1913 Protocol of Constantinople and the work of the 1914 boundary commission, to fix their river frontier along the thalweg, and to restore mutual security and trust along the common border. Each undertook to exercise strict and effective control to prevent subversive infiltration from either side.

Analysis: The text is valuable because it shows what each government considered essential: Iraq obtained an end to Iranian aid for the Kurds, and Iran obtained the thalweg. It also shows the interconnection of frontier and internal security; the same document treats a river line and the prevention of cross-border subversion as a single bargain. Its limitations are those of any bilateral agreement signed under pressure. Iraq's leadership had accepted terms it later described as imposed by weakness, and the Shah's Iran was far stronger than Iraq at the time. A treaty may record a settlement, but it does not guarantee that the weaker party will regard the settlement as legitimate. The text also says nothing about how either government would respond if internal political change altered one party's willingness to honor it, which is exactly what 1979 produced.

Questions for students:

  1. What did each government gain, and what did each concede, in the Algiers settlement?
  2. Who created this document, and what interests did each negotiator represent?
  3. What can historians learn from the linking of border demarcation and counter-subversion commitments?
  4. What can the text alone not tell historians about why Iraq later repudiated it?

The Iranian Revolution altered this balance. The Shah left Iran in January 1979; the monarchy collapsed in February, and the Islamic Republic was proclaimed in April. Iran's army was purged and disorganized; its supply of American spare parts was disrupted, and relations with Washington collapsed after the seizure of the U.S. embassy in November 1979. Khomeini's followers openly denounced the Ba'ath government, and Iranian leaders called on Iraqis to overthrow it. Iraq's rulers, for their part, had been alarmed by the activism of Shi'a opposition groups at home and by disturbances in Najaf and Karbala. They hosted Iranian exiles and Arab separatist groups from Iran's Khuzestan province, and Iran sheltered Iraqi Dawa activists and Kurdish forces. On 1 April 1980 an attempt on the life of Deputy Prime Minister Tariq Aziz at Mustansiriyya University was attributed by Baghdad to Dawa and to Iran, and was followed by the executions and deportations mentioned above. Border shelling grew more frequent. On 17 September 1980 Saddam announced before the National Assembly that Iraq regarded the 1975 agreement as annulled and that the whole Shatt al-Arab was Iraqi.

Iraqi grievances were real in the sense that Iran had violated the spirit of the 1975 accord by encouraging dissent and by its propaganda. But a grievance is not the same as a reason to invade, and historians distinguish the stated causes from the decision's full logic. Iraq's leadership also considered Iran's weakness an opportunity, regarded the revolution as a threat to its own Shi'a-majority population, and sought a role as the leading power of the Gulf and a guardian of the Gulf Arab states. The decision to attack should be understood as a political choice by a particular government, made in a particular moment, not as the necessary result of an ancient Sunni–Shi'a hostility. The two governments' ideological confrontation was between a secular Arab nationalist party-state and a revolutionary Islamic republic. The populations they ruled did not divide neatly along those lines; the Iraqi army that fought Iran consisted largely of Shi'a conscripts, and Iranian Arabs in Khuzestan did not, as Baghdad expected, rise against Tehran.

Historical Debate

Why Did Saddam Hussein Invade Iran in 1980?

The question: It is firmly established that Iraqi forces launched a large-scale air and ground assault on Iran on 22 September 1980, five days after Saddam Hussein publicly cancelled the Algiers Agreement. What historians debate is why the Iraqi leadership took this decision and which factors weighed most heavily.

Interpretation A — Territorial and frontier objectives: Iraq's stated aim was to restore full sovereignty over the Shatt al-Arab and to reverse what it regarded as the humiliating concessions of 1975. On this view, the Iranian revolution presented a chance to correct the settlement while Iran was weak.
Interpretation B — Fear of revolutionary contagion: Scholars emphasizing regime security argue that Saddam regarded Khomeini's appeals to Iraqi Shi'a as a direct threat to the survival of his government, especially after the unrest of 1977 and the Dawa campaign of 1979–1980. The invasion was meant to cripple the revolution before it could spread.
Interpretation C — Opportunism and regional leadership: Others stress that Iran's apparent disintegration offered a rare opportunity, and that Saddam hoped for a quick victory that would give Iraq the position of leading Gulf power, with Arab and international prestige, and perhaps lead to the fall of the Islamic Republic or its reduction to a weaker state.

Evidence: The explanations draw on Saddam's public speeches, Iraqi diplomatic exchanges, memoirs by former Iraqi and Iranian officials, accounts of Iraqi military planning, and later captured records. Public statements emphasize territory and sovereignty but are propaganda in part; internal sources show greater concern for regime security but are fragmentary and sometimes retrospective. Most scholars treat the explanations as overlapping rather than exclusive, differing mainly in emphasis. Iranian rhetoric, border incidents, and Iraqi misjudgment about Iran's capacity to resist all appear in most accounts.

Why it matters: If the war was chiefly territorial, it might have ended when territory was exchanged or recovered. If it was chiefly about regime survival, neither government could easily accept a settlement that left the other in power. The latter reading helps explain why the war lasted so long after Iran had recovered its land in 1982. Debate over motives does not alter the fact that Iraq initiated the large-scale invasion, a point recognized by the United Nations Secretary-General in the 1991 report discussed in Section 69.8.

Section 69.3

Iraq's September 1980 Invasion and the Opening Campaigns

Border clashes escalated through the summer of 1980, with artillery duels near Qasr-e Shirin, Khanaqin, and Mandali, and air raids on frontier towns. On 22 September Iraqi aircraft struck Iranian airbases, including those near Tehran, in an attempt to destroy Iran's air force on the ground as Israel had done in 1967. The attempt largely failed: Iranian aircraft were sheltered and survived to retaliate. At the same time Iraqi ground forces crossed the frontier on several axes. In the north and center they advanced toward Qasr-e Shirin and Mehran. The principal effort was in the south, in the oil-producing Iranian province of Khuzestan, with objectives that included the cities of Khorramshahr and Abadan on the Shatt al-Arab, and the provincial capital Ahvaz.

Iraq's leaders expected a short war. They counted on the disarray of the Iranian armed forces, on the purges and executions of officers since the revolution, and on a supposed readiness of Khuzestan's Arab population to welcome the invaders. The Iraqi army was large and well equipped with Soviet armor and artillery, but its planning was cautious and its operational skill limited. Its columns advanced slowly and halted frequently, giving Iran time to organize. The Iraqi leadership also wished to avoid heavy casualties, which it believed would erode domestic support, and so chose a methodical advance rather than a deep thrust. On 28 September the Security Council adopted Resolution 479 calling for a ceasefire without naming an aggressor or demanding withdrawal. Iraq indicated willingness to stop if Iran recognized its claims; Iran refused to negotiate while Iraqi troops stood on its soil.

The fighting for Khorramshahr, Iran's principal commercial port, became the war's first great urban battle. Iranian defenders, a mixture of regular soldiers, Revolutionary Guards, police, and local volunteers, fought street by street for weeks before the city fell in the last days of October and early November. Iranians remembered it as the place where their resistance hardened, and later named the city "Khuninshahr," the city of blood. Abadan, with its major refinery, was besieged but never taken. Iraqi forces advanced as far as the outskirts of Susangerd and Dezful, but in November they stopped, and by the end of the year the front was stabilizing. Iran's navy carried out a successful attack in late November on Iraq's offshore oil terminals, which halted much of Iraq's exports through the Gulf. The invasion thus gave Iraq a strip of Iranian territory but failed in its larger purpose, and it changed the nature of the dispute. A frontier quarrel had become interstate warfare in which Iran's government could present itself as defending the nation against foreign aggression, which strengthened rather than weakened the revolutionary leadership's hold.

Military geography shaped the course of this phase. Southern Iraq and southwestern Iran form a flat alluvial plain crossed by rivers, canals, and marshes, with few natural defensive lines. Heavy rain turned much of it to mud. Date plantations, irrigation channels, and the built-up areas of the port cities favored defenders, and the Shatt al-Arab itself, a broad water barrier, limited maneuver. These conditions meant that fighting soon came to depend on fortifications, artillery, and the ability to concentrate infantry, features that would dominate the war for years.

Section 69.4

Iran's Mobilization, Iraqi Setbacks, and the Strategic Turning Point, 1981–1982

Iran's response combined three elements. The regular armed forces, damaged by the revolutionary purges, were partly rebuilt as officers were released from prison and returned to command. The Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps (Pasdaran), created in 1979 as a politically reliable force loyal to the new order, expanded quickly and acquired its own ground units. The Basij, a volunteer mobilization organization founded early in the revolutionary period, brought in large numbers of civilians, many of them very young or older than normal conscripts, who were trained briefly and sent to the front. Religious institutions, mosque networks, and the language of martyrdom, drawn from Shi'a tradition, supplied motivation and structure. These forces were not always well coordinated, and there was tension between the regular army and the Guards over strategy, but together they gave Iran a large reserve of manpower and a determination that Iraq's planners had underestimated.

Counteroffensives began in 1981. In September Iranian forces broke the siege of Abadan. In November and the following spring, operations near Bostan and in the Karun River area pushed Iraqi troops back, and in March 1982 an Iranian offensive in the Dezful–Shush region drove Iraqi forces from large areas. The decisive operation was the offensive of late April and May 1982, which ended with the recapture of Khorramshahr on 24 May. A significant number of Iraqi troops were taken prisoner, and the loss was a heavy blow to Iraq's military and to Saddam's prestige. Iraqi forces fell back toward the frontier. In June 1982 Saddam announced that Iraq would withdraw from Iranian territory and offered a ceasefire, and Iraqi troops did leave most of the land they had taken.

The Iraqi government's decision to shift to the defensive coincided with the first serious strains on its war economy. Syria, whose government under Hafez al-Assad was hostile to Saddam and sympathetic to Tehran, closed the Iraqi oil pipeline across its territory in April 1982, and Iraq's exports through the Gulf were already crippled by the loss of its offshore terminals. Iraq turned to its Arab neighbors. Kuwait, Saudi Arabia, and other Gulf states gave financial assistance on a growing scale, and Jordan and Egypt provided transit, supplies, and eventually personnel. For the Gulf monarchies, which had created the Gulf Cooperation Council in 1981 largely in response to the war and the revolution, this was a matter of regional security: an Iranian victory seemed likely to threaten their own positions.

Iran, however, rejected the offer. Having recovered most of its lost territory, its leadership had to decide whether to accept a settlement or to continue. Khomeini and a majority of the leadership decided to go on. Their reasons were several: they believed Iraq was the aggressor and should be punished, that Iran was entitled to reparations, that the war was a means to defeat the Ba'athist government and perhaps to spread the revolution, and that the military momentum favored Iran. There were differing views among Iranian leaders, and some favored a negotiated end to the war, but the position of the Supreme Leader prevailed. In July 1982 Iran launched Operation Ramadan, an offensive toward Basra. It was a failure; Iraqi defenses, now concentrated on their own territory with prepared positions and heavy artillery, held. The distinction between military recovery and political decision is the essential point of this phase. Iran's victories of 1981–1982 were a defensive success; the choice to invade Iraq turned the war into a long contest in which Iran, too, was an initiator of further destruction. As the next section shows, the result was neither the collapse of the Ba'ath government nor an Iranian victory, but years of attrition.

Section 69.5

From Counteroffensive to Attrition: The Long War, 1982–1986

After the failure of Operation Ramadan in July 1982, Iran returned again and again to offensives in the south and center, each aimed at Basra, at the marshlands that protected it, or at the road network that linked Iraqi forces. Operations in October 1982 and in 1983 won small areas of ground. In February 1984 Operation Khaibar seized the Majnoon Islands, marshy territory above one of Iraq's largest known oil reserves, at heavy cost. Further offensives in 1985 failed to cut the Baghdad–Basra road. Iraq, meanwhile, relied on a defensive strategy built around fortified lines, the flooding of approaches, extensive minefields, massed artillery, air power, and, increasingly, chemical weapons. It rebuilt its army, which grew to over a million men by the end of the decade, and drew on the money and supplies of its Arab supporters.

The character of the fighting resembled the static warfare of the First World War more than a modern war of maneuver. Both armies dug trench systems and bunkers, laid wire and mines, and relied on artillery bombardment; armor was hard to use in the flooded terrain of the south. Iran lacked heavy equipment and spare parts because of arms embargoes, and it acquired weapons through a complex and often clandestine network that included Libya, Syria, North Korea, China, and, in smaller quantities and for its own reasons, Israel. In 1985–1986 the United States secretly sold missiles to Iran in the arrangement later known as the Iran–Contra affair, even as it was in other respects moving toward Iraq. Iraq received weapons from the Soviet Union, France, and other suppliers, and, from 1984, when the United States restored diplomatic relations with Baghdad, benefited from American commodity credits and, according to later accounts, shared intelligence about Iranian forces.

Iranian infantry assaults have often been described as "human waves." Scholars regard the term as partly accurate and partly misleading. Iran did send large numbers of lightly armed volunteers and Guards forward, often at night and across marshes where armor could not follow, and casualties were very high. But the attacks were typically planned operations involving engineers, boats, and, in some cases, helicopter or amphibious elements, not simply masses of men advancing without direction. The phrase became part of a narrative, repeated in Iraqi and Western accounts alike, that emphasized fanaticism, and historians caution that it can obscure the planning, the political strategy behind the offensives, and the soldiers' own diverse motives.

The capture of the Faw Peninsula in February 1986 was the most important Iranian gain after 1982. In a surprise amphibious crossing of the Shatt al-Arab, Iranian forces took the town of Faw and the surrounding land, close to the Kuwaiti border and Iraq's remaining sea access. The success alarmed Kuwait and Saudi Arabia, increasing the urgency of their support for Baghdad. It did not, however, produce a decisive result: Iraq built a defensive perimeter that Iran could not break, and a large Iranian offensive against Basra in December 1986 and January 1987 (Operation Karbala-5) reached the outskirts of the city before being halted with great loss. Historians generally conclude that by 1987 neither side had a realistic prospect of the victory it sought. Iraq could not drive Iran from the field; Iran could not break through the defenses of Basra or overthrow Saddam.

Why, then, was no settlement reached? Iraq repeatedly offered to end the war after 1982 and held onto the claim that it was entitled to terms recognizing its frontier demands. The Iranian leadership demanded that Iraq be formally identified as the aggressor, that Saddam Hussein be removed, and that Iraq pay reparations. Neither condition was acceptable to the other side. Iran's leaders regarded the war as proof of the revolution's legitimacy; Iraq's regarded a defeat as a threat to its survival. The two governments also adapted institutionally: Iran created a war economy based on rationing, volunteer mobilization, and the Revolutionary Guards; Iraq built a massive military machine funded by foreign loans. Each of these adaptations created constituencies with an interest in continuing the war and reduced the flexibility of both regimes.

Section 69.6

The War of the Cities and the Social and Economic Cost of Eight Years of Fighting

The war was never confined to the front. In several episodes, in 1984, 1985, 1987, and most intensely in February–April 1988, Iraq and Iran attacked each other's cities with aircraft and surface-to-surface missiles. Iraq fired modified Soviet Scud missiles, with extended range, at Tehran, Qom, and other cities; Iran responded with its own missiles and air attacks on Baghdad and other Iraqi cities. The attacks killed civilians and damaged residential districts, power plants, and industrial sites. Their military effect was small, but their psychological effect was large, and in 1988 many residents fled Tehran. Both governments also struck at oil facilities, ports, and refineries, treating the enemy's economy as a legitimate target.

The war's human costs are difficult to measure precisely. Both governments manipulated figures for propaganda, and neither released complete and independently verified statistics. Most scholarly estimates place total deaths, military and civilian, on both sides in the hundreds of thousands, with some higher estimates exceeding a million; far larger numbers were wounded, disabled, or displaced. Prisoners of war numbered in the tens of thousands on each side, and exchanges, mediated in part by the International Committee of the Red Cross, continued for years after the ceasefire. The figures cited in official commemorations, such as Iran's lists of "martyrs," and in Iraqi state media should be treated as claims by interested parties. Historians therefore give ranges and indicate the nature of the source.

Economically, the war consumed the revenues that had underwritten Iraq's development in the 1970s. Military expenditure rose sharply; oil exports were reduced by the loss of Gulf terminals and by the closure of the Syrian pipeline, only partly offset as capacity through Turkey was expanded and a link across Saudi Arabia was built in the late 1980s; and Iraq borrowed heavily from Gulf states, Western banks, and others. By 1988 its foreign debt was often estimated at tens of billions of dollars, a figure that mattered greatly for the events of 1990 (see Section 69.9). Inflation rose, civilian consumption was squeezed, and the state increasingly relied on foreign workers, including very large numbers of Egyptians, to replace Iraqis in the mobilized armed forces. Iran's economy suffered from damage to ports and refineries, from the diversion of resources to the war, and from reduced oil income when prices fell in 1986. Both governments used the war to justify tighter control. In Iran, the conflict enabled the revolutionary institutions to marginalize rivals; in Iraq, it deepened the security state's reach over daily life.

Everyday Life and Social History

Conscription, Displacement, and the Household in Wartime

For millions of families, the war was above all a matter of who was absent. Iraq conscripted young men on a large scale, and the army's rotation between front and home left households continually waiting for news. Iranian families experienced a parallel pattern, with some young men volunteering through the Basij and others being conscripted. In both countries the dead were commemorated in public ceremonies, murals, and state-managed networks of support for the families of those killed, which tied households to the government's cause but also created an official language of grief that did not always match private feeling.

Women's work expanded and changed. In Iraq, where the state had promoted women's education and employment since the 1970s, women filled positions in offices, factories, and agriculture left vacant by mobilized men, while the government simultaneously urged them to bear children for the nation. In Iran, many women worked in support roles for the war effort, in hospitals, and in organizations that supported the families of the dead, even as the revolutionary state imposed new legal and dress restrictions. Both states relied on women to maintain households in conditions of shortage, rationing, and fear.

Education and health were disrupted. Iranian universities, which had been closed for several years after the revolution, reopened in the early 1980s under a changed curriculum, and schoolchildren took part in wartime mobilization and commemoration. In Iraq, school classes and youth organizations were used to instill loyalty to the leader and the war. Hospitals in frontline cities such as Basra and Abadan received large numbers of wounded, medical personnel were stretched thin, and chemical-weapons victims suffered long-term respiratory, skin, and eye illnesses that persisted for decades. Border cities were shelled, and families moved inland. Displacement followed frontline movements: residents of Khorramshahr and Abadan fled in 1980, and later Iraqis from Basra and from villages in the north moved away from bombardment.

Evidence and its limits: Our knowledge of daily life comes from memoirs, oral histories, novels, wartime photography, state media, and surveys by international agencies. State media were instruments of mobilization and cannot be taken as straightforward descriptions of public mood. Oral histories gathered later reflect the memory of survivors, which has been shaped by subsequent events, including the 1991 war and sanctions. Historians must read each source with its purpose in mind and attend especially to the experiences of women, rural families, and minorities, which official sources underrepresent.

Section 69.7

Chemical Weapons, Kurdish Communities, and the Anfal Campaign

Iraq used chemical weapons repeatedly during the war. Iranian allegations in late 1983 led the United Nations Secretary-General to send teams of specialists, the first of which visited Iran in March 1984 and concluded that chemical weapons had been used. Later missions between 1984 and 1988 confirmed Iraqi use of mustard gas and nerve agents against Iranian troops and, in some cases, civilians. Chemical attacks occurred around Majnoon in 1984, at Faw in 1986, and in the Iranian Kurdish town of Sardasht in June 1987, where civilians were killed and many more injured. Iraq's government denied or deflected the charges for years but later acknowledged its programs. The international response was restrained: in 1984 the Council's statement condemned the use of chemical weapons without naming Iraq, and Security Council Resolution 612 in May 1988 again condemned chemical-weapons use in general terms. Many governments, preoccupied with the conflict's strategic implications, limited their reaction to diplomatic expressions of concern.

The most extensive use of violence against civilians, however, took place inside Iraq. The Iraqi government treated the Kurdish north as a security problem of the first order. Many Kurdish political forces, the KDP and the PUK among them, had allied with Iran after 1980, and Iranian forces advanced into Iraqi Kurdistan in 1987–1988. In 1983 the security services had seized thousands of men and boys of the Barzani tribal group, who were never seen again. In March 1987 Ali Hassan al-Majid, a relative of Saddam, was appointed to head the northern bureau of the Ba'ath Party, with extraordinary powers over the army, security services, and administration in the Kurdish provinces. Under decrees that he issued, villages in broad "prohibited zones" were declared off-limits, and their inhabitants subject to forced removal and, in the case of resistance, to being shot on sight. Thousands of villages were destroyed, and their populations relocated to resettlement camps or held in detention.

The campaign that the government called al-Anfal, after a chapter of the Qur'an, took place in eight operations between February and September 1988. Army units and security forces, supported by aircraft and, in several operations, chemical weapons, surrounded rural districts, destroyed villages, and rounded up the people. Women, children, and the elderly were generally held in camps; men and boys of fighting age were separated from them, taken to detention centers, and many were executed and buried in mass graves, notably in the desert in southern Iraq. Estimates of the dead differ. Human Rights Watch, which conducted extensive investigations, concluded that between fifty thousand and one hundred thousand people were killed; Kurdish sources give much higher numbers. What is certain is that the campaign was organized from the top of the state, carried out by official bodies, and aimed at the Kurdish rural population as a whole, not at armed combatants alone. Human Rights Watch concluded that it constituted genocide, and the Iraqi High Tribunal later convicted Ali Hassan al-Majid and others of genocide and crimes against humanity in connection with the campaign.

The best-known single episode of the broader anti-Kurdish violence was the chemical attack on the town of Halabja on 16 March 1988. Halabja was not technically one of the eight named Anfal operations, although it occurred during the same genocidal campaign against Kurdish civilians and is often discussed alongside Anfal. Iranian forces and Kurdish fighters had taken the town the day before. Iraqi aircraft then bombed it with a combination of chemical agents, killing a large number of civilians; estimates commonly run between three thousand and five thousand dead, and many more were injured. Photographs and testimony reached the world press, but the attack was only one of dozens of chemical attacks on Kurdish areas. The documentary record for Anfal is exceptionally strong. When Kurdish forces captured Iraqi government offices in March 1991, they obtained a very large body of internal records, which were later studied by researchers and used in legal proceedings; these documents, together with forensic excavation of mass graves and survivor interviews, allow historians to reconstruct the campaign's organization. The Iraqi government's claim that the operations were counterinsurgency against forces collaborating with Iran does not explain the targeting of civilians. Iran's ties to Kurdish parties are part of the context; they do not constitute a justification, and the civilian victims were not combatants.

Section 69.8

The Tanker War, International Naval Intervention, and the Ceasefire of 1988

From 1984 both belligerents attacked shipping in the Gulf. Iraq, unable to cripple Iran's army, targeted the oil exports on which Iran's economy depended, and struck tankers loading near Kharg Island. Iran retaliated against ships serving Iraq's Arab supporters, and increasingly against Kuwaiti and Saudi vessels, and later laid mines. For the Gulf monarchies the stakes were direct: their own oil exports passed through the Strait of Hormuz, and Iranian attacks on Kuwait, including missile strikes on its territory and ports, were meant to pressure it to reduce its support for Iraq. The Tanker War did not close the Gulf, and most oil continued to flow, but insurance rates rose and the risk of a wider conflict grew.

In late 1986 Kuwait asked the United States and the Soviet Union for protection of its tankers. The Soviet Union leased several vessels, and the United States agreed to place eleven Kuwaiti tankers under its flag and escort them, an operation (Earnest Will) that began in July 1987. The decision reflected American concerns about oil supply, the credibility of its commitments to Gulf partners, and the prospect of Soviet influence. It also marked a decisive step in the internationalization of Gulf security. In May 1987 an Iraqi aircraft fired Exocet missiles at the American frigate USS Stark, killing thirty-seven sailors; Iraq apologized, and the United States treated it as an accident. In July 1987 a reflagged tanker struck a mine, and the following months saw clashes between U.S. forces and Iranian craft and attacks on Iranian oil platforms. In April 1988, after the USS Samuel B. Roberts was damaged by a mine, U.S. forces destroyed Iranian platforms and sank or damaged several Iranian ships in Operation Praying Mantis. On 3 July 1988 the cruiser USS Vincennes, in a confused engagement with Iranian small craft, shot down Iran Air Flight 655, an airliner carrying 290 people, all of whom died. The United States later expressed regret and paid compensation without admitting legal liability. Iranian leaders regarded the incident as evidence of American hostility.

The war's end came in 1988, in a few months. Iraq, with its defenses reinforced and its army reorganized, launched a series of offensives in the spring and summer. In April it retook the Faw Peninsula in a rapid operation preceded by heavy artillery and chemical weapons, and further attacks in May–July pushed Iranian forces from the Majnoon area and parts of the border. Iran, by contrast, was strained by the loss of men and equipment, the economic cost of the war, the U.S. naval confrontation, and the missile and air attacks on its cities. On 18 July 1988 the Iranian government informed the Secretary-General that it accepted Resolution 598. Khomeini described the decision, in a public statement, as a bitter one that he took in the interest of the revolution. The ceasefire took effect on 20 August 1988, and a UN observer mission monitored it. The dates must be kept distinct: the resolution was adopted in July 1987, accepted by Iran in July 1988, and implemented as a ceasefire a month after that.

The ceasefire ended large-scale combat but left much unresolved. Iraqi troops remained on some Iranian territory pending negotiation, prisoners of war were not immediately returned, the question of the river boundary remained open, and the inquiry into responsibility was not concluded until the Secretary-General's report in December 1991, which stated that Iraq's initial attack could not be justified under the United Nations Charter. The war had not changed the frontier, had cost an enormous number of lives, and had left both governments with large armies and deep resentments. As the next division shows, its legacy was to be most immediately felt not in the relations between Baghdad and Tehran but between Baghdad and its Arab neighbors.

Section 69.9

The Aftermath of the Iran–Iraq War and the Security of the Gulf Monarchies

Iraq emerged from the war with the largest army in the Arab world, a sizable domestic arms industry, and a government that regarded its survival as a victory. It also emerged with severe economic problems. The foreign debt, estimated in the range of seventy to eighty billion dollars by common accounts, included large sums owed to Western and Soviet-bloc creditors and tens of billions owed to Arab states, principally Saudi Arabia and Kuwait. Oil prices, which had collapsed in 1986, remained low. Reconstruction of Basra, Faw, and the damaged southern economy required money the state did not have, and demobilizing hundreds of thousands of soldiers into an economy with few jobs threatened social stability. The leadership responded by keeping much of the army mobilized, continuing military programs, including the development of missiles and unconventional weapons, and seeking additional revenue from oil. The security state, which had grown during the war, remained in place, and Saddam Hussein cultivated an image as the victor over Iran and the champion of the Arab cause.

The Gulf monarchies had their own war legacy. Saudi Arabia, Kuwait, Bahrain, Qatar, the United Arab Emirates, and Oman had founded the Gulf Cooperation Council in Abu Dhabi in May 1981, in the shadow of the revolution and the new war. The GCC was a mechanism for consultation on defense, economic coordination, and internal security, and in 1984 its members agreed to form a joint force (the Peninsula Shield) whose size was small relative to the threats they perceived. The member states did not have identical interests. Saudi Arabia and Kuwait, closest to the war, were most heavily committed to Iraq's side; Oman maintained working relations with Iran and was cautious about alienating it; the UAE was divided between Abu Dhabi, aligned with Iraq's supporters, and Dubai, with its extensive trade ties with Iran; Qatar and Bahrain trod carefully. Each also faced internal concerns. Kuwait, with a large Shi'a minority, experienced bombings in 1983 and an attempt on the emir's life in 1985, attributed to groups linked to Iran or the Iraqi Dawa Party. Bahrain announced the discovery of a coup plot in 1981. In Saudi Arabia, clashes between Iranian pilgrims and security forces in Mecca in July 1987 left hundreds dead, most of them Iranians, and led to a break in diplomatic relations in 1988.

The Gulf states' wartime support for Iraq had been substantial. Loans and grants totaling tens of billions of dollars, access to ports and transit routes, and diplomatic backing sustained Baghdad's war effort. Iraqi and Gulf accounts of what this support meant diverged. The Gulf governments regarded much of the money as loans to be repaid, and viewed Iraq as a necessary buffer against Iran, but one whose ambitions they did not wish to enlarge. Iraqi leaders, by contrast, argued that their country had borne the human cost of defending the Arab Gulf against revolutionary Iran, and that this sacrifice should be recognized by cancellation of debts and further assistance. The Gulf governments also sought stronger security cooperation after 1988, not only with each other but with outside powers, including the United States, Britain, and France. They did not regard this as passivity but as hedging: they were actively diversifying their security ties, and they did so with differing assessments of whether Iraq or Iran was the more dangerous neighbor. By 1989 many Gulf rulers were uneasy about both.

Section 69.10

Iraq, Kuwait, Oil, Debt, and the Collapse of Regional Diplomacy

In 1989 and 1990 relations between Baghdad and Kuwait deteriorated. The sources of the dispute were several. Iraq wanted Kuwait to cancel the war debt, which Iraqi leaders described as a contribution to a common defense. It wanted additional financial aid for reconstruction. And it wanted higher oil prices, which depended on the OPEC members respecting their production quotas. Kuwait and the United Arab Emirates were producing above their quotas in 1989–1990, and prices stayed in the range of fifteen to eighteen dollars a barrel. Saddam and his ministers argued that each dollar's fall in price cost Iraq a billion dollars a year in revenue, and described overproduction as economic warfare. Kuwait's government responded that it was an independent state with its own economic needs, and that the debt was a commercial obligation. In February 1989 Iraq had joined Egypt, Jordan, and North Yemen in the Arab Cooperation Council, an arrangement from which the Gulf monarchies were excluded and which Saddam may have intended as a counterweight to the GCC.

Iraq added specific accusations. It claimed that Kuwait had pumped oil from the Iraqi side of the Rumaila field, which straddles the border, and demanded compensation, with a figure of billions of dollars cited. Rumaila's reservoir is in fact a single geological structure shared by both countries, so drilling on either side could draw from the same oil; but independent investigation never established the allegation that Kuwait had deliberately drilled slantwise into Iraqi territory, and historians treat it as an Iraqi claim rather than a documented fact. Iraq also claimed historical rights to Kuwait, pointing to Ottoman administrative arrangements in which Kuwait had been attached to the province of Basra, and to its desire for secure access to the Gulf through Warba and Bubiyan islands, which control the approaches to Umm Qasr. These claims had a long history. Iraq had asserted sovereignty over Kuwait in 1961, when Kuwait became independent, but in 1963 a new Iraqi government recognized Kuwait's independence and border in an agreement, and Kuwait was admitted to the United Nations that year.

The point is essential: Kuwait was a sovereign state, a member of the United Nations and the Arab League, and had been recognized by Iraq. Economic disagreements and historical claims of this kind, however deeply felt, do not constitute a legal justification for invasion. Historians' interest is therefore in why Iraq's leaders decided that force was the appropriate response. Saddam's speeches in spring and summer 1990 became increasingly hostile, accusing the Gulf governments of a conspiracy, possibly with the United States, to weaken Iraq. In the same months he threatened Israel with chemical weapons. By 17 July, in a Revolution Day speech, he accused Kuwait and the UAE of stabbing Iraq in the back, and the Iraqi foreign minister sent a memorandum to the Arab League listing complaints. Mediation by Egypt, Saudi Arabia, and Jordan produced a short-lived de-escalation: OPEC agreed late in July to a higher price target and tighter quotas, a partial Iraqi gain. But Iraq had meanwhile moved a growing number of troops, including Republican Guard units, to the Kuwaiti frontier, and on 31 July talks between Iraqi and Kuwaiti delegations at Jeddah ended without agreement. Some Arab leaders, including Egypt's Hosni Mubarak, believed that Saddam did not intend to invade and was seeking leverage. They were mistaken.

Saddam's reasoning remains the subject of study, using captured recordings of his meetings and the accounts of officials. It appears that he considered the economic crisis a threat to regime stability, believed that military force could solve it, miscalculated the likelihood of a strong international response, and expected the United States, constrained after Vietnam, and the Arab states to accept a fait accompli. A meeting on 25 July with the U.S. ambassador, April Glaspie, has been widely discussed. The American position, as she stated it, was that Washington took no position on Arab–Arab border disputes but insisted on peaceful settlement. Some have argued that this ambiguity encouraged Saddam; most scholars conclude that it did not cause his decision, but that American signals had not clearly deterred it, and that Washington, like regional capitals, had underestimated the danger.

Cause and Effect

The Iraqi Invasion of Kuwait

Long-term causes

Iraq's historical claims to Kuwait and its limited coastline; the militarization of Iraqi society and the concentrated power of the president; the debts and damage left by the war with Iran; Iraq's perception that it had earned Gulf gratitude and money; the weakening of Soviet power, which had long underwritten Iraq's diplomacy.

Short-term causes

Oil prices in the range of fifteen to eighteen dollars, which Iraq blamed on overproduction by Kuwait and the UAE; Kuwait's refusal to cancel loans; the Rumaila and border disputes; a bellicose spring and summer 1990 in Iraqi rhetoric; failed mediation at Jeddah on 31 July.

The decision

Saddam Hussein and a small circle chose to invade on 2 August 1990. The choice depended on assumptions about the weakness of Kuwait's army, the passivity of the Arab states, and the reluctance of the United States to use force on a large scale.

Immediate effects

Occupation and attempted annexation of Kuwait; the flight of hundreds of thousands of foreign workers; unanimous UN condemnation and sanctions; deployment of foreign forces to Saudi Arabia; a split in the Arab League.

Long-term consequences

The Gulf War of 1991; sanctions and inspections lasting twelve years; a continuing U.S. military presence in the Gulf; the weakening of Arab nationalism as an organizing principle of inter-Arab politics; the long confrontation that led to 2003.

Historical complexity

Grievances over debt and oil were genuine, but other states with similar grievances did not invade neighbors. The invasion cannot be explained by economic pressure alone; it required a decision-making system in which one man could stake the country's future on a miscalculation.

Historical complexity: Historians weigh the relative importance of economic desperation, personal ambition, regime insecurity, and a perception of opportunity. No single cause suffices, and the same facts have been used both to explain the decision and, incorrectly, to excuse it.

Section 69.11

Iraq's Invasion and Occupation of Kuwait, August 1990

Early on 2 August 1990 Iraqi armored and mechanized divisions of the Republican Guard crossed into Kuwait, supported by helicopters and special forces landing near Kuwait City. Kuwait's small army, of some sixteen thousand soldiers, could offer only brief and localized resistance; there was fighting around the Dasman Palace, where the emir's half-brother, Sheikh Fahd al-Ahmad al-Sabah, was killed, and some units retreated to Saudi Arabia. The emir, Sheikh Jaber al-Ahmad al-Sabah, and most of the government left for Saudi Arabia, where they established a government-in-exile. Within about two days Iraq controlled the country. Iraq announced the formation of a "Provisional Free Kuwait Government," which it quickly dissolved, and on 8 August announced the merger of Kuwait with Iraq; at the end of the month it declared Kuwait Iraq's nineteenth province. The Iraqi government's public justifications, that it was responding to a call from Kuwaiti revolutionaries and righting a historical wrong, had no documentary basis and were rejected by nearly every government.

The occupation lasted seven months and its character is documented by Kuwaiti and international sources, including testimony, reports by human rights organizations, and later legal proceedings. Iraqi forces and officials looted government institutions, banks, museums, hospitals, shops, and homes; removed equipment, vehicles, and records to Iraq; and attempted to strip Kuwait of its distinct institutions. They detained, tortured, and killed many people, among them members of the armed resistance, civilians suspected of supporting it, and others. Hundreds of Kuwaitis and other residents remained missing after the war. Kuwaiti society organized opposition through civil disobedience, underground networks, and an armed resistance that carried out attacks despite harsh reprisals. A very large proportion of Kuwait's population was foreign. Many hundreds of thousands of Egyptians, South Asians, Filipinos, Palestinians, and others fled across the desert to Jordan and elsewhere in the first weeks, creating a refugee crisis; others remained, and Western and some other foreign nationals in Kuwait and Iraq were held as "guests," many at strategic sites as shields against attack, until their release in December 1990.

The invasion was condemned immediately. On the day it began, the Security Council adopted Resolution 660 by fourteen votes to none, with Yemen not participating. Four days later Resolution 661 imposed comprehensive economic sanctions, and on 9 August Resolution 662 declared the annexation null and void. Arab governments divided in their immediate responses. A meeting of Arab foreign ministers in Cairo condemned the invasion, but Iraq, Libya, and the PLO opposed what followed. King Hussein of Jordan, whose country depended on trade with Iraq and whose population was strongly sympathetic to Iraq, tried to mediate while resisting the coalition. These differences are examined in Section 69.12.

Section 69.12

The United Nations, Arab Diplomacy, and the Formation of the Anti-Iraq Coalition

Over the following weeks the Security Council tightened its measures. Resolution 665 authorized naval enforcement of the embargo, and Resolution 670 extended it to air traffic. On 29 November 1990, in Resolution 678, the Council authorized member states cooperating with Kuwait to use "all necessary means" to uphold earlier resolutions and restore peace and security if Iraq did not withdraw by 15 January 1991. The vote was twelve in favor, two against (Cuba and Yemen), with China abstaining. The resolution was possible because the Soviet Union, under Mikhail Gorbachev, was willing to cooperate with Washington, and China chose not to use its veto, a pattern that would have been unlikely a few years earlier. The authorization was not the same as a UN-commanded war: the Council authorized states to act, and the coalition's military operations were commanded by national governments and coordinated among them.

Historical Vocabulary
Chapter VII and "All Necessary Means"

Chapter VII of the United Nations Charter (Articles 39–51) governs "action with respect to threats to the peace, breaches of the peace, and acts of aggression." Under it the Security Council may determine that a threat exists and decide on measures, from sanctions (Article 41) to military action (Article 42). Resolutions adopted under Chapter VII are legally binding on all members, which gave the Iraq sanctions and later demands their force. Article 51 recognizes the inherent right of individual or collective self-defense, which Resolution 661 invoked on Kuwait's behalf.

"All necessary means" is diplomatic language for military force. It was used in Resolution 678 because some Council members preferred not to write the word "force." The phrase has been used in many later resolutions, and its interpretation, especially whether it authorizes force beyond the immediate purpose, became contested after 1991.

Students should avoid assuming that the Council "ran" the war. Chapter VII authorizes; it does not command. The coalition was an alliance of states operating under national control with Council authorization, not a United Nations army.

The Arab response to the crisis exposed deep divisions. On 10 August 1990 an emergency Arab League summit in Cairo adopted a resolution endorsing the dispatch of Arab forces to Saudi Arabia; by most accounts twelve of the League's members voted for it, with Iraq, Libya, and the PLO delegation reported among the opponents and several governments abstaining or entering reservations. Accounts of the exact division of votes and reservations vary, so these categories should not be treated as interchangeable. Saudi Arabia, Kuwait, and the other Gulf monarchies, whose security was directly threatened, were the principal organizers, and they paid a large share of the coalition's costs. Egypt, under Hosni Mubarak, sent tens of thousands of troops, motivated by its alignment with Washington and the Gulf states, by Saddam's threat to Arab order, and by the prospect of debt relief. Syria, under Hafez al-Assad, an old rival of Saddam's Ba'ath, also deployed forces, and its participation signaled a realignment in Damascus's relations with Washington and the Gulf. Morocco, Oman, Qatar, Bahrain, the UAE, and others contributed units. Jordan, Yemen, Sudan, and the PLO leadership under Yasser Arafat were among those critical of the coalition or sympathetic to Baghdad, and Algeria and Tunisia saw large public demonstrations against military intervention.

Arab public opinion did not mirror governments' positions. In Jordan, the Maghreb, and among Palestinians, many people regarded Saddam as a defiant leader confronting Western power and Gulf wealth, and they accepted the argument he offered on 12 August, that Iraq would withdraw from Kuwait if Israel withdrew from the occupied territories and Syria from Lebanon. Washington rejected this linkage, and the Council did not accept it. Other Arabs, including Kuwaitis, Gulf residents, and many intellectuals, regarded the invasion as an attack on Arab sovereignty and saw no contradiction in opposing both Saddam and Western intervention, though the choice between inaction and military action left few comfortable options. The crisis thus exposed the long-term erosion of Arab nationalism as an organizing political principle: the invasion of one Arab state by another was justified by Iraq in the language of Arab unity, while its opponents invoked the principle of sovereignty. The result damaged the already weakened mechanisms of Arab League collective action.

The coalition that took shape was the largest since the Second World War, with some three dozen states contributing forces or support, led by the United States under President George H. W. Bush. The Soviet Union did not join militarily but supported the Council measures and attempted late mediation. The coalition's formation also revealed a changed international order: with the Cold War ending, the United States could organize and finance a broad military and diplomatic alliance, and did so with considerable skill, by arranging payments from the Gulf states, Germany, and Japan, and the forgiveness of some of Egypt's debt. Diplomacy continued until the last week; the American and Iraqi foreign ministers met in Geneva on 9 January 1991 without result, and the U.S. Congress voted on 12 January to authorize force. The deadline passed on 15 January.

Section 69.13

Operation Desert Shield: Saudi Arabia and the International Military Buildup

Within days of the invasion, the central strategic question was whether Iraqi forces would continue south into Saudi Arabia, whose eastern oil fields and ports lay a short distance from the Kuwaiti frontier. The Saudi army was small and the kingdom had no experience of defending itself against a large mechanized force. On 6 August 1990 U.S. Secretary of Defense Dick Cheney and military officers presented King Fahd with intelligence assessments showing Iraqi forces massed near the border, and the king agreed to the stationing of foreign forces in the kingdom. The decision was taken after consultation within the royal family and with senior religious scholars, and it was not made lightly, since it placed non-Muslim troops in the country that contains Mecca and Medina. First American aircraft and airborne troops arrived on 7–8 August, and the operation became known as Desert Shield.

The buildup was enormous. By the end of 1990 hundreds of thousands of American personnel were in Saudi Arabia, a number that rose to about half a million in total American forces by January, and other states contributed forces in addition: the United Kingdom and France sent armored and air formations, and Egypt, Syria, Morocco, Pakistan, Bangladesh, and others deployed troops, alongside contingents from Gulf states and Kuwait's own reconstituted units. Overall command was divided. U.S. General Norman Schwarzkopf, as head of Central Command, directed American forces, while the Saudi Lieutenant General Prince Khalid bin Sultan commanded the Joint Forces and Theater of Operations, which included the Arab and Islamic units. This arrangement reflected both military convenience and a political need to present the operation as including Arab governments in decision-making. Logistics depended on Saudi ports at Dammam and Jubail, airfields, road networks, and fuel supplies, and on extensive host-nation support, with Gulf governments covering a large share of the cost.

The deployment had political consequences within the kingdom and beyond. Saudi authorities faced objections from some religious figures and activists, who considered the presence of foreign troops improper. In 1991 groups of Saudi scholars and professionals submitted petitions for political and religious reform, indicating that the crisis stimulated domestic debate about governance. The kingdom also expelled or lost large numbers of Yemeni workers, whose government had opposed the coalition; hundreds of thousands left in the following months. The Kuwaiti government-in-exile, based in the Saudi city of Taif, coordinated aid to Kuwaitis who had fled and began to prepare for the restoration of the state. For the Gulf monarchies as a whole, the buildup represented a decisive change. Where in the 1980s they had sought to maintain a balance of relationships with outside powers, in 1990 they invited a very large foreign military presence to protect them. That choice, with its costs and benefits, shaped the security arrangements examined in Division V. Iraqi forces in Kuwait, meanwhile, grew to a force in the hundreds of thousands, with fortifications along the frontier, and Saddam released the foreign hostages in December in an attempt to reduce pressure.

Section 69.14

Operation Desert Storm: Air Power, Ground Warfare, and Kuwait's Liberation

The coalition air campaign began in the early hours of 17 January 1991, shortly after the deadline in Resolution 678 expired. Aircraft and cruise missiles struck Iraqi air defenses, command-and-control facilities, airfields, missile sites, and weapons programs, and in the following days and weeks targeted the Iraqi army in Kuwait and southern Iraq, as well as infrastructure including bridges, power stations, communications, and oil facilities. Coalition planners considered damage to electrical and communications systems a way to paralyze the Iraqi command; the effect on civilians was serious, since power outages disrupted water treatment, sanitation, and hospitals. Civilians were also killed directly. The bombing of the Amiriyah shelter in Baghdad on 13 February, which killed hundreds of civilians according to most accounts, became a symbol of the war's human cost, and the coalition said the building had been identified as a command bunker.

Saddam Hussein responded by firing Scud missiles at Israel from 18 January, hoping to provoke an Israeli response that would divide the coalition's Arab members from the others. Under American pressure, and with Patriot air-defense batteries deployed, Israel did not retaliate. Iraq also fired missiles at Saudi Arabia and Bahrain; one struck a barracks at Dhahran on 25 February, killing twenty-eight American soldiers. At the end of January Iraqi units attacked and briefly occupied the Saudi town of Khafji, and were driven out by Saudi, Qatari, and American forces after several days of fighting, a clash that demonstrated the Arab units' role and the weakness of the Iraqi offensive capability. In January, Iraqi forces had also released large quantities of oil into the Gulf, producing an immense spill.

The ground offensive began on 24 February. Coalition planners had deceived Iraqi commanders into expecting an amphibious attack and a direct assault from Saudi Arabia into Kuwait. They sent U.S. and British armored corps and French and American airborne forces in a wide flanking movement west of Kuwait, into southern Iraq, intended to cut off the Republican Guard and Iraqi forces in the theater. Marines and Arab forces from Saudi Arabia, Egypt, Syria, Kuwait, and other states attacked directly into Kuwait, and Arab units took the lead in entering Kuwait City on 26–27 February. Iraqi resistance collapsed quickly in many sectors; thousands of soldiers surrendered. On 25–26 February Iraq announced its withdrawal, and columns of vehicles streamed north along the highways from Kuwait City, where they were attacked from the air in two areas near Mutla Ridge, the episode known as the "Highway of Death." The extent to which these were fleeing or still combatant units, the number of casualties, and the proportionality of the attacks have remained controversial; coalition spokespeople described them as legitimate military targets, while critics questioned the necessity of destroying retreating forces. President Bush declared a suspension of offensive operations effective on 28 February, after about a hundred hours of ground fighting.

Explanations of this outcome should avoid reducing it to technology. The coalition possessed precision munitions, stealth aircraft, advanced reconnaissance, and overwhelming air superiority. But it also had months to prepare, a huge advantage in numbers and logistics, a unified command, well-trained forces, and a plan that exploited Iraqi weaknesses. Iraq's army was large but many of its soldiers were poorly trained, ill-supplied, and unwilling to fight; its leadership was highly centralized, and its commanders adopted a static defense that the coalition could outflank. Estimates of Iraqi military dead range widely, from the low tens of thousands upward, and no authoritative figure exists; coalition deaths were by contrast very low, in the low hundreds, a fact that influenced American views of the war's lessons. A substantial part of the Republican Guard escaped northward, which would prove significant for what followed. The coalition stopped at the Kuwait frontier for reasons of mandate, politics, and strategy: Resolution 678 spoke of Kuwait's liberation; Arab members of the coalition had no wish to occupy Iraq; and American leaders feared that dismembering Iraq would empower Iran and create a power vacuum. On 3 March Iraqi and coalition commanders met at Safwan and agreed on ceasefire terms, which permitted Iraq to fly helicopters, a concession it would exploit.

Section 69.15

The Iraqi Uprisings of 1991: Kurds, Shi'a Communities, and State Repression

In the first days of March 1991, as defeated Iraqi soldiers returned to southern cities, revolts broke out. They began in Basra and spread rapidly through Najaf, Karbala, Nasiriyya, Amara, and other southern towns. The participants were diverse: army deserters, local residents, activists from the Islamic Dawa Party and from the Supreme Council for the Islamic Revolution in Iraq, which was based in Iran, clerical networks, and ordinary people angered by the war, deprivations, and decades of repression. Some insurgents carried images of Iranian leaders or used Islamic revolutionary language, which the Iraqi government used to depict the uprising as an Iranian plot; in fact the movement was largely a spontaneous local rising, and its political aims were unclear and varied. Iraqi Shi'a are not a single political bloc, and many were secular or nationalist; they should not be equated with Iran, whose war against Iraq most had fought on Iraq's side only a few years earlier.

In the north, Kurdish uprisings began in early March in towns including Rania and Sulaymaniyah, and Kurdish forces, including the peshmerga of the Kurdistan Democratic Party and the Patriotic Union of Kurdistan, working with other groups in the Iraqi Kurdistan Front, took control of much of the Kurdish region and, around 20 March, of the oil city of Kirkuk. Kurdish political aims differed, from autonomy within Iraq to federalism, and the main parties had a history of rivalry as well as cooperation. The Kurdish movement was also encouraged by public statements by American leaders, notably President Bush's call in February for the Iraqi people to take matters into their own hands, though the United States did not intend to provide military support and did not do so. Washington feared that the collapse of the Iraqi state would produce disintegration, strengthen Iran, and unsettle Turkey, an ally that opposed any Kurdish independence.

The government recovered quickly. The Republican Guard, which had mostly escaped destruction, and other loyal units, using helicopters, tanks, and artillery, retook the southern cities in March, and the northern towns by the end of the month. The Safwan terms, which allowed Iraqi helicopters to fly, were used to attack rebels. Repression in the south was severe: mass arrests, executions, and the shelling of neighborhoods and shrines in Najaf and Karbala, with many thousands killed by most estimates and mass graves discovered later. In the north, the fear of a repeat of Anfal drove an exodus. Between one and a half and two million Kurds fled toward the Turkish and Iranian borders in late March and April, many into mountains in harsh weather without shelter, food, or medical supplies. Turkey, wary of a Kurdish influx, initially restricted entry; Iran admitted very large numbers. Thousands died of exposure, disease, and violence.

The international response developed in April. On 5 April the Security Council adopted Resolution 688, by ten votes to three (Cuba, Yemen, and Zimbabwe against) with two abstentions (China and India). It condemned the repression of the Iraqi civilian population, including in Kurdish areas, called it a threat to international peace and security, demanded that Iraq end it, and insisted that Iraq allow humanitarian organizations access. Western governments then launched a relief and protection operation, Provide Comfort, which began in mid-April, creating a safe area in northern Iraq north of the 36th parallel in which Kurdish refugees could return. Iraqi forces withdrew from the Kurdish provinces in October 1991, leaving the region under Kurdish administration, and Kurds held elections in 1992, though the region was under an economic blockade by Baghdad and later suffered conflict between its main parties. No comparable protection was provided to the southern population.

Their actions in suppressing it included mass killing; fear of regime collapse is part of the context but does not alter the nature of the violence.
Neighboring states: Turkey feared an influx of Kurdish refugees and the encouragement of its own Kurdish insurgents; Iran received more than a million refugees and watched the southern uprising with interest; Saudi Arabia and Kuwait wanted Saddam gone but feared Iraqi fragmentation and Iranian influence.
International humanitarian organizations and coalition governments: Relief agencies faced a refugee emergency in remote mountain areas; Western governments, under public pressure after television coverage of the exodus, chose a limited intervention to protect Kurds, while avoiding commitment to overthrowing the government or protecting the south.
Section 69.16

Kuwait's Liberation, Environmental Destruction, and the New Regional Balance

Kuwait's government returned in mid-March 1991 to a country badly damaged by the occupation. The emir restored the institutions of the state under martial law, which lasted for several months. Reconstruction was rapid, financed by Kuwait's assets abroad and oil revenues. Tensions also marked the aftermath. Kuwait's population had included several hundred thousand Palestinians, many of whom had lived there for decades and filled professional posts; because the PLO leadership had sided with Baghdad, many Kuwaitis regarded them with suspicion, and some Palestinians were detained, harassed, or expelled in the months after liberation. The great majority of the Palestinian community eventually left or was forced out. Kuwait also tried suspected collaborators in special courts, in proceedings that human rights groups criticized as lacking adequate safeguards. The issue of Kuwaiti and other prisoners and missing persons from the occupation remained an obstacle between Kuwait and Iraq for years. In 1992 Kuwait held parliamentary elections, a restoration of the political life that had been curtailed in 1986.

Environmental destruction was a major consequence. As they withdrew, Iraqi forces sabotaged Kuwait's oil infrastructure. The United Nations Compensation Commission has recorded that more than seven hundred oil wells were damaged or destroyed, a large proportion of them set on fire. Burning wells produced vast smoke plumes and lost very large volumes of oil each day, and it took international teams until early November 1991 to extinguish the fires. Oil also collected in "lakes" in the desert, damaging soil and groundwater, and the earlier release of oil into the Gulf contaminated coastal waters, affecting marine life and fisheries along the Saudi and Kuwaiti coasts. The long-term ecological and public-health effects remain studied and debated, with scientists disagreeing about the extent of effects on air quality beyond the region. The environmental damage was treated by the Compensation Commission as a category of claims against Iraq, and large awards were made to Kuwait and neighboring states.

The war's political outcome was paradoxical. Kuwait's sovereignty was restored, which was the coalition's stated aim, yet the government that had committed the invasion remained in power in Baghdad, having suppressed the uprisings, and its civilians were left in a condition of extreme vulnerability. A UN mission under Martti Ahtisaari that visited Iraq in March 1991 reported in unusually severe terms that bombing had reduced the country's infrastructure to a pre-industrial condition, with electricity, water, and sanitation systems severely damaged. As the next division explains, this conjunction, a surviving regime and a population reliant on a damaged infrastructure, was to define the following decade. For the Gulf monarchies, the war confirmed their security dependence on the United States and showed their ability to mobilize resources; for Arab politics more broadly, it deepened divisions that the later peace diplomacy discussed in Chapter 70 would encounter.

Section 69.17

UN Resolution 687, Disarmament, Weapons Inspections, and Iraqi Sovereignty

On 3 April 1991 the Security Council adopted Resolution 687, the most detailed and far-reaching of the resolutions on Iraq. It was a ceasefire resolution, but it was also much more: a framework for the disarmament of Iraq, the settlement of its border with Kuwait, the payment of compensation, and the continuation of sanctions. Iraq's government accepted it on 6 April, and the formal ceasefire took effect on 11 April. Its adoption was by twelve votes in favor, one against (Cuba), and two abstentions (Ecuador and Yemen).

Historical Vocabulary
Weapons of Mass Destruction and Dual-Use Goods

Weapons of mass destruction (WMD) is a term applied in international discussion to nuclear, biological, and chemical weapons, and sometimes to the ballistic missiles that deliver them. Resolution 687 used a related formulation, requiring Iraq to give up chemical and biological weapons, ballistic missiles with a range over 150 kilometers, and any nuclear weapons or weapons-usable materials. Students should note that the term groups together weapons of very different effect and military use. A nuclear weapon, a nerve agent, and a biological agent have different technical requirements, different effects, and different political meanings, and treating them as a single category can obscure these distinctions.

Dual-use goods are materials, equipment, and technologies with both civilian and military applications. Chemicals used in water purification, growth media used in hospitals, pharmaceuticals, and machinery can also serve weapons programs. The sanctions committee scrutinized contracts for such goods, which meant that decisions about disarmament and decisions about humanitarian supply were often entangled, a tension examined in Section 69.18.

This ambiguity became a source of dispute about what Iraq had to demonstrate and who judged it. Iraq's acceptance came under conditions of military defeat, and Iraqi officials later described the resolution as an infringement of sovereignty. The text says nothing of how the inspection regime would work in practice or how often Iraq would cooperate, which is found in later inspection reports and Council discussions.

The Special Commission (UNSCOM), led for its first six years by the Swedish diplomat Rolf Ekeus and then by the Australian Richard Butler, began inspections in mid-1991 alongside the International Atomic Energy Agency. The relationship between inspectors and Iraq was marked by repeated confrontation. Iraq's declarations of its weapons programs were incomplete from the start, and inspectors found evidence of concealment; in 1991 the IAEA discovered an extensive clandestine nuclear program whose scale had not been known before the war. After the defection to Jordan in August 1995 of Hussein Kamel, Saddam's son-in-law and the former head of military industries, Iraq disclosed much more about its programs, including a biological-weapons program it had earlier denied. Inspectors destroyed large quantities of chemical agents, munitions, and missiles in the 1990s. Iraq, for its part, objected to intrusive inspections, particularly of sites associated with the presidency and the security services, and argued that its cooperation was not being recognized by an inspection regime that would continue to withhold sanctions relief. In 1998 and 1999 allegations that the United States had used UNSCOM to gather intelligence on Iraq's leadership further damaged the commission's credibility in Baghdad and among some Council members.

It is important to separate what was documented from what was later claimed. Iraq's chemical-weapons use in the 1980s is established; so are its biological and nuclear programs as of 1991 and 1995, discovered by inspectors and through Iraq's own admissions. What inspectors could not do was certify that all programs had been dismantled, and the continuing uncertainty fed both the case for ongoing coercion and the case that Iraq had in fact disarmed. By late 1998 UNSCOM and Iraq were in open dispute; in December inspectors left Iraq, and the Council later replaced UNSCOM with a new body, UNMOVIC, in Resolution 1284 of December 1999, which Iraq did not accept. The episode raises a broader question that historians address: how a state's sovereignty relates to the enforcement of international disarmament obligations that it accepted under duress.

Section 69.18

Economic Sanctions, Humanitarian Conditions, and the Oil-for-Food Programme

The comprehensive sanctions imposed in August 1990 remained in place after the war. Iraq could not export oil, with limited exceptions, or import most goods or arrange financial transactions. The economy was already burdened by war debt and by the damage of 1991. Its infrastructure for electricity generation, water purification, and sewage treatment had been heavily damaged, and the sanctions complicated the import of spare parts and chemicals needed to repair it. Inflation was severe and the dinar lost most of its value; salaried professionals, teachers, and civil servants saw their income collapse, and many emigrated or sold possessions. The government introduced a food ration system that gave most households a basic, though inadequate, supply, and many families came to depend on it. Malnutrition, disease, and deterioration of the health and education systems followed, in conditions that observers from UN agencies and humanitarian organizations described as grave.

Responsibility for the humanitarian situation is a matter of historical dispute. The Iraqi government attributed it to the sanctions; the sanctions' supporters pointed to the choices of the government, which spent heavily on the security services and on palaces and in the early 1990s resisted arrangements that would have allowed oil sales for humanitarian purposes. The Council offered such an arrangement in Resolutions 706 and 712 in 1991, which Iraq rejected on grounds of sovereignty and control. In April 1995 it adopted Resolution 986, which authorized Iraq to export oil up to a limit of one billion dollars every ninety days, with proceeds placed in a UN-supervised escrow account to pay for food, medicine, and other humanitarian supplies, for UN operations, and for the compensation fund. Iraq initially rejected the terms, then concluded a memorandum of understanding with the UN in May 1996; oil exports under what became known as the Oil-for-Food Programme began in December 1996, and the first food shipments arrived in 1997. The ceiling was later raised, and abolished in 1999.

The programme eased conditions but did not eliminate hardship. It was administered through a complicated bureaucracy: the Iraqi government decided on contracts for much of the country, and the sanctions committee, where any member could place a hold, had to approve them. Holds on contracts for dual-use items were sources of delay and dispute. Two UN humanitarian coordinators, Denis Halliday and Hans von Sponeck, resigned in 1998 and 2000, in protest at what they considered the sanctions' effects. The programme also became the object of corruption. Investigations, notably an independent inquiry headed by Paul Volcker that reported in 2005, found that the Iraqi government had obtained substantial sums through illicit surcharges and kickbacks on contracts and oil sales, and that much more oil was smuggled outside the programme, to Jordan, Turkey, and other neighbors, with the knowledge of regional governments. The regime also used the distribution of oil allocations to reward foreign supporters. The northern region, where distribution was administered by the UN and the Kurdish authorities, fared better on some indicators than the center and south.

Historical Debate

Did Comprehensive Sanctions Strengthen International Security at an Unacceptable Humanitarian Cost?

The question: The sanctions regime had two aims: to compel disarmament and to restrain a government that had invaded a neighbor. Historians and policy analysts disagree about whether it achieved them and whether the humanitarian costs were justified. This debate has two parts that should be kept apart: factual disputes about the scale and causes of civilian suffering, and normative judgments about whether the policy was acceptable.

Interpretation A — The sanctions were a legitimate and partly effective instrument: Supporters argue that the regime prevented Iraq from rearming, allowed inspectors to dismantle major weapons programs, and constrained a government with a record of aggression and chemical-weapons use. They hold that Iraqi government decisions, such as the delay in accepting humanitarian arrangements, diversion of resources, and manipulation of distribution, were primary causes of civilian hardship, and that Oil-for-Food showed the Council's willingness to address it.
Interpretation B — The humanitarian cost was unacceptable and the policy flawed: Critics argue that comprehensive sanctions devastated an economy and a society already damaged by war, that the humanitarian exemptions were inadequate and slowly applied, and that the Council bore responsibility for the consequences of its own measures. They note that the regime survived, even grew stronger domestically, while ordinary Iraqis lost income and health and professionals left the country.
Interpretation C — A mixed assessment: Many scholars argue that the evidence does not support either simple account. Disarmament was substantially achieved, but the regime's survival and its manipulation of the programme limited the sanctions' political effect; civilian suffering resulted from the war, sanctions, and government policy in proportions that are difficult to separate. They see the experience as a case for more targeted sanctions.

Evidence: The debate rests on UN agency reports, household surveys, Iraqi government statistics, investigations of the programme, and accounts by humanitarian staff. These sources have serious limits. Iraqi official data were controlled by the government; the best-known surveys, including those by UNICEF in 1999, suggested sharply rising child mortality in the center and south, and figures of hundreds of thousands of excess child deaths circulated widely. Later demographic analyses, including a 2017 study published in BMJ Global Health, have questioned the reliability of the data behind the highest estimates and argued that evidence of large excess child deaths is weaker than claimed, though they do not deny serious deterioration in living conditions; these reassessments have themselves been debated. Mortality figures should therefore be presented as disputed estimates and not as settled facts.

Why it matters: The debate affects how historians interpret the effectiveness of sanctions as a tool and the extent to which humanitarian harm was foreseeable and avoidable. It also shaped later international practice, including the preference for targeted measures in place of comprehensive embargoes.

Student task: Identify one claim in this debate that is mainly factual and one that is mainly a matter of values. For each, explain what kind of evidence would support or challenge it.

Section 69.19

No-Fly Zones, Military Containment, and the Continuing Gulf Crisis

While the Council maintained sanctions, the United States, Britain, and, for a time, France enforced air exclusion zones over Iraq. The northern zone, north of the 36th parallel, was established in 1991 in connection with Operation Provide Comfort, to protect Kurds, and was later policed under the name Northern Watch. A southern zone, south of the 32nd parallel, was declared in August 1992 to protect the Shi'a population from air attack and extended to the 33rd parallel in September 1996. Coalition aircraft patrolled both zones for twelve years, and responded to Iraqi air defense activity with strikes. The zones provided real protection to the Kurdish region, which developed under its own administration, but critics also noted that the south, where the regime waged a campaign against the marsh communities, including drainage of the wetlands, received no equivalent safeguard from ground attack.

The legal basis for the zones was disputed. The governments that enforced them argued that Resolution 688 and the wider body of Council resolutions on Iraq provided justification. But Resolution 688 does not authorize force, and the Council never explicitly authorized the zones; France withdrew from the southern patrols in 1996 and the northern in 1998, and Russia and China, along with many Arab governments, regarded the zones as unauthorized. Supporters stressed the humanitarian and security rationale; opponents emphasized that unilateral enforcement of an ambiguous mandate undermined the Council's authority. Iraq challenged the zones constantly, firing at aircraft and moving air-defense units.

A series of crises tested the policy of containment, as the American approach to Iraq and Iran in the 1990s was called. In April 1993 Kuwait uncovered a plot to assassinate former President Bush during a visit, and in June the United States launched cruise missiles at the Iraqi intelligence headquarters in Baghdad. In October 1994 Iraqi troop movements near Kuwait led to a rapid American deployment, and Iraq subsequently recognized Kuwait's sovereignty and the UN-demarcated border. In August 1996, when Iraqi forces entered the Kurdish city of Irbil at the invitation of one Kurdish party in its conflict with another, the United States responded with missile strikes and extended the southern zone. Throughout the decade the United States supported Iraqi opposition groups, with limited results, and in 1998 Congress passed the Iraq Liberation Act, which made regime change a stated objective of American policy but did not authorize military action.

The inspections crisis of 1998 brought the confrontation to a head. Iraq suspended cooperation with UNSCOM in the autumn, and after a report by its chairman that Iraq was not cooperating, the United States and Britain launched four days of air and missile strikes in December 1998, known as Operation Desert Fox, targeting suspected weapons-related and security facilities. Russia, China, and France criticized the action as lacking Council authorization. Inspectors did not return, and in the following years coalition aircraft clashed regularly with Iraqi air defenses. By 2000 a growing number of states, including several Arab governments and permanent Council members, favored easing sanctions, while the United States and Britain insisted on compliance first. The Gulf War, which had been presented as a temporary operation, had produced an enduring military presence. American forces remained in Saudi Arabia, Kuwait, and elsewhere in the Gulf, and the Gulf monarchies hosted air bases, naval headquarters, and prepositioned equipment under bilateral defense agreements. For some critics in Arab and Muslim societies, including activists in Saudi Arabia, this presence was a grievance, and it was among the objections later voiced by militant groups, a subject taken up in subsequent chapters.

Section 69.20

From the Gulf Crisis to the 2003 Invasion: The Transformation of the Regional Order

By the beginning of the twenty-first century the Gulf's political order differed greatly from that of 1979. The Shah's Iran, once a pillar of American strategy, had given way to the Islamic Republic, which was hostile to Washington. Iraq had become an isolated state under sanctions, its military diminished but its government intact. The Gulf monarchies had moved from relying chiefly on their own resources and informal balances to hosting large foreign deployments. Kuwait, Saudi Arabia, Bahrain, Qatar, and the UAE signed defense cooperation agreements with the United States and other Western powers. Gulf governments were also more assertive in using their wealth to shape outcomes, financing coalitions and funding rival Arab causes, which complicates any view of them as dependents of foreign power. Meanwhile, the idea of Arab collective security, promoted by the Damascus Declaration of March 1991 among the Gulf states, Egypt, and Syria, never produced a lasting structure.

Gulf Security from the Late 1970s to 2003

What Changed

  • The informal balance based on Iran as the leading local power gave way to confrontation with Iran and a direct foreign military presence
  • Gulf monarchies created the GCC in 1981 and moved from limited collective defense to hosting large foreign forces
  • The Security Council became an active manager of Iraq's armaments, border, finances, and, in part, oil exports
  • Arab nationalism lost much of its power as an organizing principle of inter-Arab politics after one Arab state invaded another

What Stayed the Same

  • The Gulf remained central to the world's energy supply, and control of shipping and oil facilities remained a strategic concern
  • Gulf rulers continued to rely on oil revenue and to prefer to hedge among partners
  • Authoritarian governments in Iraq and Iran kept a dominant position over their societies
  • Iraq's frontier with Kuwait and its access to the sea remained sensitive issues

Who experienced change most directly: Kuwaitis and Iraqi Kurds, whose states or communities were directly invaded, and Iraqi households in the center and south, who lived with sanctions. Who experienced greater continuity: The ruling families of the Gulf monarchies, whose governments survived each crisis, and the Iraqi leadership, which retained power.

Why the pattern matters: The chapter's wars did not produce a stable settlement but a series of improvised arrangements that depended on external force and continuing confrontation. Recognizing both change and continuity avoids treating 2003 as the product of a single decision or as unconnected to the earlier history.

The path to the 2003 invasion was shaped by this inheritance but not determined by it. After the attacks of 11 September 2001, the administration of George W. Bush reconsidered the threat posed by hostile states with unconventional weapons, and in January 2002 it described Iraq, with Iran and North Korea, as an "axis of evil." Washington argued that Iraq had not complied with the disarmament resolutions and possessed or was pursuing weapons of mass destruction, and some officials also asserted links between Saddam's government and terrorist groups, a claim later investigations did not substantiate. In November 2002 the Security Council unanimously adopted Resolution 1441, which found Iraq in material breach and gave it a final opportunity to comply, and inspectors, now under UNMOVIC and the IAEA, returned to Iraq. They reported cooperation on access, with some unresolved questions, and found no evidence of reconstituted programs. The United States and Britain sought a further resolution authorizing force, which France, Russia, and others opposed, and abandoned the effort in March 2003. Most Arab governments and publics opposed war; Kuwait, however, served as the principal staging area, and Gulf states hosted facilities. The invasion began on 20 March 2003, and Baghdad fell in April. The United States and Britain argued that earlier resolutions provided authority; many governments and the UN Secretary-General, Kofi Annan, later said that the war lacked proper Council authorization.

The controversy over the stated justifications requires precision. Iraq's use of chemical weapons in the 1980s, its biological and nuclear programs as discovered in 1991 and 1995, and its obstruction of inspections are all documented. The claim that Iraq in 2002–2003 possessed active stockpiles of chemical and biological weapons and was reconstituting a nuclear program was not substantiated: the Iraq Survey Group, whose final report was published in 2004, concluded that Iraq had no such stockpiles and that its programs had largely been dismantled after 1991, though Saddam had retained the intention to rebuild capability if sanctions ended. The gap between earlier documented programs and the later claims made the war's justification a lasting source of political controversy, in the Arab world and elsewhere.

The 2003 invasion is the concluding bridge of this chapter and not its subject. The occupation, the insurgency, the sectarian conflict, and the emergence of ISIS belong to the later chapters of this textbook. What the history of 1980–2003 shows is how, over twenty-three years, the Gulf was militarized and internationalized. By 2003 the American role in the region had shifted from distant patron to direct military actor; Arab governments faced the dilemma of sharing security with a foreign power that many of their publics distrusted; and the problems of Iraqi sovereignty, political legitimacy, and foreign intervention remained unresolved. These questions would shape the contemporary Arab world examined in Chapters 70 through 72.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced a connected sequence of wars and confrontations in the Gulf between 1980 and 2003. Its central argument is that the Iran–Iraq War and the Gulf Crisis were stages in the militarization and internationalization of Gulf politics, and that their outcomes arose from the interaction of authoritarian state-building, territorial disputes, revolutionary fears, oil revenues, economic pressures, military choices, regional rivalries, and international intervention. Neither war was inevitable. Each followed from decisions by identifiable governments responding to particular pressures.

The chapter began with the Iraqi state that Saddam Hussein led. The Ba'ath Party's seizure of power in 1968, Saddam's consolidation of authority and assumption of the presidency in 1979, and the oil revenues of the 1970s created a state with large resources and a concentrated, security-dominated system of decision-making. Iraq's treatment of Shi'a political opposition and Kurdish communities combined incentives and repression, and the narrowing of advice around the president shaped the decisions of 1980.

The Iranian Revolution of 1979 transformed the strategic setting. A long-standing dispute over the Shatt al-Arab, partly settled in the Algiers Agreement of 1975, became entangled with revolutionary rhetoric, border incidents, and Iraqi fears for the stability of its own regime. Iraq's invasion on 22 September 1980 was a political choice, explained by historians through overlapping motives of territorial revision, fear of revolutionary contagion, and opportunism. It was not the result of a primordial sectarian hostility, and it was fought by Shi'a conscripts for Iraq as well as by Iranians.

The initial Iraqi advance stalled, and Iran recovered its territory by 1982, recapturing Khorramshahr in May. Iran then chose to continue the war, seeking to remove Saddam's government, which turned a defensive success into a long conflict. Through the middle years the fighting settled into attrition, with trench warfare, massive casualties, and attacks on cities and economic targets. The war consumed resources, reshaped civilian life, deepened authoritarian controls in both countries, and left Iraq with large debts to its Arab neighbors.

The war's most severe violations against civilians included Iraq's use of chemical weapons against Iranian forces and civilians, and, within Iraq, the Anfal campaign against Kurdish villagers in 1987–1988 and the chemical attack on Halabja. These were documented through United Nations investigations, captured Iraqi documents, forensic evidence, and testimony, and they have been characterized as genocide by Human Rights Watch and by the Iraqi High Tribunal. The international response at the time was muted, an outcome shaped by strategic interests.

The war became regional and international. The Gulf monarchies, which formed the GCC in 1981, financed and supported Iraq while pursuing varied policies toward Iran. The Tanker War brought American naval forces into the Gulf, and Resolution 598 of 1987 and the ceasefire of August 1988 ended the large-scale fighting without resolving the war's political questions. By the end, Iraq had the largest army in the Arab world and severe economic problems, and the Gulf states had become both its creditors and its potential targets.

Iraq's invasion of Kuwait on 2 August 1990 grew from disputes over debt, oil prices and production, the Rumaila field, and historical claims, combined with a decision-making system in which Saddam could stake the state's future on a miscalculation. Kuwait was a sovereign state, and these grievances did not justify invasion. The Security Council's response, from Resolution 660 to Resolution 678, and the formation of a broad coalition, were made possible by the end of the Cold War, and exposed deep divisions among Arab governments and publics.

The coalition victory of February 1991 liberated Kuwait but left Saddam Hussein in power. The uprisings in the south and the Kurdish north were suppressed with great violence, producing a mass exodus and a limited international humanitarian intervention in the north. Kuwait faced reconstruction, the destruction of hundreds of oil wells and serious environmental damage, and difficult questions about collaboration and the status of Palestinians and other residents. Comparing the two wars shows a shift from a regional conflict with outside support to a war led by an external coalition, and from a war ended without a political settlement to one ended with an elaborate international regime.

That regime, set out in Resolution 687, combined disarmament, inspections, border demarcation, compensation, and sanctions. Inspectors eliminated significant weapons capacity, but confrontation over access and compliance continued, and comprehensive sanctions, together with the damage of the war and the Iraqi government's own policies, produced severe hardship in the center and south. The Oil-for-Food Programme eased but did not eliminate these problems and became associated with corruption. The humanitarian cost of sanctions, and the evidence for mortality, remain disputed.

Containment through no-fly zones, strikes, and diplomatic pressure created an enduring foreign military presence in the Gulf, and it divided the Security Council. By 2003 the American role had changed from distant patron to direct military actor, and the Gulf monarchies hosted foreign forces while continuing to pursue their own interests. The 2003 invasion, justified by claims about weapons stockpiles that were not substantiated, brought the system to an end, but it left unresolved the problems of Iraqi sovereignty, political legitimacy, and foreign intervention, which later chapters take up.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how the destructive legacy of one war helped create the conditions for another, and how the second war established a long-lived system of containment and intervention. The Iran–Iraq War left behind debt, a huge army, and a leadership convinced of its own resilience; the Gulf Crisis tested those legacies and produced a regime of sanctions and inspections that lasted until 2003. Understanding these wars as linked episodes, not isolated events, avoids both the view that each was inevitable and the view that each was a mere accident.

The chapter also reveals larger processes. It shows the effects of concentrated authority, in which a small circle's decisions determined the fate of millions; the role of oil revenue in financing both state capacity and war; and the transformation of Gulf security from an arrangement dependent on regional balance and distant patrons to one with a continuous foreign military presence. It shows the Gulf monarchies as active participants, financing, hosting, and bargaining. And it shows the human dimension of these events, from conscripts and refugees to Kurdish villagers and Iraqi households under sanctions, whose experiences remind us that state decisions were lived by people.

Many of the chapter's consequences extended beyond its dates. Arab politics after 1991 was marked by the weakening of Arab nationalism as a shared project, which the peace diplomacy of Chapter 70 had to confront. The foreign military presence in the Gulf shaped political debate in the region, and the unresolved questions of Iraqi sovereignty and legitimacy lay behind the events of 2003 and after, which are examined in Chapters 71 and 72.

Finally, the chapter connects regions that are sometimes studied separately: the Gulf, the Levant, North Africa, and the wider world. Egyptian and Syrian troops in Saudi Arabia, Palestinian communities in Kuwait, Yemeni workers in the kingdom, Turkish and Iranian borders crowded with Kurdish refugees, and the Security Council's deliberations in New York all belonged to one history. It is a reminder that the Arab world's history in the late twentieth century cannot be written from a single capital or a single perspective.
