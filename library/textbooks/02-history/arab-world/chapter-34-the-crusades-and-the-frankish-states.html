Part VI — Regional Powers and the Crusading Age
34

The Crusades and the Frankish States

A History of the Arab World: From Ancient Crossroads to the Modern Arab World

Chapter Opening

In the summer of 1097, an army unlike any the eastern Mediterranean had recently seen appeared outside the walls of Nicaea in Byzantine Anatolia — tens of thousands of Latin Christian knights, foot soldiers, clergy, and pilgrims, many of them still recovering from a punishing overland march from western Europe. To the rulers of Aleppo, Damascus, Antioch, and Cairo, this force was, at first, difficult to categorize. It was not a Byzantine army, though it marched with Byzantine cooperation. It was not a single kingdom's army, since it had no single king. It called itself an army of pilgrims bound for Jerusalem, and yet it fought major battles, besieged fortified cities, and would soon carve out permanent states of its own. Contemporary Arabic writers reached for several different terms to describe these newcomers before settling, over time, on al-Faranj — the Franks — a label borrowed, appropriately enough, from the Frankish kingdoms of western Europe that had supplied so many of the expedition's leaders.

This chapter opens where Chapter 33 left off, not in western Europe. By 1095, the eastern Mediterranean was not a unified "Muslim world" awaiting confrontation with a unified "Christian world." It was a landscape of rival Seljuk princes squabbling over Aleppo and Damascus, an Abbasid caliph in Baghdad who possessed enormous religious prestige but little independent military power, a Fatimid state in Cairo that had spent decades contesting Syria and Palestine with the Seljuks, an Armenian population recently displaced into Cilicia and northern Syria, and a Byzantine emperor in Constantinople eager to recover Anatolian territory lost to Turkish forces after the battle of Manzikert in 1071. Into this fragmented and already contested world marched an armed pilgrimage that western Europeans would later call, retrospectively, the First Crusade.

The central question this chapter asks is not simply "what happened" during the First Crusade, though the narrative of marches, sieges, and battles matters and will be told in some detail. The deeper historical question is why an expedition mounted by feuding western European lords, with uncertain supply lines and no unified command, managed to seize Jerusalem in 1099 and then hold substantial territory in Syria and Palestine for nearly two centuries. Part of the answer lies in the motivations, organization, and military experience of the Crusaders themselves. But a large part of the answer, this chapter argues, lies in the very fragmentation that Chapter 33 has already described — a political landscape in which no single Muslim ruler possessed both the authority and the immediate interest to mount a united defense.

Once established, the four Frankish states — the County of Edessa, the Principality of Antioch, the Kingdom of Jerusalem, and the County of Tripoli — did not remain foreign enclaves sealed off from their surroundings. They became regional political actors, embedded in the diplomatic, commercial, and military life of Syria and Palestine. Frankish lords made truces with Muslim neighbors, paid and received tribute, allied with Damascus against Aleppo or with one Muslim ruler against another, and depended on local agricultural populations, most of them Muslim or eastern Christian, to sustain their estates. Muslim rulers, for their part, sometimes fought the Franks and sometimes negotiated with them as one more regional power among several, a pattern this chapter will trace through truces, tribute arrangements, and shifting alliances that rarely followed a simple line between "Christian" and "Muslim."

This chapter also asks students to hold several distinctions in mind throughout. Not every western European Christian in this period was a Crusader, and not every Crusader remained in the Levant after 1099 to become a permanent Frankish settler. Not every Christian living under Frankish rule was a Frank; Armenian, Greek Orthodox, Syriac, and Maronite Christians had their own distinct histories, institutions, and relationships with the new Latin rulers, often as complicated as those of the region's Muslim and Jewish populations. Muslim rulers across Syria, northern Mesopotamia, and Egypt were not a single political bloc responding with one voice, and the religious concept of jihad, while it existed well before 1099, took time to develop into the kind of unified political mobilization that would eventually threaten the Frankish states under later rulers. And throughout the period this chapter covers, religious identity did not eliminate pragmatic diplomacy: rulers on every side calculated their interests, made alliances that crossed religious lines when convenient, and treated warfare as one tool among several rather than a permanent, all-consuming holy war.

The evidence for this period is unusually rich and unusually contested. Latin chroniclers such as Fulcher of Chartres and William of Tyre wrote detailed narratives from a Frankish perspective, while Arabic historians such as Ibn al-Athir and Ibn al-Qalanisi recorded events from Damascus and Mosul. The Damascene gentleman and soldier Usama ibn Munqidh left an invaluable memoir describing decades of contact, conflict, and occasional friendship with Frankish neighbors. Archaeological remains of Frankish castles, churches, and fortified towns across modern Syria, Lebanon, Israel, the Palestinian territories, and Jordan supplement and sometimes complicate the written record. Students should read all of these sources critically, aware that chroniclers on every side wrote to justify, celebrate, or explain the actions of their own community.

By the chapter's end, students should understand the Crusades not as an isolated collision between two unified civilizations, but as the insertion of new Latin Christian states into an already fragmented, interconnected eastern Mediterranean political system — a system that Chapter 35 will show gradually reorganizing around a more unified Muslim response, and Chapter 36 will show transformed again by the arrival of the Mongols and the rise of the Mamluk Sultanate.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how the political fragmentation described in Chapter 33 shaped the conditions in which the First Crusade achieved its early successes.
  2. Trace the course of the First Crusade from its origins in western Europe through the sieges of Nicaea and Antioch to the capture of Jerusalem in 1099.
  3. Distinguish the Seljuk, Fatimid, Abbasid, Byzantine, and Armenian actors involved in the eastern Mediterranean of the 1090s, and explain why they did not act as a unified bloc.
  4. Analyze the multiple, interacting causes historians identify for the First Crusade's success, avoiding single-factor explanations.
  5. Describe the formation, government, and institutions of the four Frankish states: Edessa, Antioch, Jerusalem, and Tripoli.
  6. Distinguish Frankish settlers from other Christian communities of the Levant, including Armenian, Greek Orthodox, Syriac, and Maronite Christians.
  7. Explain how local Muslim, Jewish, and eastern Christian populations experienced Frankish rule, including continuities in agriculture, taxation, and local administration.
  8. Evaluate the role of warfare, truce, tribute, and alliance in relations between Frankish and Muslim polities, rejecting a simple permanent-conflict model.
  9. Describe the commercial role of Italian maritime cities and Levantine ports in sustaining the Frankish states and connecting them to the wider Mediterranean economy.
  10. Explain the origins, organization, and function of the military orders, and place their emergence correctly after the First Crusade rather than as a cause of it.
  11. Assess the extent and limits of cultural contact and exchange between Frankish and regional populations.
  12. Contextualize the gradual development of organized Muslim resistance to the Frankish states as a process that intensified over time, culminating in developments examined further in Chapter 35.

Historical Context

Chapter 33 examined how Seljuk Turkish military power reorganized Iraq and Syria during the eleventh century while the Abbasid caliphate in Baghdad retained religious legitimacy but exercised little direct military authority. By the 1090s, the Great Seljuk sultanate itself had fragmented following the death of Malik-Shah in 1092, leaving Syria divided among rival Turkish and Turkoman commanders. Ridwan, based in Aleppo, and his brother Duqaq, based in Damascus, feuded openly with one another. Antioch was held by the Seljuk governor Yaghi-Siyan, whose position depended on careful balancing among larger neighbors. Farther south, the Fatimid Caliphate of Cairo, examined in Chapter 31, had spent decades contesting control of Palestine with the Seljuks and had only recently reasserted authority over Jerusalem itself.

To the north, the Byzantine Empire, weakened in central and eastern Anatolia by the Turkish migration and political expansion that accelerated in the decades following the 1071 defeat at Manzikert — a longer process rather than an immediate conquest — remained a major Mediterranean power under Emperor Alexios I Komnenos. Alexios's appeal to the West for military assistance against Turkish advances — intended chiefly to recover lost Byzantine territory in Anatolia — became one of the direct triggers for Pope Urban II's call to crusade in 1095, even though the resulting expedition would soon pursue an agenda considerably more ambitious than Byzantine recovery.

This chapter does not retell the history of western European society at length; that context belongs primarily to European historiography rather than to a textbook organized around the Arab world. It draws only on the background necessary to explain why a Latin Christian armed pilgrimage set out for Jerusalem at this particular moment, and it then follows that expedition into the region this textbook studies, where it becomes a regional actor requiring the same kind of sustained, evidence-based historical attention given to the Seljuks, Fatimids, and Abbasids in preceding chapters.

Section 34.1

The Eastern Mediterranean, 1095–1097

When Pope Urban II issued his call at Clermont in November 1095, the region toward which the resulting expedition would eventually march was, as Chapter 33 established, anything but unified. Seljuk Syria had fractured following the death of Malik-Shah in 1092 into rival principalities centered on Aleppo and Damascus, governed respectively by the brothers Ridwan and Duqaq, whose mutual hostility repeatedly prevented coordinated action even against common threats. Antioch, a formidable fortified city commanding the routes between Anatolia and Syria, was held by the Seljuk governor Yaghi-Siyan, whose authority rested on a delicate balance among larger neighbors rather than on secure, independent power.

Farther south, the Fatimid Caliphate retained a strong administrative and naval base in Egypt and continued to contest control of Palestine, a rivalry examined in Chapter 31 that had already produced decades of intermittent warfare between Fatimid and Seljuk forces over Jerusalem, Damascus, and the coastal towns of the Levant. The Abbasid caliph in Baghdad, whose religious authority Chapter 33 described as largely detached from direct military command, possessed no army capable of intervening in Syrian or Palestinian affairs on short notice. Armenian populations, many displaced from the Armenian highlands by earlier Byzantine and Seljuk military campaigns, had established new communities and small principalities in Cilicia and parts of northern Syria, forming yet another distinct political and religious presence in the region the Crusaders would soon enter. The Byzantine Empire, though weakened in Anatolia since Manzikert, remained a major regional power under Alexios I Komnenos, with its own strategic interests that only partly overlapped with those of the Crusader leadership.

No single authority in this landscape possessed both the military capacity and the immediate incentive to organize a unified defense against a large foreign army approaching from Anatolia. This condition — not the personal failings of any single ruler, and not a uniquely "disorganized" character attributed to the Muslim world in older, biased historiography — is essential to understanding what followed.

Section 34.2

Why Did Crusading Begin?

Explaining the First Crusade's origins requires brief attention to conditions in western Europe, even though this textbook's primary subject lies farther east. Urban II's call at Clermont combined several threads: a broader papal reform movement within which historians situate the appeal, though Urban's own precise personal intentions remain a matter of scholarly interpretation rather than settled fact; a well-established tradition of pilgrimage to Jerusalem that had continued, with interruptions, for centuries, and which provided the expedition's basic devotional and penitential framework; genuine religious enthusiasm and the promise of spiritual reward, formalized through the granting of indulgences; and the immediate occasion of Alexios I's appeal for military assistance against Turkish advances in Anatolia, which gave the call a concrete strategic context.

For participants, motivations are best understood as varied and often mixed rather than reducible to a single type. Penitential and devotional commitment ran through participants of differing rank and means, and modern scholarship has substantially complicated an older assumption that crusading chiefly attracted landless younger sons seeking fortune abroad; surviving charters and family records instead show participants of varied family position, including established landholders who mortgaged or sold property to finance the journey, since crusading itself imposed very heavy financial costs, often equivalent to several years of a knight's income. For some participants, prospects of status, booty, patronage, or eventual settlement accompanied penitential and devotional motives, but these incentives varied sharply from one individual to another, and historians caution against reducing the Crusaders' motivations to either purely religious zeal or purely material ambition.

Urban's call produced not a single organized army but several distinct contingents, led by figures such as Godfrey of Bouillon, Raymond of Toulouse, Bohemond of Taranto, and Baldwin of Boulogne, who gathered separately in western Europe and converged on Constantinople before crossing into Anatolia. Their rivalries, differing resources, and independent ambitions would shape the expedition's course throughout the campaign that followed.

Section 34.3

The First Crusade Crosses Anatolia

Arriving at Constantinople in stages during late 1096 and early 1097, the Crusader leaders entered an uneasy partnership with Alexios I, who sought oaths and commitments from the Crusader princes, including promises by most leaders to restore former Byzantine territories, in exchange for guides, supplies, and naval support; the exact terms differed from one leader to another, and Raymond of Toulouse notably resisted formal homage, offering Alexios a more limited pledge instead. Byzantine and Crusader forces cooperated in the successful siege of Nicaea in June 1097, a joint operation in which Byzantine diplomacy, rather than a prolonged Crusader assault, ultimately secured the city's surrender — a resolution that left many Crusaders resentful at being denied the opportunity to plunder a city they had besieged at considerable cost.

The march across Anatolia that followed exposed the Crusader army to Seljuk Turkish light cavalry tactics unfamiliar to most western European soldiers. At the Battle of Dorylaeum in July 1097, a Seljuk force under Kilij Arslan nearly overwhelmed the vanguard of the Crusader army before the arrival of the main body secured a hard-fought victory. The crossing of central Anatolia that followed proved brutally difficult: intense heat, scarce water, and inadequate supplies killed significant numbers of soldiers, pilgrims, and animals, and historians treat this stretch of the campaign as a serious test of the expedition's cohesion well before it reached Syria.

Byzantine-Crusader cooperation, cordial at Nicaea, grew steadily more strained as the campaign proceeded into Syria, where Byzantine strategic interests and Crusader ambitions increasingly diverged — a tension that would resurface directly in the dispute over Antioch.

Section 34.4

Antioch: Siege, Famine, and Political Fragmentation

The Crusader army reached Antioch in October 1097 and began a siege that would last, in its various phases, for nearly nine months. Antioch's massive fortifications, built on a scale that dwarfed most contemporary western European defenses, made direct assault impractical, and the besiegers themselves soon suffered severe shortages of food as winter set in, a period chroniclers on both sides describe in terms of genuine famine and mass suffering among the Crusader host.

Yaghi-Siyan, Antioch's governor, appealed repeatedly to neighboring Muslim rulers for relief, but the response illustrates precisely the fragmentation Chapter 33 described. Ridwan of Aleppo and Duqaq of Damascus, still locked in mutual rivalry, each mounted only limited and uncoordinated relief efforts that Crusader forces defeated separately in the field. Only in the spring of 1098 did a larger relief force under Kerbogha, the Turkish atabeg of Mosul, finally advance toward Antioch — but by the time Kerbogha arrived in early June, the city had already fallen to the Crusaders through the treachery of a tower commander persuaded to open a gate, an event Latin and Arabic sources both record, though with differing emphases and details.

The Crusaders, now inside Antioch but themselves besieged by Kerbogha's substantial army and again suffering severe hunger, faced a genuine crisis. Their eventual sortie against Kerbogha's forces in June 1098 succeeded, historians generally agree, partly because of Crusader desperation and cohesion at a critical moment and partly because Kerbogha's own coalition, assembled from rival Muslim contingents with competing interests, proved unable to sustain unified command under pressure — a second, sharper demonstration of the costs of the region's political fragmentation. Antioch became the Crusade's most consequential proving ground, and its capture established the Principality of Antioch under Bohemond of Taranto, who claimed the city for himself in a decision that produced lasting friction with Byzantium, which regarded Antioch as rightfully Byzantine territory under the earlier oaths sworn at Constantinople.

Section 34.5

Jerusalem before 1099

A crucial development often overlooked in popular narratives of the Crusade occurred in 1098, while the Crusader army was still occupied at Antioch: Fatimid forces from Egypt took advantage of the ongoing chaos in Syria to retake Jerusalem from its Seljuk-aligned Artuqid governor, restoring the city to Fatimid control for the first time in over two decades. This meant that when the Crusader army finally approached Jerusalem in the summer of 1099, the defenders they would face were Fatimid, not Seljuk, and the eventual conquest of the city cannot be understood simply as a Crusader victory over "the Seljuks" or, more broadly and inaccurately, over a unified Muslim adversary.

Fatimid authorities and Crusader representatives engaged in negotiations, both before and during the march toward Jerusalem, over the possibility of cooperation against their common Seljuk rivals and over possible territorial arrangements in Palestine; the precise terms under discussion remain disputed among historians, with some reconstructions suggesting the Fatimids may have been prepared to cede parts of Palestine to the Crusaders while retaining Jerusalem itself, and others suggesting broader territorial questions, including Jerusalem, were at least raised. No durable agreement resulted, and the Crusader army continued its advance on the city. This episode underscores a theme this chapter will return to repeatedly: negotiation, not only warfare, was a standing option available to rulers on every side of this conflict, even at its most intense moments, even though in this particular case negotiation did not prevent the eventual siege.

Section 34.6

The Capture of Jerusalem, 1099

The Crusader army besieged Jerusalem beginning in early June 1099, facing severe shortages of water and provisions in the arid country surrounding the city. After several weeks of preparation, including the construction of siege towers from timber obtained with difficulty in the region, the Crusaders breached the city's defenses on July 15, 1099.

What followed was a period of large-scale killing that both Latin and Arabic sources describe, though with figures that later medieval chroniclers on both sides inflated for rhetorical effect and that modern historians treat with considerable caution. Muslim residents, including many who had taken refuge in the al-Aqsa Mosque compound, and members of the city's Jewish community, some of whom sought shelter in a synagogue that was set ablaze, were killed in substantial numbers during and immediately after the assault; the city's small Eastern Christian population, some of whom had reportedly been expelled by the Fatimid garrison before the siege on suspicion of disloyalty, largely escaped the massacre that befell Muslims and Jews. Historians debate the precise scale of the killing — medieval chroniclers' claims of tens of thousands of dead in a city whose total population was likely considerably smaller than such figures imply should not be taken as precise demographic data — but the consensus among modern scholars is that substantial, deliberate killing of noncombatants took place, alongside extensive looting, and that the event became a defining, and starkly differently remembered, moment in both Christian and Muslim historical memory.

The capture of Jerusalem completed the Crusade's stated religious objective and established the Kingdom of Jerusalem, with Godfrey of Bouillon assuming leadership under the modest title of Advocate of the Holy Sepulchre rather than king, a title his successor Baldwin I would set aside in favor of outright kingship the following year.

Section 34.7

Why Did the First Crusade Succeed?

No single factor adequately explains the First Crusade's improbable success. Crusader cohesion at decisive moments — the sortie from Antioch against Kerbogha, the final assault on Jerusalem — mattered, as did the genuine military experience many participants brought from decades of warfare in western Europe, and the intense religious motivation that sustained the expedition through catastrophic losses of life from combat, disease, and starvation. Byzantine logistical and naval support proved valuable, particularly in the campaign's earlier Anatolian phase, even as Byzantine-Crusader relations deteriorated over Antioch.

Equally important, however, was the political fragmentation this chapter and Chapter 33 have both emphasized. Seljuk Syria's division between rival, mutually hostile princes in Aleppo and Damascus prevented early coordinated resistance. The Fatimid-Seljuk rivalry over Palestine meant Jerusalem's eventual defenders were themselves recently installed and had not had time to fully consolidate their position. Kerbogha's relief coalition at Antioch dissolved under pressure precisely because it was an alliance of convenience among rulers with competing interests rather than a unified command. The Abbasid caliphate's religious authority translated into no meaningful military intervention. No ruler in the region initially treated the Crusader advance as a threat serious enough to override longstanding local rivalries.

Historians today generally reject both older explanations that credited European military or religious superiority alone and equally simplistic explanations that attribute the outcome solely to Muslim disunity. The most persuasive accounts treat Crusader cohesion and regional fragmentation as interacting causes: a determined, religiously motivated force with real military capability exploited, and was aided by, a political landscape unable to mount a unified response.

Section 34.8

The Formation of the Frankish States

Between 1098 and roughly 1109, four distinct Frankish states took shape across northern Mesopotamia, Syria, and Palestine. The County of Edessa, established by Baldwin of Boulogne in 1098 even before Antioch's fall, was the first and geographically most exposed, situated well east of the other three and surrounded by Muslim and Armenian territories. The Principality of Antioch, claimed by Bohemond of Taranto after the city's capture, controlled a strategically vital region linking Anatolia to Syria. The Kingdom of Jerusalem, established after 1099, became the most prestigious and, eventually, the most institutionally developed of the four. The County of Tripoli, the last to be fully secured, emerged after a lengthy siege led by Raymond of Toulouse and his successors and completed in 1109.

Contemporaries did possess a collective geographical conception of these lands, which the Franks themselves called Outremer — "beyond the sea" — a term reflecting the perspective of western Europe looking eastward across the Mediterranean. Modern historians also commonly use "Crusader States" or "Frankish States" as analytical labels. A shared geographical label, however, should not be mistaken for unified statehood: the four territories never merged into a single political entity, and participants identified primarily with their specific principality, county, or kingdom, and with kinship and feudal ties to specific lords, rather than with a single unified government spanning all four. The four states cooperated at times but also pursued independent, sometimes conflicting policies toward their Muslim, Byzantine, and Armenian neighbors throughout the twelfth century.

Section 34.9

Who Were the Franks?

Arabic writers used the term al-Faranj broadly for western European Latin Christians in general, a usage that predated the Crusades and was not limited to permanent settlers. In this chapter, "Frank" refers most often to the Latin Christian population of the four Levantine states — settlers, knights, clergy, merchants, and pilgrims who arrived from western Europe during and after the First Crusade and who, in the case of the settled population, established and governed the states described above — while students should remember that the term itself, in Arabic usage, was not confined to this specific population. It is essential to distinguish Franks from the region's other Christian communities, which possessed distinct histories, liturgical traditions, and relationships to the new Latin rulers. Armenian Christians, many concentrated in Edessa and Cilicia, had their own political structures and sometimes allied closely with, and intermarried into, Frankish noble families, most famously through Baldwin of Boulogne's own marriage to an Armenian noblewoman. Greek Orthodox Christians, concentrated in cities with longstanding Byzantine religious ties, maintained their own church hierarchy, often uneasily, alongside the Latin ecclesiastical institutions the Franks established. Syriac Christian and Maronite communities, with their own distinct liturgical and institutional traditions, likewise remained religiously and socially separate from Frankish Catholicism even where they lived under Frankish political authority.

The Frankish population itself was never demographically dominant; estimates suggest Franks made up a distinct minority, concentrated disproportionately in coastal cities, castles, and certain rural estates, while the overwhelming majority of the population across all four states remained Muslim, Jewish, or non-Latin Christian, continuing to speak Arabic, Syriac, or Armenian in daily life.

Section 34.10

Government and Lordship

The Kingdom of Jerusalem developed a monarchy advised by a High Court of major barons, alongside a network of baronial lordships whose holders owed military service and counsel to the crown in exchange for landed fiefs, arrangements that echoed, without exactly replicating, contemporary western European practice. Students should resist importing a rigid, textbook model of "European feudalism" wholesale onto the Frankish states; historians increasingly describe Frankish institutions as adaptations shaped by local conditions, existing regional administrative practices inherited in part from Fatimid, Seljuk, and Byzantine predecessors, and the practical demands of governing a population in which Franks were a minority.

Taxation drew, in numerous documented instances, on preexisting fiscal categories and administrative personnel familiar to the region's population, including continued reliance on local Arabic-speaking officials for tax assessment and collection in many areas. Ecclesiastical institutions — a Latin patriarchate in Jerusalem, cathedral chapters, and monastic foundations — provided religious authority and, in places, significant landed wealth, while castles and fortified towns, discussed further in Section 34.14, anchored military control across the kingdom's often thinly garrisoned countryside.

Section 34.11

Local Populations under Frankish Rule

For the majority Muslim, Jewish, and eastern Christian populations of the Frankish states, daily life continued in many respects along lines already established before 1099. Village agriculture, the cultivation of grain, olives, and other Mediterranean crops, and local taxation arrangements frequently continued with substantial administrative continuity, since Frankish lords, dependent on agricultural revenue and lacking the personnel to replace an entire local administrative class, often retained existing village headmen, tax assessors, and customary land arrangements rather than imposing wholly new systems.

This continuity should not be mistaken for equality or comfort. Non-Frankish populations, particularly rural Muslim peasants, typically occupied a subordinate legal and social status, owed specific dues and labor obligations to Frankish lords, and lacked full access to the kingdom's central political institutions, which remained the preserve of the Latin nobility, clergy, and, to a lesser extent, merchant class. Jewish communities occupied their own distinct legal and social position across the Frankish states, generally excluded from landholding and political office but often engaged in commerce and craft production. Their experience varied sharply by city: Jerusalem's Jewish community, already present before 1099, was devastated in the violence that accompanied the city's capture, and the city did not maintain an established resident Jewish community for much of the period of Frankish rule that followed — a Jewish traveler visiting the city more than a century later still described it as lacking a settled community — while Jewish populations continued or later grew in other Frankish-controlled centers, particularly Acre and Tyre, which remained significant centers of Jewish life and commerce. Urban Muslim populations fared variably: some cities saw significant displacement or flight following conquest, particularly Jerusalem itself in 1099, while others, especially those taken through negotiated surrender rather than storm, retained larger resident Muslim populations under Frankish rule.

Section 34.12

War, Truce, Tribute, and Alliance

One of this chapter's central corrections to popular understanding concerns the character of Frankish-Muslim political relations after 1099. These relations did not follow a permanent, unbroken line of Christian-versus-Muslim warfare. Truces, often renewed periodically, were a normal instrument of diplomacy on both sides, allowing agricultural production, trade, and travel to continue even amid broader tension. Tribute arrangements, in which weaker neighboring Muslim towns or principalities paid regular sums to Frankish rulers, or in some periods Frankish lords paid tribute to stronger Muslim neighbors, functioned as a pragmatic alternative to costly warfare.

Alliances crossing religious lines were common and unremarkable to contemporaries operating within this political system. Frankish rulers allied with Damascus against Aleppo, or with one Muslim faction against another Frankish rival; Muslim rulers similarly allied with Frankish states against rival Muslim neighbors when it served their interests, a pattern already visible in the maneuvering among Aleppo, Damascus, and the early Frankish states during the 1110s and 1120s. This behavior should not be read as evidence that religious identity was unimportant — religious rhetoric, patronage, and institution-building mattered greatly to rulers on every side — but rather that religious identity coexisted with, rather than eliminated, calculated political self-interest.

Section 34.13

Trade and the Mediterranean Economy

The Frankish states depended heavily on maritime commerce, and their survival was closely tied to the commercial ambitions of the Italian maritime republics — chiefly Genoa, Pisa, and Venice — which provided naval support during several of the sieges that secured Levantine coastal cities and received, in return, substantial commercial privileges, tax exemptions, and dedicated merchant quarters in ports such as Acre, Tyre, and Tripoli.

These ports became major nodes connecting the Frankish states to the broader Mediterranean economy and, through Levantine and Egyptian intermediaries, to Indian Ocean trade networks examined further in Chapter 38. Merchants carried spices, textiles, sugar, and other luxury goods westward, while pilgrimage traffic, which continued and in some respects intensified after the establishment of the Kingdom of Jerusalem, brought substantial numbers of western European visitors whose spending sustained hospices, markets, and transport industries in Jerusalem and the coastal cities. Agricultural exports, including sugar cultivated on Frankish-controlled estates using techniques and labor arrangements adapted from earlier regional practice, added a further economic dimension to the kingdom's integration into Mediterranean commerce.

Section 34.14

Fortifications and Military Institutions

Given their demographic minority status and extended, often exposed frontiers, the Frankish states relied heavily on fortification. Castles such as Krak des Chevaliers, Kerak, and Saone, along with fortified towns and city walls, anchored military control across thinly garrisoned rural territory, allowing relatively small numbers of Frankish knights and men-at-arms to hold extensive lordships. Cavalry remained the core of Frankish military power in the field, though logistics, including the supply of horses, fodder, and provisions across long and often arid campaign routes, posed continual challenges familiar from the environmental patterns discussed in Chapter 3.

Military manpower remained a persistent constraint throughout the Frankish states' history, since the settler population available for military service never matched the scale required to defend extended frontiers against determined opposition, a structural vulnerability that would become increasingly significant as Muslim political consolidation proceeded in the twelfth century, examined in Chapter 35.

Section 34.15

The Military Orders

The Knights Templar, founded around 1119, and the Knights Hospitaller, whose military role developed somewhat later out of an earlier hospice foundation in Jerusalem, emerged as distinctive institutions combining monastic religious discipline with full-time military service. Students should note the chronology carefully: both orders developed after the First Crusade's success and the establishment of the Frankish states, and neither order caused or contributed to the events of 1097–1099. Their significance belongs to the subsequent history of the Frankish states rather than to the conquest narrative itself.

Supported by extensive landholdings, papal privileges, and donations from across western Europe, the military orders became major landholders and garrison forces in their own right, operating substantial castle networks and providing a standing military capability that did not depend on the more limited manpower of individual baronial lordships. Their wealth and semi-independent status would, over the following century, make them significant political as well as military actors within the Frankish states.

Section 34.16

Cultural Contact and Its Limits

Contact between Franks and the region's existing populations produced genuine, documented exchange alongside persistent legal and social boundaries. Some Frankish settlers adopted elements of regional dress, cuisine, bathing practices, and architectural style; Arabic loanwords entered the vocabulary of Frankish administration in places; and translation activity, particularly in fields such as medicine, drew on regional expertise that Frankish physicians and patients alike came to respect, a theme Usama ibn Munqidh's memoir illustrates directly.

At the same time, intermarriage between Franks and Muslim or eastern Christian populations, while documented in some cases — most prominently among the early generation of Frankish leaders who married Armenian noblewomen — remained legally and socially restricted, particularly across the Frankish-Muslim religious boundary. Legal status, landholding rights, and access to political office remained firmly stratified along religious and ethnic lines throughout the Frankish states' history. Historians accordingly caution against romanticizing the Levant under Frankish rule as a society of easy coexistence, just as they caution against depicting it as a society defined solely by permanent civilizational hatred; the evidence instead supports a more complicated picture of practical, often pragmatic contact operating within firm legal and social limits.

Section 34.17

Muslim Responses before the Zengids

Opposition to the Frankish states in the decades immediately following 1099 remained local, uncoordinated, and often subordinated to preexisting regional rivalries, echoing the fragmentation that had aided the First Crusade's success in the first place. The religious concept of jihad, understood broadly as a duty to defend or extend the territory of Islam, was not new in this period and had deep roots in earlier Islamic legal and religious thought, examined in Chapter 28. What was new, and what developed only gradually over the early twelfth century, was the political mobilization of jihad rhetoric as an organizing principle capable of uniting multiple Muslim rulers around sustained anti-Frankish military campaigns, rather than remaining, as it largely did in the years immediately after 1099, one rhetorical resource among several available to individual rulers pursuing largely local and dynastic goals.

Religious mobilization against the Franks began far earlier than political mobilization. The Damascene scholar Ali ibn Tahir al-Sulami was already preaching and writing an explicit program of anti-Frankish jihad as early as around 1105, barely six years after the fall of Jerusalem, calling on the Muslims of Syria to set aside their rivalries and unite against the Frankish states. His appeal, and those of other scholars, preachers, and poets in Damascus, Aleppo, and Baghdad over the following decades, criticized Muslim rulers who prioritized local rivalries over the defense of Islamic territory. This early preaching had limited immediate political effect, since the rulers it addressed remained divided by the same rivalries Chapter 33 described. Zengi's unification of Mosul and Aleppo in the 1120s and 1130s represented a significant step toward the political consolidation al-Sulami had called for, though historians generally locate the more systematic integration of jihad rhetoric with sustained state policy somewhat later, under Zengi's son Nur al-Din, examined fully in Chapter 35.

Section 34.18

Edessa and the Changing Balance of Power

Of the four Frankish states, the County of Edessa was consistently the most exposed, lying well to the east of the others and surrounded by Muslim and Armenian territories, while its limited manpower, political isolation, and often difficult relations with neighboring Frankish powers complicated the defense of its extensive territory. Its exposed position made it an early and natural target as regional Muslim political consolidation gathered pace.

That consolidation began in earnest with Zengi, a Turkish atabeg who established control over Mosul in 1127 and Aleppo in 1128, uniting under a single ruler two of the major Muslim power centers whose earlier rivalry had so hampered coordinated resistance to the First Crusade. Zengi's long, patient buildup of military and political power culminated in his capture of Edessa in December 1144 — the first loss of one of the four principal Frankish capitals and the beginning of the County of Edessa's collapse, though its remaining territories did not vanish overnight — an event that sent shockwaves through both the Frankish states and western Europe and prompted the Second Crusade. The fall of Edessa marks the point at which this chapter's narrative ends and Chapter 35's narrative of Zengid, and eventually Ayyubid, political consolidation begins.

Historical Vocabulary
Frank, Crusader, and Outremer

Frank (Arabic al-Faranj) refers to the Latin Christian settlers and their descendants who established and governed the four Levantine states discussed in this chapter. The term derives from the medieval Frankish kingdoms of western Europe, reflecting the disproportionate role of French-speaking nobility among the Crusade's leadership, though participants came from across Latin Christendom.

Crusader is a later historiographical term; contemporaries more often spoke of pilgrims, an armed pilgrimage, or simply "the army of God." Historians use "Crusader" as a convenient modern label while remaining aware that it imposes a retrospective coherence contemporaries did not necessarily perceive.

Outremer ("overseas"), a term used by the Franks themselves, referred collectively to the Frankish territories of the Levant as seen from a western European perspective — a reminder that these states were, for their Latin Christian inhabitants, a distant frontier requiring continual reinforcement, communication, and supply from across the Mediterranean.

Historical Vocabulary
Jihad before and after 1099

Jihad, broadly meaning struggle or striving, carries multiple meanings in Islamic thought, including inner spiritual struggle and outer defense or extension of Islamic territory through legitimate military action, developed in the legal and theological traditions examined in Chapter 28.

Military jihad against the Frankish states existed as a rhetorical and religious resource almost immediately after 1099, articulated explicitly by the Damascene scholar al-Sulami as early as around 1105. Its political mobilization into unified, sustained state campaigns, however, developed only gradually over the following decades: Zengi's consolidation of Mosul and Aleppo in the 1120s and 1130s marked an important step, while historians generally identify Zengi's son Nur al-Din as the ruler who integrated jihad rhetoric most systematically with sustained political and military policy, examined in Chapter 35. Students should avoid assuming that jihad automatically or immediately produced a unified Muslim military response in 1099; the historical record instead shows early religious preaching well ahead of the political consolidation needed to act on it.

Primary Source Window

Modern paraphrase of Usama ibn Munqidh's account of praying in Jerusalem, recalling an episode probably around 1140

Creator: Usama ibn Munqidh, Damascene nobleman, soldier, diplomat, and author · Date: the episode recalled probably occurred around 1140; the memoir itself, the Kitab al-I'tibar ("Book of Contemplation" or "Book of Learning by Example"), was composed late in Usama's life, around 1183 · Type: Retrospective autobiographical memoir combining anecdote, moral reflection, and literary convention

Context: Usama, a member of the ruling family of Shaizar in Syria, visited Jerusalem on friendly terms with the Knights Templar, who allowed him to use a small adjoining space near the al-Aqsa Mosque, then in Templar hands, for his prayers.

According to Usama's own retelling, while he was praying there one day, a newly arrived Frank — unfamiliar with local custom — seized him, turned him forcibly to face east in the Christian manner, and insisted this was the correct way to pray. The Templars he knew intervened at once, pulled the man away, and apologized to Usama, explaining that the man was a recent arrival who had never before seen anyone pray in the Muslim manner.

Analysis: This episode captures, in a single brief scene, both the real accommodation some Franks extended to Muslim visitors of standing and the persistent friction, ignorance, and boundary-testing that coexisted with it. The Templars' familiarity with Usama and their intervention on his behalf illustrate genuine personal relationships built over time; the newcomer's forcible correction illustrates how differently a recent arrival from western Europe, without such contact, might respond. Students should recognize that Usama wrote this episode decades later, selecting and shaping it for moral and literary effect as part of a longer memoir, and that his elite, male, Damascene perspective does not represent the experience of the region's peasantry, women, or urban poor. The episode nonetheless offers one of the most specific and often-cited windows into everyday Frankish-Muslim contact available to historians.

Questions for students:

  1. What does this episode suggest about the range of possible relationships between Franks and their Muslim neighbors?
  2. Why should students read a retrospective memoir, composed decades after the event it describes, differently than a contemporary administrative document?
  3. Whose experiences does Usama's account leave out, and why does that matter?
  4. How does this single episode contain both accommodation and friction at once, and what does that suggest about generalizing from anecdote?
Historical Debate

Why Did the First Crusade Succeed?

The question: Was the First Crusade's success best explained by the cohesion, religious motivation, and military effectiveness of the Crusaders themselves, or by the political fragmentation of the eastern Mediterranean world they entered?

Interpretation A — Crusader cohesion and capability: This interpretation emphasizes the Crusaders' genuine military experience, the sustaining power of religious conviction through catastrophic hardship, effective siege tactics, and moments of decisive battlefield cohesion, particularly the sortie at Antioch and the final assault on Jerusalem.
Interpretation B — Regional political fragmentation: This interpretation emphasizes the divided Seljuk princedoms of Aleppo and Damascus, the Fatimid-Seljuk rivalry over Palestine, the uncoordinated relief efforts at Antioch, and the absence of any authority, including the Abbasid caliphate, capable of organizing unified resistance.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on the same narrative sources — Latin chronicles such as those of Fulcher of Chartres and Arabic histories such as those of Ibn al-Athir and Ibn al-Qalanisi — but weigh differently the relative significance of Crusader agency versus regional circumstance.

Why it matters: This textbook treats the two interpretations as interacting rather than competing explanations. A determined, religiously motivated, militarily capable force achieved what it did in significant part because it entered a political landscape unable to mount a unified response — and that landscape's fragmentation was itself a direct consequence of the developments examined in Chapter 33.

Student task: Using evidence from Sections 34.1, 34.4, and 34.7, construct an argument that explains the First Crusade's success without relying on either explanation alone.

Multiple Perspectives

Life in a Divided Levant

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns historians have identified across many sources. They are not quotations from any single named historical actor.

From the perspective of a Frankish knight newly granted a fief near Jerusalem: Holding this land requires constant vigilance, dependable revenue from tenants who do not share my language or faith, and careful calculation of which neighboring lords, Frankish or Muslim, might prove useful allies against a common rival.
From the perspective of a Muslim merchant in Damascus: The Franks are a new and sometimes dangerous presence on the roads to the coast, yet trade continues, truces are renewed, and a shrewd merchant learns to navigate both Frankish tolls and Muslim rivalries without letting either interrupt profitable commerce.
From the perspective of an Armenian Christian resident of Edessa: Frankish rule has brought new lords who share my Christian faith but not my language, liturgy, or customs; some of my community's leading families have found advantage through marriage and alliance with the newcomers, while others watch warily for signs of Latin encroachment on our own church.
From the perspective of a Muslim peasant farming land now held by a Frankish lord: The rhythms of planting and harvest have changed little; what has changed is the identity of the lord who claims a share of the crop, and I have learned which local officials, many still Arabic-speaking as before, actually determine how much I must pay.
From the perspective of an Italian merchant at Acre: This port has become essential to my city's fortunes; the privileges our fleet secured through naval support during the conquest give us quarters, exemptions, and access to goods flowing from Egypt and farther east that no rival can easily match.
Continuity and Change Over Time

The Levant Before and After 1099

What Changed

  • Political rule in Jerusalem, Antioch, Edessa, and Tripoli passed to Latin Christian lords and a new Latin ecclesiastical hierarchy
  • New castles, fortified towns, and military institutions, including the military orders, reshaped the region's defensive landscape
  • Italian maritime republics gained substantial commercial privileges and dedicated quarters in major ports
  • Jerusalem's demographic composition shifted sharply after the 1099 conquest and subsequent settlement

What Stayed the Same

  • Village agriculture and many local taxation and administrative arrangements continued with substantial personnel and practical continuity
  • Arabic remained the everyday language of the majority population across all four Frankish states
  • Regional trade routes and commercial patterns linking Syria, Palestine, and Egypt persisted and, in places, intensified
  • Political fragmentation among neighboring Muslim rulers persisted for several decades before giving way to the consolidation examined in Chapter 35

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both change and continuity prevents treating 1099 as a total civilizational rupture; for most of the region's population, political authority changed while the underlying rhythms of agricultural, commercial, and administrative life often continued.

Cause and Effect

The Fall of Edessa, 1144

Long-term causes

Edessa's exposed geographic position east of the Euphrates, its political isolation from the other three Frankish states, chronically limited manpower relative to the territory it needed to defend, and strained relations with neighboring Frankish powers that left it without reliable regional allies, together with the gradual political consolidation of Muslim Syria and northern Mesopotamia that Zengi's rise represented.

Short-term causes

Zengi's patient buildup of military resources after uniting Mosul and Aleppo, combined with the absence of Edessa's count, Joscelin II, from the city during Zengi's advance in late 1144, though the city itself was sufficiently fortified to require a real and sustained siege rather than falling immediately.

Effect

Edessa fell to Zengi's forces in December 1144, marking the first loss of one of the four principal Frankish capitals and the beginning of the County of Edessa's collapse, while prompting alarm across the Frankish states and western Europe.

Historical complexity

The fall of Edessa should not be read as evidence that Frankish rule was collapsing generally; Antioch, Jerusalem, and Tripoli remained secure for decades afterward. It marks instead a specific consequence of Edessa's particular vulnerability and the beginning of a broader, gradual shift in the regional balance of power examined fully in Chapter 35.

Historical complexity: The event prompted the Second Crusade in Europe, but that later expedition's failures belong to the broader history of crusading rather than to this chapter's Levantine focus.

Everyday Life and Social History

Ordinary Life across the Frankish States

For most inhabitants of the Frankish states, whether Muslim, Jewish, or eastern Christian, daily life continued to revolve around agricultural cycles, market days, and religious observance largely unchanged from earlier generations. Village households cultivated grain, olives, and grapes; artisans in cities such as Jerusalem, Nablus, and Tyre continued established crafts; and religious sites sacred to multiple traditions, including Jerusalem's holy places, continued to draw pilgrims and visitors of several faiths, even as access and control shifted with political authority.

Frankish settler households, smaller in number but often wealthier, adapted in various documented ways to regional conditions: adopting local agricultural techniques for crops such as sugar cane, employing local labor and administrative expertise, and, in some households, incorporating regional cuisine, textiles, and even bathing customs into daily life. Women's experience varied considerably by community and status: Frankish noblewomen could, in some cases, inherit and hold fiefs directly, a pattern with fewer close parallels among the peasant and urban populations of the kingdom's Muslim and Jewish communities, where women's legal and economic roles followed distinct local and religious traditions predating the Frankish conquest. Historians reconstruct much of this social history from a combination of administrative and legal records, archaeological evidence of households and workshops, and the retrospective anecdotes of memoirists such as Usama ibn Munqidh.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the First Crusade and the establishment of the four Frankish states not as an isolated confrontation between a unified Christian West and a unified Muslim East, but as the insertion of a new Latin Christian political force into the already fragmented eastern Mediterranean world that Chapter 33 described. Seljuk Syria's division between rival princes in Aleppo and Damascus, the Fatimid-Seljuk rivalry over Palestine, and the Abbasid caliphate's limited direct military power together created conditions in which no single authority could mount a unified defense against the advancing Crusader army.

The chapter traced the Crusade's course from Pope Urban II's 1095 call at Clermont through the difficult crossing of Anatolia, the prolonged and consequential siege of Antioch, and the eventual capture of Jerusalem in 1099, emphasizing at each stage the interaction between Crusader cohesion and regional political fragmentation. The Fatimid recapture of Jerusalem shortly before the Crusaders' arrival was shown to be essential to understanding who actually defended the city in 1099, correcting a common oversimplification that treats the conflict as purely a Crusader-Seljuk confrontation.

Students examined the formation and governance of the four Frankish states — Edessa, Antioch, Jerusalem, and Tripoli — and the careful distinctions historians draw between Franks and other Christian communities of the region, including Armenian, Greek Orthodox, Syriac, and Maronite populations. The chapter emphasized that Franks remained a demographic minority throughout the states they governed, and that local Muslim, Jewish, and eastern Christian populations experienced significant continuity in agricultural, administrative, and commercial life alongside genuine subordination in legal and political status.

A central theme throughout has been the rejection of a simple, permanent Christian-versus-Muslim conflict model. Truces, tribute, and cross-religious alliances were normal features of Frankish-Muslim political relations, coexisting with real warfare and religious rhetoric on both sides. The chapter's Multiple Perspectives and Primary Source features, especially the memoir of Usama ibn Munqidh, illustrated the genuine complexity of contact between Frankish settlers and their neighbors.

The chapter also traced the gradual, rather than immediate, development of organized Muslim resistance to the Frankish states, distinguishing early religious preaching of anti-Frankish jihad, articulated by scholars such as al-Sulami within a decade of Jerusalem's fall, from the much slower political mobilization into unified military campaigns. That political consolidation began meaningfully with Zengi's rise in the 1120s and 1130s, culminated in his capture of Edessa in 1144, and would be integrated still more systematically by his son Nur al-Din, examined in Chapter 35.

Finally, the chapter examined the economic and military institutions that sustained the Frankish states, including Italian maritime commerce, castle-building, and the military orders, while cautioning students to place the military orders correctly after, rather than before, the First Crusade's success.

These developments set the stage directly for Chapter 35, which examines how Zengid and then Ayyubid political consolidation, culminating in Saladin, gradually reversed the regional fragmentation that had made the First Crusade's success possible, moving toward the recovery of Jerusalem itself.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has shown that the First Crusade and the Frankish states it produced cannot be understood in isolation from the fragmented political order Chapter 33 described. The Crusaders did not conquer a unified Muslim world; they entered a landscape already divided among rival Seljuk princes, a contesting Fatimid caliphate, a religiously prestigious but militarily limited Abbasid caliphate, and Armenian and Byzantine powers each pursuing their own interests. That fragmentation, as much as Crusader determination, shaped the outcome of 1099.

The chapter has also modeled an important historical corrective: the recognition that the century and a half of Frankish presence in the Levant was not a single, unbroken story of religious warfare, but a complex regional history of truce, tribute, alliance, trade, and contact alongside real conflict and real violence. Carrying this corrective forward will matter directly when Chapter 35 examines how Zengid and Ayyubid consolidation, culminating in Saladin, gradually reorganized the region's political order and eventually recovered Jerusalem itself.

Students who understand the distinction between Franks and other Levantine Christian communities, the gradual rather than immediate mobilization of jihad against the Frankish states, and the genuine continuity in the lives of the region's majority population under new Frankish lords are well equipped to read the chapters that follow with the same careful, evidence-based attention this chapter has modeled.

The story of the Frankish states is, in an important sense, a story about what happens when a new political force enters an already crowded and contested regional system rather than an empty stage. From the fragmented Syria of Chapter 33 through the consolidating Ayyubid world of Chapter 35 and the Mongol shock of Chapter 36, the eastern Mediterranean of the twelfth and thirteenth centuries remained a place of shifting power, negotiated coexistence, and recurring conflict — a pattern this chapter has traced at its formative, most consequential beginning.
