Part IV — Conquest and the Umayyad Arab Empire
19

The Great Arab Conquests

A History of the Arab World: From Ancient Crossroads to the Modern Arab World

Chapter Opening

In the spring of 630 CE, the Byzantine emperor Heraclius entered Jerusalem to restore the relic of the True Cross, which Sasanian forces had carried off sixteen years earlier. It was the ceremonial close of a war that had seen Persian armies occupy Syria, Palestine, and Egypt, and Byzantine armies march deep into Iran. Within about a decade, Jerusalem was under the authority of the caliph in Medina, and within about twenty years the Sasanian dynasty, which had ruled Iran for four centuries, had lost its last king. This chapter asks how that happened, and what it did and did not change.

The events examined here are conventionally called the Great Arab Conquests, and this chapter keeps the outline's title while often using the more precise phrase early Arab-Islamic conquests. The armies that left Arabia in the 630s emerged from the Muslim polity whose survival Chapter 18 traced, and their leadership and rank and file were overwhelmingly Arabian. Both parts of the description matter. Political authority and religious community were tightly bound together in the caliphate, yet the men who fought did not all share identical motives or backgrounds, and the societies they encountered were far from uniform.

The scale and speed of the outcome are what make the subject so arresting. By 642 the caliphate held Syria, Palestine, Iraq, and Egypt, and its forces had defeated the main Sasanian armies. By the early 650s the Sasanian king was dead and large parts of the former Sasanian realm had passed under Arab-Muslim political control, often unevenly and sometimes only nominally. Westward, armies had reached Cyrenaica, Tripolitania, and, in a raid of great symbolic importance, central Ifriqiya. The sovereignty of two great imperial systems, one of which had shaped the Near East for centuries, had been overturned or drastically reduced within a single generation.

This chapter argues that the conquests were an extraordinarily rapid political and military expansion, but that they were neither an inevitable eruption from Arabia nor an instantaneous religious and cultural transformation. Success resulted from the interaction of an increasingly mobilized Arabian polity, experienced commanders and soldiers, long-standing frontier connections, the exhaustion and political disruption of Byzantium and Sasanian Iran, regional circumstances, local negotiation, and contingency. Conquest transferred sovereignty quickly. Islamization, Arabization, settlement, and social integration unfolded unevenly over generations and centuries. Keeping those two tempos apart is the chapter's governing discipline.

The chapter also insists on regional difference. The conquest of Syria involved Mediterranean coastal corridors, walled cities, and a Byzantine field army. Iraq and Iran involved river plains, canal networks, and mountain approaches, and a state whose central authority had partly fragmented. Egypt was a compact, river-based province that could be attacked from Palestine and taken by controlling a few key points. North Africa was a vast littoral with scattered Byzantine enclaves and extensive Amazigh political communities, and it resisted incorporation for decades. Conventional maps with sweeping arrows conceal these differences, and this chapter tries to restore them.

Students will also confront an unusually acute evidence problem. The detailed narrative of the conquests comes largely from Arabic accounts compiled in the eighth, ninth, and tenth centuries, long after the events. Near-contemporary Greek, Syriac, Armenian, and Coptic-related sources, along with papyri, coins, and archaeology, sometimes confirm the broad outline while contradicting or complicating the details. Section 19.2 addresses this challenge before any battle is narrated, and the caution it establishes applies to every date, number, speech, and treaty in the chapter.

Finally, the chapter is bounded by its neighbors in the textbook's division of labor. Chapter 18 explained how the post-Muhammad polity survived and became capable of sustained expansion; this chapter does not retell the succession or the Ridda wars. Chapter 20 examines how conquerors settled among, taxed, and negotiated with conquered populations, Chapter 21 the political conflict that erupted inside the caliphate, and Chapter 24 the far slower processes of Arabization and Islamization. This chapter explains the conquests, their geography, chronology, causes, and immediate political consequences, and then stops before conquest can be mistaken for cultural transformation.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Trace the principal campaigns in Syria-Palestine, Iraq, Iran, Egypt, and North Africa, using approximate and contested dates appropriately.
  2. Evaluate the strengths and limitations of later Arabic conquest narratives compared with near-contemporary Greek, Syriac, Armenian, and papyrological evidence.
  3. Explain how the Byzantine-Sasanian war of 602–628 and the Sasanian civil conflicts shaped the conditions in which the conquests occurred, without treating imperial exhaustion as a sufficient cause.
  4. Analyze the military, organizational, and political strengths of the Arab-Muslim armies while avoiding environmental or religious determinism.
  5. Assess the strategic significance of the battles of Yarmuk and al-Qadisiyya and the limits of what a single battle decided.
  6. Compare the conquest of Syria, Iraq, Iran, Egypt, and North Africa in terms of terrain, resistance, negotiation, and duration.
  7. Distinguish conquest by surrender agreement from conquest by force as later Muslim tradition categorized them, and explain why that distinction is historiographically difficult.
  8. Interpret a near-contemporary non-Muslim source as evidence for the experience of conquered communities.
  9. Distinguish military conquest from conversion, Arabization, and social transformation, and explain why they occurred on different timescales.
  10. Evaluate competing explanations for the speed of the conquests and identify which explain particular regions best.
  11. Contextualize the chapter within Part IV and connect it to the questions addressed in Chapters 20, 21, and 24.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. What can historians securely say about the early conquests, given that the fullest narratives were composed generations later?
  2. How did the Byzantine-Sasanian war of 602–628 create conditions for the conquests, and why is that not a complete explanation?
  3. What organizational and political advantages did the caliphate's armies possess, and how should historians weigh religious, material, and kinship motives?
  4. Why did the conquest of Syria, Iraq, Egypt, Iran, and North Africa proceed at such different speeds and by such different methods?
  5. What did a single decisive battle, such as Yarmuk or al-Qadisiyya, actually settle, and what remained to be accomplished afterward?
  6. How did local communities, notables, and religious leaders respond to the arrival of Arab-Muslim forces, and why should their responses not be generalized?
  7. In what sense were the conquests a change of rulers rather than a change of society?
  8. How should historians interpret the assumption that the conquests were driven by religion alone, by economic opportunity alone, or by imperial weakness alone?

Historical Context

Part III traced the process by which the Arabian world of Chapter 14 produced Muhammad's community, unified much of the peninsula, and then survived his death under the first caliphs. Students should carry forward, rather than relearn, the results: by about 634, Medina headed a polity that had brought most of Arabia under its authority, possessed a core of experienced commanders and tribal contingents, and had already begun operations along the Syrian and Iraqi frontiers. The origins of that polity and the settlement of the succession belong to Chapters 16 through 18 and are not narrated again here.

What the conquests met was a world already organized by two large imperial systems. The Byzantine Empire, governed from Constantinople, held Syria, Palestine, Egypt, and important parts of North Africa, especially coastal cities and their hinterlands, ruling through provincial governors, a Greek-speaking imperial church, and local elites who spoke Greek, Syriac, Coptic, Aramaic, Latin-derived and Amazigh languages. The Sasanian Empire, governed from Ctesiphon on the Tigris, held Iraq and Iran and ruled through a hierarchy of nobles, provincial governors, and landed gentry, with Zoroastrianism as the court religion and large Christian, Jewish, and other communities living within its borders. Chapter 13 described how both empires managed their Arabian frontiers partly through allied Arab dynasties, and how those arrangements were fraying.

Two cautions frame everything that follows. First, the conquests are among the most heavily mythologized events in world history. Later Muslim tradition gave them a sacred, exemplary character, while some older European and modern accounts framed them as an inexplicable "explosion" from the desert. This chapter treats them as a historical problem to be explained with attention to evidence, not as either miracle or mystery. Second, the historical actors would not have used the categories of this chapter's summary. Terms like "Arab," "Muslim," and "Arab-Muslim" overlap without being identical for every participant, and the categories of "conquered" and "conqueror" cut across communities whose experiences varied widely.

Section 19.1

From Arabian Consolidation to Transregional War

The story begins where Chapter 18 ended. The polity that had survived the crisis after Muhammad's death, and had reasserted its authority across Arabia, did not confront the outside world as a stranger. Its leaders came from a society whose trade, pilgrimage, kinship, and frontier diplomacy had long linked Arabia to Syria, Iraq, Yemen, and the Red Sea, as Chapters 13 and 14 showed. The tribes of the northern Arabian steppe had served, fought, traded, and intermarried on both imperial frontiers. Operations along the edges of Syria and Iraq were therefore not a leap into the unknown. They extended relationships and routes that already existed.

Abu Bakr's last campaigns, in the tradition, crossed those frontiers in the years around 633 and 634. The evidence for their details is thin and later, and the chapter treats their sequence with caution. What is clearer is that under his successor ʿUmar (r. 634–644) raiding gave way to sustained operations with a strategic aim: to hold territory, defeat imperial field armies, and take the cities and districts that produced revenue. The chapter does not ask why an Arabian polity could have wanted such things, since political ambition, religious conviction, and the pursuit of resources are all plausible and all discussed in Section 19.4. It asks how it succeeded.

A note on vocabulary is needed. The armies are often described simply as "Muslim," but the earliest non-Muslim sources more often call them by ethnic, tribal, or biblical-genealogical labels, such as Saracens, Tayyaye, or Hagarenes, reflecting how outsiders perceived them. The armies themselves were led by members of the emerging Muslim elite and included many men from Arabian groups incorporated into the polity only recently; their public affiliations and political obligations are easier to reconstruct than the private convictions of individual fighters. This chapter uses "Arab-Muslim" for the polity's forces while recognizing that the two words did not describe every participant equally at every moment.

Section 19.2

How Do We Know the Conquests?

Historical Vocabulary
Futūḥ

Futūḥ (singular fatḥ), literally "openings," is the Arabic term for the conquests and for the genre of historical writing that narrates them. The word implies a positive, providential view of the events: territories were "opened" to the rule of the Muslim community. Historians therefore use it both as a source category, the futūḥ literature, and as a reminder that the earliest surviving narratives already carry an interpretation. Non-Muslim sources describe the same events as invasions, occupations, or the consequences of divine punishment, and modern scholars aim to treat neither vocabulary as neutral.

Before any campaign is narrated, students must understand how thin and uneven the evidence is. The detailed accounts of the conquests come chiefly from Arabic works compiled from the later eighth century onward, including the Futūḥ al-Shām usually attributed to al-Azdi, al-Baladhuri's Futūḥ al-buldān, Ibn ʿAbd al-Hakam's account of Egypt, and the great chronicle of al-Tabari, who died in 923. A separate conquest work traditionally ascribed to al-Waqidi is of disputed authorship and contains extensive legendary material. These authors drew on earlier oral and written reports, some now lost. Their works preserve material indispensable to any narrative, and in many cases they are the only sources for particular events. But they were composed generations after the events, within a community with its own theological, political, and tribal interests, and they often shape campaigns into satisfying stories: set-piece speeches, exemplary heroes, tidy sequences of battles, and enormous army numbers. Reports transmitted by Sayf ibn ʿUmar, a major source for the Iraqi campaigns, have long been regarded by specialists as especially problematic.

Against this stand the non-Arabic sources, which are fewer, briefer, and closer in time. Greek and Syriac chronicles, Armenian histories such as the one attributed to Sebeos, the Chronicle of John of Nikiu (written by a later seventh-century Egyptian bishop but surviving only through a much later Ethiopic translation made from a lost Arabic intermediary, itself translated from a lost Coptic or possibly Greek original), and short notices in Christian letters and apocalyptic texts record the arrival of the Arabs from the perspective of communities on the receiving end. Some are contemporary with the events, others written within decades. They rarely give the detailed battle narratives of the Arabic tradition, but they help anchor chronology and remind students that non-Muslims were witnesses as well as subjects.

A third body of evidence is documentary and material. Papyri from Egypt, the Negev, and elsewhere show local administration continuing after the conquest, sometimes in Greek, sometimes in bilingual Greek and Arabic. Coins minted by the new rulers at first imitated Byzantine and Sasanian types. Archaeology documents destruction at some sites, continuity at many others, and the gradual appearance of new settlements. Recent survey work, for example, has been used to propose a likely battlefield landscape for al-Qadisiyya. Scholars of the conquest of Egypt have warned that the sources for that campaign are so problematic that categorical reconstruction of details is unsafe. That warning applies far more widely, and students should hold it in mind throughout: this chapter presents the broad outline as reasonably secure, and its details as reconstructions.

Section 19.3

Why Were Byzantium and Sasanian Iran Vulnerable?

The most dramatic background to the conquests was the last and greatest war between the two empires. It began in 602, when the murder of the Byzantine emperor Maurice gave the Sasanian king Khusrau II a pretext to attack. Persian armies took cities across Mesopotamia and Syria, captured Jerusalem in 614, and later occupied Egypt, where Sasanian rule lasted until about 629. Then the emperor Heraclius reversed the war. Campaigning in Anatolia, the Caucasus, and northern Mesopotamia, he won a decisive victory near Nineveh in 627, and in early 628 Khusrau II was overthrown and killed. The peace that followed returned the territories to Byzantium. Heraclius restored the True Cross at Jerusalem in 630.

The war left both empires damaged in different ways. Byzantium had recovered its provinces but had barely reimposed administration on Syria, Palestine, and Egypt when the Arab-Muslim campaigns began. Its treasury and army were strained, local elites had lived under two governments within a few years, and the imperial religious policy, an effort to reconcile Chalcedonian and Miaphysite Christians through formulas that themselves provoked dispute, had unsettled communities in the very provinces it was meant to reconcile. Frontier arrangements with allied Arab groups had also been disrupted, and the exact status of the Ghassanid dynasty after the war is debated.

Sasanian Iran's crisis was deeper. Khusrau II's fall was followed by a rapid succession of rulers, generals with competing ambitions, and provincial powers acting independently, a period of civil conflict and competing military-aristocratic factions. Yazdegerd III's accession in 632 placed a Sasanian dynastic claimant on the throne, but it did not restore strong central control, and regional magnates and commanders remained powerful. An outbreak of plague in these years added to the disruption, and landed nobles and military commanders competed for influence over the throne. The Sasanian court thus faced the first Arab-Muslim campaigns while its capacity to coordinate resources across the empire was impaired.

These conditions matter. But this chapter resists the tempting argument that exhausted empires were therefore bound to fall. Both empires had recovered from earlier disasters, and Byzantium, in particular, remained a formidable power for centuries afterward, defending Anatolia and Constantinople against repeated Muslim assaults. Weakness created opportunities. Explaining why these particular armies exploited them so effectively, and why they succeeded so differently in different places, requires the additional factors examined next and synthesized in Section 19.16.

Section 19.4

The Strengths of the Arab-Muslim Armies

The forces that entered Syria and Iraq were not a horde. The traditional sources, and modern reconstructions, describe multiple columns under separate commanders, coordinated at times and operating independently at others. Their strengths were partly logistical. Many of the men came from a pastoral and oasis society skilled in mobility, and camels and horses allowed them to move quickly along routes and wells they already knew, to disengage when facing superior forces, and to concentrate again when opportunity arose. Historians caution against turning this into a claim that "desert warriors" were inherently superior. The conquests succeeded against armies that were themselves experienced, and in Iraq and much of Iran they demanded sieges, river crossings, and mountain campaigns far removed from open desert.

Other strengths were political and organizational. The developing caliphal state provided a framework for coordination from Medina, although the degree of central control varied and some commanders and regional forces exercised considerable autonomy. The leadership included men with experience of Arabian warfare and, in many cases, of Syrian and Iraqi conditions from trading and frontier careers. The caliphal polity provided an additional and increasingly centralized framework for coordinating contingents from different groups, supplementing rather than erasing older kinship and alliance structures. Shared allegiance to the new religious community, and to the caliph as its leader, gave that framework much of its force.

Motives are difficult to recover, and the sources shaped them retrospectively. Later tradition emphasized religious duty and reward. Some modern accounts stress plunder and the opportunity to secure stable revenue, and others stress political consolidation and the pressures of holding an Arabian coalition together. A cautious historical approach treats these motives as potentially overlapping rather than mutually exclusive. Religious commitment, kinship, status, material opportunity, and political allegiance could all have shaped participation, but the surviving sources rarely allow historians to measure their relative importance for particular individuals.

Section 19.5

The Syrian-Palestinian Front, 633/634–636

The Byzantine Levant in the early 630s was a wealthy region of coastal cities, agricultural districts, monasteries, and pilgrimage sites. Its defense depended on a limited number of regular troops, local levies, and allied Arab groups on the desert frontier, and the emperor's attention had recently been fixed on restoring order after the war with Persia. The southern districts of Palestine and Transjordan, closest to Arabia, were the first to feel the pressure. Non-Muslim sources of the period, including the Greek Doctrina Jacobi, written around the time of the first invasions, record fear and disruption in the region and show that contemporaries were aware of a new and dangerous power from the south. That text refers to the killing of a Byzantine officer and to reports of a prophet among the Saracens.

Later Arabic tradition describes several armies entering Palestine and Syria under separate commanders, then merging or cooperating as the Byzantine response grew. In this tradition a battle at Ajnadayn, in central Palestine, in the summer of 634 and another at Fihl (Pella) in the Jordan Valley in about 634–635 opened the country to further advance. A separate Syriac chronicle tradition associated with Thomas the Presbyter notes a battle east of Gaza in early 634 in which a Byzantine patrician was killed. These two independent witnesses, one Greek and one Syriac, corroborate aspects of the same early crisis, but the precise order and outcomes of individual engagements remain uncertain. Damascus, the great city of inland Syria, was besieged and taken in the following period, with sources differing on the date and on whether it fell by force or by negotiation.

The pattern that emerged was of fighting in the countryside and around key cities, with local communities and garrisons choosing whether to resist or negotiate. Byzantium's initial response was neither absent nor ineffective, but it was slow to concentrate a large army, and the empire was slow to grasp the scale of the challenge. By 636 that army had assembled.

Section 19.6

Yarmuk and the Loss of Byzantine Syria

The Byzantine counteroffensive culminated in a large battle in August 636 in the valley of the Yarmuk River, a tributary of the Jordan south of the Sea of Galilee. The Byzantine force, a composite army of imperial troops, Armenian contingents, and allied Arab units, moved against Arab-Muslim forces that had, in the tradition, withdrawn from the northern cities to concentrate in the south. Several days of fighting ended with a decisive Byzantine defeat. The Arabic sources supply detailed accounts, rich in speeches, individual duels, and enormous army sizes, that should not be taken at face value. The Greek and Syriac sources provide fewer details but confirm a major Byzantine defeat.

The battle is sometimes given a mythic quality, and this chapter prefers to explain its strategic meaning rather than reproduce its legend. Modern historians have stressed problems of cohesion within the Byzantine army, including divided command, uncertain unity among its varied contingents, and difficulties of coordination, alongside the effectiveness of the Arab-Muslim forces' maneuver and choice of ground. Some have called Yarmuk the climax of the early invasion of Byzantine Syria. Its practical result was that Byzantium no longer had a field army capable of contesting the province, and thereafter the region's cities had to rely on walls, negotiation, or withdrawal.

It is also important not to overstate what one battle settled. Yarmuk did not mean that the whole of Syria fell in a single day, or that the emperor immediately abandoned it. Resistance and negotiation continued for years in different places. But the empire's strategic position had been broken, and within a few years Heraclius, who had once saved his empire from Persia, withdrew from the region he had recently restored. He died in 641, and his successors were preoccupied with defending Anatolia and the remaining provinces.

Section 19.7

Damascus, Jerusalem, and the Consolidation of the Levant

Historical Vocabulary
Ṣulḥ and ʿAnwa

Ṣulḥ refers to a settlement or agreement, and ʿanwa to conquest by force. Later Muslim jurists distinguished between territories that had capitulated on terms and those taken by force, because the distinction was said to affect the status of land, taxation, and rights of the inhabitants. Historians recognize its importance for law but distrust it as a simple description of events. Many accounts of particular cities were shaped by later legal arguments, and the same place could be described as surrendering in one source and being taken by force in another. Students should treat the categories as evidence of how later tradition understood the conquests, not as a reliable classification of what happened in each case.

With the field army gone, the conquest of Syria became a series of local episodes. Some cities, such as Damascus in one tradition, negotiated terms. Others were besieged and taken. The coastal city of Caesarea, the provincial capital of Palestine, held out for years, in part because Byzantine sea access could help sustain it, and was taken after a long siege, probably in 640–641. Each place had its own experience, and the "conquest of Syria" was a process of several years rather than an event.

Jerusalem holds a special place in the tradition and in later religious memory. It probably surrendered around 637–638 after a siege, under an agreement between its patriarch, Sophronius, and the Arab-Muslim leadership. Later sources describe a visit by the caliph ʿUmar to receive the surrender, and preserve texts of the terms said to have been granted. The details, and especially the surviving texts, are heavily shaped by later concerns, and historians treat them as a reflection of how the event was remembered and as a basis for later legal ideas. What is likely is that the city changed hands by negotiation, that its Christian institutions continued to function, and that Muslim rule increasingly incorporated Jerusalem into an emerging Islamic sacred geography.

By about 640, most of Syria and Palestine was in Arab-Muslim hands, and the commanders had begun to look east and south. The consequences for governance, settlement, taxation, and the relationship between conquerors and conquered are the subject of Chapter 20. What matters here is that the Byzantine Empire had lost one of its richest regions, and had lost it largely in a single decade.

Section 19.8

The Iraqi Front and the Sasanian Borderlands

The eastern theater centered on lower Iraq, the fertile alluvial plain of the Tigris and Euphrates. Later Arabic sources called much of this cultivated zone al-Sawād, “the black land,” while the Sasanian province of Babylonia was known as Āsōristān. This was among the richest and most heavily taxed provinces of the empire, dense with canals and estates, and the site of the capital, Ctesiphon. It was also a frontier region already inhabited by substantial Arab groups, including Christian Arabs around al-Hira, the old seat of the Lakhmid dynasty, whose Sasanian-aligned client kingdom Khusrau II had abolished in the early seventh century. The area had Arabic-speaking tribal communities, a large Christian population associated with the Church of the East, Jewish communities, and a Zoroastrian aristocracy.

Raiding and campaigning in southern Iraq began during the last years of Abu Bakr's caliphate, and later Arabic tradition assigns leading roles to commanders such as Khalid ibn al-Walid and the tribal leader al-Muthanna ibn Haritha. As with Syria, the details of these early operations, including the routes, the sequence of engagements, and the responsibility of particular commanders, come from later tradition, and must be used with caution. What appears likely is that frontier raiding, agreements with local Arab and Christian communities, and the disruption of Sasanian border defenses paved the way for larger operations after 634.

The environment mattered. Iraq's canals and marshes complicated movement, but its rivers also offered routes, and its dispersed landholding and taxation structures meant that local landowners and officials could sometimes reach separate arrangements with the newcomers. Sasanian authorities could count on a professional army and a complex administrative apparatus, but the court's internal instability limited their ability to mobilize resources quickly. The scene was thus set for a decisive engagement.

Section 19.9

Al-Qadisiyya and the Fall of Ctesiphon

Al-Qadisiyya is the eastern counterpart to Yarmuk in tradition and in memory. It was fought near the western edge of the Iraqi alluvium, close to the Euphrates and the old frontier zone around al-Hira, between a Sasanian army under the general Rostam Farrokhzad and Arab-Muslim forces led, in tradition, by Sa'd ibn Abi Waqqas. The year is disputed. Arabic sources give dates ranging across the mid-630s, and modern scholars use a range of about 636 to 638. A 2024 archaeological study, working with the commonly used c. 637/8 date, used satellite imagery and field survey to propose a likely battlefield landscape in southwestern Iraq. This chapter therefore gives the date as c. 636–638 and does not claim more.

The Arabic accounts describe several days of fighting, the use of elephants and cavalry by the Sasanian army, the death of Rostam, and a decisive Arab-Muslim victory. As with Yarmuk, the vivid narrative details, the speeches, and the army numbers reflect later storytelling. What is well grounded is a major Sasanian defeat that opened the way toward Ctesiphon. The death of Rostam is a consistent and important element of the later Arabic conquest tradition. Soon afterward, the caliphate's forces advanced to Ctesiphon. The Sasanian court withdrew, and the city, with its palaces, treasury, and the great arch of its royal hall, passed into Arab-Muslim hands, probably around 637–638. Later tradition dwelt on the wealth seized there.

It would be a serious mistake to say that Persia "fell at al-Qadisiyya." The Sasanian king survived, the empire retained large territories on the Iranian plateau, and the Sasanian court continued to seek to resist. What had fallen was Sasanian control of the Iraqi plain, one of the empire's richest and most strategically central regions. That mattered enormously for revenue, for the balance of power, and for the future of the caliphate, whose commanders soon founded new garrison settlements in the region, developments examined in Chapter 20.

Section 19.10

From Iraq onto the Iranian Plateau

Iraq is a plain, but Iran is a plateau ringed by mountains, with deserts in its interior and a patchwork of regions with their own aristocracies and traditions. Defeating the Sasanian army in Iraq did not automatically deliver the plateau. Arab-Muslim forces had to advance through the Zagros passes, secure Khuzistan in the southwest, and campaign in Fars, Media, and beyond, often against regional commanders and local rulers who fought, negotiated, or shifted allegiance according to circumstance. A battle at Jalula, on the road to the Zagros around 637 or 638, is one of the engagements the tradition associates with this stage.

Different regions were incorporated at different rates and in different ways. Some districts made agreements with the newcomers and paid tribute in exchange for keeping local government in place. Others resisted, and several rose in revolt after initial submission, requiring repeated campaigns. Local landed gentry (dihqans) and higher provincial or frontier authorities, including officials styled marzbans in some regions, could bargain with the newcomers, and some retained substantial local power for generations. Political control thus advanced unevenly, and in some regions was more a matter of periodic tribute than of continuous administration.

Section 19.11

Nihawand and the Collapse of the Sasanian State

A larger Sasanian army was gathered in the highlands of western Iran, and met the Arab-Muslim forces near Nihawand probably in 642, though the traditional chronology varies. The tradition calls it the "victory of victories," and the engagement decisively defeated a major Sasanian coalition and sharply weakened the possibility of coordinated resistance. Yet Nihawand was not a "final battle" after which Persia suddenly became Muslim. Its significance was that a major concentration of Sasanian and regional forces had been defeated, sharply reducing the prospects for coordinated resistance, leaving the empire's remaining defense to regional forces and to the king's efforts to rally them.

Yazdegerd III moved from one region to another, seeking support in the east, and was in the end killed near Merv in 651, by accounts that differ on the details and on who was responsible. His death ended Sasanian kingship, though not the claims of his descendants, some of whom sought refuge and support elsewhere, including at the Chinese court. It also did not end Iranian society. Regions of the plateau, especially in the east and along the Caspian coast, remained beyond effective control for years, and many were conquered again after revolts. Political sovereignty collapsed rapidly, but Iranian society remained culturally and linguistically diverse, with Middle Persian and other Iranian languages widely used, and with Zoroastrian and other non-Muslim communities remaining dominant in many regions. The transformations that eventually changed Iranian religion and administration took generations, and Chapter 24 returns to the slower processes of Islamization and Arabization.

Primary Source Window

The Chronicle of Khuzistan (Syriac), mid-seventh century

Creator: Anonymous Christian author, writing in Syriac and associated with the Church of the East in southwestern Iran · Date: composed in the seventh century, well before the surviving full Arabic conquest histories were compiled · Type: Regional chronicle of church and political events, with a continuation giving a detailed account of the conquest of Khuzistan; preserved within a later East Syriac manuscript tradition and studied by specialists in Syriac literature

Context: The chronicle is one of the earliest surviving narratives to describe the collapse of Sasanian power and the arrival of Arab forces from the point of view of a Christian community that had lived under Sasanian rule in southwestern Iran. Its author was not writing a history of the conquests as such. He was recording the affairs of the church and of the kingdom in his own region, and the conquest appears as one stage in a longer story of Sasanian decline and religious history. Its language for the newcomers, drawn from the Syriac name for Arabs, reflects an outsider's perspective.

[Modern paraphrase, not a verbatim translation.] The continuation describes resistance in Khuzistan associated with the Persian commander Hormuzan, the Arab capture of Shush, and the prolonged struggle for Tustar (Shushtar), which finally fell after attackers gained access through the city's defenses; Hormuzan was captured alive.

Analysis: This source is valuable because it gives students a different voice: a conquered Christian community recording change in its own terms and language, well before the surviving full Arabic conquest histories were compiled. It is also limited. It is selective in its interests, it reflects a specific regional and confessional viewpoint, it survives within a later East Syriac manuscript tradition, and it does not attempt to describe the conquerors' own motives or organization. It also suggests that a near-contemporary regional Christian author did not necessarily frame the events in the terms later tradition gave them. Other Christian writings from the same generation, such as letters by the leaders of the Church of the East, suggest that the church continued to function under the new rulers, an important point for Chapter 20.

Section 19.12

Egypt: From Palestine to the Nile Valley, 639–642

The campaign against Egypt was launched from Palestine by ʿAmr ibn al-ʿAs, whose forces entered the country around the end of 639 or the beginning of 640. In tradition, he acted with some autonomy, and the caliph's role in authorizing the expedition is treated differently in different accounts. Egypt was a compact province in one sense: nearly all its wealth and population lay along the Nile and in the Delta, so control of a few key points could decide the outcome. The approach from Palestine across the northern Sinai reached the eastern Delta, and from there the Arab-Muslim forces moved toward the fortress of Babylon, at the point where the river divides toward the Delta, near the later city of Fustat.

The next stage was a siege of Babylon and engagements in the Delta and Nile Valley. Byzantine forces contested the advance, and the Egyptian campaign involved more sustained fighting than the traditional story of an easy walkover suggests. The fortress capitulated around 641, and the capital, Alexandria, was surrendered through an arrangement with Byzantine authorities reached in late 641, with the Byzantine evacuation completed in 642, in the tradition negotiated by the Alexandrian patriarch and imperial official Cyrus, whom many later Arabic accounts identify with al-Muqawqis, although that identification remains debated. Under the arrangement, Byzantine forces departed and the city passed to the invaders. The settlement was not yet secure: a Byzantine expedition retook Alexandria in 645, and ʿAmr recovered it in 646, a reminder that surrender did not automatically mean irreversible control.

Papyri and other documentary evidence show the outlines of what happened next: Greek-language administration continued for a period, with new officials at the top and local personnel largely in place. That continuity is discussed further in Chapter 20. What matters here is that within about three years the caliphate had taken one of the Byzantine Empire's richest agrarian provinces. Egypt had historically been crucial to Constantinople's provisioning, although the old state grain-distribution system had already been disrupted during the Persian occupation.

Section 19.13

Why Byzantine Egypt Fell

Several factors help explain the outcome. Byzantine Egypt had just been through Sasanian occupation and reconquest, which disrupted administration and loyalties. The imperial army in the province was limited and its command divided. Distance from Constantinople, and the empire's simultaneous problems in Syria and elsewhere, meant that reinforcements were slow. Egypt's geography also mattered: an invader who held Babylon and the Delta approaches could dominate the river valley, and the Nile's main cities were vulnerable.

Religion is the point at which a widely repeated claim needs to be corrected. Older textbooks often said that Egypt's Coptic Christians, resentful of Chalcedonian imperial persecution, welcomed the Arabs as liberators. The evidence does not support that generalization. The imperial church was Chalcedonian, while many Egyptian Christians were Miaphysite, and conflict over doctrine was real. But responses to the invasion varied widely, some Egyptians resisted, some fought alongside Byzantine forces, some appear to have cooperated with the new rulers after the outcome was clear, and many simply sought to protect their families and property. A later seventh-century Egyptian Christian source, the Chronicle of John of Nikiu, presents the conquest in deeply hostile and mournful terms rather than as liberation. Nor is there good evidence that Arab taxation was lighter from the outset. Taxation under the conquerors is examined in Chapter 20.

The fair conclusion is that religious tension within the Byzantine church weakened imperial cohesion in some places, while local calculations of survival and advantage shaped how individual communities behaved. It would be as mistaken to describe Egypt as a passive prize as to describe it as a willing partner.

Section 19.14

Libya, Tripolitania, and the First Moves into North Africa

From Egypt, Arab-Muslim forces moved westward. In tradition, the coastal region of Cyrenaica (Barqa) was reached about 642–643, and Tripolitania soon afterward, with Tripoli taken around 643 or later by ʿAmr's forces. Later Arabic accounts portray these early western operations largely as raids, negotiated payments, and temporary submissions; their exact chronology, scale, and degree of continuing control are uncertain. The regions were politically fragmented, with coastal cities, Byzantine garrisons, and the extensive Amazigh communities of the interior, whose leaders controlled substantial territory and could bargain, ally, or resist.

The first major penetration into central North Africa came in 647, when an expedition under ʿAbdallah ibn Saʿd advanced into Ifriqiya, the Roman province of Africa, roughly modern Tunisia and neighboring lands. It defeated forces associated with the exarch Gregory, who had broken with Constantinople and proclaimed himself emperor in 646/647, near Sbeitla; later Arabic tradition reports Gregory's death in the fighting and that the expedition accepted a large payment before withdrawing. The raid demonstrated the region's vulnerability and its political fragmentation. It did not conquer North Africa, and the caliphate would not establish a durable presence in Ifriqiya for a generation.

Section 19.15

Why North Africa Was Different

Ordinary conquest maps show a broad arrow sweeping west along the Mediterranean. The reality was very different. Byzantine power covered only parts of the region, principally coastal cities and their hinterlands, while Amazigh political communities controlled extensive stretches of the interior and the mountains and were not simply subjects of the empire. There was no single capital whose fall could decide the outcome, no river plain that concentrated wealth as in Iraq or Egypt, and much less of the administrative centralization that let a few victories transfer control of a province. The distances were immense, the terrain often mountainous or desert, and supply lines from Egypt were long.

The result was decades of campaigning, retreats, renewed offensives, alliances with some local groups against others, and repeated revolts by those who had accepted terms. Recent scholarship describes the process as extending far into the eighth century, much longer than the conquests of Syria and Egypt, and as politically difficult throughout. The founding of Qayrawan and the later Umayyad campaigns that extended caliphal control across the Maghreb belong mainly to Chapter 22, while Chapter 32 returns to later Maghrebi states, Amazigh political power, and connections with al-Andalus. This chapter simply establishes the crucial point: North Africa was a prolonged process, not a seventh-century sweep.

Section 19.16

Why Did the Conquests Succeed?

No single explanation suffices, and the strongest accounts combine several. Imperial conditions mattered: the Byzantine-Sasanian war and the Sasanian civil conflict left both states weakened and their frontiers disrupted. The Arab-Muslim side mattered: a mobilized polity with some degree of central coordination, experienced commanders, and mobile forces; religious commitment, political allegiance, and material opportunity may all have contributed to participation, though their relative importance is difficult to recover. Local circumstances mattered: the ability of towns, notables, and communities to negotiate, surrender, or resist, and the different geography of each region. And contingency mattered, since particular battles, deaths, and decisions could have gone otherwise.

These factors do not explain every region equally. Imperial weakness accounts well for Iraq and helps for Syria, but for North Africa it explains only the vulnerability exposed in the 647 expedition, not why conquest remained so prolonged; geography, long supply lines, Byzantine enclaves, and autonomous Amazigh political communities were also crucial. Arab-Muslim organization helps explain success in Syria and Iraq, where large field armies were defeated. Local negotiation is especially important in Jerusalem, Egypt, and many Iranian districts. A serious account uses the factors together and asks which mattered most in each region.

Historical Debate

Why Did the Conquests Succeed So Quickly?

The question: What best explains the speed with which Arab-Muslim forces overturned Byzantine and Sasanian rule across the Near East? Historians rarely present the answer as a choice between two camps, so this feature presents three overlapping explanations.

Interpretation A — Imperial exhaustion and instability: Emphasizes the effects of the 602–628 war, Sasanian civil conflict, plague, fiscal strain, disrupted frontiers, and religious divisions in Byzantium. On this view the empires were unusually vulnerable when the caliphate's forces arrived.
Interpretation B — Arab-Muslim political and military organization and motivation: Emphasizes the caliphate's central direction, mobile forces, experienced commanders, cohesion around a new religious community, and plausibly overlapping religious, material, and political motives.
Interpretation C — Regional circumstances, local alliances, and contingency: Emphasizes that outcomes differed by region, that local elites and communities negotiated and chose, that geography shaped what was possible, and that chance played a role.

Evidence: Each interpretation draws on the same limited evidence, including later Arabic narratives, near-contemporary Greek, Syriac, Armenian, and Coptic-related writings, papyri, coins, and archaeology, and differs mainly in emphasis and in how much weight to put on sources that reflect the victors' later memory.

Why it matters: The answer affects how historians view the conquests' inevitability, and how far their success depended on conditions that were temporary. A view that stresses imperial weakness alone risks treating the conquests as accidental, while one stressing religion alone risks treating them as inevitable.

Student task: For each of Syria, Iraq, Egypt, Iran, and North Africa, identify which explanation or combination of explanations fits best, and which fits least well. What additional evidence would help decide?

Cause and Effect

From Byzantine-Sasanian War to Arab-Islamic Empire

Long-term causes

Centuries of frontier integration between Arabia and the Near East, ties of trade, kinship, and service across imperial borders, and the long-standing use of Arab groups as imperial allies.

Short-term causes

The 602–628 war, Sasanian civil conflict after 628, the consolidation of the Medina polity after Muhammad's death, and the launching of sustained campaigns under Abu Bakr and ʿUmar.

Event or transformation

The campaigns of the 630s to 650s in Syria, Iraq, Iran, Egypt, and North Africa.

Immediate effects

Collapse or retreat of imperial sovereignty across large territories, transfer of tribute and revenue to the caliphate, and the establishment of new military centers.

Long-term consequences

The creation of an enormous, multiethnic empire whose rulers were a small minority among the ruled, setting the stage for the questions of Chapters 20 through 24.

Historical complexity

Imperial weakness created opportunities but cannot by itself explain why these armies successfully exploited them, or why the results differed so sharply from region to region.
Section 19.17

Conquest Was Not Conversion

This is the chapter's essential conceptual point. In the 630s and 640s, conquered Syria and Egypt remained overwhelmingly Christian. Iraq contained Christians, Jews, Zoroastrians, and others, and Iran remained overwhelmingly non-Muslim. Greek, Syriac, Coptic, Middle Persian, and other languages did not disappear, and many local administrative routines, village structures, and religious institutions continued, while fiscal authority and the destination of revenue changed immediately and detailed tax arrangements evolved unevenly. The new rulers governed very large non-Muslim populations, and they were themselves a small minority in the territories they had taken.

Nothing in this chapter should therefore be read as saying that Islam spread across the Middle East in twenty years. What spread in that time was military and political rule. The conversion of large populations, the adoption of Arabic as a common language of administration and daily life, and the emergence of new societies with shared identities took far longer, and proceeded at different speeds in different regions. Arrangements for settlement, taxation, administrative continuity, treaties, and relations between conquerors and conquered belong to Chapter 20. The distinction between Arabization and Islamization, and the eventual mass conversions, belong especially to Chapter 24.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Sovereignty and Society in the Conquered Lands

What Changed Quickly

  • The ruler and the source of military sovereignty
  • The destination of tribute and revenue
  • Strategic and political centers
  • Elite political networks, especially for imperial officials and military commanders

What Often Changed Slowly

  • Religion and the structures of the churches, synagogues, and Zoroastrian institutions
  • Spoken and administrative languages
  • Agricultural practices and village life
  • Local communities and many administrative routines

Who experienced change most directly? Imperial officials, garrison soldiers, and elites tied to the old courts felt the change of sovereignty most sharply. Who experienced greater continuity? Villagers, artisans, and many local notables often found daily life continuing along familiar lines. Why the pattern matters: It explains why conquest and transformation must be studied as separate processes, and why Chapter 20 turns to the arrangements between them.

Multiple Perspectives

Six Vantage Points on the Conquests

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns historians have identified across many sources. They are not quotations from any single named historical actor, and the groups did not have equal power or freedom of action.

A Byzantine soldier before Yarmuk: Serving an emperor who had just won a great war, he expects to defend a province his army had only recently recovered, and depends on commanders and allied units whose cohesion he cannot take for granted.
A Christian resident of Jerusalem negotiating surrender: The city's leaders must weigh the danger of a siege against uncertain terms, and their priority is the survival of the community and its holy places.
An Arab-Muslim fighter campaigning in Iraq: Fighting far from Arabia as part of a religious and political community, he may be driven by faith, kinship, status, and the prospect of a share of the spoils, in proportions no source lets us measure.
A Persian landholder after Ctesiphon's fall: With the court gone and imperial authority uncertain, he must decide whether to support the king, negotiate, or seek terms that protect his lands and local authority.
A Coptic-speaking Egyptian villager during the conquest: Most concerned with harvests, taxes, and safety, the villager experiences the conquest as another change of masters in a province that had recently had several.
An Amazigh community leader confronting the first incursions west of Egypt: Neither subject to Byzantium nor simply an ally of it, he may bargain, resist, or withdraw depending on the terms offered and the strength of the invaders.
Everyday Life and Social History

War, Movement, and the Ordinary Population

The campaigns were experienced by ordinary people as sieges, requisitions, displacement, and negotiations over safety, and also, in some localities, as a period in which much of daily routine continued even as political and fiscal authority changed. Sources rarely record village life directly, and what survives comes disproportionately from clerics, chroniclers, and officials. Papyri from Egypt and the Negev are among the few sources that let historians see administrative routine continuing at the local level, and archaeology suggests that many rural settlements were little disturbed.

For the conquerors, life on campaign meant long marches, dependence on animals, shared provisions, and living far from families. Women and children accompanied at least some armies in the traditional accounts, and later settlement in new towns, examined in Chapter 20, made these households a core of the new communities. The conquests also involved the taking of captives and the movement of enslaved people, a painful reality that later sources record unevenly, and which affected both the conquered and the shaping of early Muslim societies. Historians must read all of this evidence critically, aware that the people who fought, fled, or farmed left few accounts of their own.

Section 19.18

A New Empire, but Not Yet a New Society

Within roughly two decades of Muhammad's death, the geopolitical map of the Near East had been redrawn. By 642 the caliphate controlled Syria, Palestine, Iraq, and Egypt, and by the middle of the seventh century large parts of the former Sasanian realm, with Iran taking longer and proceeding more unevenly. Its forces had begun the long process of moving into North Africa. The Byzantine Empire had lost Syria, Palestine, and Egypt but still retained Anatolia, substantial Balkan territories, the Exarchate of Africa, Sicily, and possessions in Italy and elsewhere in the Mediterranean, while the Sasanian dynasty had disappeared.

Yet the political change ran far ahead of social change. A relatively small conquering elite now ruled territories whose peoples spoke different languages, followed different religions, and were accustomed to very different systems of government. The immediate challenge for the caliphate was not only to defeat armies but to organize garrisons, collect revenue, keep the peace, and prevent its own commanders and tribes from fragmenting the new empire. How did relatively small conquering armies live among, govern, tax, and negotiate with vastly larger provincial populations? That is the question Chapter 20 takes up.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced the early Arab-Islamic conquests of the 630s through the 650s: the campaigns that carried the polity consolidated in Chapter 18 into Syria, Palestine, Iraq, Iran, Egypt, and North Africa. Its central argument is that the conquests were an extraordinarily rapid political and military expansion, but neither an inevitable eruption from Arabia nor an instantaneous religious and cultural transformation. Sovereignty changed quickly. Society changed slowly.

The chapter began with the evidence problem. The fullest narratives of the conquests are later Arabic accounts, shaped by the concerns of the community that composed them, while near-contemporary Greek, Syriac, Armenian, and Coptic-related sources, papyri, coins, and archaeology offer a smaller and sometimes contradictory body of evidence. Chronology, army sizes, speeches, and treaty texts must therefore be treated as reconstructions, and the broad outline is more secure than any detail.

The conditions in which the conquests occurred were shaped by the last Byzantine-Sasanian war of 602–628 and the Sasanian civil conflicts that followed. Both empires had been strained, their frontiers disrupted, and, in Sasanian Iran, central authority fragmented. But imperial weakness was an opportunity, not a cause sufficient in itself, and Byzantium in particular endured for centuries afterward.

The Arab-Muslim armies possessed real strengths: mobility, some degree of central coordination, experienced commanders, cohesion around a new religious community, and long-standing familiarity with the frontier zones. Religious commitment, kinship, status, material opportunity, and political allegiance could all have shaped participation, but the sources rarely allow historians to measure their relative importance for particular individuals. Explaining them by religion alone, or by plunder alone, or by desert environment alone, would oversimplify.

In Syria, the fighting of the mid-630s culminated in the decisive defeat of Byzantium's main field force at Yarmuk in August 636, after which cities negotiated, resisted, or fell over a period of years. Jerusalem probably surrendered by agreement around 637–638, while Caesarea, sustained in part by Byzantine sea access, held out until about 640–641. In Iraq, al-Qadisiyya, dated c. 636–638, opened the way toward Ctesiphon and contributed to the collapse of Sasanian control of the alluvial plain, but the Sasanian king survived and resistance continued on the plateau through Jalula, Nihawand, and beyond until Yazdegerd III's death in 651.

Egypt fell in a campaign that began around the end of 639 or the start of 640, centered on the siege of Babylon and the surrender of Alexandria in 642, although a Byzantine expedition briefly retook it in 645 before Arab forces recovered it in 646. Its fall reflected Byzantine weakness, geography, and local calculation, but not, as older accounts claimed, a general welcome by Egyptian Christians. In North Africa, raids and campaigns into Cyrenaica, Tripolitania, and Ifriqiya, including the victory near Sbeitla in 647, revealed weakness but did not produce conquest. There the caliphate encountered fragmented Byzantine enclaves and extensive Amazigh political communities, and incorporation took generations.

The chapter emphasized that success varied by region. Imperial collapse explains Iraq better than North Africa, organized armies explain Syria better than Egypt, and local negotiation explains Jerusalem and many Iranian districts better than the field battles. Timeline, geography, and the Historical Debate feature all reinforced the importance of asking which factors mattered in which places.

Finally, the chapter distinguished conquest from conversion. Conquered Syria, Egypt, Iraq, and Iran remained overwhelmingly non-Muslim and continued to speak Greek, Syriac, Coptic, Middle Persian, and other languages. The new rulers were a small minority among vast populations. This is the distinction on which Chapter 20, on settlement and governance, and Chapter 24, on Arabization and Islamization, build, and it is the reason this chapter closes with the question of how conquerors lived among the conquered.

A mobile military force capable of campaigning and meeting an enemy in major battle, as distinct from local garrisons; defeat of a province's principal field force could leave individual cities dependent on walls, local troops, negotiation, or outside relief.
Sovereignty
Recognized ultimate political authority over a territory, which changed hands in the conquests far faster than religion or language.

MGU Library Research Project

Comparing Conquest Narratives from Different Communities

This project asks you to compare how different communities recorded one conquest episode, and to evaluate what each source can and cannot show.

  1. Choose an episode. Select a battle, siege, or surrender, such as Yarmuk, Jerusalem, al-Qadisiyya, or Alexandria.
  2. Gather sources. Using library resources, locate at least one later Arabic account and at least one Greek, Syriac, Armenian, or Coptic-related account or document.
  3. Compare. Identify where the accounts agree, disagree, or are silent, and consider authorship, date, language, and purpose.
  4. Add material evidence. Consult, where available, papyri, coins, or archaeological reports relevant to the episode.
  5. Write your findings. In a 3–5 page essay or equivalent presentation, explain what can be established with confidence, what remains uncertain, and how the sources' perspectives shape their accounts.

Library resources to use: Scholarly translations of Syriac, Greek, and Arabic chronicles, specialist studies of the early Islamic conquests, papyrological editions, and the MGU History Library's reference materials (see also Appendix A, Glossary of Regional and Historical Terminology).

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it explains how the political map on which the rest of the textbook depends came to exist. Without the conquests, there would be no transregional caliphate, no Umayyad Damascus, no Abbasid Baghdad, and none of the linked provinces from Iraq to Egypt whose histories Parts IV through VII follow. At the same time, it warns against a common misreading: that this political transformation was also, at once, a religious and cultural one.

The chapter also reveals larger processes. It shows how frontier societies, imperial rivalry, and local negotiation can combine with political mobilization to produce sudden shifts of sovereignty, and how such shifts differ by terrain and political structure. It shows that a coherent story can be built from limited and problematic evidence only if the historian keeps the limits of that evidence visible.

Later developments grew directly from these events. Chapter 20 will examine how a small conquering elite settled, governed, and taxed vast provincial populations. Chapter 21 will show how conflict within the caliphate arose from the strains of empire. Chapters 22 and 23 will trace the Umayyad state that consolidated the conquests, and Chapter 24 will show that Arabization and Islamization were slow, distinct processes that gradually transformed the societies the conquests had brought under a single rule.

Continuities also outlasted the conquests: Syriac-, Coptic-, and Persian-speaking communities, Christian and Jewish institutions, Zoroastrian practice, Amazigh political communities, and village life all persisted and continued to shape the caliphate, the societies of the Arab world, and neighboring Iran. By the end of this chapter, Arabia, the Fertile Crescent, Egypt, and large parts of the former Sasanian realm had come under caliphal authority, while the long conquest of the Maghreb had only begun. How these politically connected but socially diverse regions gradually developed into interconnected societies of the Arab and wider Islamic worlds is the subject of the chapters that follow.
