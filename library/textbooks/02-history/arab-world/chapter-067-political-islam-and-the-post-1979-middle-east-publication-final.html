Chapter Opening

Political Islam was not born in 1979. That year changed the regional political environment in which older movements, states, and communities confronted one another.

In November 1979 two deeply different crises drew global attention to the political authority of Islam. Iran’s monarchy had fallen earlier that year to a revolution whose emerging leadership proposed an Islamic republic. On 20 November, armed Saudi militants seized the Grand Mosque in Mecca in an uprising of distinctly Saudi origins. Neither event was the beginning of Muslim religious politics. Together, however, they exposed assumptions that modern states could securely regulate religion while keeping most political power beyond public challenge. Across the Arab world, religious movements and established authorities were already debating who could speak for Islam, how law should be made, and what social obligations states owed their citizens.

The decade that followed brought very different outcomes. Egypt amended its constitutional language about Islamic law, then witnessed the assassination of President Anwar al-Sadat by militants outside the mainstream Muslim Brotherhood. Syria’s conflict with armed Islamist opposition culminated in the destruction of much of Hama. Jordan allowed a Brotherhood-connected movement into electoral politics, while Tunisian Islamists faced persecution after a short opening. In Algeria, the political opening of 1989 gave the Islamic Salvation Front electoral success before the military interrupted national elections. Sudan established a military–Islamist government after a coup. These developments must be explained through specific institutions, societies, and choices, not a presumed single Islamic revolution.

Political Islam is an imperfect but useful designation for organized projects seeking a public role for particular interpretations of Islam. It is not synonymous with the faith practiced by Muslims, the ordinary work of religious scholars, or revival movements dedicated principally to worship and ethical reform. Some Islamist parties entered elections and operated social clinics. Others sought to govern without meaningful competition. Some militant groups attacked state personnel and civilians; those crimes were neither the act of all Islamists nor an inevitable outcome of religious education. Distinguishing actors is the foundation of moral and historical accuracy.

Arab societies faced accelerated urbanization, rising educational expectations, migration to wealthy Gulf economies, changes in official economic policy, and unequal access to services. For some residents a charitable clinic or study association was a more accessible institution than a government office. Others defended secular national identities, minority rights, existing religious practices, or independent trade unions. Women were organizers as well as subjects of competing laws and moral arguments. No government’s claim to protect religion and no movement’s claim to embody popular values can be accepted without examining its practices and the experiences of those it governed or sought to organize.

The chapter studies political Islam principally from 1979 to about 2000, supplying earlier history only where essential. It gives Iran attention as a consequential non-Arab neighbor without making Iran the center of Arab history. It distinguishes Saudi religious legitimacy from revolutionary opposition, and parliamentary participation from armed rebellion. The detailed histories of Lebanon and regional intervention, the Iran–Iraq War and the Gulf crisis, the peace processes, and the uprisings after 2010 have their own chapters. That division of labor permits the present chapter to analyze movements and political institutions without repeating the wider regional chronology.

The evidence includes dated constitutions, movement statements, an emergency diplomatic telegram, election records, contemporary rights reports, legal analysis, and later historical scholarship. Students will find eight carefully identified source windows and three historical geography exercises. Editorial source passages are expressly paraphrases; they should not be cited as verified quotations from the original languages. Estimates of deaths in Hama and Algeria are especially uncertain, and this chapter avoids inventing a definitive total. The objective is to explain a major transformation in Arab political life while preserving the difference between documented events, interpretations, and unverified claims.

Historical Context

Before the rise of contemporary Islamist parties, the region contained centuries-old traditions of religious scholarship, Sufi networks, legal interpretation, pilgrimage, charitable endowments, and relationships between rulers and scholars. The Muslim Brotherhood, founded in Egypt in 1928, introduced an influential modern organizational model combining religious reform, social services, and political ambitions. The state-building that followed independence in many Arab countries altered but did not remove these older religious institutions.

After the Second World War, rulers in Egypt, Syria, Iraq, Algeria, and other states promised national sovereignty and development. Their projects varied and included significant educational, administrative, and economic achievements; they also built restrictive political systems. The 1967 military defeat damaged the legitimacy of some governments but did not end Arab nationalism, secular ideas, or state-sponsored religious authority. In the 1970s, demographic expansion, urban migration, professional education, and access to new publications enlarged the audiences for competing public philosophies.

Economic differentiation was decisive. Oil income and labor migration generated resources and new cultural exchanges; state budgets elsewhere came under pressure. Families encountered inequality through housing, schooling, medical access, and employment, and religious associations sometimes supplied practical aid. Yet movements were not simply spontaneous welfare agencies. They had leaders, ideologies, organizational hierarchies, and disagreements about legal authority, gender, minorities, and public accountability.

This book is about the Arab world rather than an undifferentiated Middle East. Iran appears here as an external regional influence. Afghanistan and Pakistan appear where their conflicts affected cross-border Arab activism. The religious and demographic diversity of Sudan, the Gulf, North Africa, and the Levant remains visible. Earlier chapters addressed Nasserism, Arab nationalism, oil economies, Palestine, and the wars of 1967 and 1973. The following chapters take up the specific regional conflicts that developed alongside the movement histories examined here.

Division I — Foundations, the Brotherhood, Revival, and 1979 (67.1–67.4)

Section 67.1

The Crisis of Secular Nationalism and Religious Revival

The political prominence of Islamist movements after 1979 did not mean that Arab societies had suddenly discovered religion. Islamic institutions, devotional practices, jurisprudence, reform movements, and religious associations had long been central to social life. What changed was the political configuration in which activists demanded that Islamic principles help determine public policy, social organization, and the legitimacy of rulers. Nationalist governments had promised independence, economic development, territorial security, and representative national dignity. Some achieved mass education, industrial expansion, and social mobility, but many failed to establish accountable institutions. Their opponents could argue that the state had accumulated extraordinary power without fulfilling all its promises. The defeat of Arab armies in 1967 sharpened criticism, although it did not by itself extinguish secular nationalism. The key historical problem is why different forms of religious activism gained an audience in particular institutions and neighborhoods, not whether Islam somehow replaced a previously irreligious Arab world.

The transformations of the 1960s and 1970s created new political publics. Universities enrolled first-generation students from towns and villages; graduates entered cities where affordable housing and secure professional employment often failed to keep pace with expectations. Village migrants encountered new networks of residence, study, work, and mutual aid. Governments expanded schools and public-sector employment, yet fiscal pressures and population growth complicated their developmental contracts. Religious student societies provided companionship, study circles, inexpensive publications, and moral languages through which young people could criticize corruption or ostentatious privilege. Some groups organized tutoring, small clinics, marriage assistance, and food distribution. These services mattered because participation in a movement could be grounded in everyday trust rather than allegiance to an abstract constitution. Such networks could also exclude dissenters or reinforce unequal gender hierarchies; their popularity did not make their social claims beyond criticism.

Economic policies varied sharply. Egypt's liberalization under Anwar al-Sadat, commonly called the infitah, opened some opportunities while producing visible inequalities. Oil income transformed parts of the Arabian Peninsula and supported employment and remittances across the region. Teachers, engineers, laborers, and professionals carried religious books, educational methods, political ideas, and household expectations between Egypt, Sudan, Yemen, Jordan, and Gulf states. Saudi-funded religious outreach was significant but did not single-handedly create indigenous movements with their own histories. Urban commerce and migrant remittances could finance independent organizations, while governments attempted to supervise mosque appointments, charitable societies, broadcasting, and education. These intersecting changes cannot be reduced to one formula of unemployment causing Islamism: some prominent organizers were prosperous professionals, and others came from communities burdened by poverty and police power.

A central distinction separates revival from political Islam. A family that observed fasting, a Sufi lodge maintaining spiritual instruction, a mosque committee repairing buildings, and an organization seeking to legislate a religious program all participated in Islamic social life, but they did not pursue the same political project. Islamist organizations themselves diverged over what they considered legitimate action. Some prioritized preaching and individual reform, some joined trade unions or elections, and others argued that governments should be replaced through confrontation. Salafi movements likewise ranged from quietist scholarship to political campaigning and armed activism, and these positions changed over time. Neither a Muslim population nor the visibility of religious practice can be used as a proxy for support for militant violence. The terms political Islam and Islamism are analytical labels whose boundaries are debated; identifying a movement's actual program is more reliable than assuming a common essence.

The changing public authority of Islam also reflected choices by states. Rulers who publicly claimed secular or national-developmental goals continued to patronize religious institutions, appoint clerics, maintain ministries for religious endowments, and regulate family law. Faced with leftist opponents or legitimacy crises, some encouraged selected religious actors, only to confront demands they could no longer control. Other governments prohibited most independent organizations while preserving religious language in official ceremonies and law. This interaction makes it misleading to describe late twentieth-century Islamism solely as rebellion against a secular state. State-sponsored Islamization, independent moral revival, legal reform, welfare provision, and resistance to government could develop simultaneously inside one country. The ensuing history therefore requires attention to institutional access, coercion, and the uneven meaning of citizenship.

Historians reconstruct these changes through party pamphlets, press reports, sermons, student publications, elections, legal judgments, memoirs, social surveys, and interviews. Each has limits. Police files may magnify threats; movement memoirs may portray an unbroken record of principle; official vote totals under authoritarian supervision do not straightforwardly measure public support. An association's hospital may be documented more easily than the private reasons people used it. The most credible explanation compares different types of evidence and distinguishes what actors claimed to intend from what their actions produced. Chapter 67 follows that approach through national cases and cross-border networks, reserving the broader wars, Lebanon's detailed conflict, and post-Cold War peace diplomacy for their assigned chapters.

Historical Vocabulary

Islam, revival, Islamism, and jihadism

Islam denotes a diverse religious tradition and its communities. Islamic revival describes renewed religious learning, practice, institution-building, and moral discussion; it may be political or nonpolitical. Islamism or political Islam refers here to organized projects that seek to shape public authority or social order according to an explicitly articulated understanding of Islam.

Jihad has multiple historical meanings in Islamic thought, not all military. Jihadism is a modern analytical term for political currents that make armed struggle central to their program. Salafism refers broadly to currents invoking the exemplary first generations of Muslims, including quietist, activist, and militant approaches. None of these categories should be treated as synonyms, and each group must be identified through its actual practices.

Section 67.2

The Muslim Brotherhood: Organization, Reform, and Political Disagreement

Hasan al-Banna established the Muslim Brotherhood in Ismailia, Egypt, in 1928, in a society marked by British influence, a constitutional monarchy, expanding associations, and disagreement over national reform. His movement combined religious instruction with youth activity, publishing, charitable work, and efforts to transform law and government. It grew through branches and affiliated institutions, but it was not a single uniform constituency: urban professionals, students, small traders, and workers could share a banner while disagreeing over strategy. Brotherhood discourse asserted that Islam furnished guidance for public as well as personal affairs. Its practical activities often moved more incrementally than its most comprehensive language suggested. Historical analysis must also confront the organization's involvement in political confrontation and the existence of a secret apparatus associated with episodes of violence in the 1940s; welfare activity alone does not capture its early history.

The Egyptian revolution of 1952 initially created tactical openings but developed into a rupture between Gamal Abdel Nasser's regime and the Brotherhood. After an attempted assassination of Nasser in 1954, authorities suppressed the organization, imprisoned thousands, and used severe coercion. The state's security apparatus reshaped both institutional continuity and the memory of persecution. Underground communication, prison encounters, and exile became important channels for preserving networks, but repression did not produce a single doctrinal conclusion. Some survivors came to emphasize gradual social reconstruction and refusal of immediate rebellion. Others interpreted the coercive nationalist state as proof of an illegitimate order. One must avoid describing each later Islamist organization as a direct result of imprisonment without examining the intermediating ideas and people.

Sayyid Qutb became one of the most influential but also most disputed figures in this history. His writings challenged the moral and political foundations of existing regimes and developed concepts of divine sovereignty and modern jahiliyya, an alleged condition of ignorance or deviation from God's rule. Qutb was executed in 1966 after a state prosecution. Later readers drew different conclusions from his work: some emphasized moral critique, while revolutionary groups treated selected arguments as warrants for confronting rulers and redefining membership in a legitimate community. The trajectory from a text to militant action is not automatic. The Brotherhood's mainstream leadership did not simply adopt every interpretation of Qutb, and local militant groups often disputed its caution. Historians must specify which faction, edition, translation, and period of reception they are describing.

Sadat's presidency permitted a partial reemergence of Brotherhood figures and social institutions without granting full party legality. Egyptian members pursued careers in universities, professional syndicates, publishing, business, and neighborhood welfare. Electoral strategies depended on alliances because the movement could not simply register as a normal political party. In other Arab states, branches or related movements developed distinctive national identities. The Jordanian Brotherhood operated for decades in a comparatively cooperative relationship with the monarchy; Syrian Brothers confronted a different party-state and security system; Palestinian organizations developed amid occupation and national liberation struggles. The shared founding vocabulary thus concealed profound differences in political opportunity, leadership, and constituency.

Organization matters as much as ideology. Small educational circles could recruit members and transmit discipline, while professional associations offered a public route into disputes over social services, freedom of expression, and representation. Yet disciplined organizations could also demand loyalty, police conduct, and elevate male leadership. Women participated through educational networks, charitable activities, and activism, sometimes pressing against the movement's own limits. A reader should ask not merely whether the Brotherhood supported elections but what internal consultation, public legal commitments, and treatment of competitors accompanied that choice. Positions developed over decades; an electoral alliance in one year does not establish a stable democratic doctrine for every branch.

The Brotherhood became a reference point for many forms of Sunni activism, but treating all such activism as a branch of it erases independence. Salafi movements, Sufi reformers, locally rooted mosque organizations, and revolutionary factions often criticized Brotherhood compromises or its theological orientation. In particular, armed Egyptian jihadist networks that confronted the state in 1981 and later should not be collapsed into the Brotherhood's organizational responsibility. The analytically useful distinction is between a movement's membership and decisions, the circulation of ideas beyond it, and the retrospective claims of sympathizers or opponents. It is this mixture of organizational continuity and political divergence that made the Brotherhood important after 1979.

Cause and Effect

The political expansion of religious movements

Long-term conditions included authoritarian state-building, the persistence of religious institutions, contested nationalist legitimacy, unequal economic development, and the growth of education and migration. These processes did not operate identically in each country.

Short-term catalysts included the erosion of official credibility after 1967, economic crises, selective state encouragement of religious organizations, the Iranian Revolution, local political openings, and severe episodes of repression. The outcome was not a uniform Islamist victory but an expanded field of organized religious politics, accompanied in some cases by electoral bargaining or armed conflict.

Immediate effects included new associations, constitutional and legal debates, and intensified contests over public authority. Longer-term consequences included new forms of opposition and authoritarian adaptation, international activism, and enduring disputes over pluralism. A causal explanation fails if it treats the 1979 revolution as the origin of movements founded decades earlier or treats unemployment as sufficient to explain organizational strategy.

Section 67.3

Islamic Revival beyond the Brotherhood

The growth of Islamic public life also took forms that were neither Brotherhood activism nor opposition politics. Mosques were places of worship, learning, neighborhood mediation, and charity; their political significance depended on who controlled the pulpit and how communities used the institution. Governments appointed many preachers while allowing or tolerating others with different messages. Religious lectures circulated on cassette tapes and inexpensive printed pamphlets, permitting speakers to reach audiences beyond a particular congregation. Students organized study groups, and merchants funded local projects. These activities could raise the standing of religious scholars outside traditional official hierarchies, but did not eliminate the influence of long-established centers such as al-Azhar, Sufi networks, or local jurists. Much of this revival was primarily devotional or ethical rather than a campaign for an Islamic state.

Salafism illustrates the danger of equating theological language with one political strategy. Movements emphasizing the practices of early Muslim generations shared some texts and methods but differed over authority and action. Quietist currents discouraged rebellion, stressing religious education and obedience to rulers in most circumstances. Activist or reformist currents participated in public argument, while later jihadist currents presented an armed program. These labels are retrospective scholarly aids as much as the self-descriptions of participants. State patronage of some religious schools and missionary institutions helped particular scholarly networks expand, but local communities selected, contested, or reinterpreted what they received. The Arabian Peninsula, Egypt, North Africa, and the Levant did not become doctrinally identical because of cross-border religious funding.

Sufi orders and other established religious associations also responded to urbanization and political change. In Sudan, Morocco, Egypt, and elsewhere, devotional brotherhoods maintained teaching, social assistance, and authority through family and scholarly connections. Some supported ruling parties, others criticized them, and many focused on religious practice rather than state capture. Popular healing, pilgrimage, celebrations, and neighborhood religious life could be criticized by purist reformers but remained part of the lived Islamic landscape. The contrast was not between authentic Islamists and allegedly passive traditionalists: groups disagreed about the proper sources of authority, ritual, and the meaning of social reform. Nor should Islamic activism be imagined as exclusively male; women developed lessons, associations, and networks of religious learning that were sometimes independent of formal parties.

The Islamic charitable sector expanded through a mixture of private donations, religious endowments, zakat funds, business patronage, and migration-linked remittances. Clinics, schools, food distribution, and assistance to vulnerable households could fill gaps left by an overburdened state. Their accessibility created loyalties and a sense of local accountability, but evidence of service provision is not by itself evidence of electoral consent. Beneficiaries might welcome help while rejecting an organization's social conservatism or political ambitions. Rival organizations and governmental institutions also provided assistance. In authoritarian systems where party competition was restricted, such welfare networks were sometimes among the few durable forms of voluntary collective life, which gave them political potential even if their founders did not initially seek office.

Religious renewal intersected with other intellectual projects rather than displacing them. Arab liberal thinkers, leftists, trade unionists, secular nationalists, Christian intellectuals, feminist activists, and independent Muslim scholars debated education, gender, colonial legacies, law, and inequality. Islamists argued over concepts such as consultation, popular sovereignty, public interest, and legal interpretation. Some attempted to reconcile elections and constitutional limits with an Islamic reference; critics questioned whether the proposed public role of religion would protect equal citizenship for women and religious minorities. These arguments unfolded in newspaper columns, conferences, homes, universities, and court cases. The historical question is how public claims acquired organizational influence and state recognition, not simply which slogans were loudest.

Geography and language broadened the revival. Pilgrimage and Gulf migration introduced contact between communities long connected by scholarship and trade. Migrants carried ideas in both directions rather than receiving doctrine in a one-way flow from wealthy patrons. Afghan and Pakistani networks later fostered different forms of transnational activism, but an Egyptian engineer in Kuwait, a Moroccan student in France, and a Sudanese teacher in Riyadh did not share the same political trajectory. Distinguishing religious mobility, social solidarity, organized opposition, and armed recruitment is indispensable to explaining why the post-1979 Arab world produced varied outcomes from partially overlapping cultural exchanges.

Section 67.4

The Iranian Revolution and Its Regional Consequences

The Iranian Revolution of 1978–1979 overthrew the monarchy of Mohammad Reza Shah Pahlavi after mass mobilization involving clerics, students, workers, merchants, liberals, leftists, and other opponents. Ayatollah Ruhollah Khomeini became the leading figure of a new Islamic Republic, but the resulting political order emerged through conflicts among revolutionary currents, not an immediate consensus. Iran was not an Arab state; its Persian-language institutions and distinctive Shiʿi clerical history must not be folded into Arab national history. Its significance for this chapter is transregional. The overthrow of a powerful monarchy aligned with the United States challenged assumptions about the durability of authoritarian modernization and showed that religiously framed political authority could mobilize formidable constituencies. Events across Arab countries, however, cannot be read as simple imitation of the Iranian experience.

Khomeini's theory of the guardianship of the jurist, commonly rendered velayat-e faqih, invested a qualified Islamic jurist with an exceptional role in government during the occultation of the Twelfth Imam. It differed institutionally from most Sunni Islamist programs and was contested within Shiʿi intellectual life as well. Iran's 1979 Constitution combined republican elections and governing institutions with clerical oversight; a significant constitutional revision occurred in 1989. A source study that quotes only a later consolidated English translation risks projecting revised provisions backward into 1979. Students must distinguish the original moment of constitutional ratification from the later structure. Iranian leaders presented their revolution in universal Islamic language, yet their state also pursued security, diplomacy, and strategic interests as a particular government.

Some Sunni Islamist writers initially welcomed the fall of an autocratic ruler and the example of successful mobilization; others distrusted the revolution's Shiʿi theology or its political direction. Appreciation of an anti-monarchical victory was not agreement with clerical guardianship, and responses changed as regional tensions intensified. Arab governments also reacted differently. Saudi leaders were concerned with their own claim to religious legitimacy and with the potential for protest among Shiʿi citizens in the Eastern Province. Iraqi Baʿthist authorities interpreted revolutionary discourse through their confrontation with Iran and fears about domestic opposition. Arab Shiʿi communities were not a single Iranian political constituency: leaders, parties, and worshippers had their own local grievances, legal traditions, and sometimes deep reservations about Tehran.

The November 1979 seizure of the Grand Mosque in Mecca was a separate Saudi crisis, not an Iranian-directed operation. Their close timing nevertheless intensified public uncertainty and official alarm. In eastern Saudi Arabia, Shiʿi demonstrations also reflected local discrimination and grievances and were influenced by the broader climate of religious political mobilization. Explaining these movements by attributing them to foreign orders would discount Saudi citizens' own histories. Similarly, Shiʿi political activism in Bahrain, Iraq, or Lebanon developed through interactions among local structures and regional influences. The detailed course of the Iran–Iraq War is reserved for Chapter 69; here the concern is how its outbreak and continuation sharpened sectarian rhetoric, security practices, and political boundaries across the Arab world.

Iran's revolution also affected languages of solidarity and threat. Governments could invoke the danger of externally inspired revolution to justify surveillance, emergency law, and repression, while opponents could point to the apparent possibility of transforming an entrenched regime. The vocabulary of Islamic unity sometimes obscured disagreement over religious authority and the status of non-Muslim citizens. For international observers, the temptation was to view religious movements as interchangeable manifestations of a regional wave. That interpretation fails against the evidence of Jordanian parliamentary alliances, Moroccan monarchical religious authority, Tunisia's party-registration disputes, and Algeria's later election crisis. Each country had developed its own organizations long before the Iranian monarchy fell.

As a historical turning point, 1979 is therefore a useful organizing date rather than an absolute beginning. It linked the new Islamic Republic, Saudi religious controversy, and intensified international attention to religious movements, while earlier histories continued to shape what was possible. Comparisons should ask which actors actually cited Iranian experiences, which governments changed policies for independently documented reasons, and how contemporary observers interpreted incomplete information. The strongest conclusion is neither that Tehran engineered Arab Islamism nor that its revolution had no effect: it altered the regional political imagination and security environment without determining a common Arab future.

Section 67.5

Saudi Arabia: Religious Legitimacy, the Grand Mosque, and the Sahwa

Saudi Arabia's political order rested on a longstanding alliance between the Saudi ruling family and religious institutions associated with the teachings of Muhammad ibn ʿAbd al-Wahhab. The alliance had changed across centuries of state-building, and by the 1970s it coexisted with ministries, bureaucracies, universities, oil-dependent modernization, and a rapidly growing public infrastructure. Religious scholars supervised or influenced important aspects of education, courts, and moral regulation, but the monarchy retained decisive political authority. Oil income enabled vast construction programs, migration, and increased educational opportunities while also producing anxieties about inequality, foreign influence, and the pace of cultural change. A critique expressed in religious language could thus challenge both excessive modernizing practices and the credibility of rulers who publicly defended religious orthodoxy. The state's religiosity did not immunize it against religious dissent.

On 20 November 1979, armed followers of Juhayman al-ʿUtaybi seized the Grand Mosque in Mecca and proclaimed a member of their circle to be the expected Mahdi. The confrontation occurred at the beginning of a new Islamic century and shocked Muslims far beyond the kingdom. Saudi forces ultimately retook the sanctuary after a prolonged and deadly siege. Early diplomatic reports were confused about the leader's identity, numbers, and political implications; those errors are themselves instructive evidence of the constraints on outsiders trying to understand an unfolding emergency. The seizure was not an operation ordered by Iran, although its timing alongside the Iranian Revolution fueled international rumor. The group's challenge arose from tensions within Saudi religious and political society and should not be treated as an ordinary episode of the Iranian upheaval.

The state's response combined security action, public defense of legitimacy, and a strengthened emphasis on approved religious norms. Commentators often present the aftermath as the single moment when Saudi society became conservative. That is historically misleading: religious authority and restrictive social arrangements predated the siege, and later changes had multiple causes. Authorities sought clerical backing for the use of force at a sacred site, increased vigilance against unauthorized preaching and radical groups, and maintained close control over public institutions. Meanwhile the enormous economic and physical modernization of the oil era continued. The relevant tension was not religion versus modernization, but who could authorize change and whether state-controlled religious institutions adequately represented the population.

In the Eastern Province, Shiʿi citizens expressed grievances concerning political exclusion, religious discrimination, services, and economic opportunity. Demonstrations in late 1979 occurred in an atmosphere transformed partly by events in Iran, but their local social and political causes cannot be dismissed as mere foreign manipulation. Security responses deepened distrust. Saudi religious policies also reflected disagreements among Sunnis themselves: scholars differed about reform, the limits of royal authority, and whether criticism of government was legitimate. When describing state Islamization, students should distinguish the administration of existing institutions from novel laws or restrictions and ask what contemporary evidence establishes the date and responsibility for each policy.

The Sahwa, or Islamic Awakening, emerged through intersections between Saudi religious education and intellectual currents associated partly with Brotherhood-influenced teachers and activists. Its public proponents increasingly criticized corruption, dependence on foreign troops, and limits on consultation, especially after the 1990–1991 Gulf crisis. Some sought stronger Islamic accountability from rulers; others adopted confrontational rhetoric that the government considered threatening. The movement should not be collapsed into Juhayman's earlier armed group, nor should every Saudi religious scholar be classified as Sahwa. Shifts in the state's stance toward preaching and dissent affected the political space in which such currents developed. The crisis surrounding foreign military deployment made differences over loyalty and criticism especially visible.

Saudi policy and outreach influenced neighboring societies through scholarships, religious publications, migrant networks, and financial support for religious institutions. These relationships were neither uniform nor simply one-way: expatriate professionals brought their own political traditions into the kingdom. Saudi leaders also acted through conventional state diplomacy and security cooperation rather than religious solidarity alone. Chapter 65 treated the oil economy and monarchical transformations in depth; Chapter 67 shows how the kingdom's specific combination of religious legitimacy, bureaucratic authority, public dissent, and international position shaped post-1979 Islamism. Its experience demonstrates that the principal conflict could arise within an already explicitly religious political order.

Section 67.6

Egypt under Sadat: State Islamization and the Crisis of 1981

Anwar al-Sadat inherited the Egyptian presidency after Nasser's death in 1970 and gradually altered the country's political direction. He cultivated religious symbolism, encouraged selected Islamist students and public personalities as counterweights to Nasserists and Marxist organizations, and permitted some Brotherhood figures to return to public activity. Yet these measures did not amount to an unconditional alliance with Islamists. Sadat pursued his own presidential authority, economic liberalization, strategic realignment, and diplomatic initiative with Israel. Religious groups responded according to their distinct priorities. Some appreciated room for preaching and publication; others challenged the state's direction, social inequalities, or its restrictions on independent political activity. Government recognition and coercion could alternate rapidly, making the picture of a unified Islamist movement supported by Sadat inadequate.

Constitutional reform illustrated how rulers themselves could use Islamic language. Egypt's 1971 Constitution identified the principles of Islamic shariʿa as a principal source of legislation, while the 1980 amendment made them the principal source. This modification mattered symbolically in the contest over the state's authority to define law. It did not automatically replace the existing civil, criminal, and administrative legal systems, nor did its effect depend on a single cleric's interpretation. Courts, particularly the Supreme Constitutional Court, later mediated how the clause would apply, including limits on retrospectively invalidating preexisting legislation. The wording specifically concerned the principles of shariʿa; interpreting the provision as an order to reproduce every classical juristic rule would be a historical and legal mistake. Modern constitutional law is itself part of the story of Islamization.

Student Islamists established organizations on campuses where university expansion had created political constituencies. Contestation with leftist and nationalist students could involve demonstrations, disputes over student unions, and at times violence. Brotherhood-associated leaders generally favored gradual social penetration and selected electoral engagement, whereas more confrontational groups questioned the legitimacy of the regime itself. Egyptian Islamic Jihad and al-Jamaʿa al-Islamiyya developed differing structures, local networks, and tactical positions. Their relation to the mainstream Brotherhood was not organizational identity. Religious reformers who criticized Sadat were not automatically prepared to use armed force, just as participation in campus welfare did not guarantee a commitment to pluralist politics. Political histories require separating disputed ideas, networks of association, and specific acts.

Sadat's diplomatic turn, culminating in the 1978 Camp David frameworks and 1979 Egyptian-Israeli peace treaty, polarized domestic politics. Opponents included Islamists, Arab nationalists, leftists, and others who feared that Egypt's settlement separated its interests from wider Arab demands. The treaty did not cause every form of Islamist opposition, which already had domestic social and religious roots. Egypt's economic policies, concentration of presidential authority, and legal restrictions remained major sources of grievance. By 1981, Sadat launched sweeping arrests across ideological and religious lines, illustrating how quickly a regime that had encouraged certain organizations could move against a broader opposition. Religious discourse did not protect critics from detention.

On 6 October 1981, militants connected to Egyptian Islamic Jihad assassinated Sadat during a military parade. The attackers should be identified with their specific armed network, not with the Brotherhood as an institution. The killing revealed the limits of state calculations that religious opposition could be permanently fragmented or co-opted. It also prompted renewed emphasis on emergency powers and security management under Hosni Mubarak. At the same time, the great majority of Egyptian citizens did not participate in the assassination, and many members of religious organizations did not endorse its strategy. Students should compare the conspirators' claimed justifications with contemporary evidence of their actual organization, targets, and actions.

The Sadat years demonstrate the central paradox of state Islamization. A president could deepen the constitutional prominence of Islamic law while denying autonomous Islamist organizations political sovereignty; he could authorize public religious activity while suppressing groups that contested his decisions. Citizens encountered these changes through university rules, sermons, legal debates, family law, prices, and access to public employment. Whether constitutional religious language made a regime more legitimate depended on social position and political expectations. The subsequent Egyptian story is therefore not the replacement of a secular government by a religious movement, but a continuing contest over who spoke for religion, how the state regulated it, and whether public accountability would accompany religious symbolism.

Section 67.7

Egypt under Mubarak: Electoral Openings, Syndicates, and Armed Conflict

Hosni Mubarak assumed the presidency after Sadat's assassination and maintained a political system in which elections, parties, courts, and professional organizations operated under powerful executive and security constraints. The Muslim Brotherhood was not legalized as an ordinary political party, yet it developed public influence through associations, mosques, businesses, charitable services, and professional syndicates. Electoral participation often required independent candidates or alliances with other recognized parties. These tactics reflected pragmatism but also the narrowing of formal political opportunities. The movement's social presence was substantial in some urban areas, but it was never the only source of religious authority or civic service. The state continued to support al-Azhar, regulate official preaching, and provide public services on a far greater scale than any voluntary association.

Professional syndicates offered a particularly revealing institutional arena. Lawyers, doctors, engineers, and other educated groups used elections in their associations to challenge corruption, demand better services, and assert professional autonomy. Brotherhood candidates sometimes prospered because of disciplined organization and practical proposals rather than because every member agreed with a comprehensive religious state project. Syndicates became contested spaces for broader demands for representation. Government interventions, legal changes, and security pressures constrained them, showing that an organization could gain limited influence while remaining excluded from national executive power. The relationship between the state and the Brotherhood thus included periods of tacit accommodation as well as arrests and asset seizures, not a single uninterrupted policy.

In the 1980s and especially the 1990s, separate armed Islamist groups pursued violent confrontation with the state. Al-Jamaʿa al-Islamiyya became particularly associated with insurgency in Upper Egypt, while Egyptian Islamic Jihad had different organizational histories and leadership. Police, security personnel, public figures, tourists, and civilians became targets in a violent cycle; authorities responded with extensive emergency powers, mass detentions, and reported abuses. Violence against visitors damaged livelihoods in tourism-dependent areas, while coercive policing could alienate communities far removed from militant activity. Treating the entire Islamist political field as an armed insurgency would obscure these distinctions, but presenting militants solely as products of security repression would also erase their deliberate targets and ideological arguments.

The 1997 attack at Luxor, in which militants killed foreign tourists and Egyptian personnel, marked an especially devastating episode. Its human cost, effect on tourism, and domestic political repercussions illustrated the gulf between insurgent strategy and the aspirations of many Egyptians who sought stability or reform without violence. Subsequent reconsiderations by some imprisoned or former leaders involved arguments over the permissibility and effectiveness of armed struggle. Those revisions deserve analysis as political developments rather than proof that every earlier militant was merely misunderstood. The state's public claims of security success must be read beside evidence of arbitrary detention and the experiences of residents in areas subjected to intensive police operations.

Everyday Egyptian religious life was more diverse than the movement-state confrontation suggests. Women organized charitable associations and religious study while debating veiling, public work, marriage, and legal status. Independent preachers reached audiences through recordings and television; mosque attendance did not imply Brotherhood membership. Christians, especially the Coptic community, experienced political debates over religious identity and sometimes sectarian intimidation or violence, with local conditions varying significantly. Secular activists and other Muslims questioned the restrictions that some religious political projects might place on equal rights. Government rhetoric about national unity was not sufficient evidence that such problems had been resolved. The appropriate historical unit is the interaction of a complex society with competing institutions, not a binary of pious population and secular state.

By the end of the 1990s, Egyptian Islamism included a mainstream movement seeking influence through constrained public institutions, smaller currents focused on preaching or scholarship, and networks that had pursued armed rebellion. These categories changed as individuals reassessed strategy. Mubarak's government preserved executive control and emergency mechanisms while permitting enough public participation to keep political possibilities open but limited. The ensuing contradictions contributed to later political crises, addressed in the appropriate contemporary chapters. In this chapter, Egypt is the clearest demonstration that a state could use Islamic constitutional language, restrict independent Islamists, and fight militant groups simultaneously without resolving the question of political accountability.

Section 67.8

Syria: The Muslim Brotherhood, Armed Opposition, and Hama

Syria's Islamist opposition developed within a political system and social geography distinct from Egypt's. The Baʿth Party's rise, military rule, land reform, and construction of an extensive security state reordered relationships among urban commercial elites, rural populations, and religious institutions. Hafez al-Asad, who consolidated power in 1970, combined authoritarian party rule with patronage networks and state control of political life. The regime's leadership included many Alawites, but the state was not simply an undifferentiated sectarian institution and Syrian communities cannot be assigned collective responsibility for its decisions. Islamist dissent arose from particular organizational histories as well as objections to political exclusion, repression, and the perceived religious legitimacy of the ruling order.

The Syrian Muslim Brotherhood had developed roots in urban settings such as Aleppo, Hama, and Damascus. Its leadership and supporters did not always agree on tactics. During the late 1970s and early 1980s, armed factions associated with or adjacent to Islamist opposition carried out attacks, including killings that sharpened sectarian fear. The regime responded with arrests, executions, emergency measures, and operations involving security forces. A careful narrative distinguishes armed organizations from peaceful sympathizers and from civilians who became victims of attacks or collective punishment. It also distinguishes documented incidents from allegations circulated in partisan accounts. Neither state representations of a monolithic terrorist enemy nor opposition narratives that erase violence by militants provide adequate historical explanation.

The confrontation culminated in February 1982 in Hama, where the government deployed major force against an Islamist uprising. Syrian troops and security units used heavy weapons, and large parts of the city were devastated. Thousands of civilians died along with combatants, but the exact toll remains disputed, with wide disparities among contemporary reports, later testimony, and estimates by organizations with different access. The absence of a definitive count is no reason to obscure the scale of destruction or documented state responsibility for severe repression. Civilians experienced bombardment, displacement, imprisonment, loss of relatives, and the destruction of ordinary urban infrastructure. Students should be wary of single exact figures repeated without an identified source and a method of counting.

Hama's significance extended beyond battlefield victory. It created a political warning about organized dissent, drove activists into exile or underground networks, and severely curtailed the public capacity of the Syrian Brotherhood for years. Exile communities developed different relationships with regional patrons and political ideas; their situation cannot be taken as evidence that Syrian society had no remaining religious debate. Mosques, official Islamic institutions, business networks, and personal devotion continued under state supervision. The government's control of public narrative made later historical reconstruction difficult, and independent testimony often emerged under conditions of fear. Evidence is particularly uneven for detainees, disappearances, and the experiences of neighborhoods outside the centers of armed combat.

A purely sectarian explanation is too narrow. Class, region, merchant networks, government redistribution, party monopolization, military organization, and rival conceptions of legitimate Islamic authority all contributed to conflict. Alawite identity mattered to the perceptions and political networks of some actors, but not all Alawites supported the regime, and Sunni Syrians occupied numerous positions inside the state. Islamist political actors also disagreed over relations with other Muslim communities and over what order should replace Baʿthist rule. The question is how a particular authoritarian state translated social and ideological conflict into overwhelming repression, not whether religious identity alone made such violence inevitable.

The Syrian case is important for later comparisons. Jordanian Islamists entered limited parliamentary politics; Egyptian Brothers used syndicates and alliances; Syrian armed opposition encountered a state willing to inflict vast collective costs in restoring control. Yet differences among these outcomes do not prove that inclusion always produces moderation or that exclusion automatically generates militancy. Organizational strategy, state coercive capacity, popular support, external circumstances, and the actions of individual factions all mattered. Chapter 68 treats Lebanon's interlocking conflicts and Syria's regional interventions in their own setting. Here Hama demonstrates the destructive potential of a struggle over state power in which civilians were denied protection and independent verification was constrained.

Historical Debate

Does participation moderate movements, or does exclusion encourage militancy?

Historical question: Under what conditions does access to lawful politics change an Islamist movement’s behavior? One interpretation emphasizes the incentives of parliamentary bargaining: parties in Jordan and Morocco developed electoral platforms, negotiated compromises, and learned to speak to constituencies beyond committed members.

An alternative interpretation argues that ideology, internal organization, regime structure, and strategic circumstances remain crucial. Some organizations use political openings instrumentally; others reject violence despite repression. Algeria shows the catastrophic effect of an interrupted electoral process, but armed factions later disagreed profoundly with the FIS and with one another. Syria’s conflict had a distinct preceding history, while Egyptian jihadist groups were not the Brotherhood.

Evidence and evaluation: Compare party documents, parliamentary voting, statements about rivals’ rights, factional splits, repression, and civilian targeting. These explanations overlap and should not be forced into a false binary. The presence of an election is not sufficient evidence of liberal democracy, and the presence of repression does not excuse violence against civilians.

Division III — Jordan, the Gulf, Tunisia, and Morocco: Political Adaptation (67.9–67.12)

Section 67.9

Jordan: Brotherhood Accommodation and Parliamentary Politics

Jordan provides a useful contrast because its monarchy maintained a longer, more publicly cooperative relationship with the Muslim Brotherhood than did several neighboring governments. Brotherhood charitable and educational networks developed within a political order organized around Hashemite legitimacy, security institutions, and careful regulation of parties. The movement's ability to operate did not mean it possessed unrestricted freedom, nor did accommodation mean the monarchy surrendered religious authority. Rather, rulers and Islamist leaders sometimes found overlapping interests in social stability and opposition to leftist or radical nationalist challengers. Changes in relations depended on regional politics, particularly Palestine, and on the kingdom's internal debates over economic policy and political participation.

The late 1980s brought fiscal distress and protests, creating pressure for a new political opening. Elections in November 1989 restored parliamentary competition on a significant scale after a prolonged interruption, and Brotherhood-associated candidates performed strongly. Their success reflected organizational networks, the credibility of social work, and the weakness of some other organized political forces, but official results should not be interpreted as a referendum for a single religious program. Candidates worked within constituencies structured by electoral rules, local alliances, tribal relationships, and urban-rural differences. Participation required negotiation over what could be pursued in parliament and what remained under executive authority. It also exposed tensions between the movement's moral program and its role as a representative political organization accountable to varied constituents.

The National Charter adopted in June 1991 set out a program of legal pluralism, peaceful party activity, and stated civic protections within the Hashemite constitutional framework. It is an important primary source because it combined references to Islamic values with pledges concerning citizenship, legal equality, and representative institutions. The document's normative language, however, should not be confused with proof that its aspirations were fully implemented. The Islamic Action Front, founded in 1992, supplied a political vehicle connected to the Brotherhood. Coalition choices, election-law changes, and differences over regional treaties created both opportunities and constraints. The distinction between charter promises and actual executive power is a recurring theme in comparative constitutional history.

The Palestinian question strongly shaped Jordanian politics. A large population of Palestinian origin, memories of earlier confrontations, and the Arab-Israeli peace process affected how Islamist parties addressed national identity, refugee rights, and diplomacy. Debates over the kingdom's 1994 peace treaty with Israel intensified political divisions without making all opponents supporters of armed conflict. The Brotherhood's standing among different social groups also changed as activists assessed how much parliamentary presence could accomplish. Some supported participation as the best available path to reform; others feared that election rules and government control would turn public representation into a symbolic exercise. These debates complicated the familiar opposition between radical and moderate Islamism.

Jordanian religious life extended beyond Brotherhood institutions. Official clerics, Sufi traditions, independent teachers, Christian communities, and politically unaligned Muslims contributed to public culture. Women participated in charitable and educational networks while facing legal and social expectations that differed among families and movements. Electoral language about national unity did not erase unequal influence or differences in class and residence. The monarchy defended its own religious and dynastic authority, and government institutions regulated mosques and associations. Therefore an Islamist politician's use of religious vocabulary was neither unprecedented nor an automatic claim to represent every Muslim voter.

By the end of the century, Jordan's example suggested that partial electoral incorporation could induce bargaining and public adaptation, yet did not by itself guarantee democratic consolidation. The effects of participation must be measured through actual legislative outcomes, tolerance of opponents, and the changing behavior of parties and officials. Its contrasts with Syria and Algeria are striking, but simplistic causal formulas obscure differences in coercive history, military institutions, and electoral systems. Jordan's distinctive experience illustrates the chapter's central theme: the relationship between political Islam and state authority was negotiated within each national political order.

Section 67.10

Kuwait, Bahrain, and the Gulf Monarchies

The Gulf monarchies combined hereditary rule, commercial societies, large-scale oil development, and religious institutions in varied ways. It is misleading to classify them as a single political system merely because they shared petroleum wealth and dynastic government. Kuwait retained an elected National Assembly whose authority and interruptions became important to public political life. Bahrain's social and sectarian balance differed from Kuwait's, and other Gulf states imposed tighter limits on organized parliamentary activism. Migrant workers constituted substantial portions of several populations but possessed different rights from citizens, an essential distinction when discussing welfare, political participation, and claims of Islamic solidarity. Religious activism occurred within these structures rather than outside them.

In Kuwait, Sunni Islamist associations and candidates engaged with parliamentary and civic institutions alongside merchants, liberals, tribal figures, and Shiʿi activists. The relationship between movements and government was not a fixed alliance: legislative debate could address education, social policy, moral regulation, finance, and executive accountability. Islamic constitutional language appeared alongside practical arguments over corruption and public expenditure. Parliamentary activity gave activists experience in bargaining and public justification, while limits on electoral participation and periodic suspension of the assembly constrained the process. Students should distinguish an elected Islamist representative from an underground armed group and ask what such participation revealed about changing conceptions of legitimate opposition.

Kuwaiti political life was also affected by the Iraqi invasion of August 1990 and liberation in 1991. This chapter treats those events principally as shifts in the context of domestic political and religious mobilization, leaving the war's military and diplomatic history to Chapter 69. Religious groups took different positions on national defense and the role of foreign forces. Wartime solidarity did not eliminate later disagreements over parliamentary authority, social regulation, and state spending. The aftermath also underscored the unequal legal and social positions of migrants and noncitizens, whose contributions to Gulf economies were not matched by equivalent political rights. This inequality is part of the social setting in which appeals to shared religious community operated.

Bahrain's political conflicts were rooted in relations among the ruling family, representative institutions, workers, and religious communities. Shiʿi activists included currents with differing orientations toward clerical authority, constitutional participation, and the government. Protests and unrest in the 1990s reflected demands for representation and grievances over discrimination, rather than one externally directed scheme. Iranian revolutionary rhetoric and regional security policies formed part of the context, but it would be analytically unsound to attribute all Bahraini Shiʿi dissent to Iran. Sunni political currents likewise differed over authority and reform. The state used security methods and selective reform amid arguments over the boundaries of legitimate political activity.

Across the Gulf, governments financed schools, mosque networks, broadcasting, and religious charitable work; some institutions operated through private or semiofficial networks. In countries with large expatriate populations, teachers, preachers, and professionals from Egypt, the Levant, and South Asia helped transmit ideas, while local elites selected those that fitted national policies. Labor migration and pilgrimages linked people who otherwise lived under different legal systems. Women's education expanded in parts of the region, while opportunities for employment, organizing, and political representation remained uneven. Religious revival affected families and public norms without necessarily producing autonomous Islamist parties. The everyday effects of legislation and administrative control differed sharply between citizens and migrants.

Gulf cases therefore test the limits of general claims about political Islam. A monarchy might use religion to strengthen its own legitimacy, tolerate selected parliamentarians, repress perceived opponents, and rely on foreign-trained teachers at the same time. A movement could favor strict social norms but pursue legislative bargaining rather than violence. Government opponents could use the language of constitutional rights while also organizing through religious institutions. These differences were produced by the interaction of local political institutions, demographic structures, state resources, and regional crises—not by the mere presence of Islamic identity.

Section 67.11

Tunisia: Islamic Tendency, Ennahda, and Political Exclusion

Tunisia's Islamist history developed under a republic that publicly privileged state-directed modernization, women's education, and legal reform. After independence, Habib Bourguiba's government reduced the autonomy of many religious institutions and reshaped family law in ways that became defining features of the national political project. Yet state control did not eliminate religious attachment or debates about identity. Students and intellectuals entering universities during the 1970s encountered official institutions, leftist organizations, and renewed Islamic study groups. Activists criticized one-party rule, economic inequality, and what they regarded as excessive cultural dependence on Europe. Their response was not simply an effort to reverse every social reform: debates within the movement included democracy, labor, economic justice, and the relation between religious ethics and political rights.

Rached Ghannouchi, Abdelfattah Mourou, and associates developed an Islamic current during the 1970s. On 6 June 1981, the Islamic Tendency Movement, or MTI, publicly announced itself and sought political recognition. Its foundational public statement asserted the relevance of Islamic principles to cultural, economic, and political life, while also presenting claims concerning public freedoms and popular participation. Scholars disagree about the degree to which later moderation represented continuity or strategic adaptation; the 1981 document must therefore be read as a political statement by particular organizers, not an accurate forecast of every future action. The government did not legalize the movement, and arrests of leading members followed. The repression occurred in a wider authoritarian setting that also restricted other forms of opposition.

Tunisia's university milieu was particularly important. Religious organizations disputed student-union influence with leftist groups, and disputes over identity and public morality sometimes became confrontational. Islamists developed channels for disseminating lectures and publications, while official authorities could describe broad dissent through the language of security. The relationship between peaceful political advocacy and incidents of violence must be investigated specifically rather than presumed from group identity. Family choices about dress, education, and work became charged with political symbolism. Women participated in the movement and in secular feminist activism; neither position can be reduced to an external observer's single stereotype. The state itself claimed to protect women's rights even while restricting opposition political freedoms.

Zine el-Abidine Ben Ali removed Bourguiba from office in November 1987 and initially projected an image of political opening. The MTI adopted the name Ennahda, or Renaissance, in 1989. Because the party remained unrecognized, candidates associated with its networks participated as independents in the April 1989 legislative elections. They received a notable portion of votes in some areas but did not gain parliamentary seats under the electoral system and official results. The episode illustrates the difference between public support, legal recognition, and actual representation. Rather than opening a stable parliamentary road, the authorities imposed increasingly severe restrictions and repression in the early 1990s. Leaders were jailed, went into exile, or continued activity clandestinely.

Movement members later described their commitments to pluralism, but historians should evaluate self-presentation alongside contemporary manifestos, reports of internal debate, the treatment of rivals, and official records. Likewise, the regime's security claims require corroboration; state allegations cannot be treated as settled truth simply because they appeared in court proceedings controlled by an authoritarian system. The governing party preserved extensive power over unions, media, and elections, and the repression of Islamists formed part of a broader restriction of independent politics. Tunisia's special relationship to France and Europe shaped public arguments about secularism and cultural legitimacy, but it did not determine citizens' political choices mechanically.

At the century's end, Ennahda was not a legalized governing party but a suppressed movement with domestic and exiled networks. Its trajectory differed from Jordanian parliamentary accommodation and from Algeria's violent rupture after interrupted elections. The case warns that an Islamist movement's ideological statements and the state's claims of modernity cannot substitute for empirical study of civil liberties, organizational practices, and electoral rules. Later political developments, especially those following 2011, belong chiefly to Chapter 71. Here the focus remains the late twentieth-century emergence of a religiously framed opposition under a restrictive republican state.

Section 67.12

Morocco: Monarchical Religious Authority and Islamist Alternatives

Morocco was neither a secular republic nor a state whose rulers lacked religious claims. The Alaouite monarchy cultivated dynastic and religious authority, and the king's title Amir al-Muʾminin, or Commander of the Faithful, formed part of the constitutional and political language of rule. Religious authority was therefore already institutionally central when new Islamist groups sought reform. The monarchy controlled or influenced mosques and major religious appointments while maintaining parties, elections, and political mechanisms whose openness was limited and varied over time. Arguments over religion became arguments about whether the king's religious legitimacy excluded independent Islamic political projects, or whether competing activists could claim a moral mandate to criticize the state.

Abdessalam Yassine developed a movement commonly known as Justice and Spirituality, or al-ʿAdl wa-l-Ihsan, that joined religious education and social organization with a challenge to established political authority. His thought drew on spiritual discipline as well as criticism of authoritarian power. The movement's unwillingness to accept royal supremacy as the final limit on its political claims separated it from currents willing to compete within the existing constitutional system. State restrictions, surveillance, and periods of confinement shaped its capacity to organize. Its social following should not be inferred simply from the prominence of its leader; students should ask how local circles, publications, and community practice transmitted its arguments and how ordinary members understood the costs of opposition.

Another Islamist current moved toward formal political participation by accepting the monarchy's constitutional framework. Reform and Unity activists joined an existing legal party during the 1990s, and the party became the Party of Justice and Development, or PJD, in 1998. This gradual development should not be projected backward as if the PJD already existed in its later form during the early 1980s. Organizational mergers, renaming, and negotiation over electoral legality were themselves politically significant. Participation required restraint about challenging the religious standing of the king, while enabling activists to contest social policies, corruption, and public administration through recognized institutions. The resulting relationship was not one of simple state control; both authorities and activists retained bargaining interests.

Morocco's social setting was wider than the visible party contest. Arabic-speaking and Amazigh communities inhabited distinct historical regions, and debates about identity included language, education, rural development, and migration. Sufi networks and traditional scholars remained influential, while urban professionals and students formed new publics. Women disputed family law, education, and representation, with Islamic reformers, rights advocates, and state institutions advancing different projects. The monarchy sometimes appeared as sponsor of social reform and sometimes as guardian of a religious order criticized by opposing movements. A comparison with Tunisia must therefore begin from different constitutional premises rather than assuming that all Maghrebi governments confronted the same kind of religious opposition.

International ties also mattered. Moroccan migrants to Europe transmitted remittances, religious publications, and experiences of pluralism and discrimination; connections to the broader Maghreb and Middle East shaped debate. Yet the influence of external ideologies operated through domestic institutions. The kingdom's controlled pluralism offered opportunities that had been unavailable to Algeria's FIS after 1992, but it limited the extent to which parliamentary actors could determine core state policy. Students should look for evidence of actual legislative achievements and concessions, not assume that election participation always reflects either full democracy or complete co-optation.

Morocco illustrates two Islamist strategies under one religiously legitimated monarchy: oppositional social mobilization that contested the constitutional order, and participation that accepted important limits in exchange for legal access. The difference did not derive from a simple distinction between believers and secularists. It emerged from organizational ideas, interpretations of legitimate authority, political constraints, and evolving social constituencies. This paired case helps explain why political Islam after 1979 cannot be treated as a uniform revolutionary movement.

Section 67.13

Algeria: One-Party Crisis and the Islamic Salvation Front

Algeria's post-independence system drew legitimacy from the struggle against French colonialism and the leadership of the National Liberation Front, or FLN. The state invested in education, national industry, social programs, and a powerful security apparatus, but the one-party framework restricted organized competition. By the 1980s falling oil revenue, unemployment, housing pressure, and dissatisfaction with corruption threatened the government's developmental promises. Young Algerians could find official nationalist rhetoric increasingly distant from everyday scarcity. Islam had never been absent from Algerian public life; religious associations, scholars, mosques, and arguments over postcolonial identity predated the crisis. The issue was why a particular Islamist coalition came to represent substantial opposition to a government whose revolutionary legitimacy seemed exhausted to many citizens.

Protests in October 1988 exposed public anger over economic conditions and political exclusion. Security forces used deadly violence, and the crisis helped induce constitutional and party reforms. The 1989 Constitution opened the way for multiparty politics, although the state retained powerful presidential and military institutions. Political pluralism was contested from its beginning: the rules of competition and acceptable party identity remained politically charged. Newly legal or emerging organizations faced uneven access to media, resources, and state protection. The FLN's problems did not create a vacuum filled only by Islamists; other parties, unions, regional movements, and independent intellectuals also challenged government authority, but they had differing capacities to mobilize voters.

The Islamic Salvation Front, known by its French initials FIS, formed in 1989 under leaders including Abbassi Madani and Ali Belhadj. It combined preachers, neighborhood organizers, religious militants, and supporters with different views of political law and representation. Its social base included urban districts shaped by youth unemployment and dissatisfaction with state services, but economic hardship alone cannot explain the movement's strength. Religious authority, mosque networks, protest against FLN patronage, and a reputation for discipline all contributed. Some FIS rhetoric raised serious concerns about political pluralism, women's equality, and whether democratic elections would remain meaningful if the party gained power. Historians must not conceal these statements in order to criticize the subsequent cancellation of elections.

In June 1990 the FIS won a striking victory in municipal and provincial elections, demonstrating that Islamist organization could translate social protest into electoral offices. Local governments gave activists responsibility for services and administrative decisions rather than only the ability to denounce national policy. Assessing their conduct requires attention to specific municipalities, budget constraints, and disputes with central authorities. Voters might support the party as a vehicle against the FLN without embracing every element of its ideological program. Conversely, electoral victory did not make allegations about the movement's willingness to respect opposition rights irrelevant. Algeria had entered a genuine political contest whose institutions remained fragile.

The national political confrontation sharpened in 1991. Strikes, government restrictions, and clashes changed the terms of participation before the December legislative election. When voters went to the polls for the first round on 26 December 1991, the FIS won a large plurality of seats decided at that stage and appeared positioned to gain a parliamentary majority. The mechanics of the electoral system mattered: seats won were not identical to the percentage of all eligible citizens supporting a religious state, and a second round remained scheduled. Leading military figures and others feared the consequences of allowing the election to proceed, while critics argued that interruption would destroy emerging constitutional legitimacy. Neither side's fears can be treated as evidence that the future was predetermined.

The Algerian case thus joins economic crisis, political opening, Islamist mobilization, and institutional distrust. It should not be compressed into a claim that poverty automatically radicalized young people or that the entire population wanted religious rule. Evidence includes election returns, local administrative records, contemporary journalism, movement programs, accounts from women and labor activists, and human-rights monitoring. These materials reveal an increasingly polarized polity in which the terms of peaceful competition were unresolved. The interruption and ensuing war are examined in §67.14, where chronology and responsibility must be traced separately rather than treated as one instantaneous collapse.

Section 67.14

Algeria’s Interrupted Election and Civil War, 1991–2000

The second round of Algeria's parliamentary election never occurred. After the FIS's strong first-round performance in December 1991, President Chadli Bendjedid was pushed from office on 11 January 1992 amid decisive military intervention. Authorities cancelled the remaining electoral process and created a High Council of State. The sequence matters: the election was interrupted in January, a state of emergency followed in February, and the FIS was formally dissolved by judicial decision on 4 March. Treating the interruption and banning as simultaneous obscures the steps through which Algeria's political opening was dismantled. The government claimed to be preventing the collapse of republican institutions, while opponents challenged the use of coercive power to negate an election. Human Rights Watch reported detentions, shootings, and abuses during the first months of the crackdown.

Violence escalated through an expanding and fragmented conflict rather than a single unified Islamist army. The FIS had been an electoral and political organization with internal disagreements. The Islamic Salvation Army, or AIS, later developed as an armed formation linked to the FIS, while the Armed Islamic Group, or GIA, pursued a more extreme strategy and was not interchangeable with the FIS leadership. Other factions and regional networks appeared and split. Some attacked police, soldiers, officials, intellectuals, journalists, and civilians; the GIA became especially associated with mass violence against civilians. The state's security forces and affiliated armed structures were also implicated in abuses, including arbitrary detention, torture, disappearances, and unlawful killings. The record requires identifying perpetrators where evidence supports attribution, not distributing responsibility evenly without grounds.

Political organizations did not react alike to the cancellation. Secular activists and women's rights advocates feared the prospect of authoritarian religious legislation but also criticized the destruction of legal political competition. Other parties questioned whether either the military leadership or Islamist commanders could secure representative government. Families experienced threats from armed actors and intrusive security operations while remaining largely unable to influence national decisions. The conflict fractured social trust and transformed ordinary acts—commuting to work, attending school, reporting news, or visiting relatives—into potential risks. It is historically inaccurate to equate being religious, voting for the FIS, joining an armed organization, and approving attacks on civilians; those are separate positions and actions.

The violence of the mid- and late 1990s included assassinations and massacres that produced widespread fear. Some major atrocities were widely attributed to armed Islamist groups, including the GIA, while contemporaneous accounts and later investigations raised questions about security-force conduct, failures to protect civilians, and possible complicity in particular episodes. Those controversies must be handled as questions about specific events and sources. Lack of unrestricted access to military records or conflict sites does not erase the evidence for killings committed by identified perpetrators, nor does a government assertion remove the need for independent scrutiny. Exact national death totals vary among sources and counting methods; a textbook should not invent a single authoritative figure.

Public institutions continued to function under pressure. Teachers, doctors, civil servants, judges, farmers, and shopkeepers negotiated threats from militias and police, economic disruption, internal displacement, and restrictions on speech. Journalists were vulnerable both to militant violence and government limitations, making the available evidence incomplete. The crisis interacted with political efforts to restore civilian electoral institutions, internal splits within armed groups, and contested initiatives toward reconciliation. By the end of the decade, the AIS's ceasefire and the government's Civil Concord policy signaled changing conflict dynamics, although they did not fully account for victims' demands for truth and justice. Algeria's recovery cannot be described as a simple decisive victory by one side.

Historical analysis should separate four judgments: whether FIS political programs threatened pluralist rights; whether the electoral interruption violated constitutional expectations; whether specific armed groups committed atrocities; and whether the state's counterinsurgency violated human rights. Evidence for one proposition does not automatically answer the others. Algeria's tragedy demonstrates that an opening without trustworthy rules and institutions can collapse under pressure, yet it does not prove that civil war was inevitable once Islamists achieved electoral success. Comparative claims about inclusion and militancy must consider military organization, factional choices, international circumstances, and the inability of ordinary citizens to secure protection from competing armed actors.

Section 67.15

Sudan: Nimeiri, the September Laws, and Renewed War

Sudan's political history combined Islamic reform movements, powerful Sufi and sectarian networks, military interventions, and deep conflicts over the distribution of authority between Khartoum and other regions. Jaʿfar Nimeiri took power in 1969 and shifted alliances repeatedly during his presidency. The country's northern, southern, western, and eastern societies differed in language, religion, livelihood, and political history. Describing the south simply as non-Muslim would erase southern Muslims and the varied Christian and indigenous religious communities, just as describing the north as uniformly Arab and Muslim would obscure its own diversity. These distinctions are essential because national legislation about religious identity interacted with grievances over regional autonomy, public employment, land, security, and access to state resources.

The 1972 Addis Ababa Agreement had ended a previous phase of north-south war and provided for southern regional autonomy. During the early 1980s the regime undermined that settlement, redividing the south and confronting military dissent and regional discontent. In May 1983, mutinies and armed rebellion developed, leading to the formation of the Sudan People's Liberation Movement and Army under John Garang and others. The civil war thus resumed before the September 1983 legal changes. An explanation that makes the September Laws the sole cause of the conflict reverses chronology and ignores preexisting struggles over autonomy, political incorporation, and the economy. The southern movement itself framed claims differently across time; its early documents included arguments for a transformed, united Sudan rather than simply an inevitable separate nation-state.

In September 1983 Nimeiri issued a series of legal measures usually known as the September Laws. Key enactments included criminal and evidentiary provisions that placed Islamic legal claims at the center of state punishment and judicial practice; other related measures followed. Some penalties provoked intense opposition, and the laws raised major questions about their application to non-Muslim citizens and the relationship between political authority and jurisprudence. The enactments did not merely reflect an independent clerical consensus: they were a presidential program shaped by Nimeiri's search for legitimacy and allies. Legal scholarship shows disagreements about how faithfully individual provisions reflected classical Islamic legal traditions. The official designation of laws as Islamic must not substitute for examining their exact text, drafting process, and enforcement.

Islamist intellectual and organizational actors occupied an ambiguous place in Nimeiri's political strategy. Hasan al-Turabi had developed an influential Sudanese Islamist network, but relations with the president were marked by alliance, competition, and repression. Other religious leaders, including representatives of established sectarian parties and Sufi constituencies, did not uniformly support the new laws. Muslim citizens who opposed Nimeiri might object to authoritarian enforcement, the exclusion of independent jurisprudence, or the regime's disregard of regional rights while continuing to value Islamic law as a moral tradition. Southern critics often regarded national legal Islamization as evidence that the central state would not respect religious and regional pluralism. These differences cannot be compressed into Muslims supporting and Christians opposing a single set of decrees.

The crisis of 1985 brought social, economic, and political pressures together. Protests and strikes undermined Nimeiri's hold on power, and military leaders removed him in April while he was abroad. The subsequent transition created opportunities for parties and unions, but did not end the southern war or settle the legal controversy. Governments inherited multiple urgent issues: debt, regional insecurity, questions over the September Laws, and relations between civilian and military authorities. For ordinary families, the period meant uncertainty about courts, schools, recruitment, prices, and basic safety rather than solely arguments among elite jurists.

Sudan demonstrates the distinction between Islamization by presidential decree and the seizure of government by an Islamist-organized coalition. Nimeiri was not simply an Islamist party leader and his policy preceded the military–Islamist order established in 1989. The two projects must be related without conflating their motives or institutional mechanisms. By tracing the chronology of autonomy, May 1983 rebellion, September legal enactments, and April 1985 overthrow, historians can explain why religion entered an already fractured state rather than inventing a religious cause for every division. The following section analyzes the distinct political order that emerged after the 1989 coup.

Section 67.16

Sudan after 1989: Bashir, Turabi, and Islamist State-Building

Sudan returned to multiparty civilian rule after the 1985 uprising, but governments faced severe economic problems, divided coalitions, and continuing war. The Umma Party, Democratic Unionist Party, southern political actors, and the National Islamic Front, or NIF, competed over legal policy and the country's direction. Hasan al-Turabi's NIF developed parliamentary, professional, educational, and organizational influence while seeking a more comprehensive transformation of state and society. Peace initiatives involving the Sudan People's Liberation Movement/Army raised the possibility of freezing or revising Islamic legal provisions and negotiating constitutional principles. These pressures shaped the political choices of the Islamist movement and the military. Sudan's subsequent military coup cannot be explained by abstract religious ideas alone; it occurred amid an immediate contest over who would control the state and its peace process.

On 30 June 1989, officers led by Omar al-Bashir overthrew the elected government in a coup organized with support from the NIF. The new rulers presented themselves as a national salvation regime and initially obscured aspects of their relationship with Islamist organizers, even detaining Turabi for a period. The relationship between military command and ideological movement was complex from the outset. Over time the regime dissolved parties, restricted unions, reorganized administration and security, and pursued an explicitly Islamist political program. Its institutions combined military coercion, religious authorization, and methods of authoritarian governance. It would be inaccurate to say a spontaneous wave of popular religious enthusiasm had simply elevated an Islamist party through elections; political control changed through force.

The Islamist state sought to reshape law, education, public employment, financial institutions, and cultural life, but its ability to implement a unified vision was uneven. Political loyalists gained access to important institutions, while opponents were dismissed, detained, or marginalized. State claims about religious legitimacy interacted with disputes among Islamic scholars and competition for bureaucratic influence. Social expectations concerning gender, public conduct, and dress were enforced through laws and administrative practices that could affect women disproportionately. Independent journalists, unions, professionals, and civic organizations faced restrictions. As elsewhere, students must distinguish the regime's stated aspiration to build an Islamic order from documented administrative measures and their consequences for different groups.

The civil war continued and intensified in parts of the country. Southern communities faced fighting, displacement, coercion, and contested control of land and transport. The government's Islamic political program sharpened questions about whether equal citizenship could survive under a state that privileged one religious interpretation. Yet the war's roots also included struggles over political authority, regional autonomy, historical inequalities, and resources. Members of the SPLM/A developed their own political agenda and internal conflicts, and civilians were not automatically represented by either armed organization. Describing Sudan's suffering only as a religious war misses the many Sudanese Muslims who opposed the government and the diversity among southerners and northerners alike.

Sudan also became important to cross-border political networks. The regime's relationship with Islamist movements and transnational activists, including the presence of Osama bin Laden in Sudan during part of the 1990s, prompted international concern and sanctions. International isolation had economic and diplomatic effects but did not determine every internal decision. Relations among Turabi, Bashir, and military-security figures became increasingly strained, producing an open rupture toward the end of the century. That struggle showed that an Islamist movement and a state apparatus could possess different priorities even after cooperating to seize power. Religious ideology did not eliminate competition over executive control.

For historians, Sudan offers a difficult but essential comparison with Egypt and Algeria. In Egypt, a president adopted Islamic constitutional language while restricting the Brotherhood; in Algeria, a competitive opening collapsed and produced civil war; in Sudan, an Islamist-aligned network joined military officers to govern. None of these paths followed automatically from shared religious vocabulary. Sudan's experience must be judged through the evidence of political coercion, social policies, and war, not through the mere assertion that its rulers were Islamic. Chapter 67 ends its main Sudanese narrative around 2000, reserving later peace processes, partition, and subsequent transformations for the modern synthesis where appropriate.

Historical Debate

What best explains Islamist organizational durability?

One interpretation emphasizes the usefulness of voluntary welfare services, education, and religious community. In settings with limited municipal resources, associations built trust and acquired practical influence through clinics, schools, professional networks, and neighborhood help. This interpretation explains why movements could survive restrictions on formal party activity.

A second interpretation emphasizes political institutions and coercion: a monarchy’s limits on party competition, an authoritarian republic’s repression, or military seizure of office could determine what organizations were able to do. Ideology and leadership mattered too, as shown by differences between nonviolent reformers, revolutionary groups, and armed factions. Sudan’s 1989 coup cannot be explained merely by the popularity of charities.

These explanations are not mutually exclusive. The relevant evidence includes service budgets, beneficiary interviews, election returns, legal rules, records of repression, organizational decisions, and the treatment of rivals. A genuine debate must weigh the relative importance of these factors rather than claim every service recipient supported a movement’s entire political program.

  1. What evidence would show whether welfare recipients also voted for a movement?
  2. Which case suggests that state power can override social popularity?
  3. How would you distinguish an ideological change from a tactical response to repression?

Section 67.17

Palestinian Political Islam and the Emergence of Hamas

Palestinian Islamist politics developed within the distinctive conditions of dispossession, exile, military occupation, and a national movement that included secular and leftist organizations. Palestinian Muslim Brotherhood networks existed before 1979 and maintained religious education, charitable associations, and social institutions in Gaza and the West Bank. They did not initially follow exactly the same political priorities as the Palestine Liberation Organization or its constituent factions, which had emphasized armed national liberation and representation in international diplomacy. After the Israeli occupation of 1967, Palestinians faced land and mobility restrictions, political policing, and debates over the appropriate methods of national resistance. The wider history of occupation and Palestinian political organization belongs to Chapters 61, 66, 68, and 70; this section examines the specifically Islamist organizational transformation.

Brotherhood-associated activities built durable community networks through mosques, schools, welfare institutions, sports, and religious teaching. Such institutions could provide needed services while developing leadership and local visibility. Their relation to Israeli military administration was complicated by differing moments of registration, tolerance, restriction, and confrontation; an oversimplified statement that Israel created Hamas would erase Palestinian organizers' agency and the independent ideological history of the Brotherhood. Likewise, an account that ignores the occupation would omit the central political conditions of mobilization. Muslim Palestinians did not share a single attitude toward the Brotherhood, and participation in a local charity was not equivalent to agreement with an armed program.

The First Intifada began in December 1987 as a mass Palestinian uprising. Hamas emerged from Palestinian Brotherhood networks during its opening phase, associated particularly with Sheikh Ahmad Yassin and other Gaza activists. Its development marked a shift toward direct national resistance, organized in competition and sometimes cooperation with existing Palestinian political currents. The movement combined religious ideology, social activity, political organization, and armed action, though the balance of these changed over time. Its formation cannot be read as proof that all earlier Brotherhood work aimed inevitably at an armed organization. Historical explanation requires attention to the uprising's immediate catalysts, the movement's internal choices, and the pressure to respond to other Palestinian organizations.

The Hamas Covenant of August 1988 is an essential but disturbing source. It joined claims to Palestinian national rights to an explicitly Islamic conception of struggle and contained antisemitic conspiracy allegations and hostility toward Jews as Jews, not merely criticism of Israeli government policy. Those claims are false and should be identified as antisemitism, rather than excused as ordinary political rhetoric or conflated with the views of Palestinians generally. The charter is evidence of the movement's ideology at a particular moment; it is not a neutral description of historical events, a statement issued by all Palestinians, or a reliable account of Jewish history. Later changes in Hamas political language should be studied through later documents, not silently read backward into the 1988 text.

Competition among Palestinian organizations shaped questions about representation, social authority, and strategy. Islamist activists criticized secular nationalist leaders, while Palestinians differed on the value and costs of armed action, negotiation, civil resistance, and religiously defined nationalism. Women, labor organizers, students, and neighborhood associations played important roles in the Intifada, often pursuing goals not reducible to one party's program. Israeli security operations, arrests, closures, and violence affected the choices and lives of civilians across political lines. The emergence of Hamas occurred in this densely populated political field. Distinguishing it from other Palestinian groups is necessary for understanding both its appeal to some constituencies and the criticisms it faced from others.

The Palestinian case complicates generalizations drawn from Egypt or Jordan. The primary question of political authority concerned not only domestic representation but also the national status of a population under occupation and in exile. Religion could supply a framework for solidarity, service provision, territorial claims, and militancy, but it was never the only language of Palestinian politics. By the end of the twentieth century, Hamas had become a durable actor, while disputes over violence, political participation, and national representation remained unresolved. Detailed treatment of subsequent conflict, peace initiatives, and Palestinian self-government follows in the adjacent chapters, avoiding the duplication of a full Israeli-Palestinian history here.

Section 67.18

Transnational Islamic Activism and Jihadist Networks

Political and religious networks crossed Arab state borders through scholarship, migration, pilgrimage, commerce, publishing, and international organizations. These connections were far older than the late twentieth century, but cheaper travel, expanded universities, the cassette and print media, and oil-era migration gave them new reach. A religious lecture recorded in one country might circulate in several others; an Egyptian or Sudanese teacher employed in the Gulf might transmit local experiences and foreign reading lists in both directions. Islamic charities collected donations for education and humanitarian relief, sometimes operating through communities with little direct political representation. Most cross-border religious interaction was not connected to armed militancy. Treating the circulation of religious ideas as a terrorism network would badly distort the historical scale and purpose of these ordinary forms of exchange.

The Soviet intervention in Afghanistan beginning in December 1979 became a major site of international mobilization. Afghan resistance forces carried the principal military burden of the war, while outside governments supported differing factions for strategic reasons. Volunteers from several Arab countries traveled to the region, sometimes receiving religious legitimation, donations, training, or organizational support. Their numbers were small compared with Afghan participants, and their motivations and subsequent actions varied. Some became involved in humanitarian activity or local support; others participated in combat and developed transnational military contacts. Pakistan, the Gulf, and Afghanistan formed important routes, but the political consequences differed from those of routine labor migration or pilgrimage. The larger Afghan war must not be rewritten as primarily an Arab conflict.

Networks later associated with al-Qaeda emerged from part of this wartime environment and from subsequent decisions by activists and leaders. Abdullah Azzam, Osama bin Laden, and others promoted different interpretations of foreign jihad and the obligations of Muslims, but their organizational histories should not be collapsed into one timeless movement. The rise of al-Qaeda in the late 1980s and its subsequent development reflected specific relationships, resources, ideological shifts, and geopolitical ruptures. Violence against civilians and indiscriminate attacks were strategic choices for which perpetrators bore responsibility, not inevitable consequences of Islamic religious revival. Many Muslim scholars, organizations, and communities rejected these methods, and some former participants reconsidered militant positions.

The 1990–1991 Gulf crisis changed debates over foreign military deployments, Saudi legitimacy, and the direction of Islamist activism. In several countries, governments used emergency law to monitor or repress returning militants and unrelated religious opponents alike. The distinction matters: evidence of an individual's travel to Pakistan or Afghanistan does not establish participation in a later armed organization. Likewise, the presence of transnational fundraising cannot be taken as proof that every recipient school or charity adopted a militant program. Historical investigations rely on organizational correspondence, travel accounts, criminal proceedings, investigative journalism, and critical scholarship. Each evidence type is uneven, and retrospective narratives written after major attacks can impose an artificial sense of inevitability on earlier, more fragmented networks.

Other transnational networks used different methods. Islamic scholars debated law through conferences and universities, international relief institutions distributed resources, and parties shared public ideas while remaining anchored in particular states. Shiʿi seminaries and clerical relationships linked Iraq, Iran, Lebanon, and Gulf communities but did not make every institution an instrument of one government. Sunni organizations had equally complex relations with states and patrons. Language, denomination, and funding could facilitate contact without producing identical political projects. Students should map routes and distinguish the nationality of participants, the locations of organizations, and the purposes of particular links rather than coloring whole countries as though they belonged to homogeneous blocs.

By about 2000, the term jihadism was increasingly used in scholarship and policy to describe organized currents that interpreted armed struggle as a central political method. It must be distinguished from the multiple historical meanings of jihad in Islamic thought and from ordinary religious practice. The later global security environment should not overwhelm the chapter's historical focus: the great majority of Arab Islamist organizations and religious networks in this period did not follow the trajectory of al-Qaeda. Transnational activism matters because it expanded possibilities for solidarity, ideological learning, and sometimes violence while interacting with fundamentally local struggles over law, elections, public services, and state power.

Section 67.19

Religion in Everyday Society: Gender, Education, Welfare, and Dissent

The history of political Islam is incomplete if it follows only presidents, party leaders, and confrontations with security forces. Millions of people experienced changing religious public life through schools, workplaces, households, clinics, newspapers, and community organizations. Urban growth created neighborhoods where municipal services lagged behind need; voluntary associations sometimes distributed food, operated low-cost clinics, or organized tutoring. These activities could establish a reputation for probity and neighborliness, but the users of a clinic were not a political constituency with a single voice. Some welcomed services without accepting the organizers' constitutional aims. Others turned to government hospitals, secular associations, trade unions, kinship networks, or religious institutions outside Islamist movements. Political historians should distinguish what services were offered from the motivations of those who offered or received them.

Women were organizers, professionals, teachers, donors, students, and political thinkers as well as subjects of debate about dress and family law. Women's Islamic study groups sometimes opened public arenas for learning, leadership, and professional networking within societies where conventional politics was tightly policed. Some participants interpreted veiling as religious commitment, social respectability, or autonomy, while others experienced pressure from relatives, institutions, or activists. Secular and religious women's organizations disputed marriage, divorce, inheritance, employment, and citizenship. State policy could advance educational opportunity while restricting political association; Islamist networks could offer leadership opportunities while defending unequal rules. Neither the language of liberation nor that of tradition should substitute for the record of individual choice and legal consequence.

Education provided another channel of change. Expanding universities and teacher training programs exposed young adults to new social contacts and printed arguments over religion, nation, and modernity. School curricula reflected government efforts to regulate public morals and historical identity, while informal circles and recordings distributed views that official institutions did not monopolize. Some university associations offered lectures and welfare assistance; others engaged in coercive contests over campus activity. Students from working-class households, rural backgrounds, and wealthy urban families often experienced these institutions differently. Differences in class, gender, residence, and political opportunity shaped the appeal of religious activism more directly than national labels alone can convey.

Non-Muslim communities had important perspectives on public Islamization. Egyptian Copts, Christians in the Levant, and religiously diverse populations in Sudan and elsewhere could interpret constitutional religious language through questions about equality, personal status, officeholding, and physical safety. Some cooperated with Islamist neighbors on shared social problems, while others feared exclusion by projects that defined legitimate national membership narrowly. Muslims also disagreed profoundly over religion's public role, so the comparison is not simply between an Islamic majority and non-Muslim minorities. A constitutional document might declare civic equality while administrative practice produced uneven results. Historical evidence requires studying specific court rulings, employment regulations, educational policies, and experiences of local communities.

The everyday effects of repression likewise crossed ideological boundaries. After violent confrontations in Egypt, Algeria, Syria, and Sudan, residents faced curfews, raids, detention, and fear of insurgent attacks. Families of prisoners struggled with income loss and political stigma; displaced populations confronted interrupted schooling and work. Activists defending civil liberties could oppose both armed groups and security agencies. A journalist criticizing violence committed by militants was not necessarily endorsing a government's treatment of suspects. Conversely, a critic of police abuses did not thereby endorse Islamist armed action. This double insistence on civilian rights and accountability for specific perpetrators is indispensable to serious history.

Attention to ordinary life helps explain why political Islam survived changing state strategies. Movements could persist through professional networks and neighborhood institutions when parliamentary access closed; states could sustain their own public authority through welfare, education, and religious bureaucracies. Social relations were not secondary consequences of ideology but part of the terrain on which political programs gained or lost credibility. Religious identity remained multifaceted, ranging from private devotion to public legal claims and from local charity to international solidarity. The chapter's comparative conclusions therefore have to be tested against citizens' actual opportunities, protections, and constraints, rather than only the speeches of leaders.

Section 67.20

Political Islam after 1979: Comparative Synthesis

By the end of the twentieth century, political Islam was a durable but varied feature of Arab public life. Iran's revolution had altered regional expectations without generating an identical Arab program. Egypt had adopted Islamic constitutional language and tolerated limited Brotherhood participation while suppressing armed insurgency. Saudi rulers had defended their religious authority against challengers and faced debate over official policies and foreign security ties. Syrian security forces had crushed organized Islamist revolt in Hama. Jordanian and Moroccan activists had entered controlled forms of parliamentary politics, while Tunisia's Ennahda remained repressed. Algeria suffered an interrupted electoral opening and civil war. Sudan's military–Islamist state combined religious claims and coercive political restructuring. No single chronology of modernization, revival, and revolution captures these divergent outcomes.

The cases point to several interacting variables. State institutions determined whether political opponents could run for office, organize charities, publish, contest laws, or protect themselves against repression. Movement organization shaped whether activists built electoral coalitions, worked through professional institutions, or supported clandestine violence. Economic change produced opportunities and grievances, but these affected communities differently. International circumstances influenced funding, migration, diplomacy, and security fears, while regional religious traditions conditioned the legitimacy of particular leaders and legal reforms. Comparisons should not attribute events solely to the same cause because they occurred within the same decade. Similar slogans can express dissimilar political projects, and the same organization can adapt its methods over time.

The distinction between participation and violence is crucial but not sufficient. Electoral candidates may accept constitutional rules sincerely, strategically, or with internal disagreements; armed factions may pursue doctrines that cannot be explained merely as responses to blocked elections. Algeria's collapse does not establish that any Islamist electoral victory must end in dictatorship, nor does Jordan prove that every movement becomes tolerant when it gains seats. Meaningful evaluation requires attention to how actors treated rivals, women, minorities, civilians, and institutional limits. State governments also require equivalent scrutiny: their use of religious constitutional language cannot excuse torture, disappearances, censorship, or cancellation of political competition. A good historical account separates evaluating a political doctrine from investigating the conduct of specific people and institutions.

Religion itself remained more extensive than Islamism. Scholars, ordinary worshippers, Sufi orders, charities, and families contributed to social change without seeking a religious party-state. The state's own mosques, schools, and courts brought religious law and public authority together in different ways. Claims of a simple confrontation between a secular state and religious society therefore obscure much of the evidence. Arab societies contained believers of different traditions, people with secular political commitments, and communities that negotiated religious identity without affiliation to organized movements. These citizens were not passive spectators of elite conflict; their actions supported, constrained, transformed, or resisted the organizations claiming to represent them.

Regional connections were significant but did not eliminate national difference. Iranian revolutionary discourse, Saudi religious patronage, Gulf labor migration, the Afghan conflict, and transnational publications all crossed borders. Their impact depended on the institutions that received them and the political struggles in which they were used. A student examining a movement's foreign links should distinguish ideas from finance, finance from operational command, and general influence from responsibility for specific actions. These distinctions are especially important where state propaganda and armed-group self-presentation exaggerate transnational connections for political advantage. Textual evidence, local history, and cross-national comparison together provide a firmer basis than sweeping theories of a single Islamic wave.

This chapter has also shown the limits of the available record. Official elections held under authoritarian conditions, unverified casualty counts, survivor testimony constrained by fear, and partisan claims about motive require careful comparison. A constitutional declaration identifies what an authority announced, not necessarily what it enforced. An organizational charter reveals a movement's stated ideology at a moment, not the beliefs of every constituent. Human-rights reports may document abuse while acknowledging limits on access. Those cautions do not mean historians cannot reach strong conclusions about established events and documented atrocities; they mean conclusions should fit the quality and scope of evidence. The chapter closes around 2000 not because the questions ended then, but because later episodes require their own explanatory chapters.

The central historical result is a transformation of political competition, not an inevitable religious destiny. Arab states and social movements contested who could define legitimate law, national belonging, welfare, and public morality. Sometimes the disputes entered legislatures; sometimes they were contained through coercion; sometimes political actors adopted armed methods with disastrous civilian consequences. The patterns prepare readers for Lebanon's conflicts and intervention, the Gulf wars, the post-Cold War peace processes, and the uprisings after 2010. The future of the Arab world was not already written in 1979: it remained open to institutional choice, social organization, and the struggles of different communities to secure representation and safety.

Chapter Summary

Political Islam after 1979 grew through older religious organizations interacting with new social and political conditions. The regional turning point did not create Arab Islamic activism from nothing; it changed the political horizons in which these movements acted. National political history cannot be told as a replacement of secular society by religious society: families, officials, and institutions had long combined religious and public practices. The new disputes concerned organization, legitimacy, and the legal powers of rulers.

The Muslim Brotherhood supplied an influential organizational precedent, but its Egyptian, Syrian, Jordanian, and Palestinian experiences diverged. Distinctive internal debates over reform and militancy show that doctrinal texts do not produce automatic political outcomes. Brotherhood educational circles, professional syndicates, and welfare institutions made its influence durable even where it could not legally become a party. Its leadership was not identical with groups that later advocated assassinations or indiscriminate violence.

Islamic revival involved mosques, Sufi orders, scholars, educational groups, and charities beyond parties. Students must distinguish devotional and ethical projects from demands to control public law or replace governments. Economic reform, urbanization, and media distribution increased the reach of sermons and organizations, but they did not give all believers the same politics. This range explains why the vocabulary of religious renewal cannot establish political affiliation.

Iran’s revolution mattered across the region as an example and a perceived threat. Its Shiʿi juristic political system cannot simply be assigned to Sunni Arab movements, and local Arab histories governed the response to its example. Gulf states feared unrest and responded through their own state-security calculations, while Sunni Islamist intellectuals differed over what they admired and rejected. The close timing of Iran’s revolution and the Saudi mosque seizure should not be transformed into an invented operational connection.

Saudi Arabia combined long-standing religious claims with modern bureaucratic monarchy and dissent over reform. Egypt’s constitutional change in 1980 illustrates official Islamization, while the state’s response to political Islamists and armed militants followed different logics. The 1979 Grand Mosque emergency arose from Saudi dissent and reinforced political contests about royal and religious authority. In Egypt, constitutional principles, court interpretation, Brotherhood syndicate strategy, and armed groups operated within the same national history without forming one organization.

Syria’s Hama catastrophe revealed the human costs of authoritarian counterinsurgency and armed confrontation. Jordan and Morocco demonstrated forms of limited parliamentary accommodation, whereas Tunisian Islamists faced exclusion and repression. Each case presents a different combination of executive power and the practical possibilities for opposition. Hama requires transparent acknowledgment of mass civilian suffering and disputed death totals, while the parliamentary cases require examination of how much authority elected representatives actually possessed.

Algeria’s opening gave the FIS electoral success before the process was stopped in January 1992 and the party formally dissolved in March. Armed groups subsequently fractured, and civilians experienced atrocities and abuses from different actors. The political FIS, the AIS, and the GIA were not organizational synonyms; identifying their different objectives and conduct is necessary for attributing crimes accurately. State security conduct likewise must be assessed through evidence without assuming unverified attribution for every massacre.

Sudan’s southern war resumed before the September 1983 legal reforms. The 1989 military coup supported by Islamist organizers then produced a distinctive experiment in state Islamization through coercive executive rule. The country’s religious, linguistic, and regional diversity complicates any account of a simple Muslim north versus non-Muslim south. Military–Islamist cooperation also demonstrated that a movement and a governing security apparatus could become rivals after they had jointly displaced civilian rule.

Hamas emerged from Palestinian Brotherhood networks during the First Intifada. Its 1988 covenant offers evidence of its ideology, including antisemitic assertions that historians must reject as false and distinguish from the views of Palestinian society. The charter’s historical purpose must be weighed beside contemporary Palestinian national movements, the occupation, and the actions of civilians whose political positions differed. The document itself is a source on the organization’s ideological claims, not a substitute for a history of all Palestinians.

By 2000 transnational migration, media, charitable organizations, and some armed networks had increased political connections without erasing national difference. The central lesson is to study state institutions, movement choices, social experience, and evidence together instead of portraying all Islamic public activity as one movement. Afghanistan, the Gulf, and migration networks widened opportunities for exchange; only some developed into violent organizations. Comparison is strongest when the researcher distinguishes circulation of ideas, charitable finance, electoral bargaining, armed recruitment, and operational responsibility.

Chapter Reflection

The rise of political Islam illuminates a major problem in modern historical explanation: the same broad vocabulary can be used by organizations with radically different political methods and relationships to power. Neither shared faith nor common regional circumstances eliminated local institutions, leadership choices, and the agency of communities.

The chapter also demonstrates why legal and moral claims must be tested against practice. A constitution may promise civic equality while courts or security agencies undermine it. A movement may provide valuable social services while advancing illiberal ideas. An authoritarian state may invoke protection of pluralism while cancelling elections or repressing dissent. The historian must examine evidence of institutional behavior rather than accept any actor’s description of itself.

The most enduring legacy of the period may be the expansion of organized arguments about public legitimacy: who may interpret law, what rights political opponents possess, and how governments owe protection to those they rule. Many people encountered these disputes not in ideological treatises but at a clinic, university, border crossing, courtroom, or checkpoint. Their experiences are part of the evidence of political change.

Later chapters examine Lebanon, international wars, negotiations, and Arab uprisings. Those developments were influenced by the political and religious movements studied here, but not mechanically determined by them. The diversity of outcomes between 1979 and 2000 reminds readers that history is shaped through conflict, institutions, and decisions that remain open to scrutiny.
