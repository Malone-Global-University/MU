Part IX — Reform, Nahda, and European Expansion
50

Tanzimat and Ottoman Reform in the Arab Provinces

A History of the Arab World: From Ancient Crossroads to the Modern Arab World

Chapter Opening

Chapter 49 followed the construction of an unusually autonomous Egyptian state. This chapter follows the opposite effort: the attempt of the Ottoman government itself to rebuild its authority, province by province, through new law, new offices, new taxes, new schools, and new armies. The reforms promised security and equality, and they delivered some of both. They also delivered registers, tax assessors, and recruiting officers, and they reached Damascus, Baghdad, Jerusalem, Sana'a, and Tripoli in very different ways.

In November 1839, in a garden courtyard of the imperial palace at Istanbul, a senior minister read aloud a proclamation issued in the name of the young sultan Abdülmecid I. Ottoman dignitaries, religious leaders, and foreign diplomats were present. The text, known as the Gülhane Edict, promised the sultan's subjects security of life, honor, and property; a fair and regular system of taxation; and orderly rules for military recruitment. It was a short document, and it contained no machinery for carrying out its own promises. Historians treat it as the symbolic opening of the era called the Tanzimat, or "reorganization." Its real significance lay in what officials, over the next four decades, tried to build in its name.

Now picture how such a proclamation might have been experienced far from the capital. A cultivator in the Syrian interior would have encountered Tanzimat less as a declaration of principles than as a change in who assessed the harvest, who recorded the village's name in a register, and whether a young man would be taken for the army. A Damascus merchant might have encountered it as a new commercial court or a municipal council on which he could sit. A Druze or Maronite peasant on Mount Lebanon met it amid a violent struggle over land, taxation, and political authority. A tribal shaykh in southern Iraq encountered it as an offer of title to land and a threat to independence at the same moment. Tanzimat was not one experience; it was many.

This chapter examines the Ottoman reform program and its implementation in the Arab provinces between roughly 1839 and 1878, with earlier reforms under Sultan Mahmud II providing necessary background. It is organized in five divisions. The first treats the origins and principles of reform, including the Gülhane Edict of 1839, the Reform Edict of 1856, and the evolving idea of Ottoman citizenship. The second reconstructs the institutional machinery: provincial administration, councils, courts and codes, land law, taxation, conscription, schooling, and infrastructure. The third, which is essential to a history of the Arab world, follows those reforms into particular provinces: Syria, Mount Lebanon, Palestine, Iraq, the Hijaz and Yemen, and Ottoman Tripolitania and Cyrenaica. The fourth evaluates provincial society and the constitutional experiment of 1876, and the fifth offers a synthesis.

The chapter rejects two simple stories. The first is that Tanzimat was merely the Europeanization of a declining empire, as though Ottoman statesmen were passive imitators of foreign models. Ottoman reformers borrowed selectively, drew on earlier Ottoman practice and Islamic legal reasoning, and pursued goals that were their own: fiscal capacity, military effectiveness, and the survival of the empire. The second story is that the reforms were a liberal gift of equality. They did extend legal equality in principle, but they also deepened state surveillance, taxation, and conscription, and they gave unequal advantages to those who could use the new institutions: notables, merchants, and officials. The chapter keeps both the ambition and the coercion in view.

The evidence is varied and uneven. Ottoman archival records, including imperial decrees, provincial correspondence, and registers of land, population, and taxation, show what the government intended and how officials reported events. Official provincial yearbooks (salname) summarize administrative structure. Records of local Islamic courts preserve everyday disputes. European consular reports, missionary accounts, and travel writing describe the Arab provinces from outside, with their own interests and prejudices. Arabic chronicles, memoirs, and later local histories, such as the account of events in Syria and Lebanon by the Damascus-based Mikha'il Mishaqa, offer perspectives from within provincial society. No single body of evidence tells the whole story, and historians have repeatedly revised their picture as more Ottoman archival material has been studied alongside Arabic and European sources.

This chapter occupies a particular place in the textbook. It builds on Chapters 43 through 47, which examined Ottoman rule and provincial power before the nineteenth century, and on Chapter 49, which explained Muhammad Ali's Egypt. It prepares for Chapter 51, which turns to the Nahda, the literary and intellectual renaissance whose institutions of printing and education partly grew from the conditions described here; for Chapters 52 and 53, which treat European conquest and imperial pressure in North Africa and the Nile Valley; and for Chapter 54, which traces the competing identities and movements that took shape in the late Ottoman order. Readers should treat this chapter as the account of how the Ottoman state tried to transform itself, and why the outcome was a more powerful but still contested imperial order.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why Tanzimat grew out of earlier Ottoman reform and crisis rather than beginning abruptly in 1839.
  2. Compare the Gülhane Edict of 1839 and the Reform Edict of 1856, identifying continuities, differences, and the circumstances that shaped each.
  3. Distinguish between imperial subjecthood and modern citizenship, and trace how Ottoman legal membership developed through 1869.
  4. Describe the structure of the vilayet system and analyze how it standardized provincial administration while permitting local variation.
  5. Analyze the coexistence of Islamic, nizamiye, commercial, and communal legal institutions rather than treating reform as the replacement of one legal system by another.
  6. Assess the effects of the Land Code of 1858 and of taxation, census-taking, and conscription on cultivators, notables, and tribal communities.
  7. Contrast the implementation of reform in Syria, Mount Lebanon, Palestine, Iraq, the Hijaz, Yemen, and Tripolitania, and explain why outcomes differed.
  8. Interpret the crisis of 1860 in Mount Lebanon and Damascus as the product of social, economic, political, and international factors rather than religious hostility alone.
  9. Evaluate the experiences of different provincial groups, including notables, ulama, merchants, women, cultivators, and tribal communities, under reform.
  10. Evaluate competing historical interpretations of whether Tanzimat strengthened imperial control, expanded provincial participation, or both.
  11. Connect the constitutional experiment of 1876 to the larger reform program and to later developments examined in Chapters 51 and 54.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How did military defeat, fiscal pressure, and the Egyptian challenge shape the decision to reform, and what did Ottoman statesmen hope to preserve?
  2. What did it mean to promise legal equality to subjects who belonged to different religious communities, and why did that promise provoke both support and resistance?
  3. Why did a single legislative program, such as the Vilayet Law, produce different results in Damascus, Baghdad, and Sana'a?
  4. In what ways did land registration, taxation, and conscription increase the state's reach, and who benefited from the new rules?

  6. Why did the violence of 1860 occur, and what does its aftermath reveal about the relationship between local conflict and international diplomacy?
  7. To what extent did greater formal centralization produce effective control, especially in rural and tribal areas?
  8. Did provincial notables experience Tanzimat primarily as cooperation, as competition, or as both?

Historical Context

Chapters 43 through 47 described how the Ottoman Empire incorporated the Arab lands in the sixteenth century and how, over the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, imperial authority became negotiated and decentralized. Provincial governors, military households, urban notables (a'yan), tribal confederations, and local dynasties exercised power in the sultan's name, and the empire often worked by bargaining rather than by direct administration. Chapter 47 stressed that this arrangement should not be read as simple collapse. It was a workable system of imperial rule that depended on cooperation with local elites. By the early nineteenth century, however, the arrangement left the central government with limited revenue, limited control of military manpower, and limited ability to resist external pressure.

Three pressures converged. The first was military: defeats by Russia, the loss of territory in the Balkans, and the shock of Muhammad Ali's armies, examined in Chapter 49, made it clear that the empire needed disciplined, centrally paid troops. The second was fiscal: the empire's reliance on tax farming and on revenue shared with intermediaries meant that much of what was collected did not reach the treasury. The third was international: European powers, especially Britain, France, Russia, and Austria, competed for influence and increasingly claimed a right to speak for particular Ottoman communities. Commercial treaties, called capitulations, gave European merchants and their local protégés privileges that the Ottoman state could not easily revise. Reform was therefore both a response to these pressures and an attempt to regain the initiative against them.

It is important to distinguish this chapter's subject from neighboring chapters. Muhammad Ali's Egypt, treated in Chapter 49, appears here only as background and as a rival model; Egyptian institutions are not the subject of this chapter. The literary, journalistic, and intellectual renaissance of the nineteenth century belongs to Chapter 51, even though schooling and printing appear here where they were part of state policy. The French conquest of Algeria and the occupation of Tunisia are examined in Chapter 52, and later British, Italian, and other European interventions in Egypt, Sudan, Libya, and Morocco in Chapter 53. The later constitutionalist, Ottomanist, Arabist, and Zionist movements belong to Chapter 54. This chapter concerns the Ottoman reform program itself, and the Arab provinces where it operated.

Division I — Origins and Principles of Reform

Four sections trace the crisis that produced Tanzimat, the two founding edicts, and the early attempts to turn promises into practice.

Section 50.1

The Ottoman Crisis and the Origins of Tanzimat

Tanzimat did not begin in 1839. Ottoman rulers had experimented with military and administrative reform since the late eighteenth century, most notably under Selim III, whose "New Order" (Nizam-ı Cedid) army was destroyed by conservative opposition in 1807–1808. Mahmud II, who came to power in that turbulence, drew a lesson from the failure: reform required first breaking the institutions that could resist it. In 1808 he negotiated a charter with leading provincial magnates that recognized their power, but he spent much of the following two decades reducing it, subduing regional dynasties, and building a more reliable administrative core. In June 1826 he ordered the destruction of the Janissary corps, a once-formidable infantry that had become a hereditary, politically powerful body resistant to change. The episode, often called the Auspicious Incident, was violent, and it cleared the way for a new army trained along European lines.

Military and fiscal weakness continued. Greek independence, the Russian war of 1828–1829, and above all the campaigns of Muhammad Ali's son Ibrahim Pasha, who occupied Syria in the 1830s and defeated Ottoman forces, demonstrated how far the empire lagged behind a state that had built a disciplined, centrally financed army (see Chapter 49). Egyptian rule in Syria was itself a reform experiment, with heavier taxation, conscription, and disarmament that provoked revolts, and Ottoman statesmen watched it closely as both threat and example. When Ottoman forces were defeated again at Nezib in June 1839, and Mahmud II died days later, the empire faced a double crisis. Its new sultan was a teenager, its fleet had defected to Egypt, and its European partners would decide whether Egyptian ambitions were checked. The proclamation of reform in November 1839 should be read in this context: it was a statement of intent to European governments and to Ottoman subjects alike that the empire could govern justly and effectively.

Historians have debated how to interpret these origins. Older accounts emphasized Western influence and the Westernizing zeal of a small group of diplomats trained in European capitals. More recent scholarship stresses continuity with earlier Ottoman reform, the role of a growing professional bureaucracy that gained from regular, rule-bound government, and the importance of Islamic legal and political ideas of justice (adalet) as a framework for reform. The evidence for all of these includes Ottoman legislation, the memoirs and histories of officials such as Ahmed Cevdet Pasha, and European diplomatic correspondence. Students should note that most surviving documents record the views of officials; the opinions of provincial subjects must often be reconstructed indirectly.

Section 50.2

The Gülhane Edict of 1839

The Hatt-ı Şerif of Gülhane (the "Noble Rescript of the Rose Chamber"), proclaimed in November 1839, was drafted under the direction of the foreign minister Mustafa Reşid Pasha, a diplomat who had served in Paris and London. Its language drew on traditional Ottoman statements of justice, presenting the reform as a return to the principles of the sharia and to the empire's earlier strength. It then set out practical commitments. The state would guarantee its subjects' life, honor, and property; replace arbitrary and extortionate taxation with fair assessment based on ability to pay; regulate military recruitment so that service was orderly and of fixed duration; and ensure that no one would be punished without lawful trial. The text stated that these guarantees applied to subjects of every religion.

The edict has to be read for both what it said and what it did not. It declared principles but established no courts, no code, and no tax administration to deliver them. It did not abolish the sultan's authority; it presented the guarantees as an act of sovereign grace. Its promise to end tax farming, in which the right to collect taxes was sold to private contractors, proved especially difficult, because the state lacked the officials and the cash to replace them. And its guarantee of equality was ambiguous, since the empire's legal order had long distinguished among religious communities. Nevertheless, the proclamation was a turning point because it made justice, fiscal regularity, and the legal status of subjects matters of explicit state policy. Later reformers could and did cite it, and European governments treated it as a standard against which Ottoman conduct could be measured.

Section 50.3

Implementing Reform: The Early Tanzimat Experiments

The first practical steps came in the early 1840s. The government introduced salaried tax collectors, called muhassıl, to replace tax farmers, and it created provincial councils to supervise their work and to hear local concerns. The experiment ran into immediate difficulties. The new collectors were few, unfamiliar with local conditions, and often dependent on the very notables and former tax farmers they were meant to supplant. Revenue fell, and by the early 1840s the government had returned in practice to tax farming in many places, though with greater supervision. Similar problems affected other early measures: new offices were established faster than trained personnel could be found, and instructions issued from the capital depended on provincial officials for execution.

Military reorganization proceeded in parallel and illustrates the gap between design and execution. The regulations of 1843–1844 divided the empire into regional army commands and introduced selection by lot for fixed terms of service followed by reserve duty. The Arab provinces fell within the command known as the Fifth Army, headquartered at Damascus. A separate Sixth Army, based at Baghdad and responsible for Iraq and the Hijaz, was not established until 1848, so the Baghdad command belongs to a second stage of military organization and not to the original 1843–1844 plan; its creation shows how the geography of the Arab east led officials to revise the scheme. Each command needed barracks, supplies, and pay, and each competed with civil administration for scarce revenue.

Provincial responses varied. In some places notables adapted quickly and secured positions on the new councils, which gave them a voice in assessment and a channel to the capital. In others, resistance was open. In October 1850, riots in Aleppo, in which resentment of new taxes, anxiety about recruitment, and tension among communities over changes in status all played a part, were suppressed by Ottoman troops; in the Hauran, Druze communities resisted recruitment in the early 1850s. The government's responses combined concessions with force. The lesson the reformers drew was that centralization required patient cooperation with provincial elites, and that a proclamation of principle would not create administration by itself. The evidence for this period comes chiefly from Ottoman correspondence between governors and Istanbul and from European consular reports, both of which tend to describe disorder from the standpoint of those responsible for restoring it.

Section 50.4

The Reform Edict of 1856 and the Meaning of Ottoman Citizenship

The Crimean War (1853–1856), in which the Ottoman Empire fought alongside Britain and France against Russia, changed the reform agenda. The allies pressed for a fuller statement of the equality of Ottoman Christians, partly to forestall Russian claims to protect them, and the Reform Edict (Islahat Fermanı) of February 1856 was issued shortly before the Treaty of Paris, which admitted the Ottoman Empire to the European diplomatic system. The edict reaffirmed the guarantees of 1839 and went further: it confirmed the privileges of non-Muslim religious communities and provided for the reform of their internal governance; opened state schools, military service, and public office to subjects of all faiths in principle; and called for mixed courts and councils and for equal treatment in taxation.

Taxation and military service need to be distinguished carefully here. The poll tax (cizye), which non-Muslims had traditionally paid, was abolished beginning in 1855, shortly before the edict, and was replaced by a payment in lieu of military service (bedel-i askeri). The 1856 edict reinforced the principle of religious equality that this change implied, but it did not make military obligations equal in practice. Non-Muslim subjects were liable in principle to conscription, yet in most cases they paid the exemption instead, and the large-scale recruitment of non-Muslims into the army did not occur until the early twentieth century. The new payment therefore removed a symbolic mark of inferior status while preserving a practical distinction that many Muslim subjects resented, and that many non-Muslims accepted willingly because they feared the burdens of service.

Reaction to the edict was mixed. Many non-Muslims welcomed the principle of equality but worried about the obligations it might bring. Many Muslim subjects, including scholars and ordinary townspeople, resented what they saw as the removal of long-recognized distinctions imposed under European pressure. Some religious leaders of non-Muslim communities, whose authority over their communities depended on older communal arrangements, were uneasy about the reform of those institutions, and in the following decade several communities, including the Greek Orthodox, the Armenians, and the Jews, issued new internal regulations that gave lay members a larger role. The edict also highlighted a tension: the empire presented equality as an expression of Ottoman justice, while European powers claimed a role in monitoring it. Historians therefore read 1856 as both a genuine reform and a diplomatic compromise.

The concept of citizenship also developed. The Nationality Law of 1869 defined who was an Ottoman subject, using principles of descent and residence, and it was designed partly to regulate the status of the many people who claimed foreign protection. Students should distinguish carefully between imperial subjecthood and modern citizenship. Ottoman subjects held legal status within a multi-religious empire whose sovereign remained the sultan, and the new legislation expanded their equality before the law without creating a sovereign citizenry in the sense later understood. At the same time, new ideas of Ottoman membership began to circulate among officials and writers, some of whom spoke of a shared Ottoman identity transcending religion; these ideas are examined in Chapter 54.

The Gülhane Edict (1839) and the Reform Edict (1856) Compared

Creators: The Ottoman imperial government, in the name of Sultan Abdülmecid I · Dates: 3 November 1839 and 18 February 1856 · Type: Imperial proclamations (hatt-ı hümayun and related texts), public statements of policy addressed to subjects and to foreign governments

Context: The 1839 edict was issued during the Egyptian crisis and addressed the empire's own subjects and European observers. The 1856 edict was issued at the close of the Crimean War, at a moment when Britain and France were allies and exerted heavy pressure for a clearer statement of equality. Because both texts are public documents of state, they are evidence of what the government wished to announce, not of what actually happened in the provinces. The wording is paraphrased below rather than quoted.

1839 (paraphrase): The sultan promises his subjects security of life, honor, and property; assessment of taxes according to means rather than arbitrary demand; orderly and fixed-term military service; and lawful trial. The guarantees are presented as the restoration of justice according to the sharia, and they extend to subjects of every religion.

1856 (paraphrase): The sultan confirms those guarantees, reaffirms the spiritual privileges of non-Muslim communities and promises reform of their internal organization, opens schools and public office to all subjects, and provides for mixed courts and for the more equal treatment of subjects in matters of taxation and conscription. (The poll tax itself had been abolished and replaced by an exemption payment in 1855, shortly before the edict.)

Analysis: Read side by side, the two texts show both continuity and development. The 1839 edict speaks the language of justice and the restoration of the sharia; the 1856 edict is more specific about the position of non-Muslims and more directly shaped by diplomacy. Neither text describes mechanisms of enforcement. They show what the central government aspired to and what international audiences expected to hear. They do not show how provincial officials interpreted them, how ordinary Muslims and non-Muslims reacted, or how the provisions worked in practice; for that, historians turn to provincial court records, correspondence between governors and Istanbul, consular reports, and local Arabic chronicles.

Questions for students:

  1. What commitments does each edict make, and how do they differ?
  2. Who issued these texts, to whom were they addressed, and for what purposes?
  3. What can historians learn from the edicts about Ottoman aims and about the relationship between reform and European diplomacy?
  4. What can historians not establish from these texts alone about how reform affected a village in Syria or Iraq?
  5. Why is it useful to compare an official proclamation with provincial court records or consular reports?
Historical Vocabulary
Tanzimat

Tanzimat is the Ottoman Turkish plural of an Arabic word meaning "reorderings" or "reorganizations." It names the period of reform that began, by convention, with the Gülhane Edict of 1839 and continued through the constitutional experiment of 1876–1878. The term did not refer to a single law or program but to a continuous series of reorganizations of administration, law, finance, education, and the military.

Students should avoid equating Tanzimat with "Westernization." Ottoman reformers borrowed from European models, including the French administrative system, French codes, and European military organization, but they also drew on earlier Ottoman practice and on Islamic legal reasoning, and they adapted foreign ideas to Ottoman circumstances. Nor was Tanzimat simply "liberal." It extended equality in principle and created new councils, but it also increased the state's capacity to tax, register, and conscript. The term is best understood as a program of state-building and imperial survival, pursued by a bureaucratic elite, whose benefits and burdens were distributed unevenly.

Division II — Reconstructing the Ottoman State

Eight sections examine the institutional machinery of reform: provinces, councils, law, land, taxation, military, schools, and infrastructure.

Section 50.5

The Provincial Reorganization: The Vilayet System

The Vilayet Law of 1864, first applied in the Danube province under the governorship of Midhat Pasha and then extended to other regions, reorganized provincial government on a uniform pattern. The empire's older provinces, called eyalet, were replaced by larger vilayet, each under a governor (vali) who represented the sultan. Beneath the vilayet lay a descending hierarchy: the sanjak (liva), headed by a mutasarrıf; the kaza, headed by a kaymakam; the nahiye, headed by a müdür; and the village, with an elected headman (muhtar) and a council of elders. A revised law in 1871 added detail and adjusted the balance between governor and council. The design was modeled in part on the French departmental system, but it also formalized older Ottoman divisions and placed the governor's considerable authority within a clear chain of command to Istanbul.

The purpose was to replace the older pattern in which governors, military households, and local magnates each held overlapping claims on revenue and obedience. A governor now headed a civil hierarchy with separate officers for finance, correspondence, and the registration of land and population, and subordinate officials were paid salaries and could be transferred. In principle this reduced the opportunity for a local dynasty to entrench itself in office, as the Mamluk governors of Baghdad or the households of Damascus and Aleppo had done in earlier generations (Chapters 44 and 47). In practice, officials were frequently reassigned after short tenures, so continuity often rested with locally recruited clerks and with the notables who sat on councils. A governor who understood this could govern well; one who did not often found instructions ignored.

In the Arab provinces the new structure was introduced gradually and with local adaptation. The Aleppo vilayet was created in 1864 and a Syrian vilayet centered on Damascus followed in the mid-1860s; Baghdad was reorganized as a vilayet at the end of the decade, and Yemen and Tripoli received vilayet status later and in more qualified form. Boundaries and titles changed repeatedly. The Syrian provinces were divided and recombined, the Iraqi provinces of Baghdad, Mosul, and Basra were regrouped several times, and Mount Lebanon after 1861 stood outside the ordinary system altogether. Students should therefore treat administrative maps of the period as snapshots, not as fixed borders, and should resist reading modern state frontiers back into them.

The reform standardized the vocabulary and hierarchy of imperial authority while leaving considerable room for provincial variation in how that authority was exercised. Evidence for the structure comes from the legislation itself and from the provincial yearbooks (salname), which list officials, councils, and districts. These sources describe the formal system rather than the lived one, and historians use them alongside governors' correspondence and local court records to see where the formal hierarchy was reality and where it was fiction.

Section 50.6

Provincial Councils, Municipal Government, and Local Representation

Councils were central to the reform program. At each level of the hierarchy an administrative council combined officials with members drawn from local society, who were meant to advise governors on taxation, public works, and law. At the vilayet level the council included, as ex officio members, the provincial finance and legal officers and the heads of religious institutions, among them the mufti and the heads of the principal non-Muslim communities, together with elected members. The 1864 and 1871 legislation provided for the representation of both Muslim and non-Muslim subjects, but the franchise was narrow and elections were often managed by officials. In practice councils tended to be dominated by prominent landowners, merchants, and religious figures, whose participation gave the state legitimacy and local knowledge and gave the notables a way to defend their interests.

The councils also provided a forum in which the line between state and society blurred. Council members helped assess taxes on their districts, advised on the allocation of public works, and heard petitions, and in doing so they could steer burdens toward rivals and away from themselves. Non-Muslim communities, including Christians and Jews, were represented in these bodies in numbers and with a degree of influence that had not existed before, though often less than their share of the population or wealth in particular places. Disputes about who deserved a seat, and whether a community's representative was truly chosen by it, became a recurring feature of provincial politics.

Municipal government developed alongside the councils. Municipal councils, funded by local fees and responsible for streets, markets, sanitation, and public order, were established in major Arab cities during the 1860s and 1870s, including Jerusalem in the early 1860s, Beirut and Damascus in the years after the crisis of 1860, and Baghdad under Midhat Pasha. These institutions combined limited participation with stronger imperial supervision: councillors came from local elites, but they remained subject to the governor and to central regulation. For notable families they were an arena of competition and a source of influence; for ordinary residents they meant new taxes and new regulations as well as paving, lighting, and the clearing of markets. Historians such as Philip Khoury have argued that these institutions helped reshape the politics of notables in Syrian cities, giving established families new offices and new ways to bargain with the state.

The significance of these bodies is therefore double. They increased the state's capacity to learn about and manage localities, and they trained a generation of provincial figures in the procedures of representation and petition. Their limits were equally clear: they could not override the governor, they did not represent the poor or most rural people, and they existed to implement imperial policy. Later political movements built on this experience, a subject for Chapter 54.

Section 50.7

Law, Courts, and the Reorganization of Justice

Reform of law was among the most consequential and most misunderstood parts of Tanzimat. Before reform, justice in the Arab provinces was administered by sharia courts presided over by judges (qadi), who applied Islamic law in matters of family, property, and contract for Muslims and, in many matters, for others, alongside the communal courts of Christian and Jewish communities and the customary law of tribes. The Tanzimat added new layers rather than replacing the old. The Ottoman commercial code of 1850 and later commercial and maritime codes, based largely on French models, were applied in new commercial tribunals, in which European merchants and their local partners had a particular stake. The penal code, first issued in 1840 and revised in 1851 and 1858, drew heavily on French law.

A system of secular courts, called nizamiye courts, was gradually developed in the 1860s and 1870s, with a Ministry of Justice created in 1868 and appellate and cassation courts above the provincial tribunals. These courts handled commercial, criminal, and some civil matters and were staffed by judges and clerks who often had a mixed training. They were designed to include both Muslim and non-Muslim members, and they admitted non-Muslim testimony more readily than sharia courts had traditionally done, a feature that mattered greatly to the equality promised in 1856.

The reform of justice did not simply substitute European law for Islamic law. The Mecelle, compiled between 1869 and 1876 by a commission headed by Ahmed Cevdet Pasha, presented the rules of the Hanafi school on contracts, property, and civil procedure in the form of numbered articles, running to more than eighteen hundred. It was the first modern codification of Islamic civil law and was applied in the nizamiye courts. It represented an attempt to give Islamic law a modern, accessible form rather than to discard it. Family law, including marriage, divorce, and inheritance, remained with the sharia courts.

The result was a plural system in which sharia courts, nizamiye courts, commercial courts, consular and mixed arrangements for foreigners, and communal courts coexisted, sometimes cooperating and sometimes competing. Litigants chose among them strategically: a debtor might seek a sharia court's hearing to avoid a commercial judgment, while a merchant might prefer the codified procedure of a commercial tribunal. Historians who study court records have found that people used the new institutions as well as the old. The consequence was greater uniformity in some fields and confusion over jurisdiction in others, and the role of religious scholars in that conflict connects to Chapters 28 and 46.

Section 50.8

The Land Code of 1858 and Rural Property

The Ottoman Land Code of 1858 was one of the most important pieces of legislation of the century for rural society. It classified land into categories, including privately owned land (mülk), state land (miri) in which cultivators held rights of use, land endowed for religious or charitable purposes (waqf), abandoned or public land, and unclaimed waste. For miri land the code regularized the right of possession, allowed inheritance and, with permission, transfer, and required registration. Cultivators were to receive title deeds (tapu), and registration would, in principle, clarify who owed taxes on which land and give cultivators security of tenure. The state's aims were fiscal and administrative: land that was registered could be taxed, and cultivation could be encouraged.

In practice, registration produced mixed results that differed by region. In much of Syria and Palestine, village land was held communally and periodically redistributed among households, a system known as musha'a, which did not fit the code's emphasis on individual title. Peasants often avoided registration, which they associated with higher taxes and conscription, and sometimes allowed village headmen, notables, or urban merchants to register land in their own names. Over time this enabled elite families to accumulate large estates, sometimes at the expense of cultivators who became tenants or sharecroppers on land they had previously worked as holders. The Hauran, where wheat grew for export through Beirut, and the Palestinian plains offered examples of this pattern. In 1872 the state sold large tracts of its own land in the Marj Ibn Amir (Jezreel) valley, which were bought by Beirut merchant families, an episode that shows how registration and sale could move land from the hands of cultivators into those of absentee owners.

A separate law, issued in 1867, granted foreigners the right to own property in the Ottoman Empire, with the exception of the Hijaz, where the presence of the holy cities led to an explicit exclusion. The law did not take effect automatically. Foreign governments had to accede to a protocol accepting that such property would be subject to Ottoman law and to ordinary Ottoman taxation, and powers acceded at different times, some in the late 1860s and others later. The measure was intended to encourage investment and to reduce disputes over foreign claims, but it also opened the way for foreign purchase of land and for the growth of European property holdings around Jerusalem, Beirut, and the coast.

In Iraq, where tribal authority and irrigation were more important, registration and the sale of state land in the late 1860s often benefited tribal shaykhs and urban notables (see Section 50.18). Historians therefore disagree about whether the Land Code primarily strengthened cultivators' claims or facilitated elite accumulation; the evidence suggests that it did both, depending on local power relations, and that its effects unfolded over decades. Land registers and title records are the main evidence, though they are patchy and were produced by the state for its own purposes.

Section 50.9

Taxation, Census-Taking, and the Fiscal State

Fiscal capacity was the engine of reform. The state needed revenue to pay a standing army and a growing bureaucracy, and it had to deal with the fact that much tax revenue was collected by intermediaries. The principal rural levy was the tithe (öşür) on agricultural produce, supplemented by taxes on livestock and on property; in towns, levies on trade, property, and income were added. After the early muhassıl experiment, the government tried again to regularize assessment, to record property and population, and to replace shared or negotiated payments with fixed obligations. Tax farming survived, in modified forms, throughout the period, and governors continued to auction the right to collect the tithe in many districts, because it guaranteed the treasury a sum in advance.

The differences between legislation and implementation were visible in the provinces. In Syria, village tax assessment depended heavily on the cooperation of headmen and council members, who knew the harvests, and who could also shield friends and kin. In Iraq, the state's reach beyond the river towns was so limited that revenue often came from negotiated payments with tribal chiefs. In Mount Lebanon after 1861 the mutasarrifate paid a fixed contribution in place of the ordinary system. Because the same law produced different arrangements, the Ottoman treasury could not simply add up what the empire owed it.

Registration of the population was part of the same effort. Ottoman officials conducted censuses in the 1830s and 1840s and later pursued more systematic registration of households, property, and military-age males, relying on village headmen and neighborhood officials. For historians these records are valuable but incomplete; they were designed to count taxpayers and potential recruits, and they often undercounted women and mobile populations. For subjects, being registered meant being identifiable and therefore liable to taxation and conscription, which gave many a reason to evade or to bargain. The expansion of administrative surveillance was uneven. It was most effective in towns and settled agricultural districts and least effective among pastoral populations and in remote regions.

Meanwhile the government's finances remained strained. The Crimean War led to the first large foreign loans in 1854, and borrowing increased through the 1860s, with the Imperial Ottoman Bank (1863) serving as a channel between Ottoman officials and European capital. By 1875 the empire could not meet its obligations, a development that tied Ottoman finances to European creditors and intensified foreign influence. The fiscal history of reform thus ended in a paradox: the state had become better able to tax its subjects, and yet it was increasingly in debt to outsiders.

Section 50.10

Military Reform, Conscription, and Provincial Resistance

The new Ottoman army that arose after 1826 was meant to be a regular, centrally paid force recruited through a fair system. The conscription regulations of 1843–1844 divided the empire into regional army commands, including the Fifth Army for Syria, headquartered at Damascus, and established selection by lot, fixed periods of active service followed by reserve duty, and a system of reserves. A Sixth Army, based at Baghdad and responsible for Iraq and the Hijaz, followed in 1848, extending the structure to the eastern provinces. Later legislation, especially in 1869, reorganized service into active, reserve, and second-line (redif) obligations and strengthened the provincial structure of recruitment. The Ottoman program differed from Muhammad Ali's Egyptian army in important ways: it was built on a multi-ethnic empire's administrative structure rather than on the forced mobilization of a single agrarian population, it relied more on lottery and exemption, and it was more bound by the need to negotiate with local elites. Muhammad Ali's methods are the subject of Chapter 49.

Conscription was among the most resented aspects of reform. For a peasant household, losing a young man for years meant losing labor and income. Exemptions were available for a payment, and non-Muslims were generally excused on payment of the exemption tax that had replaced the poll tax, which created a perceived inequality that fed resentment among Muslims and kept the principle of equal service from being realized in practice. Mount Lebanon, under its special regime after 1861, was exempt from ordinary conscription, a privilege that neighboring districts noticed.

Responses varied. Villagers hid young men, bribed officials, or fled; tribes sometimes refused to supply recruits and negotiated alternative arrangements; local elites might use their influence to secure exemptions for their dependents or to supply substitutes. In some places, such as the Hauran in the early 1850s and the marshlands of southern Iraq, resistance led to armed confrontation. Recruitment also took Syrian and Iraqi soldiers to distant fronts, as in the Balkan war of 1877–1878, and the families who sent them rarely saw the benefits of the empire's defense. In Yemen, where garrisons were stationed far from home and supplied with difficulty, service became associated with disease and hardship.

The army became more effective over time, and it was a tool of state authority in the provinces, used to collect taxes, suppress revolts, and garrison towns. But its recruitment strained the relationship between the state and many provincial communities. Evidence includes military regulations, provincial correspondence about recruitment, and local accounts, which together show that conscription was a negotiation between officials and communities and not merely an imposition.

Section 50.11

State Education and the Training of Officials

Reformers needed officials who could administer the new institutions. In the 1840s and 1850s the government created civil and military schools, teacher-training institutions, and, in 1859, a school for civil officials (Mülkiye). The Public Education Regulation of 1869 laid out a structure of primary, middle (rüşdiye), preparatory (idadi), and higher schools, and it provided that state schools should be established across the provinces according to the size of towns and districts. It also asked for oversight of the many schools run by religious communities and foreign missions. In the Arab provinces, however, the state school network grew slowly and unevenly. Towns and provincial capitals received schools earlier than villages; Muslim boys were the principal beneficiaries; and girls' education, though it developed, remained limited.

Provincial examples show both ambition and limits. In Baghdad, Midhat Pasha founded or expanded state schools, including one for crafts and trades, and an official newspaper to publicize the government's program (Section 50.17). In Damascus and Aleppo, rüşdiye schools were opened for Muslim boys in the 1860s and 1870s, and graduates sought positions in the new councils and courts. In Yemen and the Hijaz, state schooling remained thin and was tied to garrison towns. The staffing of provincial schools depended on teachers trained in Istanbul, many of whom were unwilling to serve in remote places.

State schools coexisted with others. Traditional Quranic and religious schools persisted; Christian and Jewish communities maintained their own institutions; and foreign missionary schools, particularly American Protestant and French Catholic, were influential, especially in Syria and Lebanon, where they educated many Arabic-speaking Christians and some Muslims. The Syrian Protestant College, founded in Beirut in 1866, and the Jesuit Université Saint-Joseph, founded in 1875, grew from this milieu. The result was a plural educational landscape in which students' opportunities depended heavily on religion, wealth, and place of residence. The expansion of schooling also contributed to the growth of a literate, politically aware public, a development that belongs more fully to the Nahda, the subject of Chapter 51; here the important point is that the state was trying to train a new class of officials and that its efforts reached some provinces and groups far more than others.

Section 50.12

Infrastructure, Communications, and Economic Integration

Reform required that officials, soldiers, and taxes move more quickly. Telegraph lines, first introduced in the Ottoman Empire during the Crimean War, reached Syria and Iraq in the following decade and allowed governors and the capital to communicate within hours rather than weeks. A carriage road from Beirut to Damascus, built by a French company and opened in 1863, shortened the journey between the coast and the interior, and a carriage road from Jaffa to Jerusalem followed in the late 1860s. Steamships connected Mediterranean and Red Sea ports with Europe, and in Iraq steamers on the Tigris, introduced by British commercial enterprises and then by the Ottoman government, reshaped river commerce. The opening of the Suez Canal in 1869 altered global trade routes and increased the strategic and commercial importance of the Red Sea and the Arab provinces.

These changes served imperial goals: military mobility, revenue collection, and administrative control. A governor who could telegraph the capital and a battalion that could be moved by steamer were a different kind of state from one that relied on months-long correspondence. They also facilitated commercial integration. European merchants and capital, aided by the capitulations, expanded their role in the export of agricultural goods and the import of manufactures, and port cities such as Beirut grew rapidly as links between the interior and the world economy. Hauran grain, Mount Lebanon silk, and Iraqi dates and wool were among the exports that tied provincial producers to distant markets.

The benefits were unevenly shared. Local merchants, particularly some Christian and Jewish families with international connections, benefited, while artisans competing with imported goods often suffered; the hand-weaving of Damascus and Aleppo was a frequent example, though historians debate how severe the decline was. The Damascus governor's historic role in organizing the pilgrimage caravan declined as steamers made the sea route to the Hijaz more attractive. The state sought to use infrastructure to extend its authority, and sometimes it succeeded; but it did not control the larger commercial forces that infrastructure brought. Railways in the Arab provinces came mostly after the period of this chapter, with the lines to Damascus and Jerusalem, and later the Hijaz and Baghdad railways, belonging to the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. The European conquest of North Africa and the imperial rivalries of Egypt and Sudan, sometimes linked to this commercial expansion, are treated in Chapters 52 and 53.

Historical Vocabulary
Vilayet and A'yan (Notables)

A vilayet was the large province created by the 1864 Vilayet Law, headed by a governor (vali) appointed by the central government and subdivided into sanjaks, kazas, and nahiyes. It replaced the earlier eyalet. The word means "province" in Ottoman usage and should not be confused with modern state borders; vilayet boundaries changed frequently.

The a'yan (singular 'ayn), usually translated "notables," were prominent local figures: landowners, tax collectors, merchants, religious scholars, and heads of important families. Albert Hourani used the phrase "politics of notables" to describe how such groups mediated between rulers and the population. The notables were not a single class and not necessarily opponents of reform; many used the new councils, courts, and land law to strengthen their position. Understanding their role is essential to explaining why reform worked where it did and failed where it did.

Historical Vocabulary
Miri Land and Tapu

Miri land was land whose ultimate ownership was held by the state, while cultivators held rights of possession and use that they could pass on under rules. Tapu was the title deed issued to a registered holder under the 1858 Land Code. The terms do not translate neatly into modern concepts of private property. A tapu holder possessed rights of use and inheritance but remained subject to the state's rules and obligations, and the right could be lost if land was left uncultivated.

The significance of tapu lay in what it did socially. Registration could secure a peasant's claim, but it could also make land a commodity, facilitate its sale to those with capital, and give the state a record of who owed taxes. Misleading modern equivalents include "private ownership" and "land reform"; the Ottoman code was neither simply a privatization nor a redistribution.

Section 50.13

Syria after Egyptian Withdrawal: Damascus, Aleppo, and Ottoman Reconstruction

Ottoman authority in Syria was restored after the allied intervention of 1840, when British, Austrian, and Ottoman forces, acting under a diplomatic settlement arranged in London, bombarded Mediterranean ports and pressed Ibrahim Pasha's withdrawal in 1841. The returning administration faced a population that had experienced a decade of Egyptian taxation, disarmament, and conscription, which it had both resented and, in some cases, benefited from, through new security and new commercial openings. The government's task was to rebuild its authority without provoking renewed revolt. It reappointed governors in Damascus, Aleppo, and Sidon (whose seat shifted toward Beirut), reintroduced provincial councils, and tried, with limited success, to replace tax farming with salaried collection. The Damascus governor held a role of special religious and political importance as commander of the annual pilgrimage caravan to Mecca (see Chapter 45).

Provincial society was shaped by established notable families. In Damascus, families with religious, landowning, and military-administrative standing, such as the al-'Azm and al-Quwatli, dominated councils and local offices; in Aleppo, a similar elite, including leading descendants of the Prophet (ashraf), mediated between state and population. Governors depended on them for information, for the assessment of taxes, and for the maintenance of order, and in return the notables expected access to offices and to protection of their property. Studies by scholars such as Moshe Ma'oz, Albert Hourani, and Philip Khoury have shown that the councils of the 1840s and 1850s gave these families an institutional setting for the politics of notables that they had previously conducted through personal networks.

Economic change reshaped the cities and their hinterlands. Beirut grew from a modest port into a commercial center that rivaled the older inland cities, as steamers, European trading houses, and the export of silk and grain connected Syria to Europe. The Hauran plain, cultivated increasingly for wheat sold through Beirut, drew peasants, Druze and Bedouin communities, and Damascene merchants into new relationships. Damascus's own commerce and its craft sector faced competition from imported textiles, and the decline of overland caravan routes affected some trading families, while others, including Christian and Jewish merchants with European connections, prospered. These shifts altered the balance among communities in ways that fed the tensions discussed in Section 50.14.

Reform also met open resistance. Aleppo's riots of 1850 showed how new taxes, rumors of conscription, and anxiety over the changing legal position of Christians could combine. In the Hauran, Druze communities fought the imposition of recruitment in the early 1850s, and the government responded with expeditions that restored an uneasy order. After the 1864 reorganization the Aleppo vilayet and the Syrian vilayet centered at Damascus became arenas of administrative reform: governors used councils and municipal institutions to integrate local elites into the state, to repair roads and markets, and to register land and people. Midhat Pasha's later governorship of Syria (1878–1880), after the period of this chapter, extended some of these methods.

The reconstruction of Ottoman Syria shows the central pattern of the chapter in miniature. A central government that wished to be more present in the provinces could succeed only by working through local powers, and those powers, in turn, adapted the new institutions to their own purposes. The result was a state that was visibly stronger in 1870 than in 1840, and yet one whose authority rested on the continuing cooperation of those it governed.

Section 50.14

Mount Lebanon, Communal Conflict, and the Crisis of 1860

Mount Lebanon had been governed since the late eighteenth century by Emirs of the Shihab family, whose authority rested on a hierarchy of landholding lords (muqata'ji), mostly Druze and Maronite, who collected taxes from peasants and passed a fixed sum to the Ottoman governor. Bashir II's rule ended in 1840 with his exile after the Egyptian withdrawal, and the Ottoman government, seeking to end the emirate, experimented with direct rule, which failed. In 1842 the mountain was divided into two districts under deputy governors (qa'immaqam): a northern district with a Maronite Christian majority under a Maronite deputy, and a southern district with a mixed Druze and Maronite population under a Druze deputy. The arrangement, encouraged by European diplomacy, was meant to stabilize the region, but in practice it politicized communal identity and left the southern district, where Druze lords ruled Maronite and other peasants, unstable. Clashes occurred in 1841 and 1845, and an Ottoman attempt in 1845 to regulate the system did not end the instability.

The social basis of the conflict was changing. In the northern district, a peasant movement against the lords' exactions, led in 1858 by Tanyus Shahin in Kisrawan, challenged the old hierarchy and encouraged peasants elsewhere. The growth of silk production for European markets enriched some merchants and clergy, increased the cash obligations of cultivators, and altered relationships between communities. Maronite clergy, strongly influential in northern society, sought a greater political voice; Druze lords feared the loss of their position; and European powers backed different communities: France championed the Maronites, Britain was closer to the Druze, and Russia and Austria had their own interests. Ottoman officials, for their part, were accused by contemporaries of manipulating rivalries or failing to restrain them.

In May and June 1860 fighting spread across the southern mountain. Villages were burned, towns such as Zahle and Deir al-Qamar suffered heavily, and Christians, who were the principal victims, fled in large numbers. Druze fighters were also killed, and communities of other faiths were drawn in. Ottoman troops in the region were accused of failing to protect Christians, and some officials were later punished for complicity or negligence, though the degree of official responsibility remains debated.

In July 1860 violence reached Damascus, where a mob attacked the Christian quarter and killed many residents over several days. Contemporary estimates of the dead, in Lebanon and in Damascus, vary widely, and the figures should be treated cautiously; the scale was large, but historians have not established a precise toll. Some Muslim notables sheltered Christian neighbors, and the Algerian exile Abd al-Qadir al-Jaza'iri, then living in Damascus, and his followers protected many. Explanations for the violence include resentment of the legal equality promised in 1856, economic grievances connected to the rise of Christian merchants and European trade, the presence of unemployed and disaffected men, and the effects of the Lebanese conflict.

Historians such as Leila Fawaz and Ussama Makdisi have emphasized that the violence cannot be explained by religious hostility alone. It grew from specific social, political, and economic circumstances, and the very idea of a sectarian conflict was shaped by the way participants, officials, and Europeans interpreted events. Primary evidence includes Ottoman investigation reports, European consular dispatches, and eyewitness accounts in Arabic, such as the memoir of Mikha'il Mishaqa. Each of these has a perspective: Ottoman officials wrote to justify their conduct, consuls often championed particular communities, and Arabic memoirists wrote from within the communities they described. A careful historian reads them against each other.

Section 50.15

The Mount Lebanon Mutasarrifate and the Settlement of 1861

The Ottoman response was swift. The government sent Fuad Pasha, one of its leading statesmen, as an extraordinary commissioner to Syria with broad powers. He punished officials and participants in the Damascus violence, executing and exiling a number of them, including the governor, Ahmed Pasha, and he sought to restore order. France, with the consent of the other powers, landed an expeditionary force in Beirut in August 1860, and an international commission of the European powers and the Ottoman Empire met to examine the causes and design a settlement. The episode illustrates a distinct method of Ottoman reform, one shaped by negotiation with the European powers and by the need to demonstrate that the empire could protect its Christian subjects without outside intervention.

The result was the Organic Statute (Règlement organique) of 1861. Mount Lebanon became a special district (mutasarrifate) governed by a Christian Ottoman subject, who was not from the mountain itself and was appointed by the Ottoman government with the agreement of the powers; the first, Davud Pasha, was an Armenian Catholic. The statute proclaimed equality before the law and abolished the old feudal privileges of the muqata'ji. Mount Lebanon was exempted from ordinary conscription, paid a fixed contribution, and had its own local police.

The administrative council that advised the governor changed its composition in a way that reveals the politics of representation. Under the statute of 1861 the council had twelve members, two from each of six communities: Maronites, Druze, Greek Orthodox, Greek Catholics, Sunni Muslims, and Shi'a Muslims. The Maronites, who formed the largest community in the mountain, argued that equal seats understated their numbers and importance, while the Druze, whose leaders had lost power and land, resisted any reduction in their share. The revised statute of 1864, negotiated among the powers and the Ottoman government, kept twelve seats but redistributed them: four Maronites, three Druze, two Greek Orthodox, and one each for Greek Catholics, Sunnis, and Shi'a. The revision reflected contested political representation, with the Maronite plurality acknowledged and the Druze retaining a larger voice than their numbers alone might have given them. The new arrangement also extended the governor's term, which gave the mutasarrifate more stability.

The settlement was not accepted by all. Yusuf Karam, a Maronite notable who had hoped for a leading role in the mountain's government, rebelled against Davud Pasha in the mid-1860s, and was defeated and sent into exile in 1867. Davud was succeeded in 1868 by another Catholic governor. For several decades the mutasarrifate enjoyed relative stability and economic growth, though the arrangement also entrenched communal representation as a principle of government and left the mountain dependent on Ottoman and European diplomacy, so its autonomy had real limits. The development of Lebanese confessional politics under the later French mandate is examined in Chapter 58.

Section 50.16

Palestine, Jerusalem, and the Reorganization of Southern Syria

In the mid-nineteenth century the territory later called Palestine was not a single administrative unit. Its districts, including Jerusalem, Jaffa, Gaza, Nablus, and Acre, were organized in different sanjaks attached to the provinces of Syria and Sidon, and their governance involved local notable families, such as the Husayni and Khalidi families of Jerusalem and the Nimr and Tuqan families of Nablus, who held offices and exercised considerable influence. The Egyptian occupation of the 1830s had provoked a major revolt in 1834, and the Ottoman restoration after 1840 proceeded cautiously. Reform measures arrived piecemeal: councils, tax reorganization, and conscription, which was often resisted in the hill districts, where villages relied on the hills for security and on musha'a land for subsistence.

Jerusalem's position was unique. As a city holy to Muslims, Christians, and Jews, it attracted pilgrims and European attention. European consulates opened in the 1830s and 1840s; the Latin Patriarchate was restored in 1847; and Russia, Britain, France, and others competed to protect their coreligionists and to build churches, hospices, and schools, a rivalry that fed into the disputes over the holy places that preceded the Crimean War. Ottoman officials sought to preserve the sultan's sovereignty while accommodating the foreign presence: they negotiated with consuls, issued building permits, and invested in roads, including the Jaffa–Jerusalem carriage road, and in telegraph connections in the 1860s.

The administrative status of Jerusalem changed in the early 1870s, and the changes need to be kept distinct. In May 1872 the government established a Jerusalem vilayet, giving the city and its district the rank of a province. The experiment lasted only until July 1872, when the vilayet was discontinued. Jerusalem then became an independent sanjak, a mutasarrifate connected directly to Istanbul instead of to a provincial capital, and this arrangement was established and consolidated by 1874. The special status reflected the city's religious importance and the government's wish to handle European interests directly. A municipal council had been established in the early 1860s, and it assumed responsibility for markets, sanitation, and streets, with members drawn from notable families and religious communities.

Land registration affected the districts differently. Under the 1858 code and the property law of 1867, notable families, urban merchants, and some foreign buyers acquired property around Jerusalem and Jaffa, while in the countryside villagers often avoided registration or entrusted it to headmen. The sale in 1872 of state lands in the Marj Ibn Amir to Beirut merchants showed how the state's own revenue needs shaped landholding. Nablus and its district, with their tradition of resistance, retained strong local families; Acre and Gaza had their own administrations. Ottoman officials responded to European involvement by regulating foreign property and by trying to keep the holy places under imperial control.

The later political movements that transformed Palestine, including Arab nationalism and Zionism, belong to Chapter 54 and are not narrated here. The point for this chapter is that Palestine in this period was an integral part of Ottoman provincial administration, shaped by the same reform legislation as Syria and Iraq, with its own mixture of notable politics, religious institutions, and foreign intervention.

Section 50.17

Iraq and the Reforming Governorship of Midhat Pasha

Iraq had been ruled since the eighteenth century by a succession of Georgian-origin Mamluk governors based in Baghdad, who maintained a semi-autonomous regime. Mahmud II's government ended it in 1831, installing a governor loyal to Istanbul, in the same year that a devastating plague and flood struck Baghdad and sharply reduced its population. For several decades thereafter Ottoman authority was weak beyond the cities. Governors in the 1840s and 1850s, such as Necib Pasha, who attacked the Shi'i shrine city of Karbala in 1843, used force to assert control; the establishment of the Sixth Army at Baghdad in 1848 gave them a regular military instrument. Tanzimat measures arrived slowly, and the provincial structure of Baghdad, Basra, and Mosul was reorganized several times.

A turning point came with Midhat Pasha's governorship of Baghdad from 1869 to 1872. Midhat, who had applied the Vilayet Law in the Danube province, brought an ambitious program. He established municipal government, expanded schooling, including a school for crafts and trades, and founded an official newspaper, al-Zawra', one of the first printed periodicals in Iraq. He promoted steam navigation on the Tigris, where British commercial steamers already competed with Ottoman vessels, and public works, including a horse-drawn tramway in Baghdad.

Land and administration were central. Midhat's government promoted the registration of land and the sale of state land to individual buyers, a policy that the Land Code had made possible and that served the dual aim of raising revenue and creating a class of landholders with an interest in order (Section 50.18). He strengthened the Ottoman military presence, including an expedition in 1871 that brought al-Hasa on the Gulf coast under Ottoman authority, and he sought to settle tribes and to extend the reach of courts and tax officials. The opening of the Suez Canal in 1869 increased the province's connection to global commerce and strengthened the empire's interest in the Gulf.

Baghdad's society framed these changes. The city's population included Sunni and Shi'i Muslims, a large and commercially important Jewish community, Christians, and Persian and Indian merchants, and its trade connected Iran, India, and the Gulf with Syria and Europe. The new institutions drew on this diversity: municipal and administrative councils included members of several communities, and the official yearbooks recorded their composition. Basra, the port, was linked by river steamers and by the Gulf trade, and Mosul, in the north, looked toward Aleppo and Anatolia.

Midhat's tenure was short, as he was recalled in 1872, but it exemplified how an energetic governor could use the new framework to extend state power, and it left institutions that outlasted him. Evidence comes from the official yearbooks of the province, European consular reports, and later Arab and Ottoman memoirs. Each emphasizes different aspects: the yearbooks the structures, the consuls the commerce and the dangers to it, and the memoirs the personalities. Historians read the three together to separate what Midhat built from what his admirers and critics said about him.

Section 50.18

Tribes, Land, and Imperial Authority in Ottoman Iraq

Rural Iraq was dominated by large tribal confederations, such as the Muntafiq in the south and the Shammar in the north, whose chiefs controlled irrigated land, grazing, and the river routes. Midhat Pasha's government attempted to extend control by settling tribes, registering land, and creating new administrative centers; the town of Nasiriyya, founded in 1870 on the Euphrates, was part of this effort, and it was associated with the leading family of the Muntafiq, the Sa'dun, whose head was given an official post. The strategy was typically Ottoman: to turn a rival power into an agent of the state by offering it recognition, office, and legal rights over land.

The land measures of the late 1860s allowed the sale of state land to individual buyers and the registration of title, and in southern Iraq many shaykhs, as well as urban notables and officials, acquired large estates, in some cases securing title to land that tribal members had cultivated. Over time, historians have argued, this transformed the relationship between tribal chiefs and their followers, turning some shaykhs into landlords and some cultivators into tenants. Later in the century, the sultan's private estates (sanniye) enlarged state landholding in Iraq, a development beyond this chapter's period. Studies by Hala Fattah and others emphasize that the economic ties of Iraq to Gulf and Indian trade shaped how tribal groups used land and water.

Greater formal centralization did not produce effective control of the countryside. Tribal chiefs retained armed followers, resisted taxation and conscription, and often played the government against rival tribes. Irrigation, dependent on the Tigris and Euphrates and on channels that required continuous labor, was in poor repair in many districts, and the flooding and shifting of the rivers constrained settlement. The marshes of the south sheltered communities that the state reached with difficulty. In practice Ottoman authority depended on bargaining with chiefs, whose titles and offices the state recognized in exchange for revenue and recruits.

The Shi'i holy cities of Najaf and Karbala, home to important religious scholars, had their own relationship with the Sunni Ottoman state and with tribal populations. Some scholars have argued that nineteenth-century settlement and the spread of Shi'i institutions led many tribal groups in the south to adopt Shi'ism, a development that complicated the state's relationship with them; the interpretation is debated, and the chronology is not firmly established. The topic connects to the later land politics of mandate Iraq, examined in Chapter 57.

Section 50.19

The Hijaz, Yemen, and the Limits of Ottoman Centralization

In the Hijaz, Ottoman authority was exercised through a distinctive arrangement. After the Egyptian withdrawal in the early 1840s, the Ottoman government restored its authority over Mecca, Medina, and Jeddah, with the Sharif of Mecca, a member of a prominent family of descendants of the Prophet, serving as the local ruler alongside an Ottoman governor. The Hijaz's religious importance, the annual pilgrimage, and the sultan's claim to protect the holy cities gave the province special status and special obligations: the government subsidized the cities with grain and funds, and it also excluded the Hijaz from the foreign-property law of 1867. The earlier history of the Saudi state in Najd is treated in Chapter 48 and is not repeated here.

The pilgrimage was transformed by steam navigation and, after 1869, by the Suez Canal, which increased the number of pilgrims and brought epidemic disease, especially cholera, leading to international sanitary conferences in the 1860s. Jeddah was the scene of anti-Christian violence in 1858, followed by British naval bombardment. The Ottoman state sought to strengthen its control through telegraph, garrisons, and administrative reorganization, but it never fully displaced the Sharif's authority. In the Hijaz the limits of centralization were set by the sanctity of the place and by the need to avoid offending Muslim opinion.

Yemen presented a contrast, and its history needs to be distinguished from the Hijaz's. The Ottomans had occupied parts of Yemen in the sixteenth century, but that first occupation ended in the seventeenth century, when local Zaydi imams expelled Ottoman forces, and for roughly two centuries thereafter the highlands were governed by imams and tribal leaders while the coast was divided among local rulers. The nineteenth-century return began on the Red Sea coast, the Tihama, where Ottoman forces occupied Hodeidah in 1849, in the context of the Egyptian withdrawal, British competition from Aden (occupied in 1839), and the growing importance of the Red Sea route. In 1872, following the opening of the Suez Canal, Ottoman forces entered Sana'a, establishing a renewed provincial administration in the Yemen vilayet.

The highlands were home to Zaydi Shi'i religious leaders, tribal confederations, and local rulers whose relationship with the state was fraught. The Ottoman presence provoked repeated revolts, and the province was costly to garrison. Ottoman governors imposed taxation and courts and attempted to introduce schools, but their reach in rural areas was limited. Garrison troops, many from other provinces, suffered from disease and from isolation, and officials found it difficult to separate what was the sultan's authority from what was a negotiated arrangement with local powers.

The contrast shows why Ottoman administration worked differently in the two provinces. In the Hijaz, an indigenous elite with sacred standing was integrated into the imperial order, and the state's presence was shaped by the religious significance of the cities. In Yemen, the Ottomans were returning after a long absence to a society that had its own religious and tribal institutions, and their presence was seen by many as foreign. Both cases demonstrate how far the limits of centralization were set by geography, tribal power, and religious authority.

Section 50.20

Tripolitania, Cyrenaica, and Reform on the Western Frontier

Tripolitania had been ruled since 1711 by the Karamanli dynasty, a locally entrenched family that governed in the sultan's name with extensive autonomy. In the 1830s a succession dispute and fiscal crisis weakened the dynasty, and in 1835 an Ottoman fleet and troops landed at Tripoli, deposed the last Karamanli ruler, and restored direct Ottoman rule. This was a direct application of Mahmud II's policy of ending regional autonomies. Resistance continued for years, notably the rebellion led by Ghuma al-Mahmudi, who held out in the interior and was defeated in the 1850s. Ottoman authority was extended into Fezzan in the early 1840s, which gave the state a hold on the southern end of the trans-Saharan routes.

The province, later organized as the Tripoli vilayet, with Cyrenaica attached as a separate district centered on Benghazi, became a western frontier of the empire, with the Mediterranean on one side and the Sahara on the other. Ottoman reform here differed from Syria and Iraq. There were few large cities beyond Tripoli and Benghazi; the economy rested on Mediterranean ports, agriculture in coastal districts, and trans-Saharan trade through oasis towns such as Ghadames and Murzuq. Tribal populations were large and mobile, and the state's resources were limited. Governors built customs administrations, councils, and garrisons, but they relied heavily on tribal leaders to maintain order and collect taxes.

Trade in slaves was a significant part of the Saharan economy. In 1857 the Ottoman government issued a prohibition of the slave trade in Black Africans. The prohibition's enforcement in Tripolitania was limited: traders adapted by changing routes and by relying on officials who could be influenced, and the trade continued in various forms for decades. It is important to distinguish the prohibition of the trade from the abolition of slavery itself. The Ottoman decree did not abolish slavery; people already enslaved remained so, and the status continued to be recognized in law and custom. The decree reflects the pressure of European antislavery diplomacy and the state's own interest in regulating commerce, and it shows again the gap between Istanbul's legislation and provincial practice.

In Cyrenaica, the Sanusi order, a Sufi movement founded by Muhammad ibn Ali al-Sanusi, who had left Algeria and established his first lodge near Mecca in the 1830s, built a network of lodges (zawiya) from 1843, beginning near al-Bayda. The lodges provided religious instruction, mediation among tribes, and support for trade and travel, and the order later moved its center deeper into the desert, to Jaghbub in 1856. Ottoman authorities generally cooperated with the order, which gave them influence in the interior without large garrisons; the order, for its part, benefited from Ottoman recognition without submitting to direct administration.

The contrast with Syria and Iraq shows that Tanzimat in the Arab provinces took different forms where the state's presence was thinner. In Tripolitania and Cyrenaica, reform meant the restoration of direct rule, the introduction of councils and customs administration, and an uneasy partnership with tribes and religious orders. Later Italian expansion in Libya belongs to Chapter 53.

Division IV — Society, Political Change, and the Limits of Reform

Two sections evaluate reform from the perspective of provincial society and follow it to the constitutional experiment of 1876.

Section 50.21

Provincial Society under Tanzimat: Cooperation, Resistance, and Unequal Change

Reform affected different groups differently. Provincial notables, especially landowning and office-holding families, were often the principal beneficiaries: they sat on councils, acquired land under the new law, filled new offices, and secured places for their sons in new schools. Yet some notables lost out when the state displaced them from tax collection or limited their autonomy. In Damascus and Aleppo, families that had held tax-farming rights found their position eroded when salaried officials replaced them, while others converted old offices into landholding and new council seats. The outcome depended on a family's ability to move between the old and new institutions.

The ulama experienced both loss and adaptation. Their monopoly over law and education was challenged by secular courts and schools, but many retained influence as judges, teachers, and legal experts, and some, like Ahmed Cevdet Pasha, led the reform of law itself. Many provincial scholars were wary of the equality promised to non-Muslims and of the growing influence of European consuls, and the violence of 1860 in Damascus has been read partly as an expression of such anxieties, though historians caution against attributing it to the ulama as a body. Merchants, particularly those with links to European trade, prospered, as did the Christian and Jewish families who served as intermediaries; artisans in some cities suffered from imported goods.

For cultivators and tribal communities, the new obligations were most visible. Taxes and conscription fell heavily on villagers, and registration of land could erode their rights. Tribal groups faced pressure to settle and to pay taxes, though many found ways to negotiate. Women, who appear in court records as litigants, property owners, and endowers, experienced reform mostly through changes in the courts and, for a minority, through new schools; their legal position in family matters remained governed by the sharia courts. Non-Muslim communities gained legal equality in principle and new representation on councils, but they also faced new burdens and, at times, hostility, as the events of 1850 and 1860 showed. Jewish communities in the Arab provinces, which had experienced the Damascus blood libel affair of 1840 and an Ottoman edict rejecting such accusations later that year, gained from the legal protection but remained vulnerable to local prejudice.

Historians have emphasized that provincial subjects were not passive: they used the new institutions, petitioned the government, resisted rules they disliked, and shaped how reform worked on the ground. The sources for these experiences are uneven. Official records show what the state demanded; court records show how people litigated; consular reports show how foreign observers saw particular communities; and local chronicles reveal the voices of educated townspeople. Peasants, women, and the poor appear mainly through these mediated accounts, which is why the chapter's generalizations about them must be tentative.

Section 50.22

The Constitution of 1876 and the Unfinished Tanzimat Project

By the mid-1870s the empire faced a financial crisis, a revolt in the Balkans, and renewed European pressure. A group of statesmen, including Midhat Pasha, together with supporters among officials and intellectuals, forced the removal of Sultan Abdülaziz in May 1876, and after the brief reign of Murad V, Abdülhamid II came to the throne in August. Midhat and his allies persuaded the new sultan to promulgate a constitution, the Kanun-i Esasi, on 23 December 1876, at a moment when a European conference in Istanbul was considering reforms for the Balkans. The constitution declared the unity of the empire, the sultan's sovereignty, the equality of Ottoman subjects before the law, and a bicameral parliament: an appointed Senate and a Chamber of Deputies chosen by indirect election. It also left the sultan significant powers, including the right to banish persons deemed dangerous to the state.

The elections that produced the first parliament were conducted through a multi-stage procedure in which local electors chosen by male taxpayers selected deputies, and representation was apportioned according to population in each province. Deputies from the Arab provinces sat in the parliament that opened in March 1877, representing Syria, Aleppo, Baghdad, Basra, Tripoli, the Hijaz, and Yemen, among other provinces. Many were notables, religious figures, or officials already prominent in provincial councils, and some, such as the Jerusalem notable Yusuf Diya al-Khalidi, were figures with experience of both state office and local society. Their participation demonstrated that the provincial elites trained in the councils of the 1860s could move into a representative assembly.

The parliament's life was brief and turbulent. Deputies debated budgets, administrative abuses, and the conduct of the Balkan crisis, and some, including provincial deputies, criticized officials and demanded accountability; the sultan and his advisers were uncomfortable with the assembly's independence. Midhat Pasha was dismissed and exiled in early 1877, using the sultan's power to banish. The Russo-Ottoman war, which began in April 1877, ended in heavy defeat, and in February 1878 Abdülhamid II prorogued parliament and in practice suspended the constitution, though it was never formally abolished.

The first constitutional period was short, but it established parliamentary politics and constitutional language in Ottoman public life, to which opponents of the later autocracy returned. For the Arab provinces, it provided their first experience of sending representatives to an imperial legislature, and it showed both the promise and the limits of reform from above. The development of constitutionalism and of Arab political movements is examined in Chapter 54.

Everyday Life and Social History

Living with Reform: Registers, Recruiters, Courts, and Councils

For most people in the Arab provinces, Tanzimat arrived as a set of small but consequential changes in daily life. A household might be visited by officials compiling a population or property register; a young man might draw a lot for military service and his family might scramble to raise the exemption payment or find a substitute; a farmer might decide whether to register a plot or let a neighbor of higher standing do so. In towns, a shopkeeper might pay a new municipal fee and find streets paved and lit, while a merchant might take a dispute to a commercial court rather than to a qadi. Litigants made strategic choices among sharia, nizamiye, communal, and consular courts. Telegraph and steamship brought news and goods faster, and the sound of the new schools' bells or the Beirut road's carriages became part of urban life.

Experiences varied with gender, class, and religion. Women appear in sharia court records as property owners, litigants, and founders of endowments, and reform's effects on them were indirect, working through property law and schooling. Poor townspeople often bore the burden of new fees and military service. Because most surviving sources were produced by officials, consuls, and literate elites, historians must read them for indirect evidence about ordinary experience, supplementing them with court records, petitions, and local chronicles.

Why the pattern matters: The mixture of change and continuity explains why the reforms strengthened the state without eliminating the older networks of power on which it relied.

Division V — Historical Synthesis

Section 50.23

Ottoman Reform in the Arab Provinces: Transformation, Continuity, and Contradiction

Looking across the provinces, Tanzimat accomplished real change. It built a standardized administrative hierarchy, created new law and courts, registered land and people, extended communications, trained a new generation of officials, and gave non-Muslims and provincial notables formal places in government. In Syria it integrated urban elites into the state; in Mount Lebanon it produced a durable special regime; in Iraq it extended Ottoman authority in cities and, to a lesser degree, in the countryside; in Yemen and the Hijaz it reasserted a presence but encountered firm limits; in Tripolitania it ended dynastic autonomy and relied on cooperation with local powers such as the Sanusi. Differences among these provinces came from geography, social structure, the strength of tribal and religious authorities, the interests of European powers, and the energy of particular governors.

The reforms were also contradictory. They promised equality while keeping distinctions, notably the exemption payment for non-Muslims. They expanded participation while keeping narrow franchises and strong governors. They aimed to protect cultivators but sometimes enabled elite accumulation of land. They sought independence from Europe but depended on European loans, diplomacy, and commercial ties. And they raised the state's capacity to tax and conscript, which produced resentment alongside order. More standardized law, expanded administration, and new participation thus coexisted with strengthened imperial control, local inequality, fiscal coercion, and foreign involvement. These were not signs of failure alone; they were features of a program that aimed to preserve an empire under severe pressure.

The chapter's main lesson is that the nineteenth-century Ottoman Arab provinces were undergoing substantial institutional change well before the political movements and partitions of the twentieth century. The schools, newspapers, councils, courts, land titles, and communications of the Tanzimat shaped the societies that produced the Nahda, studied in Chapter 51, and the political identities examined in Chapter 54. They also formed the administrative inheritance that the mandate and successor states received after 1918 (Chapters 55–58). Students should therefore see Tanzimat as an essential link between the Ottoman provincial world of earlier centuries and the modern Arab world.

Historical Debate

Did Tanzimat Strengthen Imperial Control or Expand Provincial Participation?

The question: Historians disagree about the main effect of the reforms in the Arab provinces. Did they chiefly increase the central state's ability to control its subjects, or did they open new avenues for provincial participation, or both?

Interpretation A — Tanzimat as centralization: Some scholars emphasize that the reforms enlarged the state's capacity to tax, register, conscript, and police its subjects. They point to the vilayet hierarchy, land and population registers, conscription, and the expansion of the telegraph and army as evidence of a more intrusive state, and they note that councils operated under official supervision.
Interpretation B — Tanzimat as negotiated participation: Other scholars stress that provincial notables, ulama, and merchants used the new institutions to secure positions, advance interests, and bargain with the state. Seen this way, the reforms created a shared framework within which provincial elites and the government negotiated, and they provided the institutions through which later political demands were articulated.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on Ottoman legislation and archives, provincial yearbooks, court records, consular reports, and Arabic chronicles. Interpretations differ in how much weight they give to official records of state intent versus local sources showing how institutions were used, and in whether they study the capital or the provinces.

Why it matters: If centralization is emphasized, Tanzimat appears as a reconstruction of imperial power that later movements resisted. If participation is emphasized, it appears as the training ground of provincial political life. Most recent scholarship holds that both processes occurred together, which is the position adopted in this chapter.

Student task: Choose one province discussed in this chapter and evaluate which interpretation fits its evidence better. Identify what additional evidence would help.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how the Ottoman government attempted, between roughly 1839 and 1878, to rebuild a more centralized, legally standardized, militarily effective, and administratively integrated state, and how that effort played out across the Arab provinces. Its central argument is that Tanzimat was an Ottoman project of imperial survival, not merely the Westernization of a declining empire, and that it unfolded unevenly because the provinces it reached were very different.

The origins of reform lay in earlier initiatives, in the destruction of the Janissaries in 1826, and in the military and fiscal crises of the 1830s, above all the challenge posed by Muhammad Ali's Egypt. The Gülhane Edict of 1839 announced guarantees of life, honor, and property, fair taxation, and orderly conscription, but provided no machinery. Early experiments in salaried tax collection and provincial councils faltered, teaching reformers that central policy required the cooperation of local elites.

The Reform Edict of 1856, issued under the pressure of the Crimean War and European diplomacy, reaffirmed equality and clarified the position of non-Muslim communities. The Nationality Law of 1869 defined legal membership. Students were asked to distinguish imperial subjecthood from modern citizenship and to see how equality in principle coexisted with unequal obligations, particularly the exemption payment, introduced with the abolition of the poll tax in 1855, in place of military service.

The institutional core of the chapter examined the vilayet system of 1864, administrative councils and municipalities, the plural legal order of sharia, nizamiye, commercial, and communal courts, and the Mecelle. It treated the Land Code of 1858, which classified and registered land and in many places enabled notables to accumulate estates, and the expansion of taxation, census-taking, conscription, schooling, and communications. Each served the state's fiscal and military aims, and each had uneven social consequences.

The provincial sections showed how outcomes differed. In Syria, reform integrated urban notables, while commercial change and legal equality provoked conflict in Aleppo in 1850 and Damascus in 1860. On Mount Lebanon, the collapse of the old order and peasant, economic, and international pressures produced the crisis of 1860 and the mutasarrifate of 1861, a settlement shaped by European diplomacy. In Palestine and Jerusalem, notable families, European consuls, and land registration shaped administration. In Iraq, Midhat Pasha's governorship extended state power, but tribal authority and irrigation limited control of the countryside.

In the Hijaz and Yemen, religious authority, tribal power, and distance set limits on Ottoman centralization, and in Tripolitania and Cyrenaica the restoration of direct rule in 1835 led to reform that depended on cooperation with local forces, including the Sanusi order. These contrasts illustrate the regional variation that the textbook emphasizes: no single capital or province represents the Arab world.

Provincial society experienced Tanzimat unequally. Notables, merchants, and some ulama found new opportunities; cultivators and tribal groups faced new obligations; non-Muslims gained legal standing and also exposure to resentment. The constitution of 1876 added a parliament with Arab provincial deputies, but the experiment ended in 1878. Historians debate whether Tanzimat chiefly centralized power or expanded participation, and the best-supported conclusion is that it did both.

The chapter's larger significance is that the Ottoman Arab provinces were undergoing deep institutional change well before the political movements and partitions of the twentieth century. Schooling, printing, councils, courts, and land titles shaped the societies that produced the Nahda and the political identities examined in later chapters, and they formed the administrative inheritance of the states that followed the First World War.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a great empire tried to remake itself from within, and how that effort reached the Arab provinces. Tanzimat is sometimes remembered only as a prelude to Ottoman collapse. The evidence of Damascus, Baghdad, Jerusalem, and Sana'a shows instead a state actively building new institutions, and societies actively using, resisting, and reshaping them.

The chapter reveals larger processes that run through the textbook: the negotiated nature of imperial authority, the central role of provincial notables, the entanglement of local conflict with international diplomacy, and the uneven distribution of the costs and benefits of state-building. It also shows the persistence of regional diversity. Reform legislation was uniform in text but plural in outcome.

Many later developments grew from this foundation. The schools, presses, and public spheres examined in Chapter 51, the competing identities of Chapter 54, and the administrative inheritance of the mandate and successor states in Chapters 56 through 58 all depended on institutions created or reshaped in these decades. The land titles issued under the 1858 code, the communal representation of Mount Lebanon, and the practice of provincial councils reappeared in new forms in the twentieth century.

For students, the chapter models a way of reading reform: attentive to official intentions and to the evidence of how people responded, careful about the perspectives of sources, and cautious about teleology. Tanzimat did not make the modern Arab world inevitable, but it helped define the conditions in which that world took shape.
