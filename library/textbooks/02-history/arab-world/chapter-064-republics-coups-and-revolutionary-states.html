Part XI — Independence, Revolution, and the Arab Cold War
64

Republics, Coups, and Revolutionary States

A History of the Arab World: From Ancient Crossroads to the Modern Arab World

Chapter Opening

Between 1949 and 1979, officers and party organizers in Syria, Iraq, Yemen, Libya, and Sudan seized or redirected the power of the state in the name of the people. They promised dignity, land, schools, unity, and independence from foreign tutelage. Many of them delivered something real. Most of them also built governments in which the people they claimed to represent had very little say in who governed them.

On the morning of 14 July 1958, units of the Iraqi army that had been ordered toward Jordan instead occupied Baghdad, broadcast the end of the Hashemite monarchy, and within hours destroyed the royal family at the Rihab Palace. A day later, crowds seized Nuri al-Said, the monarchy's most experienced politician. The revolution was greeted by large and genuinely enthusiastic crowds in the capital, and it was also marked by killing, mutilation of bodies, and score-settling that its leaders had not planned and could not control. Both facts belong to the history of the event, and a student who retains only one of them has misunderstood it. The scene illustrates the problem this chapter addresses. Revolutionary republicanism in the Arab world was neither simply a liberation nor simply a seizure of power. It was a contested political process in which real social grievances, military ambition, ideological conviction, regional rivalry, and foreign involvement were all at work at once.

This chapter examines how military officers, revolutionary parties, and competing nationalist movements transformed several newly independent Arab states into centralized republics, and why the promises of political liberation and social reform so often produced authoritarian government. Its governing question is why military coups and revolutionary movements became major routes to political power in the postcolonial Arab world, and how the new governments sought to transform society while concentrating political authority. The chapter is comparative. It follows five cases in turn: Syria and Iraq, which receive the fullest treatment; the two Yemens; Libya; and Sudan. It then draws them together in a comparison of how revolutionary republics actually governed.

A distinction runs through the entire chapter. A coup is a method: the seizure of state power by a small group, usually from inside the armed forces or the state apparatus. A social revolution is a much longer and more uncertain transformation of property relations, class structure, political participation, and the relationship between state and society. Some of the regimes studied here achieved both; the Iraqi land reform of 1958, the South Yemeni dismantling of the sultanates, and the Syrian rural reorientation after 1963 changed the distribution of power and wealth. Others made revolutionary claims without establishing comparable change. Students should resist using the word "revolution" simply because a regime used it of itself. The word was part of the regimes' political vocabulary, and it is one of the things the chapter must explain rather than adopt.

The chapter also rejects two simple explanations. The first holds that coups happened because Arab societies were somehow inclined toward authoritarianism. The record does not support this: the same period saw lively parliamentary politics, strong trade unions, mass parties, and, in Sudan in 1964, a civilian uprising that brought down a military government. The second holds that foreign powers, above all the United States, Britain, the Soviet Union, and Egypt, simply manufactured the coups. External involvement was real and is documented where the evidence permits, but outside actors usually exploited divisions that already existed inside the armies and societies concerned. Neither Cold War rivalry nor Arab nationalist ideology alone explains why a battalion commander in Damascus or Baghdad would risk a coup, or why other officers would follow him.

The chapter pays sustained attention to groups who are easily lost in a story of officers and parties: Kurds in Iraq, Alawites, Druze, and Sunni urban notables in Syria, Zaydi tribes and Shafi'i farmers in Yemen, the peoples of Cyrenaica, Tripolitania, and the Fezzan, and the southern Sudanese. These communities had their own political aims. They were not merely the objects of other people's nationalism, and the conflicts over autonomy in Iraq and Sudan in particular were central to how the modern state in each country was formed.

The evidence available is uneven, and the chapter says so as it goes. For events such as the Iraqi revolution or the Syrian coups of 1949 there are memoirs, press reports, diplomatic cables, and a growing body of scholarship. For the internal workings of the Ba'athist security systems, the Libyan revolutionary committees, or the Yemeni civil war, much depends on the testimony of participants with reasons to shape the story, on the reports of foreign diplomats who saw only part of what happened, and on archives that remain closed or only recently opened. Official proclamations, constitutions, and party programs are valuable evidence of what regimes wished to be seen doing, but they are poor evidence of what the regimes actually did. Eight Primary Source Windows in this chapter are therefore paraphrase-based and should be read alongside the originals in the MGU Library.

This chapter builds on Chapter 62, which treats Nasser's Egypt, the Suez Crisis, and Egyptian intervention in Yemen, and Chapter 63, which treats the end of European colonial rule. It leaves the great Arab–Israeli wars to Chapter 66 and the broader petroleum and monarchical transformation of the Gulf to Chapter 65. Its subject is the republican state-building that took place in the space between those histories, and what it meant for the millions of people who lived under it.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Distinguish a military coup from a social revolution and apply the distinction to specific regimes in Syria, Iraq, Yemen, Libya, and Sudan.
  2. Explain why parliamentary institutions in newly independent Syria, Iraq, and Sudan proved fragile, and why armies became decisive political actors.
  3. Trace the sequence of Syrian coups from 1949 to 1970 and explain the consolidation of Hafez al-Assad's rule.
  4. Trace Iraq's political development from the 1958 revolution through the Ba'athist consolidation of 1968–1979, including the repeated transfers of power in between.
  5. Analyze the ideology of the Ba'ath Party and compare it with the way Ba'athist states actually operated in Syria and Iraq.
  6. Evaluate the Kurdish question in Iraq and the southern question in Sudan as independent political conflicts, not subordinate episodes of Arab nationalism.
  7. Compare the North Yemeni and South Yemeni republics in their institutions, social bases, and external alignments.
  8. Distinguish the 1969 Libyan coup from the 1977 declaration of the Jamahiriya and assess the gap between proclaimed popular authority and actual power.
  9. Assess how land reform, nationalization, education, and public employment served both social change and regime legitimacy.
  10. Interpret constitutions, party programs, and agreements as historical evidence, distinguishing declared intentions from implementation.
  11. Evaluate competing explanations for the frequency of coups and for the durability of some revolutionary regimes.
  12. Connect the developments of this chapter to the oil economies, regional wars, and later political conflicts treated in Chapters 65, 66, and beyond.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did armies, rather than parties, parliaments, or social movements, become the decisive political institutions in so many postcolonial Arab states?
  2. What is the difference between a coup and a revolution, and which of the regimes in this chapter actually carried out a social revolution?
  3. How did the Ba'ath Party move from a small intellectual movement with a doctrine of unity, freedom, and socialism to the ruling apparatus of two rival states?
  4. Why did some revolutionary governments collapse within a few years while others, notably those of Hafez al-Assad and the Iraqi Ba'ath, lasted for decades?
  5. How did Kurdish, Alawite, Druze, Zaydi, Shafi'i, southern Sudanese, and other communities shape and experience the new republics, and why should they not be treated only as objects of state policy?
  6. What role did external powers, including Egypt, Saudi Arabia, Britain, the United States, and the Soviet Union, play in these transformations, and how can historians weigh that role against domestic causes?
  7. What did land reform, nationalization, education, and public employment accomplish, and who benefited from them?
  8. Why do constitutions and declarations of popular sovereignty so often diverge from the institutions that actually governed?
  9. What does the Sudanese uprising of 1964 reveal about the limits of military rule, and why did it not produce lasting civilian government?

Historical Context

The states of this chapter emerged from different imperial pasts. Syria was a French mandate from 1920 until the withdrawal of French troops in 1946. Iraq was a British mandate until 1932 and thereafter a nominally independent monarchy tied to Britain by the 1930 treaty and, into the mid-1950s, by British air bases and a privileged British position in its oil industry. Libya, a former Italian colony, became independent under United Nations auspices in 1951 as a federal monarchy under King Idris. Sudan, an Anglo-Egyptian condominium, became independent in 1956. North Yemen was ruled by a Zaydi imamate that had never been a colony but was isolated and dynastic, while South Yemen was the British colony of Aden and its surrounding protectorates until 1967. Chapters 56 through 63 have described these transitions. What mattered for the present chapter was what the independence settlements left behind.

They left, first, narrow political systems. Independence governments in Syria and Iraq were dominated by landowning and merchant notables who had cooperated with the mandate authorities and who controlled parliaments through patronage and, often, manipulated elections. In the countryside, landlords, tribal shaykhs, and in some regions absentee owners held vast estates worked by peasants with few rights. The urban middle classes, especially schoolteachers, civil servants, lawyers, students, and military cadets, were expanding quickly but found their way into political power largely closed. Education was widening faster than opportunity, and the new schools and military academies produced young men who were nationalist, literate, and resentful of the established order.

Second, independence left armies that were among the few modern, centralized, relatively meritocratic institutions in these societies. In Syria and Iraq, the armed forces had been created or expanded under the mandates and contained a growing proportion of officers from modest, often provincial or minority backgrounds, for whom the military academy was the principal route to advancement. An army officer corps gave men organization, weapons, and a sense of corporate interest at a time when most other institutions were weak. The Arab defeat in Palestine in 1948, discussed in Chapter 61, accelerated the process: it discredited the civilian elites that had led their countries into the war, and it persuaded many officers that corruption and incompetence at the top explained the defeat.

Third, independence took place in a charged ideological environment. Arab nationalism, with its demand for unity and for an end to foreign influence, was the dominant political language. Socialist and communist ideas had mass followings among workers, students, and minorities. Parties built on those ideas competed with older notable-based factions, the Muslim Brotherhood, and each other. The Cold War then gave each of these contenders potential outside patrons: the Baghdad Pact of 1955 linked Iraq to Britain, Turkey, Iran, and Pakistan, while Egypt and later Syria drew closer to the Soviet Union. In the history of this chapter the Cold War was a framework that gave local rivalries international consequences, and it was seldom their root cause.

Finally, the example of Egypt mattered. The Free Officers' coup of 1952 and Gamal Abdel Nasser's rise, examined in Chapter 62, showed that a determined group of officers could remove an unpopular regime, win mass applause, and implement land reform. Officers in other countries drew different lessons from this precedent. Some sought to emulate Nasser, some to rival him, and some, as in Iraq in 1958, to avoid subordination to him while pursuing similar domestic policies. Egyptian influence is therefore a recurring variable, but never a sufficient explanation, in the cases that follow.

Division I — Syria · Section 64.1

Syria after Independence and the Military Coups of 1949

When the last French troops left Syria in April 1946, the new republic inherited a parliamentary constitution, a small and respected nationalist elite, and an army that was barely a decade old. The National Bloc, which had led the struggle against the mandate, governed under President Shukri al-Quwatli, and its leading families from Damascus and Aleppo divided the political landscape with the rival People's Party, which was strongest in Aleppo and the north. Parliamentary politics were real, with open newspapers, competing factions, and contested elections, but they were politics among notables. A landowning and merchant class dominated the legislature, and ordinary peasants, who made up the majority of the population, had almost no organized political voice. The army, which grew out of the Troupes Spéciales that France had recruited disproportionately from rural minority communities and the countryside, was both an instrument of the state and a body with its own interests.

The war in Palestine in 1948 shook this order. Syria's army performed poorly and the government was accused of neglect and corruption, including a scandal over defective arms purchases. Quwatli, who had also sought to amend the constitution to allow himself a further term, was widely blamed. Meanwhile there was heightened tension over proposals for closer ties among Arab states, with the Hashemite rulers of Iraq and Jordan advocating a "Greater Syria" or a union with Iraq, while Egypt and Saudi Arabia, and many Syrian republicans, opposed any such design. Syrian politics thus combined domestic discontent, military humiliation, and a regional struggle over who would lead the Arab world.

On 30 March 1949, Colonel Husni al-Za'im, the army chief of staff, seized power in a bloodless coup and arrested Quwatli. It was Syria's first successful post-independence military coup, inaugurating a prolonged era of army intervention in Syrian politics. Earlier regional precedents included General Bakr Sidqi's successful coup in Iraq on 29 October 1936. Za'im dissolved parliament, banned political parties, and ruled as a dictator, while introducing a series of modernizing measures, including the vote for literate women, civil-law reforms, and efforts to curtail the power of religious and landed elites. He signed an armistice with Israel in July 1949, an agreement that ended the formal state of fighting but was not a peace treaty. He also moved to approve the Trans-Arabian Pipeline across Syrian territory, which his predecessors had delayed. Some American officials of the time later said that the United States had been involved in preparing the coup. Historians dispute how far that claim can be supported. The diplomatic record shows that American diplomats had contacts with Za'im and welcomed his government, but it does not demonstrate that Washington directed the seizure of power. A careful reading therefore treats outside powers as interested observers and opportunistic partners, while locating the origin of the coup in the officers' own ambitions and in Syria's domestic crisis.

Za'im's rule lasted less than five months. On 14 August 1949, Colonel Sami al-Hinnawi, with the backing of Aleppo-based officers and civilian politicians of the People's Party, overthrew him and had him executed within hours. Hinnawi's government was friendlier to Iraq and to the notion of a Fertile Crescent union, and it restored a measure of civilian participation. It also allowed the election of a constituent assembly. But Hinnawi too was removed. On 19 December 1949 Colonel Adib al-Shishakli, an officer who had commanded forces in the 1948 war and who opposed the Iraqi union scheme, led a third coup, in which Hinnawi was overthrown. Shishakli did not take the presidency for himself immediately. For the next two years he remained a power behind a succession of civilian cabinets, a pattern that would recur in other republics.

These three coups in a single year need explanation beyond the personalities of the officers. Part of the answer lies in the weakness of parliamentary institutions: the elected assembly lacked mass parties behind it, the cabinets rose and fell with notable factions, and no institution could enforce a rule that officers should stay out of politics. Part lies in the army's internal divisions: regional loyalties, Aleppo against Damascus, personal followings, and the rivalry between pro-Iraqi and anti-Iraqi officers. And part lies in the regional contest over unity and alignment. No single factor suffices. The coups were Syrian events, with Syrian causes, in a regional setting that raised the stakes.

The coups also had lasting consequences. They taught Syrian officers, and officers elsewhere, that a small group could take the capital with limited force and could be restrained by neither the courts nor the legislature. They accustomed the political class to treat the army as a legitimate arbiter. And they opened a long period in which armed forces and parties competed to shape the state, a competition that the Ba'ath Party, in particular, would eventually turn into an alliance.

Division I — Syria · Section 64.2

Shishakli, Parliamentary Restoration, and the Crisis of Civilian Rule, 1950–1961

The constituent assembly elected in late 1949 completed a new constitution in September 1950. It created a parliamentary republic with a strong legislature, guaranteed civil liberties, and, after debate, declared that the president's religion was Islam and that Islamic jurisprudence was the main source of legislation (Article 3). The distinction matters: the later Syrian constitution of 1973 designated it a main source, while separately establishing the Ba'ath Party as the leading political organization. It is one of the more liberal documents in the region's history, and it is also a reminder that constitutional drafting and political stability were distinct achievements. The assembly's compromises did not stop the army from standing behind the government.

Shishakli made the point plain in November 1951, when he arrested ministers and made his control open, and in 1952 he dissolved the parties and founded his own movement, the Arab Liberation Movement. In July 1953 he secured a new constitution establishing a strong presidency, and he was elected president in a referendum. His regime combined authoritarian repression with development projects and an emphasis on Arab nationalism. It outlawed parties, jailed and exiled opponents, and relied on the army and on a network of informers. It also provoked opposition from precisely the political forces that disagreed on nearly everything else: the Ba'ath Party, the Muslim Brotherhood, the Communist Party, and the traditional notables of both major cities. In the Druze highlands, his heavy-handed treatment of the community of Jabal al-Druze, including the pursuit of its leader Sultan al-Atrash, turned a regional grievance into an armed revolt. In February 1954, officers in Aleppo and elsewhere joined a rebellion against him, and Shishakli fled the country. The episode shows that the officers' political arbitration could be reversed by other officers.

The restoration of parliamentary politics in 1954 was a genuine opening, but it was one with fragile foundations. The elections of that year returned a fragmented parliament in which independents, the People's Party, the National Party, and a small but energetic Ba'ath contingent all sat. The Ba'ath's presence was larger than its numbers would suggest. In 1953 the party of Michel Aflaq and Salah al-Din al-Bitar had merged with the Arab Socialist Party of Akram al-Hawrani, a politician from Hama who had built a peasant and officer following by speaking for tenants against landlords. The merged Arab Ba'ath Socialist Party combined the ideological doctrine of the Damascus schoolteachers with Hawrani's rural and military connections. It also recruited young officers. During the 1950s the party's strength thus lay less in electoral victories than in its penetration of schools, the officer corps, and the new educated generation.

The parliamentary years of 1954 to 1958 were also years of rising regional pressure. The assassination of Colonel Adnan al-Maliki, a prominent officer sympathetic to the left, in 1955 led to the suppression of the Syrian Social Nationalist Party, which had been blamed, and strengthened the left's position in the army and in the state. The Suez Crisis of 1956 and the Baghdad Pact aligned Syria more firmly with Egypt and, increasingly, with Soviet arms suppliers. The Syrian Communist Party under Khalid Bakdash grew in influence. Fears among Ba'athists, officers, and conservative politicians alike that the communists or foreign powers might capture the state helped drive Syria toward the idea of union with Nasser's Egypt.

In February 1958, Syria and Egypt merged into the United Arab Republic. Chapter 62 treats this union in detail. For Chapter 64 the important points are that the Ba'ath and many officers sought the union as the fulfilment of their program, that Nasser demanded in return the dissolution of all Syrian political parties, and that the Ba'ath thereby dissolved itself. Egyptian officials and security services came to dominate Syria's governance; Syrian businessmen resented nationalizations, and many Syrian officers felt marginalized by Egyptian command. The union's one lasting domestic effect was an agrarian reform that began to break up large estates. On 28 September 1961, Syrian officers led a coup against the union and restored an independent republic.

The secession did not restore a stable parliamentary order. The elections and governments of 1961–1963 were dominated by conservative notables and by officers who disagreed among themselves. A series of coups and countercoups further splintered the army. Meanwhile the Ba'athist officers who had been in Egypt during the union, and who had grown disillusioned with Nasser's methods while retaining their commitment to Arab nationalism, regrouped. Some had formed a secret Military Committee in Cairo in about 1959. Its members, who included Muhammad Umran, Salah Jadid, Hamad Ubayd, Abd al-Karim al-Jundi, and Hafez al-Assad, would soon be at the center of Syria's politics.

Division I — Syria · Section 64.3

The Ba'ath Revolution of March 1963

Ba'athism as a doctrine took shape in the 1930s and 1940s among a small circle of Damascus teachers and students. Michel Aflaq, a Greek Orthodox Christian, and Salah al-Din al-Bitar, a Sunni Muslim, trained in Paris and returned to teach, and in the early 1940s began forming a movement. Its first congress met in 1947, and its emblematic slogan was unity, freedom, and socialism. The party's core belief was that the Arabs formed a single nation divided by colonial frontiers, that the nation's renewal (the word ba'th means resurrection or renaissance) required the removal of foreign domination, the creation of a single Arab state, and social justice. The party's socialism was general and moral rather than strictly Marxist, and it described Islam as a cultural inheritance for all Arabs, including Christians. In practice the party's first decades were spent building cells, recruiting in schools and the army, and navigating between rivals.

On 8 March 1963, the Military Committee and other officers carried out a coup in Damascus, in coordination with Nasserists, independent unionists, and some figures from the older Ba'ath. A National Council of the Revolutionary Command assumed authority. Lu'ayy al-Atasi, an officer with Nasserist leanings, became president and Salah al-Din al-Bitar became prime minister. The coup had followed by a month the Iraqi coup of 8 February, in which the Ba'ath had also played a major part, and the two revolutions encouraged talk of a triple union with Egypt. Discussions with Nasser in Cairo in April produced an agreement on union, but it was never carried out. The Ba'athists did not want to dissolve their party again or to subordinate themselves to Nasser, and the Nasserists in Syria felt betrayed. Relations collapsed, and the discussions were abandoned.

The new regime's first months were dominated by struggles over who would control it. In July 1963 Nasserist officers, led by Colonel Jasim Alwan, attempted a coup; it was crushed, and a number of the plotters were executed. Syria's government then purged Nasserists from the army and administration. Within the Ba'ath, two groups were emerging: the older civilian leadership, including Aflaq, Bitar, and Amin al-Hafiz, a general who became head of state in July 1963 when Atasi resigned in protest at the Nasserist purge, and the younger officers and regional party activists of the Military Committee. The civilians were more attached to the pan-Arab program and more willing to compromise with other parties and with the traditional urban classes. The officers, many from rural and minority backgrounds, were more focused on the region (qutr) where they actually held power and on a program of social transformation.

The social policies of the new regime were substantial. The party extended and accelerated the agrarian reform that the UAR had begun, lowering the permitted size of landholdings through Law 88 of 1963 and later legislation, and redistributing land to peasants and cooperatives. In January 1965, the government nationalized the large industrial firms and banks, expanded the public sector, and moved the economy toward state control. It also expanded education and rural public services, and opened career paths in the party, the army, and the bureaucracy to groups previously excluded from them. These policies drew on genuine ideological conviction, and also on a political calculation: the sons of rural families who formed the officer corps and the party's regional apparatus wanted to remove the landed and merchant elites who had dominated Syria's politics, and these measures helped do so.

The reforms also provoked resistance. Merchants in Damascus and Aleppo went on strike against nationalizations in 1964–1965. In April 1964 a revolt broke out in Hama, a city with a strong landowning and religious establishment, and the regime put it down by force, including shelling of a mosque used by the rebels. The government presented the revolt as reactionary and sectarian, while participants described it as a defense of religion and of local interests against an atheist, minority-dominated regime. Both claims reflected genuine perceptions in a society where socioeconomic grievances and religious identities overlapped. The Hama events also suggest how closely class, region, and religious community intertwined in Syrian politics, and why simple explanations in terms of a single factor fail.

By 1965 the Ba'ath held the state, but the party was a coalition of factions that disagreed about doctrine, about leadership, and about the relationship between the military and civilian wings. It had adopted an ideology of national liberation and social reform, and had begun to build the instruments of a one-party state. The conflict between its factions was about to produce the most radical coup of the decade.

The 1966 Ba'athist Coup and the Radicalization of the Syrian State

On 23 February 1966, the Military Committee's officers, led by Salah Jadid, overthrew the government of Amin al-Hafiz and the party's older leadership, including Aflaq and Bitar, who fled into exile. The coup was not a seizure of power from outside the Ba'ath but a conflict inside it. The new rulers, often called the "neo-Ba'ath" by scholars and critics, presented themselves as a regional, socialist, and populist leadership with a commitment to radical social change and to "people's war" against Israel and imperialism. Nur al-Din al-Atasi became president and head of the party, Yusuf Zu'ayyin prime minister, and Jadid, who held the formal position of assistant secretary-general of the party's regional command, was the real center of power. Hafez al-Assad became minister of defense.

The new government deepened the social program of 1963–1965 and extended state control over the economy and the countryside. It organized peasant unions and cooperatives, favored the party's rural and urban working-class base, and promoted a radical rhetoric directed at "reactionary" classes and foreign powers. Its attitude toward the traditional religious and commercial establishments was often hostile. At the same time its foreign relations moved toward the Soviet bloc, and it sought closer ties with the Palestinian guerrilla movements and with radical states, while maintaining a bitter rivalry with Iraq's Ba'athists, whom it regarded as its illegitimate rivals. By 1966, in other words, there were two Ba'athist states with the same name and doctrine, and each regarded the other as a traitor to the cause.

The officer corps through which the regime governed was itself a product of social change. Since the mandate period, a growing proportion of officers had come from rural Alawite, Druze, Ismaili, and Sunni rural backgrounds, and from Christians and others, while the old urban Sunni notable families had moved their sons into business and the professions. The 1960s purges, after the secession of 1961 and again after each coup, removed many of the older, urban officers and promoted men whose loyalty was to the party or to personal and regional networks. Some analysts have therefore placed a heavy emphasis on sectarian identity as an explanation for Syrian political developments. The evidence suggests a more complicated picture. Alawite officers were numerous and prominent in the Military Committee, but so were Sunnis, Druze, and Ismailis; the rivalries inside the group frequently cut across sectarian lines; and Alawites themselves were divided by clan, region, and political loyalty. Religious community formed one of several overlapping networks, together with family, village, school, party cell, and military unit, and none alone explains outcomes. A struggle between Jadid's wing and the Druze officer Salim Hatum, who tried to overthrow the government in September 1966, ended in his flight to Jordan and a purge of Druze officers, showing how political rivalry could acquire communal overtones without being reducible to them.

The regime's expansion of security institutions deserves notice. From 1963 onward, the party built organized military intelligence, political security, and party-based surveillance structures, and the government used detention, torture, and trials before special courts to deal with its opponents. The apparatus, though smaller than that later constructed, was already substantial. Its development illustrates a general pattern in this chapter: regimes that came to power by conspiracy remained preoccupied with conspiracy, and invested heavily in the means of watching officers and civilians.

The defeat of June 1967, which Chapter 66 treats, and the loss of the Golan Heights placed the regime under severe strain. Jadid's government blamed Egypt, reactionary forces, and imperialism, and it largely maintained its power. The defeat nonetheless intensified a disagreement between Jadid and Assad. Jadid believed that the party should preserve its ideological commitments and mobilize society; Assad, whose power base lay in the air force and the army, argued that the regime should reduce its isolation, build working relations with other Arab states, and give priority to military reconstruction. The dispute came to a head in September 1970, when Palestinian guerrilla forces fought Jordan's army in the conflict known as Black September, and Syrian armored units briefly entered Jordan. Jadid and his allies supported the intervention. Assad, as defense minister, declined to provide it with air cover, and it failed. The conflict between the two men became unavoidable.

Division I — Syria · Section 64.5

Hafez al-Assad and the Construction of a Durable Political System, 1970–1979

On 13 November 1970, Assad's supporters in the army arrested Jadid, Atasi, and other leaders, and Assad assumed power in what the new government called the Corrective Movement. The use of the term is characteristic. Revolutionary regimes in this period regularly described each new seizure of power as a "correction" of an earlier revolution, a way of claiming continuity while justifying the removal of former colleagues. Jadid remained in prison until his death decades later. In March 1971 Assad was confirmed as president in a referendum with an overwhelming reported majority, a result typical of such plebiscites in single-party systems and not a reliable measure of public opinion.

Assad's political method was to build institutions around himself. He retained the Ba'ath as the ruling party, but he also made it an instrument of the state rather than a rival to it. In 1972 he established the National Progressive Front, a coalition that included the Ba'ath, the Syrian Communist Party, and several small Nasserist and socialist groups. The Front gave nominal pluralism and ensured that the Ba'ath retained a leading role. In 1973 a new permanent constitution was adopted. It described Syria as a democratic, popular, socialist republic, made the Ba'ath the leading party in state and society, and strengthened the presidency. A first draft omitted the requirement that the president be Muslim, and the result was demonstrations in several cities, led largely by Sunni religious leaders. Assad responded with a compromise that restored the requirement and referred to Islamic jurisprudence as a major source of law. The episode shows that Assad was ready to retreat from an ideological position when the cost of holding it seemed high.

The regime's political stability rested on several sources. The first was the army and security services. Assad appointed trusted officers, many from his own community and region, to sensitive commands, and he kept the intelligence agencies in competition with one another, reducing the risk that one could threaten him. Some scholars use the word "neo-patrimonial" to describe the combination of formal institutions, personal loyalty, and distribution of benefits that resulted. The second source was a pattern of patronage and incorporation. Public-sector employment, education, subsidies, and rural development gave many Syrians a stake in the regime. Rural families and some of the lower middle class benefited from schools, clinics, roads, and electrification. The regime also reached an accommodation with parts of the Damascene and Aleppine merchant class, and the economic opening of the 1970s allowed that class to prosper selectively. The third source was foreign policy. In the October 1973 war, discussed in Chapter 66, Syria fought Israel and achieved a measure of recognition within the Arab world, and in 1976 the regime sent troops into Lebanon, an intervention examined in Chapter 68. Substantial financial aid from Gulf states after 1973 and Soviet military supplies strengthened the state.

Stability was also built on coercion. The security services expanded, political prisoners were held without trial, and opposition parties other than the Front's permitted members were banned. Rifaat al-Assad, the president's brother, commanded the Defense Companies, a praetorian unit. By the late 1970s a growing armed opposition had emerged, led by an offshoot of the Muslim Brotherhood. A June 1979 attack on cadets at the Aleppo Artillery School, in which a considerable number of young officers, many of them Alawites, were killed, marked the beginning of a confrontation that culminated in the early 1980s. This chapter does not examine its later course, but students should recognize that the apparent stability of Assad's system was achieved by excluding large sections of society, and that the exclusion produced resistance.

The Syrian experience of the 1970s offers an answer to one of the chapter's central questions. Assad's system lasted because it combined institutionalization, patronage, and repression in a more durable mixture than the preceding regimes had. The Ba'ath's original program of unity, freedom, and socialism had been reduced: unity gave way to Syrian state interests, freedom to security, and socialism to a managed and partly liberalized state economy. The party itself had become a large apparatus of membership and control and a channel for opportunity. Whether this should be called a revolution is a question for Section 64.20. What is clear is that it was a transformation: a state dominated by notables had been replaced by one dominated by officers, party officials, and rural and provincial families whose social horizons the old order had closed.

Historical Vocabulary
Coup d'état and Revolution (inqilab, thawra)

A coup d'état is the seizure of government by a small group, usually military, using the existing machinery of the state. A revolution, in the sense used by social historians, is a profound transformation of social and political structures, normally involving mass participation and changes in property and class. The Arabic vocabulary reflected a similar distinction. Inqilab denoted an overthrow or turning over, often used for coups, while thawra carried the sense of a popular uprising or revolution. In practice the military regimes of this chapter almost always described their coups as thawra, since the word conferred legitimacy.

The distinction matters because it allows historians to ask whether a regime that called itself revolutionary actually altered the distribution of land, wealth, and power, and whether it widened or narrowed political participation. A coup might be the first step in a revolution, as in Iraq in 1958, or only a transfer of power among elites, as in Syria in 1949. The terminology a regime used is evidence of its claims, not of its achievements.

Another term often used in political science is praetorianism, borrowed from the Roman Praetorian Guard, to describe societies in which the army routinely intervenes in politics because civilian institutions are weak. It is a useful descriptive concept but is not itself an explanation.

Historical Vocabulary
Ba'ath (ba'th) and Neo-Ba'ath

The Arabic word ba'th means resurrection or renaissance. The Arab Ba'ath Socialist Party combined the idea of a single Arab nation with the demands for freedom from foreign rule and for social justice. Its structure was hierarchical, with cells and regional and national commands, and it tended to be a party of cadres rather than of mass membership.

After the split of 1966, scholars and rivals commonly distinguished the "old" Ba'ath of Aflaq and Bitar from the "neo-Ba'ath" of Jadid. The term is not always neutral: it was often used by critics to suggest that the new leadership was a sectarian or military faction, while its supporters viewed it as the party's authentic radical wing. In Iraq, the party that took power in 1968 was formally linked to the Aflaq leadership, while Damascus ruled under a different leadership, and the two states remained rivals for decades. Students should therefore avoid treating "Ba'athism" as a single system. It was a shared vocabulary with sharply different practical forms.

Division II — Iraq · Section 64.6

The Iraqi Monarchy's Final Crisis and the Revolution of July 1958

The Iraqi monarchy had been created by Britain in 1921, when Faisal I, the Hashemite leader of the Arab Revolt, was installed as king of the former Ottoman provinces of Basra, Baghdad, and Mosul. The kingdom's politics were dominated by a small group of former Ottoman officers, tribal shaykhs, large landowners, and urban notables. Its most durable politician was Nuri al-Said, a veteran of the Arab Revolt who served as prime minister repeatedly. After the death of Faisal I's son Ghazi in 1939 and the accession of the child-king Faisal II, the regent, Crown Prince Abd al-Ilah, exercised power with Nuri. The state had a parliament, parties, and a vigorous press, but elections were managed, parties were frequently suppressed, and the royal court, the British embassy, and a few politicians determined the outcome of crises.

The monarchy's social foundations were narrow. In the countryside, a small number of shaykhs and landlords held registered title to enormous tracts, and tenant cultivators, many of them Shi'a in the south and Kurds in the north, lived in conditions of dependency and poverty. In the cities, a growing class of students, teachers, clerks, and workers saw the regime as an instrument of foreign control. Oil revenues, which came to the state from the Iraq Petroleum Company, a consortium of British, American, French, and Dutch firms, increased sharply in the 1950s. A development board spent the larger share on dams, roads, and public works, but critics argued that the benefits did not reach the poor and the revenue came at the price of dependence. There was recurrent unrest. In January 1948 the Wathba, a popular uprising in Baghdad, forced the government to abandon a new treaty with Britain, and a further wave of unrest followed in 1952 and again after Suez in 1956.

Foreign policy sharpened the crisis. In 1955 Iraq joined the Baghdad Pact, a defense alignment with Britain, Turkey, Iran, and Pakistan, a decision attacked by Arab nationalists as a surrender to the West. The Suez attack of 1956 by Britain, France, and Israel deeply embarrassed the Iraqi government, which was tied to one of the aggressors. When Syria and Egypt formed the United Arab Republic in February 1958, the Hashemite monarchies of Iraq and Jordan responded by proclaiming their own Arab Union, but the move did little to settle domestic opposition. Meanwhile political organizations that had been forced underground were beginning to cooperate. The National Democratic Party, the Iraqi Communist Party, the Ba'ath Party, which was then small in Iraq, and independent nationalists formed a United National Front in 1957.

Inside the army, a network of officers, known as the Free Officers in imitation of Egypt, had been meeting for several years. Its central figures were Brigadier Abd al-Karim Qasim, an officer of Sunni and Shi'a parentage from a modest Baghdad family, and Colonel Abd al-Salam Arif, who was more openly Arab nationalist and personally close to Nasser's ideas. The opportunity came in July 1958, when the government ordered units to move toward Jordan to support the Hashemite monarchy there after the Lebanese crisis and the Jordanian upheaval. Arif's brigade, instead of proceeding, entered Baghdad on the morning of 14 July, took the radio station, and declared the republic. The royal family was killed at the Rihab Palace. Abd al-Ilah's body was mutilated and dragged through the city, and when Nuri al-Said was found the next day he too was killed. Foreign residents were attacked in places, and the American and British embassies were threatened. The British dispatched troops to Jordan and the Americans landed Marines in Lebanon, though neither intervened in Iraq.

Historians treat the revolution's popular support and its violence together. Large crowds applauded the end of the monarchy, and many Iraqis, especially in the cities and among the poor, regarded the event as liberation from a regime associated with foreign influence and inequality. The brutality of the first days, however, left a legacy. It showed that the revolution had unleashed forces the officers could not fully control, and it established a precedent of political killing as a normal means of settling disputes. In the days that followed, Qasim became prime minister and minister of defense, and Arif deputy prime minister and minister of the interior. A three-man Sovereignty Council was set up to exercise the powers of the president, and a provisional constitution, discussed below, was issued on 27 July.

The revolution thus ended the Hashemite monarchy and began a new political era, but it did not resolve who would govern or on what principles. The coalition behind it included Arab nationalists who looked toward union with Egypt, communists and leftists who did not, liberal reformers, and Kurdish nationalists who hoped for recognition. Within weeks the question of the relationship with Nasser's Egypt, and of Iraq's identity as an Arab, an Iraqi, or a multinational state, was dividing the new leadership.

Abd al-Karim Qasim, Republican Reform, and Political Fragmentation, 1958–1963

The first dispute within the revolutionary leadership was between Qasim and Arif. Arif, who favored immediate union with Egypt, made speeches that were interpreted as supporting Nasser, while Qasim, who wanted to retain Iraqi independence and perhaps feared subordination to Cairo, moved to restrict him. Arif was removed from his posts in September 1958, arrested in November, tried for plotting against Qasim, and condemned to death, though Qasim commuted the sentence. The episode defined Qasim's course. He sought to rule as a leader above factions, balancing the communists, nationalists, and others against one another, and he called himself "the sole leader," a title that signaled the personalization of his power.

Qasim's domestic reforms were significant. Law 30 of September 1958 set ceilings on the size of landholdings and provided for the redistribution of the surplus to peasants; its implementation was slow and hampered by administrative shortages, litigation, and landlord resistance, but it broke the legal basis of the great estates. Government programs built housing for the urban poor, most visibly the vast Baghdad district first called Madinat al-Thawra, "Revolution City," later renamed more than once and now known as Sadr City. Education spending rose, and women gained a more secure legal position through the 1959 personal status law, which restricted polygamy and strengthened women's rights in marriage, divorce, and inheritance. Qasim's government withdrew Iraq from the Baghdad Pact in March 1959, left the sterling bloc, and pursued closer relations with the Soviet Union. In December 1961, Law 80 restricted the Iraq Petroleum Company's concession to areas actually in production, taking back most of the concession and setting a precedent for later nationalization.

The political situation was more difficult than the social program. The Iraqi Communist Party, which had mass support among workers, students, Shi'a in the south, Kurds, and the urban poor, became the strongest civilian organization in Baghdad in 1958–1959, and its militias and "Popular Resistance" units claimed the streets. Arab nationalists and Ba'athists, who opposed the communists on ideological grounds and favored union with Egypt, mobilized in opposition, and rivalries in the officer corps grew. In March 1959, Colonel Abd al-Wahhab al-Shawwaf, commander of the Mosul garrison, led a nationalist revolt that Qasim's forces crushed with the help of communist-led volunteers. In the following months, communists and their opponents clashed in Mosul, and in Kirkuk, in July 1959, there was a bloody episode in which communist-aligned forces and others killed a number of residents, mostly Turkmen. The Kirkuk killings remain deeply remembered. The violence illustrates how national, class, ethnic, and ideological conflicts could combine in a mixed city, and how Qasim's equidistance between factions could not prevent bloodshed. Qasim ultimately turned against the communists and restrained their influence.

The Kurdish question was crucial. The provisional constitution of 1958 described Arabs and Kurds as partners in Iraq. Mustafa Barzani, the Kurdish leader who had gone into exile in the Soviet Union after the collapse of the Mahabad republic in 1946, returned in 1958 with Qasim's invitation and led the Kurdistan Democratic Party's cooperation with the new government. The partnership did not last. Disputes about the recognition of Kurdish cultural and political rights, about local administration, and about the allocation of resources soured relations. In September 1961 fighting broke out in the northern mountains, beginning an armed conflict that would, with interruptions, continue for decades. Qasim's government also, in mid-1961, laid claim to the newly independent Kuwait, a claim that isolated Iraq among Arab states and that Arab League forces, replacing British troops, effectively neutralized.

By late 1962 Qasim's coalition had narrowed. He had alienated nationalists, Ba'athists, many army officers, the landowners, the Kurds, and, to some extent, the communists, and he had no organized party of his own. His regime was one of personal rule exercised through the army, justified by a charismatic appeal to the poor, and sustained by a balancing of enemies. When his enemies finally combined, the balancing failed.

The Coups of 1963 and the Arif Governments, 1963–1968

On 8 February 1963 (14 Ramadan, which gave the event its Arabic name, the Ramadan Revolution), a coup by Ba'athist and Arab nationalist officers, supported by civilian Ba'athists who had prepared for months, overthrew Qasim. After fighting around the Ministry of Defense, Qasim surrendered and was shot the next day. The new government made Abd al-Salam Arif president, a figurehead position, with Ahmad Hasan al-Bakr as prime minister and Ba'athist leaders including Ali Salih al-Sa'di controlling the party apparatus. Some writers and later participants have alleged that American intelligence supported the coup or even provided lists of communists to the plotters. The evidence is contested, with some claims resting on later testimony of uncertain reliability, and what is firmly documented is that Western governments were pleased by the overthrow of a regime they regarded as communist-influenced. The coup's local roots are not in doubt.

The Ba'athist government's brief rule was brutal. Party militias, organized in the National Guard, hunted down communists and other leftists and killed or imprisoned thousands, in operations that the regime did not conceal. Many of the victims were from the poorer districts of Baghdad and the south. The government also launched a new offensive against the Kurds. These events produced a bitter legacy and, at the same time, discredited the party among many Iraqis. The Ba'ath was divided, with a left wing under al-Sa'di favoring rapid social revolution and a nationalist and military wing under al-Bakr more cautious. Arif, an officer who owed his power to the army and who disliked the party's militias, exploited the divisions. On 18 November 1963, he and army units struck against the Ba'athists, removing them from the government. The party's first period in power had lasted less than ten months.

Arif's regime was a military and Arab nationalist government. He presided over limited reform and, in July 1964, announced a sweeping nationalization of banks, insurance companies, and many industrial firms, modeled on Egyptian measures. He established a single political organization, the Arab Socialist Union, on the Egyptian model, but it had little life of its own. He accepted a cease-fire with the Kurds in 1964, though his government did not offer political concessions. In April 1966 Arif died in a helicopter crash, and the army and political leaders chose his brother, Abd al-Rahman Arif, as president. The younger Arif was a weaker figure who lacked his brother's base. His prime minister, Abd al-Rahman al-Bazzaz, a civilian legal scholar, presented a twelve-point plan for the Kurds in June 1966 that recognized Kurdish language and cultural rights and was welcomed by some Kurdish leaders, but officers resisted implementing it. The Arab defeat in June 1967, in which Iraq played a limited role, further undermined confidence in the government.

The decade after the revolution thus saw repeated transfers of power but little institution-building. Why did this period fail to produce durable political arrangements? Several explanations are persuasive in combination. The new republic had no agreed basis of legitimacy: was it an Arab nationalist state, an Iraqi state, a socialist state, or something else? It lacked institutions, such as an effective party system or a legislature, through which rival groups could compete without recourse to force. The army was divided by personal loyalty and ideology. And each new government, by repressing its predecessors, created enemies who had reason to conspire. By 1968 the regime had little support among either the army or the public, and Ba'athists, who had been preparing for a return to power, found an opening.

The Ba'athist Seizure of Power in 1968 and the Revolutionary Command Council

On 17 July 1968 a group of Ba'athist leaders and officers led by Ahmad Hasan al-Bakr, along with non-Ba'athist army officers, among them Abd al-Razzaq al-Nayif, deputy director of military intelligence, and Ibrahim Abd al-Rahman al-Da'ud, commander of the Republican Guard, seized the presidential palace and overthrew Abd al-Rahman Arif. A young party organizer named Saddam Hussein, a relative of al-Bakr from Tikrit, took part in the operation. Al-Bakr became president and chairman of a new Revolutionary Command Council, Nayif prime minister, and Da'ud minister of defense. The Ba'athists had understood that they could not hold power with their own limited military strength, and they had made a bargain with officers who had access to the key units of the army.

The arrangement lasted thirteen days. On 30 July, Ba'athist officers and Saddam Hussein, acting with armed men, arrested Nayif and sent him abroad, and Da'ud, who was out of the country, was prevented from returning. The Ba'ath thus took complete control. This two-stage structure is important: the Ba'ath did not seize power in a single dramatic coup but by joining, and then displacing, a coalition. It showed the party's tactical sophistication, and also its pattern of removing partners once they had served their purpose. Nayif was later assassinated in London in 1978, an event widely attributed to the Iraqi government.

The new rulers learned from the failures of 1963. The Ba'ath had been a loose network in which ideological factions and militias acted independently; the 1968 leadership consolidated authority in a small circle. The Revolutionary Command Council, formally the supreme authority, included the party's top leaders and a few officers. Al-Bakr, an elderly career officer with a reputation for prudence, held the offices of president, prime minister, and commander-in-chief. Saddam Hussein, who was in his early thirties, became deputy chairman of the Council in 1969, and he used his position to organize the party's security apparatus and its links with the army. The party expanded its membership, extended its cells into the army, the bureaucracy, schools, and unions, and developed a hierarchy that tied career advancement to loyalty. It also created and expanded security services: the party's own security bureau, the General Security Directorate, and military intelligence. The services operated against communists, Kurdish nationalists, Shi'a activists, rival Ba'athists, and officers suspected of conspiracy. In January 1969 the regime staged a public hanging of a number of alleged spies, among them several Iraqi Jews, a spectacle that was used to terrify opponents and distract attention from the new government's insecure position.

The regime's resources and its methods distinguished it from its predecessors. Relying on party organization, kinship networks, particularly connections with Tikrit, and a systematic use of surveillance, it addressed the problem of coups by making it difficult for rivals to organize. It also pursued a deliberate effort to broaden its support, by offering concessions to the Kurds, as the next section discusses, by cooperating with the Communist Party in the Progressive National and Patriotic Front of 1973, and by launching economic programs. In 1973 the director of the General Security Directorate, Nadhim Kazzar, attempted a coup, and he was arrested and executed. The communists were again suppressed later in the decade. The party thus used alliance as a tactic of consolidation, and discarded partners when they were no longer needed.

Division II — Iraq · Section 64.10

Iraqi State Consolidation, Kurdish Autonomy, and Oil Nationalization, 1970–1979

In March 1970 the Ba'athist government and the Kurdistan Democratic Party announced an agreement, often called the March Manifesto or the March 11 Agreement, that promised Kurdish autonomy. It recognized the Kurdish nationality within Iraq, made Kurdish an official language in Kurdish-majority areas alongside Arabic, provided for Kurdish participation in the government, including a Kurdish vice-president, and set four years for implementation. It was a significant document, the most extensive recognition of Kurdish rights offered by an Iraqi government to that date. It was also vague on key matters, especially the boundaries of the autonomous region. The status of Kirkuk, whose oil fields were Iraq's principal resource and whose population included Kurds, Arabs, Turkmen, and Assyrians, became the central dispute. The government sought to alter the demographic balance through policies of Arabization, while the KDP insisted that Kirkuk be included in the autonomous region.

Relations deteriorated through the early 1970s. Both sides accused each other of bad faith. An attempt on Barzani's life in 1971 was widely attributed to the government, which denied responsibility, while the Kurds sought support from Iran and, indirectly, the United States and Israel. In March 1974 the government promulgated an Autonomy Law of its own design that gave Kurdistan less than the Kurdish leadership demanded, and Barzani rejected it. A major war followed, in which Iraqi forces, now equipped with Soviet arms and increasingly financed by oil, and the Kurdish peshmerga, supported by Iran, fought heavily. On 6 March 1975, at a meeting of OPEC leaders in Algiers, Saddam Hussein and the Shah of Iran signed an agreement settling their border disputes, notably in the Shatt al-Arab, in exchange for Iran's ending its support for the Kurdish movement. The Kurdish revolt collapsed within days. Barzani went into exile, tens of thousands of Kurds fled to Iran, and the Iraqi government resettled many Kurdish villagers in new locations and strengthened its administrative and military control over the north. Younger Kurdish nationalists, led by Jalal Talabani, founded a rival organization, the Patriotic Union of Kurdistan, in 1975. The history of this conflict demonstrates that the Kurds were not a subsidiary of the Arab nationalist narrative. They were an independent political actor, whose agreements and wars with Baghdad shaped the Iraqi state.

The second foundation of Ba'athist consolidation was oil. For years Iraqi governments had argued over the Iraq Petroleum Company's concession, and the dispute over Law 80 of 1961 had not been resolved. In 1972, after negotiations failed, the government nationalized the company on 1 June 1972, an act announced to wide public acclaim and prepared in consultation with the Soviet Union, with which Iraq had just signed a treaty of friendship and cooperation. The company's operations in the Basra region were nationalized in 1975. Nationalization on its own did not guarantee prosperity, but the sharp rise in oil prices after the 1973 war, which Chapter 65 discusses, multiplied state revenues several times over in the following years. The Ba'athist state could therefore finance large programs: schools, hospitals, roads, electrification, subsidized food, and industrial investment. Women's employment and education expanded, and in the late 1970s a nationwide compulsory literacy campaign brought many adults into classrooms and earned international recognition.

Revenue from oil also changed the relationship between rulers and subjects. The state became the principal employer, supplier of services, and source of income for much of the population, and a government that controlled such resources could reward loyalty and punish dissent more effectively. It strengthened the army and security services, and reduced the government's dependence on taxation and thus on the consent of the governed. This pattern, sometimes called the rentier state, is central to the political economy of the Arab oil producers, and Chapter 65 develops it further.

The regime also faced opposition, particularly from the Shi'a majority, whose religious and political networks were organized in groups such as the Da'wa Party. Shi'a pilgrimage processions in Najaf and Karbala in 1977 turned into protests and were suppressed with mass arrests and executions. The government regarded Shi'a political activism as a threat, and it deported many Iraqis of Iranian descent, tens of thousands of them, in the 1970s. Iraq's government in these years was Sunni-led in much of its leadership, but it was also a secular, nationalist party that recruited Shi'a and Christians; sectarian identity again was one factor among several. In July 1979, al-Bakr resigned the presidency and Saddam Hussein replaced him. Within days Saddam Hussein presided over a meeting of senior Ba'athists where a number of colleagues were accused of conspiracy and removed, and contemporary reporting recorded 21 officials executed on 8 August 1979, including five members of the Revolutionary Command Council; accounts of the broader purge vary in the number arrested or implicated. The event inaugurated a more personalized and violent period of rule. It also marked the end of the formative era of Ba'athist consolidation, which had made Iraq a more centralized, wealthier, and more repressive state than at any time since independence.

Division III — Yemen · Section 64.11

The North Yemeni Revolution of September 1962

For most of the first half of the twentieth century, northern Yemen was ruled by the Hamid al-Din imams, a Zaydi dynasty claiming descent from the Prophet Muhammad. Imam Yahya, who took power after the Ottoman withdrawal, governed an inward-looking highland state that discouraged foreign contact, kept its army small, and taxed its peasants through tribal intermediaries. Zaydism, a Shi'a branch with distinctive doctrines about the imamate, was the religion of the ruling elite and of the northern highland tribes, while the Shafi'i Sunni population of the Tihama coast and the southern highlands, perhaps half of the people of North Yemen, lived under the imams' authority with limited representation. Political legitimacy in the imamate rested on descent and religious learning, and on a balance among tribal confederations, above all Hashid and Bakil, that the imams managed by gifts, hostages, and force.

The system faced a series of challenges. In 1948 a coalition of reformers, some members of the sayyid (descendants of the Prophet) elite, and tribal figures assassinated Imam Yahya and attempted to establish a constitutional imamate, but his son Ahmad recaptured Sana'a with tribal forces and executed the leaders. Ahmad moved the capital to Ta'izz, relied on tribal support and a repressive security system, and made cautious openings to the outside world, accepting Soviet and Chinese development aid in the 1950s and joining Egypt and Syria in a loose federation, the United Arab States, in 1958. An attempted coup by officers in 1955 failed. A small but growing group of students educated in Cairo, Baghdad, and Aden, officers trained abroad, merchants, and reform-minded Zaydi and Shafi'i figures formed an opposition known as the Free Yemenis, who wanted constitutional reform or an end to the imamate.

Imam Ahmad died on 19 September 1962 and was succeeded by his son Muhammad al-Badr. Within a week a group of officers led by Colonel Abdullah al-Sallal, the commander of the royal guard, seized Sana'a and proclaimed the Yemen Arab Republic on 26 September. Sallal became president. The republicans bombarded the palace, and al-Badr escaped northward, where he rallied tribal and royalist support. Egypt, whose officers had been in contact with the conspirators and which saw an opportunity to strengthen Nasser's position and to weaken Saudi Arabia, quickly recognized the republic and, in October, sent troops. Chapter 62 treats Egyptian decision-making. For this chapter the relevant fact is that a coup by a limited group of officers, supported by some urban reformers and sections of the population, immediately became a regional war.

Republicanism appealed to different people for different reasons. For modernizing officers and young urban reformers, the imamate represented isolation, poverty, and dynastic privilege. For some Shafi'i communities, it was an end to the dominance of the Zaydi sayyid class. For some tribal figures, joining the republic offered opportunity. Yet royalist authority also retained substantial support. Many highland tribes regarded the imamate as the legitimate form of government, resented the new republic's Egyptian patrons, and feared the loss of their own privileges and subsidies. The revolution thus began as a coup that created a government but did not by itself create a nation willing to accept it.

Division III — Yemen · Section 64.12

The North Yemeni Civil War, 1962–1970

The war that followed lasted eight years and cost many thousands of lives. The republicans held the main cities and the Egyptian-garrisoned corridor between Sana'a, Ta'izz, and the Red Sea ports, while the royalists, supported by Saudi Arabia, Jordan, and, covertly, Britain and other foreign sympathizers, held much of the northern and eastern mountains and operated through tribal forces. Saudi Arabia feared the example of a republic on its southern border, and provided money, arms, and sanctuary. Egypt eventually committed a force of tens of thousands, and employed aircraft and, as contemporaneous reporting and later specialist historical research establish, chemical weapons against targets in royalist-held areas, particularly in 1966–1967. The specific agents used, attribution of individual attacks, and casualty totals remain disputed, and Egyptian authorities denied the allegations. Egyptian strategy is addressed in Chapter 62; here it is enough to say that mountain warfare against dispersed tribal fighters proved costly and inconclusive, and that Egypt's troops were tied down while its economy suffered.

The conflict was not a simple contest between republican modernizers and royalist traditionalists, nor between two homogeneous populations. Tribal leaders changed sides depending on subsidies, local rivalries, and assessments of who would win. The Hashid and Bakil confederations were divided, and prominent figures such as Sheikh Abdullah ibn Husayn al-Ahmar of Hashid eventually supported the republic. Zaydi sayyids sided with both camps. The imam's own cause was weakened by divisions among royalist leaders and by disputes over strategy. Regional differences, including those between the highland north and the Tihama, mattered as well. External aid was therefore effective only to the degree that it fit local alignments, and the foreign powers repeatedly discovered the limits of their influence.

Diplomacy repeatedly failed. The United States, which recognized the republic in December 1962, tried through the Bunker mission to arrange Egyptian withdrawal and Saudi disengagement, but neither side kept its commitments. In August 1965 Nasser and King Faisal of Saudi Arabia reached the Jeddah Agreement providing for a cease-fire and plebiscite, but the agreement broke down. The decisive change came after the Arab defeat in June 1967, which forced Nasser to withdraw Egypt's troops from Yemen in the aftermath of the Khartoum Arab summit of August–September 1967. In November 1967 a coup of republican moderates, led by Qadi Abd al-Rahman al-Iryani, removed Sallal, who was out of the country.

The Egyptians had scarcely left when royalist forces and tribal allies converged on Sana'a. The siege, from December 1967 to February 1968 and often called the "Seventy Days," was a decisive trial for the republic. Its defenders included regular army units, militias, and, notably, groups of leftists and young volunteers, assisted by Soviet arms and pilots and by South Yemeni and other support. The royalists failed to take the capital. Their defeat was due in part to the republican resistance and in part to the royalists' own divisions, and the republic, which had been regarded as the Egyptians' puppet, now had a claim to independent legitimacy. After the siege, however, the republican camp divided between leftist officers and more conservative figures, and after a confrontation in 1968 the left was removed from power.

The war ended by accommodation. Over 1969–1970, republican leaders and royalist tribal figures, with Saudi encouragement, negotiated an arrangement in which the Hamid al-Din family was excluded but many royalist notables joined the republic. Saudi Arabia recognized the Yemen Arab Republic in 1970. A new constitution that year and the formation of a consultative council in 1971 created institutions in which tribal leaders, notably the head of Hashid, had prominent roles. The result was a state in which the republic's center was weak and tribal, regional, and religious notables held considerable autonomy. North Yemen's republic had not eliminated the older society; it had incorporated it. A further sequence of military coups in the mid-1970s, including Ibrahim al-Hamdi's seizure of power in 1974 and his assassination in 1977, shows that the institutionalization of the republic remained incomplete.

Division III — Yemen · Section 64.13

The People's Republic of South Yemen and Revolutionary Radicalization, 1967–1970

South Yemen's path to republican rule differed sharply from the North's. Aden had been a British possession since 1839, an important coaling station and, by the mid-twentieth century, a port with a large refinery, a substantial working class, and a cosmopolitan population of Arabs, Indians, Somalis, and Europeans. Behind it lay a patchwork of protectorates ruled by sultans, sheikhs, and emirs under treaties with Britain. In the early 1960s Britain tried to consolidate these territories in the Federation of South Arabia, intended to be a conservative state friendly to British interests and a base for a continued military presence. Opposition came from two nationalist organizations: the Front for the Liberation of Occupied South Yemen (FLOSY), backed by Egypt and favoring union with the North, and the National Liberation Front (NLF), which was more rooted in the rural hinterland and ideologically more radical, with leaders drawn from the Arab Nationalist Movement. A rebellion began in the mountains of Radfan in 1963 and spread to Aden, where guerrilla and terrorist campaigns against British forces intensified in 1967.

Britain announced its intention to withdraw by 1968, and then accelerated the departure. As the federation's army and sultans collapsed, the NLF, which had defeated FLOSY in a bitter internal struggle, emerged as the dominant force. British forces left on 30 November 1967 without a formal handover to a recognized successor, and the NLF proclaimed the People's Republic of South Yemen. Qahtan al-Shaabi, a veteran of the nationalist movement and a leader of moderate temperament, became president. The sultanates were abolished and their rulers removed or in exile. The new republic faced an empty treasury, the loss of British payments and of income from the port following the closure of the Suez Canal in 1967, and an economy that had depended on the colonial presence.

The NLF was itself divided. A pragmatic wing around al-Shaabi sought to maintain good relations with neighboring states and to proceed cautiously with reform. A left wing, whose ideology drew on Marxism, Third World revolution, and the model of Cuba and the Soviet bloc, including leaders such as Salim Rubayi Ali and Abd al-Fattah Ismail, argued that political independence meant little without a thoroughgoing social revolution. On 22 June 1969, in what the radicals called the Corrective Step, they forced al-Shaabi out. A collective leadership, a Presidential Council headed by Salim Rubayi Ali, took power, and Ismail became the dominant ideologist. Al-Shaabi, who was detained, died in 1981.

The radical government acted decisively. It nationalized banks, insurance firms, and much foreign and local commerce in 1969, introduced agrarian reform that abolished the landholdings of sultans and large owners and aimed to give land to cultivators, and organized peasants and workers into cooperatives and unions. It reorganized local government, replacing the sultanates with governorates and local councils that were party-dominated. The state's security services dealt harshly with opponents, including tribal leaders, merchants, and rival nationalists, many of whom fled to the North or to Saudi Arabia. In November 1970 the country was renamed the People's Democratic Republic of Yemen (PDRY) and adopted a new constitution that declared its commitment to socialist transformation. It maintained close ties with the Soviet Union, East Germany, Cuba, and China, and supported revolutionary movements, notably the insurgency in neighboring Oman's Dhofar province.

The South Yemeni revolution was therefore both a nationalist and a social one. Its social programs, including education, health services, and legal reforms that improved women's position (notably the 1974 family law), made a mark in a society of great poverty. Yet the struggle among its leaders was fierce, and the regime repeatedly resolved disputes by violence, leading to purges and later civil conflict in 1986. As with other revolutionary regimes in this chapter, an authentic social transformation coexisted with the concentration of power in a small party leadership.

Division III — Yemen · Section 64.14

Two Yemeni Republics: Rival Ideologies, State Formation, and Border Conflict

By 1970 the two Yemens were both republics, and both traced their legitimacy to anti-imamic and anticolonial revolutions, but they stood for different conceptions of what a republic should be. The Yemen Arab Republic in the North, with its capital in Sana'a, was a state of notables and tribes that retained private property, a mixed economy, and a close relationship with Saudi Arabia, which recognized it in 1970 and became its principal financial supporter. Its institutions, including a consultative assembly, were weak, and its army, largely drawn from the highland tribes and regions, coexisted with powerful tribal leaders. Remittances from the large number of Yemenis working in Saudi Arabia and elsewhere in the Gulf supported its economy and gave the North's society a new link to the oil states.

The PDRY in the South, with its capital at Aden, was a one-party state that placed nearly all productive property under state or cooperative ownership, destroyed the tribal and sultanic hierarchy, and aligned itself with the Soviet bloc. Its social base was in the working class of Aden, the cooperatives of the countryside, and the educated cadres of the party. Its resources were meager, and its economy depended on remittances from Yemenis abroad, the export of labor, and Soviet and other aid. The South's political life was marked by factional conflict within the ruling party, which in 1978 was reorganized as the Yemeni Socialist Party.

The meaning of republicanism thus differed in practice. In the North it meant the end of the imamate and the integration of tribal and royalist notables into a national state. In the South it meant the abolition of an older ruling order and a socialist transformation under a vanguard party. Both claimed to represent the Yemeni people, and both proclaimed a commitment to eventual unity. The idea of Yemeni unity enjoyed broad support in both territories, as the people shared language, culture, and a historical memory of a larger Yemen, and its advocates in both states regarded it as a natural outcome.

Unity nonetheless proved elusive. In September and October 1972, fighting broke out along the border, with the North's forces and tribal irregulars clashing with the South's army. Mediation by the Arab League produced a cease-fire and an agreement signed in Cairo in October 1972, followed by a declaration in Tripoli in November, committing both sides to unification. They did not implement it. Another war in early 1979 was followed by a further unity agreement, again unfulfilled. Each state supported opponents of the other. Saudi Arabia viewed the Marxist South as a danger and the unification of the Yemens as a threat to its own security, and the Soviet Union and other powers gave aid to the South. Domestic politics in each state, including the interest of ruling groups in retaining power, also hindered unification.

The history of the two Yemens shows that ideology did not alone determine politics. Northern and southern societies were divided by differences in geography, history, religion, and economic organization, but also within themselves, and the conflicts between the two republics were as much about the interests of states, tribes, and regimes as about socialism and conservatism. Understanding the Yemens requires attention to the highland tribes, the Zaydi–Shafi'i divide, the coastal and mountain economies, the legacy of colonial and imamic rule, and the role of external patrons. The unification of 1990, which later chapters examine, would build on the unresolved questions of this period.

Division IV — Libya and Sudan · Section 64.15

Libya's September 1969 Revolution and the End of the Monarchy

Libya was among the poorest countries in the world when it became independent in 1951. Italy's colonial rule, which had included the violent suppression of resistance in Cyrenaica in the 1920s and 1930s, had left a legacy of loss and bitterness, and the country's three regions, Tripolitania, Cyrenaica, and the Fezzan, had distinct histories and interests. The United Nations arranged independence under King Idris, the leader of the Sanusi religious order, which was rooted in Cyrenaica. The state was a federation until 1963, when Idris abolished the federal structure and created a unitary kingdom. Political parties were banned, parliament was controlled, and the King relied on a narrow circle of aides, tribal leaders, and notables. Britain and the United States kept military bases, including the American Wheelus Air Base near Tripoli, in return for financial support.

The discovery of oil in 1959 and the start of exports in 1961 transformed the economy. Revenues rose quickly, and Libya became one of the major oil exporters, with foreign companies, mostly American, operating under concessions. The new wealth enriched a small circle of government figures, merchants, and contractors, and fueled urban growth and migration, but it did not create a participatory political system. At the same time, a new generation of Libyans, educated in the expanding schools and affected by Arab nationalism, was exposed to the example of Nasser's Egypt. The Arab defeat of 1967 produced anti-Western riots in Libya, and the King's association with the West, along with his advanced age and his lack of an heir with a political following, made the regime vulnerable.

Within the army, a conspiracy had been taking shape among young officers, many from modest backgrounds in provincial towns and from tribal families that had not belonged to the royal elite. Their leader was Muammar al-Qaddafi, born about 1942 into a Bedouin family near Sirte, who had attended school in Sebha and Misrata and then the military academy at Benghazi. Qaddafi and his colleagues, who called themselves the Free Unionist Officers in imitation of Nasser's movement, planned their action for years. On 1 September 1969, while Idris was abroad for medical treatment, they seized key installations in Tripoli and Benghazi with little resistance, and announced the establishment of the Libyan Arab Republic. The crown prince, Hasan al-Rida, was placed under arrest and renounced his claim. The monarchy was abolished.

The new government was headed by a Revolutionary Command Council of twelve officers, with Qaddafi as chairman, and with a civilian cabinet whose members were largely technocrats. The council's membership included Abd al-Salam Jalloud, who became prime minister in 1972 and served as Qaddafi's chief lieutenant, and several other figures who would later fall from favor. The early policies reflected Nasser's example, notably in their emphasis on Arab unity, an anti-imperialist foreign policy, and social reform. In domestic affairs, the new government expelled the British and American bases, which were evacuated by 1970, restricted foreign influence in the economy, and, in 1970, confiscated the property of Italian settlers and ordered the departure of most of the remaining Italian community, while the small Jewish community, which had largely left after 1967, lost its remaining property. It also adopted measures to promote education, health, and public services, and began to apply a conservative interpretation of Islamic law, including bans on alcohol. In foreign policy, Qaddafi sought unions with Egypt, Sudan, and Syria, and the Tripoli Charter of 1969 and the 1971 Federation of Arab Republics expressed these ambitions, though none was realized.

The regime also moved against the oil companies. In 1970–1971, the new leadership negotiated forcefully with the foreign operators, beginning with the independent company Occidental Petroleum, which was more vulnerable than the major companies. The resulting agreements raised prices and the government's share of revenue, and they set precedents that other producers followed, a development examined in Chapter 65. In 1971 Libya nationalized the holdings of British Petroleum in retaliation for a British role in the Gulf, and in 1973 it took majority control of foreign oil companies. This policy, which relied on a combination of legal claims and political leverage, gave the new government control of its principal resource and the revenue to carry out its plans.

Division IV — Libya and Sudan · Section 64.16

Qaddafi's Revolutionary State and the Jamahiriya, 1969–1979

The first phase of the Libyan revolution was an officers' republic, which combined a Revolutionary Command Council, a cabinet, and a single political organization, the Arab Socialist Union, modeled on Egypt's. By the early 1970s, Qaddafi was dissatisfied with its results. The Union produced little mobilization, the bureaucracy resisted his directives, and conflict within the leadership grew. In April 1973, in a speech at Zuwara, he announced a "Popular Revolution" with five points: suspension of existing laws, purge of the "politically sick," an administrative revolution against bureaucracy, arming of the people, and a cultural revolution against ideas considered foreign. The speech was followed by the formation of popular committees in workplaces, schools, and towns, which took control of many institutions.

Between 1975 and 1979 Qaddafi published the three parts of The Green Book, in which he set out his "Third Universal Theory" as an alternative to capitalism and communism. The first part criticized parliamentary democracy as a form of rule by representatives and called for direct popular rule through committees and congresses; the second addressed the economic question, proposing the abolition of private rent and wage labor in favor of partnership; and the third discussed the social foundations of society, including the family, tribe, and nation. The book was circulated widely and studied in schools, and its themes became the official ideology of the state.

On 2 March 1977, at a General People's Congress meeting at Sebha, the Libyan leadership issued the Declaration on the Authority of the People and changed the name of the state to the Socialist People's Libyan Arab Jamahiriya. The word jamahiriya, coined from the Arabic for "masses," was presented as a state of the masses, in which the people exercised authority directly through basic people's congresses and committees. The General People's Congress became the national legislature, and a General People's Committee took the place of the cabinet. Qaddafi became secretary-general of the General People's Congress; in 1979 he gave up even this formal title and was thereafter styled the "Leader of the Revolution," with no formal office, a status that placed him above the system. The declaration, in other words, described a state without a state, in which power belonged to the people and not to any government.

The gap between the proclaimed system and the reality of power was large. The people's congresses and committees did meet and debate, and many Libyans participated in them, but decisions on foreign policy, oil, security, and the army were made elsewhere. The Revolutionary Command Council had retained a core of loyal officers, and Qaddafi relied on an inner circle, on revolutionary committees formed from 1977 and charged with guarding the revolution, on the security services, and on regional and tribal networks, including his own Qadhadhfa tribe and allied groups. The banning of political parties, restrictions on speech, the persecution of dissidents, including executions of opponents, and the suppression of independent organizations reduced the possibility of open opposition. An unsuccessful coup attempt in August 1975, involving Omar Meheishi, one of the original officers, showed that even the leadership was not united. In 1978 the government nationalized much of the remaining private economy, including retail trade and housing, steps that eliminated a good part of the traditional merchant class.

Libya's oil revenues financed the experiment. They paid for housing, education, health care, and a large public payroll, and they funded a large military and an active, often disruptive foreign policy that included support for revolutionary and insurgent movements and intervention in Chad. Women's education and employment expanded in these years, although the regime's rhetoric about women's equality coexisted with conservative social norms. The Libyan system suggests an answer to one of the chapter's questions: it presented a theory of popular sovereignty that included the abolition of the state, and in practice it produced a personalized, oil-financed authoritarianism, unusually dependent on one man and on the revenue he controlled.

Division IV — Libya and Sudan · Section 64.17

Sudan: Military Government, Popular Revolution, and Parliamentary Restoration, 1958–1969

Sudan was the largest country in Africa and one of the most diverse. Its northern population was Muslim and largely Arabic-speaking, though it included Nubians, Beja, and other communities with their own languages and traditions, and its western regions, Darfur and Kordofan, included Fur, Masalit, Baggara Arab pastoralists, and many others. Its south, which Britain had administered separately for much of the condominium period and in which Christian missionaries ran schools, was inhabited by Dinka, Nuer, Shilluk, Azande, and many other Nilotic and Equatorial peoples who spoke their own languages and had little connection with the Arabic-speaking north. Sudan's independence in 1956 was achieved through the work of northern political parties, principally the Umma Party, associated with the Ansar religious movement and the family of the Mahdi, and the National Unionist Party, linked to the Khatmiyya order. The southerners, who had been promised consideration of a federal system, were largely excluded from the new government, and a mutiny of southern troops at Torit in August 1955 had already marked the beginning of the southern conflict.

Civilian politics in the first republic were marked by factional bargaining and instability, with coalitions among the northern parties and a deadlocked relationship between the two sectarian leaders. In November 1958, with the backing of the Umma Party's leadership, which preferred army rule to a coalition with the NUP, Lieutenant General Ibrahim Abboud took power and established a Supreme Council of the Armed Forces. The regime banned political parties and unions, and its economic program was modest. Its policy in the south was assimilationist: it promoted Arabic as the language of education and administration, encouraged Islam, and in 1964 expelled foreign Christian missionaries, who were accused of encouraging separatism. These policies, and military operations against southern opposition, provoked the growth of the Anyanya rebels, who began a guerrilla war in the early 1960s.

The regime's end came through a civilian movement. In October 1964, a seminar at the University of Khartoum on the "southern problem" was broken up by police, and a student, Ahmad al-Qurashi, was killed on 21 October. His funeral and the demonstrations that followed became a mass protest, joined by professionals, doctors, lawyers, and trade unionists, who organized a general strike. Abboud, after trying to resist, dissolved the Supreme Council and the government in late November. The October Revolution, as Sudanese call it, is an important precedent: it shows that a military government could be forced out by organized civilians, including students, unions, and professional associations, and that the army, when faced with mass noncooperation, could decline to defend it. Its leaders were above all the educated and urban middle and working classes, with significant participation by the Communist Party.

The restoration of parliamentary government in 1964–1969 did not resolve the underlying problems. A transitional government included communists and unionists, but the traditional parties soon reasserted their control. A Round Table Conference on the south in March 1965 brought northern and southern leaders together but produced no settlement, and the war grew worse. In the 1965 elections, the Umma and NUP won, and the Communist Party, which had gained representation, was banned in late 1965 and its members expelled from parliament, a decision that the courts found unconstitutional and the government ignored. The Umma Party split between factions around Sadiq al-Mahdi and his uncle, the imam al-Hadi, and governments fell with regularity. The weakness of the parliamentary system lay in its dependence on sectarian-based parties, its failure to deal with the south, and a growing disillusion among the urban population, especially the educated young. In this atmosphere, a new coup found willing supporters.

Division IV — Libya and Sudan · Section 64.18

Jaafar Nimeiri, Revolutionary Government, and the Sudanese State, 1969–1979

On 25 May 1969, a group of officers led by Colonel Jaafar Nimeiri seized power and announced the establishment of the Democratic Republic of the Sudan. A Revolutionary Command Council took charge, and a largely civilian cabinet headed by Babikr Awadallah included Communist Party members and Arab nationalists. The new government pledged to deal with the south, promote socialism, and end the sectarian parties' domination. It nationalized banks and some industries, expanded state control of the economy, and adopted a foreign policy of closer relations with Egypt, Libya, and the Soviet bloc. It also suppressed its enemies. In March 1970, when the government's forces attacked Ansar supporters at Aba Island, the imam al-Hadi al-Mahdi was killed, and many followers died.

Nimeiri's coalition was unstable. The Communist Party, the strongest organized political force in the country, was divided about whether to support him, and the party's leaders, including Abd al-Khaliq Mahjub, were ambivalent. Nimeiri expelled several leftist members of the Revolutionary Command Council and moved against the party. On 19 July 1971 a group of officers associated with the left, led by Major Hashim al-Atta, overthrew him and held power for three days. Counter-forces loyal to Nimeiri, aided by Egypt and by a Libyan intervention that forced a British airliner carrying two of the coup's leaders to land in Benghazi, restored him. Nimeiri then executed Atta, Abd al-Khaliq Mahjub, and other communist leaders, and dismantled the party. The crisis ended the left's influence on his government.

Nimeiri now reoriented his rule. He was confirmed as president in a referendum in October 1971, created a single party, the Sudanese Socialist Union, and drew up a permanent constitution in 1973 that vested great authority in the presidency. He also turned to the problem of the south, where his predecessors had failed. Mediated by Ethiopia's Emperor Haile Selassie and by church bodies, including the World Council of Churches and the All Africa Conference of Churches, negotiations between the government and the Southern Sudan Liberation Movement, whose military leader was Joseph Lagu, produced the Addis Ababa Agreement in February 1972. It provided for a self-governing southern region with its own legislature and executive, recognized the south's distinct languages and culture, and incorporated Anyanya fighters into the army. The agreement ended the first civil war and was greeted with relief in both north and south.

The settlement was a major accomplishment, but it was fragile. It rested on Nimeiri's personal authority and on a presidential system in which the center could overrule the region. Disputes arose over the south's budget, the boundaries of the region, and, later, the discovery of oil in the south in the late 1970s, which raised the stakes of control. Meanwhile the regime shifted its alliances. After 1971 it moved away from the Soviet bloc and toward the United States, Egypt, and Saudi Arabia. It faced further coup attempts, including one in 1976 backed by Libya and organized by northern opposition forces, after which Nimeiri undertook a "national reconciliation" with the Umma Party and with Islamist figures, including Hasan al-Turabi, in 1977. This change brought into the state the same northern forces that would later challenge the southern settlement. Later chapters and courses address the collapse of the Addis Ababa Agreement in the early 1980s. For the period covered here, Sudan's experience shows both a military regime that ended a civil war and one that remained dependent on a single leader's shifts.

Historical Vocabulary
Jamahiriya (jamahiriyya)

The Arabic word jamahiriya derives from jamahir, "the masses," and was used by Qaddafi to denote a "state of the masses." It was proposed as a replacement for jumhuriyya, republic, which Qaddafi regarded as implying rule through representatives. It is sometimes translated into English as "state of the masses" or "people's state."

The term is a good example of how political vocabulary could assert a claim that the institutions did not match. Under the Jamahiriya, official doctrine held that sovereignty belonged directly to the people through basic people's congresses, yet decisions on the most important questions were made by Qaddafi and a small circle. Students should treat the term as evidence of the regime's self-description. The word jamahiriya was not a neutral description of a political system.

Division V — Comparative Institutions and Synthesis · Section 64.19

How Revolutionary Republics Governed: Armies, Parties, Security, and Social Reform

The revolutionary republics shared a vocabulary, and each of them depended on a few institutions. The first was the army. In every case the seizure of power came from within the armed forces, and in every case the regime had to decide how to control an institution that had made it. The solutions varied. Syria's Ba'ath penetrated the army with party cells and loyal officers, and under Assad combined a professional force with praetorian units and rival intelligence agencies. Iraq's Ba'ath created party structures inside the army, relied on kinship and regional ties, and used the General Security Directorate and military intelligence to watch officers. Libya's Revolutionary Command Council came from a single cohort of officers who later drew back from the formal army, relied on revolutionary committees and loyal units, and distrusted regular forces. North Yemen's army was partly a tribal institution, and South Yemen's was subordinated to the party. Sudan's military rulers worked through a command council, and Nimeiri used both the army and a single party.

The second institution was the party, or its substitute. Ba'athist regimes treated the party as the instrument of mobilization and a channel of recruitment, but it also became a means of control and a source of patronage, and over time it lost the character of an ideological movement. Nimeiri's Sudanese Socialist Union, like Egypt's Arab Socialist Union on which it was modeled, and like Libya's Arab Socialist Union and later popular congresses and committees, served to organize a population and to prevent the development of independent political organizations. These bodies differed in effectiveness, but they shared a function: to provide the regime with a recognized channel for participation that it controlled.

Security services were the third institution. Each republic built a set of agencies charged with detecting conspiracy, suppressing dissent, and supervising the population. In Syria and Iraq these were extensive and increasingly intrusive. Surveillance, detention without trial, and torture were used against communists, Islamists, rival nationalists, Kurds, Shi'a activists, and members of the regime's own parties. Press and broadcast media were state-controlled, and censorship was routine. The regimes justified such measures by invoking the threat of imperialism, Zionism, or counterrevolution, and the experience of repeated coups gave the threat a basis in fact. But the security systems were also tools for eliminating legitimate opposition, and for making ordinary citizens cautious about speaking.

Constitutions, in these systems, were instruments of legitimation more than limits on power. They declared the republic's social and national character, declared rights, and defined institutions, but they rarely constrained rulers. Provisional constitutions, such as Iraq's in 1958 and 1968, remained in force for years. Plebiscites and referendums, which produced results of over ninety percent in favor of the ruler, were common, and elections, where they took place, were managed. The 1973 Syrian constitution and the 1977 Libyan declaration show two forms of the pattern: one entrenching a ruling party, and the other proclaiming direct popular rule.

The social reforms of these governments were real and were also instruments of legitimacy. Land reform, in Iraq after 1958, Syria after 1958 and 1963, South Yemen after 1968, and Egypt (see Chapter 62), broke the power of the landowning classes who had dominated the old regimes, and redistributed land or income in varying degrees. Nationalization of banks, industries, and, in Iraq and Libya, oil, extended state control and gave governments resources. Education expanded dramatically: literacy campaigns, new schools and universities, and expanded teaching of girls. Public employment, in the bureaucracy, army, and state enterprises, provided jobs for the educated and bound them to the state. Social welfare programs, including subsidized food and housing, gave ordinary families benefits. Women's position changed unevenly: Iraq's 1959 personal status law, South Yemen's 1974 family law, and the opening of education and employment in Syria, Iraq, and Libya improved women's opportunities, even as political rights remained limited for everyone and conservative social norms persisted.

Economic resources differed greatly and shaped what regimes could do. Iraq and Libya, with oil revenues that grew sharply in the 1970s, could finance large programs and a large state. Syria had modest oil and substantial foreign aid, and balanced between a state sector and a revived private sector. The Yemens depended on remittances and foreign aid, and Sudan on agriculture and external support. Wealth did not determine political form, but it influenced the choices open to rulers. The oil states could afford to distribute benefits widely, while poorer regimes relied more heavily on patronage, foreign sponsors, and coercion.

Everyday Life and Social History

Living under Revolution: Villages, Schools, Neighborhoods, and Workplaces

The history of coups and party congresses can obscure what revolutionary change meant to ordinary people. In the Syrian and Iraqi countryside, land reform altered relations between tenants and landlords, though redistribution was often slow, and peasants faced new obligations to cooperatives and state agencies. Families whose sons entered the military academy or the party apparatus gained access to salaries, housing, and status, and a village with a school, clinic, or road was transformed in ways that mattered more than debates about doctrine. In cities, public employment and subsidized food became part of household budgets, and neighborhoods such as Baghdad's new housing districts gave migrants from the countryside a foothold in urban life.

For girls and women, new schools and universities offered education that earlier generations of their families had been denied. Women entered teaching, medicine, clerical work, and, in some cases, party and union organizations. Family law changes in Iraq and South Yemen gave women stronger legal protection, but the regimes did not fundamentally alter patriarchal family structures, and women's political participation remained limited and controlled. Social change was also experienced as loss. Merchants, landowners, and religious notables saw their property or influence diminish. Minority communities saw both opportunity and risk: Jewish Iraqis and Italian Libyans, among others, lost their property or left, Kurdish villagers were relocated, and southern Sudanese suffered the consequences of war.

Daily life under these regimes also involved fear and caution. In Syria and Iraq, ordinary people learned to avoid political topics in public, and families with a member in prison or exile bore the consequences. Evidence for this experience comes from memoirs, oral histories, fiction, the reports of human rights organizations, and later testimony. Because much of it was produced after the fact and by people who left or opposed the regimes, historians read it with care, and balance it against the accounts of those who benefited from the state.

Division V — Comparative Institutions and Synthesis · Section 64.20

The Promise and Contradictions of Arab Revolutionary Republicanism

Why did coups become frequent? The explanations that historians and political scientists have offered are best understood as complementary. One concerns institutions: the successor states of colonial empires inherited armies stronger than their parties and parliaments, and civilian institutions, which had not been tested by long experience, lacked the legitimacy to resist military intervention. A second concerns social change: the expansion of education and the army's recruitment from provincial and middle-class families created groups that were ambitious and organized, and whose route to power through legal politics was blocked by narrow elites. A third concerns ideology: Arab nationalism, anti-imperialism, and socialism provided a language that officers could use to justify intervention as national salvation. A fourth concerns external factors: defeat in Palestine, the rivalry of Egypt and Saudi Arabia, and Cold War competition gave coups wider significance and sometimes outside support. A fifth concerns contagion: each successful coup provided a model, and each failure of civilian government confirmed the argument for officers' intervention. No one of these explanations is sufficient, and their relative weight varied from case to case.

Why did some regimes collapse quickly and others endure? Za'im lasted months, Qasim four and a half years, and the first Iraqi Ba'ath government less than a year, while Assad and the Iraqi Ba'ath governed for decades. Durable regimes shared several features. They built institutions, meaning a party and security system, that linked the leadership to the army and society. They managed the army, preventing rival officers from mobilizing. They had resources, whether foreign aid or oil, with which to reward supporters. They reached accommodation with some groups while suppressing others, and they learned from the failures of predecessors. The regimes that collapsed tended to rest on a single leader's balancing of factions, as with Qasim, or on a narrow coalition that could not hold, as with the first Ba'athist governments of 1963. The two durable Ba'athist systems also illustrate that a shared ideology did not produce a single model, since Syria and Iraq developed rival systems with distinctive features.

What did the social reforms accomplish? The evidence shows both substantial achievement and significant limits. Land reform weakened landed elites and gave many rural families security, though implementation varied. Education, public health, and public employment expanded dramatically, and literacy rose. Nationalization strengthened state capacity, though its economic efficiency was often poor and it created large bureaucracies. State control of economies also gave regimes tools for patronage and control. The people who benefited most often included those closest to the party and the army, and not always the poorest. In some cases, as in South Yemen, social transformation was profound; in others, as in North Yemen, much of the old social order survived.

Why did political participation remain restricted? The regimes declared themselves the representatives of the people, and treated opposition as treason. Their leaders gained power through conspiracy and feared the same fate, and they invested in security systems that suppressed dissent. Revolutionary ideologies that stressed unity and the struggle against enemies justified restrictions on pluralism. Oil rents and foreign aid reduced the need to bargain with taxpayers. And the regimes' success in building loyal constituencies gave them a base that did not need open competition. Sudan's October Revolution of 1964, and the repeated mass protests that followed, showed that societies were not passive, but without durable civilian institutions, popular mobilization could not easily be translated into stable government.

The contrast between the revolutionary republics and states that kept parliamentary or monarchical arrangements is also instructive. Lebanon, Jordan, and the Gulf monarchies, which later chapters examine, were not immune to coups or unrest, and some had their own forms of authoritarianism, but they followed different paths, in part because they had different social bases and different sources of external support. The revolutionary republics' language of popular sovereignty and social justice shaped Arab politics well beyond their borders, and for a time seemed to many Arabs the wave of the future. Chapter 65 turns to the monarchies and the oil economies, Chapter 66 to the wars of 1967 and 1973 that tested all of these regimes, and later chapters to the political conflicts that arose from the structures built in these years.

Historical Debate

Why Did Officers Take Power? Modernizers, Middle Class, or Praetorians

The question: Were the officers who seized power in the Arab republics agents of social modernization, representatives of an emerging middle class, or simply an armed group pursuing institutional and personal interests?

Interpretation A — Officers as modernizers and class representatives: Some scholars, particularly in the 1960s, argued that officers from lower-middle-class and provincial backgrounds brought social change, since they were trained in modern organization and opposed to landed oligarchies, and that their regimes reflected the interests of an expanding middle class.
Interpretation B — Officers as a praetorian interest group: Other scholars argue that the army, once in power, acted chiefly to secure its own position and that of its patrons, and that reforms were means of building support. They emphasize the weakness of civilian institutions, the competition among officers, and the extent to which regimes became personal and security-based rather than class-based.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on biographical studies of officers' social origins, analyses of land and economic policy, and records of regime behavior. Interpretation A relies on the social background and on the genuine reforms enacted. Interpretation B relies on the persistence of repression, the failure to build lasting representative institutions, and the narrowing of ruling groups.

Why it matters: If officers were modernizers, their regimes can be assessed by the success of their reforms. If they were praetorians, their rule must be understood primarily as a problem of civil-military relations. Most recent scholarship combines both, recognizing that officers had social bases and corporate interests at once, and that the balance shifted over time and between countries.

Student task: Choose two of the countries in this chapter and assess which interpretation better fits each, identifying the specific evidence that supports your judgment and the evidence that would challenge it.

Historical Debate

Sect, Region, Class, and Party in Syrian and Iraqi Politics

The question: How much of the political outcome in Syria and Iraq should be explained by the religious, regional, or tribal identities of the people who held power, and how much by class, ideology, institutions, and the contingencies of struggle?

Interpretation A — Communal and regional networks as primary: Some analysts emphasize that regimes drew their core supporters and security personnel from particular communities or regions, such as Alawites in Syria or Tikritis in Iraq, and that communal solidarity (asabiyya) explains the cohesion of ruling groups.
Interpretation B — Institutions, class, and ideology as primary: Others argue that communal origin is only one factor, that rulers purged members of their own communities and recruited from others, that rural and lower-middle-class origins mattered as much as sect, and that the party and army as institutions created loyalty.

Evidence: Studies of officer origins, party membership, and appointments; the history of intra-communal rivalries; and the testimony of participants. Evidence is imperfect, since many records are closed and sources are often partisan.

Why it matters: A purely communal explanation risks treating whole communities as political blocs and ignoring the diversity of their members. A purely institutional explanation can overlook the way trust and kinship operated in conspiratorial politics. Most historians use both.

Multiple Perspectives

Land Reform and Revolution: Perspectives from Syria and Iraq

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns historians have identified. They are not quotations from named individuals.

A tenant cultivator in southern Iraq or the Syrian countryside: Land redistribution promised relief from dependence on a landlord, though delays, bureaucratic demands, and new obligations to cooperatives often limited the gains.
A landowner or tribal shaykh: The reforms meant the loss of estates, status, and political influence, and were experienced as confiscation by a regime whose officers had no legitimate claim to authority.
A party official or young officer from a rural family: The reforms fulfilled a program of social justice and created a base of support, while removing the class that had excluded his family from power.
A Damascene or Aleppine merchant: Nationalizations and state control threatened the commercial economy, and many merchants withdrew capital or went into exile, while others adapted and prospered in the state's new economic opening.

These perspectives were not equal in power. The regimes had armies and security services, and the landowners did not. The purpose of presenting them together is to show that the same policy could be liberation, dispossession, and opportunity at once.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From Colonial and Monarchical States to Revolutionary Republics

What Changed

  • Monarchies and notable-led parliaments were replaced by republics governed by officers and parties
  • Landowning elites lost power, and rural and provincial families entered the political elite
  • States expanded their role in education, employment, and the economy
  • Security services and single-party institutions became central to government

What Stayed the Same

  • Political participation remained restricted to small groups
  • Tribal, regional, and religious networks remained important
  • Dependence on external patrons and resources persisted
  • Regional unevenness, including the marginalization of Kurds, southern Sudanese, and others, continued

Who experienced change most directly? Landowners and old notables lost most; rural and provincial families who joined the army, party, and bureaucracy gained most. Who experienced greater continuity? Many peasants, minorities, and the poor continued to live with limited political voice. Why the pattern matters: It shows that a change of rulers and a change in the social order are different things, and that revolutions can transform the elite without transforming the relationship between state and citizen.

Cause and Effect

The Iraqi Revolution of 14 July 1958

Long-term causes

A narrow monarchy tied to Britain, extreme rural inequality, an expanding educated middle class, army officers drawn from modest backgrounds, and the spread of Arab nationalism and leftist ideas.

Short-term causes

The Baghdad Pact, Suez, the formation of the UAR, Free Officers' organization, and the government's order to move troops toward Jordan.

Event and immediate effects

The army occupied Baghdad, the royal family and Nuri al-Said were killed, a republic was proclaimed, a provisional constitution was issued, and Iraq left the Baghdad Pact within months.

Long-term consequences

Land reform and expanded social programs, intense conflict among nationalists, communists, and Kurds, a decade of coups, and eventually the Ba'athist state.

Historical complexity: The revolution cannot be reduced to a single cause. Foreign policy, social inequality, army politics, and chance all contributed, and the event itself did not determine its outcome. The same coalition that overthrew the monarchy fell apart within months, and the revolution's legacy was claimed by several incompatible movements.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has asked why military coups and revolutionary movements became major routes to power in the postcolonial Arab world, and how the governments they created tried to transform society while concentrating authority. The answer begins with the fragility of the first independence regimes. In Syria, Iraq, Sudan, and elsewhere, parliaments dominated by notables and landowners could not absorb the demands of an expanding educated population, and armies that were among the few effective modern institutions became arbiters. The defeat in Palestine in 1948 discredited the old elites, and the example of Egypt's Free Officers showed what was possible.

Syria offers the earliest and most intricate case. The three coups of 1949 began an era of army politics. Shishakli's dictatorship, the parliamentary restoration, and the union with Egypt followed, and the Ba'ath Party, which had grown by recruiting students, teachers, and officers, took power in 1963 through its Military Committee. Factional conflict produced the radical coup of 1966, and Hafez al-Assad's Corrective Movement of 1970 created the durable system that combined party, army, security services, patronage, and a controlled economic opening.

Iraq's story was one of revolution followed by fragmentation. The revolution of July 1958 destroyed the monarchy with broad popular support and considerable violence. Qasim's land reform and social programs were substantial, but his personal rule, his conflicts with nationalists, communists, and Kurds, and the violence of 1959 left him isolated. The Ba'athist coup of February 1963 and the repression that followed gave way to the Arif governments, which failed to build lasting institutions. The Ba'ath returned in 1968 through a two-stage seizure, and under al-Bakr and Saddam Hussein it built a party and security state financed after 1972 by nationalized oil.

The Kurdish question in Iraq and the southern question in Sudan demonstrate that minority communities were independent political actors. The 1970 agreement and the 1972 Addis Ababa Agreement show that governments could make formal concessions, and the divergent outcomes, a renewed war and collapse in Iraq, and a decade of relative peace in Sudan, show that implementation, trust, and the balance of power determined the results.

The two Yemens illustrate the regional and social variety of republicanism. In the North a coup in 1962 produced a civil war between republicans and royalists, shaped by tribal alliances and by Egyptian and Saudi intervention, and ended with an accommodation that integrated tribal and royalist notables into a weak republic. In the South the National Liberation Front took power at British withdrawal in 1967 and moved sharply left in 1969, creating a one-party socialist state with deep social reforms and recurring internal violence.

Libya and Sudan provided different models. Qaddafi's coup of 1969 overthrew a monarchy enriched by oil, and the regime developed from an officers' republic into the Jamahiriya proclaimed in 1977, in which doctrine located power in the people while real authority remained concentrated. Sudan experienced a military government under Abboud, removed by the popular October Revolution of 1964, a troubled parliamentary restoration, and Nimeiri's coup of 1969, after which a leftist phase gave way to a presidential system, the Addis Ababa settlement, and shifting alliances.

Comparing these governments shows common institutions: armies, ruling parties or their equivalents, security services, constitutions that legitimized more than they limited, and programs of land reform, nationalization, education, and public employment. The programs changed the social structure by displacing landed elites and opening careers to provincial and rural families. They also created large states with great capacity to reward and punish. Resources mattered: oil states such as Iraq and Libya could afford extensive distribution, while poorer regimes relied more on patronage, foreign support, and coercion.

The chapter's answer to why some regimes lasted is that durable regimes combined institution-building, control of the army, access to resources, selective accommodation, and the lessons of earlier failures. Those that rested on a single leader's balancing, or on a narrow coalition, collapsed quickly. Sect, region, tribe, and party all mattered, and no one of them explains outcomes. Foreign powers supported, exploited, and sometimes shaped events, but they seldom created the conditions they used.

The chapter also insists on the distinction between coup and revolution. Some regimes, notably in Iraq, South Yemen, and Syria, changed the distribution of land, wealth, and power. Others changed rulers more than society. Even the more transformative regimes restricted political participation, and the gap between the proclaimed sovereignty of the people and the institutions that governed was a defining feature of the era.

These developments set the stage for what follows. The oil wealth that strengthened Iraq and Libya belongs to the broader story in Chapter 65. The wars of 1967 and 1973 tested and reshaped these regimes, as Chapter 66 explains. And the structures of party, army, and security built in these years would shape the conflicts, repressions, and uprisings treated in the final chapters of this book.

Key Terms

Coup d'état
The seizure of government by a small group, usually military, using the existing state apparatus.
Social revolution
A deep transformation of class structure, property relations, and political power, usually involving mass participation.
Praetorianism
A pattern in which the army regularly intervenes in politics because civilian institutions are weak.
Free Officers
Name used by clandestine groups of nationalist officers in Egypt, Iraq, and elsewhere, in imitation of Egypt's 1952 movement.
Ba'ath
"Resurrection": the party advocating Arab unity, freedom, and socialism, founded by Aflaq and Bitar.
Neo-Ba'ath
Term for the more radical, regionally based leadership that ruled Syria from 1966 to 1970.
Military Committee
Secret group of Syrian Ba'athist officers that organized the 1963 coup and ruled until 1970.
Corrective Movement
Name given to Assad's 1970 seizure of power, implying a correction of the revolution's course.
National Progressive Front
Syrian coalition of 1972 grouping the Ba'ath with smaller parties under Ba'athist leadership.
Revolutionary Command Council
Supreme body of officers, in Iraq, Libya, and Sudan, that exercised authority after a coup.
Sovereignty Council
Three-member body that exercised the powers of head of state in Iraq after 1958.
Land reform
Legal limitation of large holdings and redistribution of land to cultivators, adopted in several republics.
Nationalization
State takeover of private or foreign-owned enterprises, such as Iraq's oil company in 1972.
Mukhabarat
Arabic term for intelligence and security services.
Neo-patrimonialism
A system combining formal institutions with personal loyalty and the distribution of benefits by a ruler.
Rentier state
A state financed chiefly by external revenue such as oil, reducing its dependence on taxing citizens.
Imamate
Religious-political rule by an imam; in Yemen, the Zaydi Hamid al-Din dynasty.
Zaydism
A branch of Shi'a Islam dominant in northern Yemen's highlands and among its ruling elite.
Hashid and Bakil
The two major tribal confederations of northern Yemen.
Royalists and republicans
The two camps of the North Yemeni civil war, supporting the imam and the republic respectively.
Jamahiriya
Qaddafi's "state of the masses," proclaimed in 1977.
The Green Book
Qaddafi's three-part statement of his "Third Universal Theory," published 1975–1979.
People's committees
Local bodies created in Libya after 1973 to take over institutions in the name of the people.
Anyanya
Southern Sudanese guerrilla movement of the first civil war.
Addis Ababa Agreement
The 1972 settlement granting southern Sudan regional self-government.
Sudanese Socialist Union
Nimeiri's single party, created in 1972.
October Revolution (Sudan)
The 1964 civilian uprising that ended Abboud's military government.
Peshmerga
Kurdish armed fighters, "those who face death."
Autonomy
Self-government for a region or community within a larger state, short of independence.
Arabization (of Kirkuk)
State policy of altering the population balance of contested areas by settling Arabs and relocating others.
Plebiscite
A popular vote, often with a predetermined result, used to confirm a ruler or constitution.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because the republics it describes defined the political experience of hundreds of millions of Arabs for generations. The regimes that emerged between 1949 and 1979 changed who ruled, built large states, opened schools and careers to people who had been excluded, and then closed the avenues of political participation they had promised to widen. Understanding how this happened is necessary to understand why later struggles over politics, rights, and legitimacy took the forms they did.

The chapter also reveals larger processes. Colonial and imperial legacies produced armies stronger than parties. Social change created groups with ambition but no channel for it. Ideologies of unity and liberation justified intervention and, once in power, restriction. Oil changed the relationship between states and citizens. And external rivalries, from the Cold War to the contest between Cairo and Riyadh, magnified local conflicts without creating them.

The developments of this period shaped what came after. The party-army-security systems of Syria and Iraq influenced their later wars and crises. The unresolved Kurdish and southern Sudanese questions returned in later decades. The division of Yemen, the personalized system of Libya, and the weakness of Sudan's civilian institutions all left legacies that later chapters will trace. At the same time, the experience of October 1964 in Sudan is a reminder that societies retained the capacity to resist.

Finally, the chapter contributes to the textbook's larger argument that the Arab world was never a single political unit with a single destiny. Revolution was a widespread language, but it produced a range of institutions, from the party state of Syria to the tribal compromise of North Yemen to the experimental Jamahiriya of Libya. Students who can explain why these outcomes differed are better equipped to understand the contemporary Arab world as the product of specific histories, and not as a uniform or predetermined whole.
