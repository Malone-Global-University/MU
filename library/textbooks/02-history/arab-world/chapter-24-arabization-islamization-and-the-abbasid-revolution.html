Part IV — Conquest and the Umayyad Arab Empire
24

Arabization, Islamization, and the Abbasid Revolution

A History of the Arab World: From Ancient Crossroads to the Modern Arab World

Chapter Opening

By about the year 700, the letters of provincial governors were increasingly written in Arabic, the coins in circulation carried Arabic inscriptions, and the caliph in Damascus described his authority in the language of Islam. Yet the villagers who paid the taxes that supported this state, and the townspeople who supplied its scribes, merchants, and craftsmen, overwhelmingly spoke Aramaic, Greek, Coptic, Persian, Sogdian, Berber, and other languages, and most of them were not Muslims. The empire spoke one language in its offices and many in its streets. This chapter asks how that gap narrowed, how unevenly it narrowed, and what happened to a ruling order built on conquest as it did.

Chapter 23 followed the Marwanid caliphs, above all ʿAbd al-Malik, as they made the Umayyad state more centralized, more Arabic in its administration, and more openly Islamic in its public language. That chapter described change from the top down: coinage, chancery languages, monumental buildings, and claims to authority. This chapter looks beneath that state-level transformation. It asks who actually adopted Arabic, who converted to Islam, how converts were incorporated into a society whose original Muslim members were a conquering minority, where unequal status persisted, and why experiences differed so sharply from Syria to Egypt, from Iraq to Khurasan.

The chapter's first argument is that Arabization and Islamization were related but separate processes, each gradual and each regionally uneven. Neither conquest nor the Arabicization of government instantly made conquered populations Arab or Muslim. Arabic spread widely without conversion in some places, and Islam spread widely without Arabic in others. Iran became overwhelmingly Muslim over the following centuries but did not become Arabic-speaking; much of Syria and Mesopotamia eventually did both; Egypt made the change far more slowly than Syria. Any account that treats “Arab,” “Muslim,” and “subject of the caliph” as one category will misunderstand the period.

The second argument concerns consequences. As conversion expanded, the social order created by the conquests became harder to sustain. That order had assumed that Muslims were mostly Arab soldiers, paid from the revenues of a non-Muslim, non-Arab population. Growing numbers of non-Arab Muslims, provincial Arab settlers who felt excluded from the Syrian-centered regime, religious opposition movements, and regional elites all developed grievances. These grievances overlapped without being identical, and the groups holding them frequently disagreed with one another. There was no single anti-Umayyad movement waiting to unite.

The third argument concerns the revolution itself. The Abbasid Revolution of 747–750 succeeded because a carefully organized underground movement assembled several of these constituencies under deliberately broad claims of legitimacy, especially in Khurasan, in the eastern part of the Islamic world, at the moment when the Umayyad state was crippled by civil war and factional conflict. It did not succeed because “Persians” rose against “Arabs.” Arab settlers were central to it, and the Umayyad collapse cannot be understood without the civil war that began in 743.

The chapter draws on unusually varied evidence, and it will repeatedly ask what each kind can and cannot show. Papyri from Egypt and Palestine preserve tax demands, receipts, and administrative letters written in Greek, Coptic, and Arabic. Coins, and documents in Bactrian and Sogdian from the east, show local societies interacting with Arab officials. Arabic chronicles, especially al-Ṭabarī’s vast History, compiled around 900 from earlier narrators, preserve detailed accounts of revolt and politics, but most were shaped by the Abbasid victory. Syriac Christian chronicles offer non-Muslim perspectives. Biographical dictionaries reveal how scholars of non-Arab origin entered Muslim intellectual life.

The chapter follows a deliberate boundary. It explains why Umayyad society changed and why the Umayyads fell. It ends with the overthrow in 750. What the Abbasids built after winning, including the consolidation of the dynasty, Baghdad, the bureaucracy, and the court, belongs to Chapter 25. It completes Part IV: conquest (Chapter 19), conquest society (Chapter 20), political fracture (Chapter 21), the early Umayyad solution (Chapter 22), and Marwanid state-building (Chapter 23) lead here, to a society transformed enough to overthrow the dynasty that had governed it.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Distinguish administrative Arabicization, the broader spread of the Arabic language, and Islamization as separate historical processes with different chronologies.
  2. Compare patterns of linguistic and religious change in Syria, Mesopotamia, Egypt, Iran, and the Maghreb.
  3. Evaluate the evidence historians use to study conversion, and explain the limits of quantitative models.
  4. Explain why people converted to Islam without reducing the process to a single motive such as tax avoidance.
  5. Analyze the mawālī as a flexible category of clients, freed persons, and converts rather than a single oppressed class.
  6. Assess the tension between the religious ideal of a single Muslim community and the social privileges of the Arab conquest elite.
  7. Interpret the fiscal problems created by conversion, and the case of ʿUmar II, using evidence that varies by province and time.
  8. Contextualize Khurasan as a frontier society whose Arab settlers, Iranian populations, and converts shaped the revolution.
  9. Identify the different sources of opposition to the Umayyads and explain why they did not amount to a single movement.
  10. Trace the late Umayyad civil war, the Abbasid daʿwa, and the revolution of 747–750 in sequence.
  11. Evaluate competing explanations for why the Abbasid Revolution succeeded.
  12. Connect the revolution to what it changed and what it left unresolved, as a bridge to Part V.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why is it historically misleading to equate becoming Muslim with becoming Arab?
  2. What does the word “conversion” actually describe, and why do historians reconstruct it only indirectly?
  3. How did the position of non-Arab Muslims strain a society organized around an Arab conquering elite?
  4. Why did the same broad conditions produce very different outcomes in Syria, Egypt, Iraq, and Khurasan?
  5. What can reports about ʿUmar II and Khurasan tell us about fiscal practice, and what can they not tell us?
  6. Why is the “Persians against Arabs” explanation of the Abbasid Revolution inadequate?
  7. How did a movement with ambiguous claims to legitimacy hold together diverse and sometimes conflicting supporters?
  8. Was the Umayyad collapse caused more by long-term social change or by the short-term crisis of 743–747, and how might historians argue about this?

Historical Context

Chapter 19 traced the conquests that placed Syria, Iraq, Egypt, Iran, and parts of North Africa under the authority of the early caliphate, and Chapter 20 examined how conquerors lived in garrison cities, drew stipends from a register (dīwān), and governed populations that retained their own religious communities and much of their local administration. Chapter 21 explained how the First Fitna produced enduring divisions, including the Kharijite opposition and the loyalty to ʿAlī’s family that would later be called Shiʿism. Chapters 22 and 23 followed the Umayyad recovery under Muʿāwiya and its consolidation under the Marwanid line, above all ʿAbd al-Malik (r. 685–705), whose reforms Arabicized the language of record and gave the state a more explicitly Islamic public identity.

Students should carry forward several conditions from those chapters. The early Muslim community was a small ruling minority in most conquered territories. Its members were concentrated in garrison towns such as Kufa, Basra, and Fustat, or in Syria, where the army was linked to the ruling family. Political authority flowed from Damascus to governors who often controlled several provinces. Non-Muslim communities, whether Christian, Jewish, Zoroastrian, or others, largely governed their own internal affairs under treaties and customary arrangements, and paid taxes whose names and rules varied by place. This chapter does not retell those foundations; it asks what happened when these arrangements met a century of demographic, linguistic, and religious change.

One methodological caution applies throughout. The Abbasid victory of 750 shaped the memory of the Umayyads. Most surviving Arabic narratives were compiled under the Abbasids or later, by authors with reasons to portray Umayyad rule as illegitimate and Abbasid rule as the fulfillment of a long-prepared cause. Historians therefore read these narratives critically, comparing them with papyri, coins, Syriac and other non-Muslim sources, and archaeology wherever possible. Students should expect that many claims in this chapter rest on such later reporting and should note where the text says so.

Section 24.1

From an Arab-Muslim Ruling Minority to a Changing Society

Around the year 700, the state described in Chapter 23 looked more unified than the society it governed. Its ruling core was an Arab-Muslim elite: caliphal family members, tribal leaders, and the soldiers registered in the dīwān who drew stipends from tax revenue. Its language of record was becoming Arabic. Its coins and monuments proclaimed an Islamic order. But the populations from which the revenue came included Chalcedonian and Miaphysite Christians in Syria and Egypt, East and West Syriac Christians in Mesopotamia, Jews across the Near East, Zoroastrians on the Iranian plateau, and Buddhists, Manichaeans, and Christians in the eastern lands. Outside Arabia, Muslims were still a minority in most regions, concentrated in garrison cities and, in Syria, in the army.

Historians therefore find it helpful to think in terms of three processes that ran on different clocks. Political control was established quickly, in the conquests of the 630s–640s and their sequels. Arabization, the spread of Arabic as a spoken and written language and of Arab identity, took generations and depended on local circumstances. Islamization, the adoption of Islam by conquered populations, also took generations, and in many regions centuries. Because the three did not move together, the same year could find a province firmly ruled by Arab-Muslim officials, administered partly in Greek or Persian, and inhabited mainly by non-Muslims speaking other languages.

The chapter’s central problem arises from this mismatch. A regime designed for a small conquering minority had to adapt as a growing number of its subjects entered the Muslim community, some as clients of Arab patrons, others as members of communities that gradually took on Muslim identity. That adaptation was uneven, contested, and sometimes violent. It is the social background against which the political events of 743–750 must be read.

Section 24.2

What Does “Arabization” Mean?

Chapter 23 examined administrative Arabicization: the decision to record taxes, issue coins, and write official correspondence in Arabic instead of Greek or Middle Persian. That was a change in government practice, carried out by officials, and it says little by itself about what ordinary people spoke. The wider process this chapter calls Arabization operated through several distinct channels that historians must keep apart.

Arabic spread first as a language of power and commerce. Anyone dealing with tax collectors, soldiers, or the courts of the new order had reason to acquire it, and towns with Arab garrisons or markets served by Arab customers created incentives for learning it. It spread as a language of religion, since the Qurʾān was recited in Arabic and worship, law, and increasingly scholarship were conducted in it. It spread through households, as Arab men married local women and their children grew up bilingual. It spread through learning and literature, as scholars, secretaries, and poets, including many of non-Arab origin, adopted it as their medium. And it spread as an identity: individuals and families could claim Arab lineage through descent or through attachment to an Arab tribe, though such claims were open to dispute.

It is equally important to note what Arabization did not require. Arabic could spread among Christians and Jews without conversion, and in some regions the transition to Arabic was made by non-Muslim communities that continued their own liturgical languages. Conversion, conversely, did not immediately produce Arabic speakers: a Persian- or Sogdian-speaking convert could pray the required words in Arabic while continuing to speak the language of his household. Finally, Arabic was not entirely new in every region. Chapter 13 described Arabic-speaking populations in Syria, the Jazira, and along the Iraqi frontier before the conquests, and Chapter 6 noted the close relationship between Arabic and other Semitic languages, especially Aramaic. Arabization in the Fertile Crescent was partly the expansion of a language already familiar at its edges.

Historical Vocabulary
Arabization and Islamization

Arabization refers to the spread of the Arabic language, and in a looser sense Arab identity, among populations that did not previously speak it. It must be distinguished from Arabicization, the narrower term used in this textbook for the replacement of other languages by Arabic in government records (Chapter 23). Islamization refers to the gradual adoption of Islam by conquered populations, including individual conversion, communal change, and the growth of institutions that made Muslim identity durable.

The words matter because modern usage often runs them together. In ordinary speech “Arab” and “Muslim” are sometimes treated as near-synonyms. In the eighth century they were not: most Muslims of Iran were not Arabs, and many Arabic-speaking people were Christians. This textbook uses the terms separately so that students can ask, of any place and period, whether language, religion, both, or neither was changing.

Section 24.3

Regional Patterns of Linguistic Change

Syria and Mesopotamia. In Syria, Palestine, and the Jazira, the common vernaculars before the conquests were Aramaic dialects, with Greek as the language of the Byzantine administration and elite and with Arabic already present among frontier populations. Over the following centuries Arabic became the everyday language of most of the population, Christians included, though Syriac and Greek continued in church use and Aramaic survived in some localities. In southern Iraq, the landscapes described in Chapter 3 had supported dense Aramaic-speaking and Persian-influenced farming societies, and Arabic advanced first in the garrison cities of Kufa and Basra and then outward. The shift was slow and gradual; there is no date at which a province “became Arabic-speaking.”

Egypt. Egypt shows a more prolonged transition. Greek had been the language of administration and Coptic the language of much of the population and of the church. Papyri show Greek continuing in official use in the decades after 700, then increasingly paired with Arabic in bilingual documents, and eventually giving way. Coptic remained the spoken language of many villages for centuries, and only gradually retreated in the later medieval period. The pace differed between the Muslim administrative and urban world of Fustat, where Arabic spread earlier, and rural Upper Egypt, where it took longer.

Iran and Central Asia. Iran offers the essential counter-case. Most of its population eventually became Muslim, yet it did not become Arabic-speaking. Middle Persian gave way over time to New Persian, written in the Arabic script and enriched with Arabic vocabulary, and re-emerged as a literary language in the ninth and tenth centuries. In the northeast, Sogdian and Bactrian, known from documents discussed in Section 24.11, were gradually displaced by Persian rather than by Arabic. Islamization without Arabization is thus the standard outcome across a large part of the Islamic world, and it destroys any simple equation of the two.

The Maghreb. In North Africa, Amazigh (Berber) languages remained the vernaculars of much of the population, while Arabic spread through cities, religion, and administration. Many Amazigh converted to Islam, some rose to high military positions, and others rejected Umayyad governors in the Great Berber revolt of 740–742, discussed in Section 24.10. The larger transformation of the Maghreb, including its later Arabization, belongs to Chapter 32.

Section 24.4

What Does “Conversion” Mean? The Problem of Evidence

“Conversion” seems a simple word, but historians of the early Islamic world use it with care. A conversion could be a formal declaration of the profession of faith, made before an official or a patron. It could mean joining a Muslim community by entering a patron’s tribe or household, registering in the military dīwān, adopting a Muslim name, attending communal prayer, or abandoning practices of a previous faith. It could be an individual decision or a collective shift by a family, village, or estate. It could be sincere conviction, or a step taken for reasons discussed in the next section, and the two were not mutually exclusive. Above all, it was rarely a single moment. In most places it was a slow social process of affiliation, followed by generations of learning what being Muslim meant.

Evidence for it is correspondingly indirect. Medieval authors rarely recorded how many people converted or when. Historians have therefore tried to infer patterns. The best-known approach, developed by Richard Bulliet, uses the genealogical name chains preserved in Arabic biographical dictionaries, assuming that the names of converts’ ancestors show when a family turned from non-Muslim names to Muslim ones. From such data he proposed a bell-shaped (S-curve) model of conversion, in which adoption of Islam was slow at first, accelerated, then slowed as the remaining non-Muslim minority declined, with the largest movement occurring well after the Umayyad period.

This approach is influential but debated. Biographical dictionaries record mainly the scholarly and administrative elite, mostly urban and mostly male, and may not represent villagers. Naming habits did not always change in a straightforward way, and sources may record only some ancestors. Scholars therefore treat the models as suggestive rather than as exact measurements, while agreeing on their central conclusion: the great majority of conversions occurred after the fall of the Umayyads, and Muslim majorities in most conquered regions emerged over centuries, at different times in Iran, Iraq, Syria, and Egypt. Other evidence supplements the names, including papyri, Syriac chronicles recording apostasy and pressure, hagiographies, and archaeology of mosques and churches. None gives a complete count.

Section 24.5

Why People Converted

A popular explanation holds that people converted to escape taxes. The idea has a basis: in particular circumstances, and by some rules, non-Muslims owed a poll tax that Muslims did not, as Section 24.8 explains. But it fails as a general explanation, because land tax often continued to be owed regardless of religion, because being a Muslim brought obligations of its own, and because much conversion was slow and social rather than calculated. Historians instead see many overlapping motives, differing by person, class, and place.

Some people converted from religious conviction, drawn by the message of the Qurʾān and by the example of Muslim preachers and pious communities. Others did so through marriage: wives and children in mixed households often entered the Muslim community. Others converted through patronage, when attachment to an Arab patron or tribe as a client offered protection, employment, or a path into the army. Many converted in cities and garrisons, where Muslim institutions were most visible and where new arrivals could find a place in the emerging Muslim urban society. Registration in the military dīwān brought stipends, though as we will see registration for non-Arabs was often disputed. Enslaved people, captured in war or acquired through trade, were often brought into Muslim households and, when freed, remained attached to their former owners as clients. Ambitious secretaries, scholars, and merchants found that Muslim identity opened doors in the administration and learned professions.

There were also reasons not to convert. Christian, Jewish, and Zoroastrian communities had strong institutions, priests, courts, and family networks, and local elites often saw little reason to abandon them. Landholders in Iran and Egypt may have preferred to keep their existing standing with their communities and their tax arrangements. Conversion could disrupt family ties, and Islamic law treated apostasy from Islam as a serious offense. The result was not a single movement but a patchwork of decisions, differing across generations and provinces.

Section 24.6

The Mawālī and the Problem of Incorporation

The social category at the center of this problem is the mawlā (plural mawālī). The word derives from the notion of walāʾ, a bond of patronage, and could mean “client,” “patron,” or “freed person,” depending on context. Chapter 23 introduced it briefly. Here we must examine it more closely, because it was through clientage that the early Muslim order incorporated non-Arabs.

Several kinds of people were mawālī. Some were freed slaves who remained attached to their former owners; some were captives or their descendants; and many were free non-Arabs who converted and attached themselves to an Arab tribe or individual, since the conquest society had no direct way to admit a non-Arab into an Arab tribe other than through such a tie. The status was flexible and varied by region, era, and individual. A mawlā could be a peasant, a soldier, a merchant, a scribe, or a celebrated scholar. Important early legal and religious scholars, such as Nāfiʿ, freedman of Ibn ʿUmar, and Ḥasan al-Baṣrī and Muḥammad ibn Sīrīn, either were mawālī or were the sons of mawālī. Some mawālī rose to military command, as did Mūsā ibn Nuṣayr in North Africa, a client of the Umayyad house. The category therefore cannot be treated as a unified oppressed class.

Yet the tie itself created a hierarchy. A mawlā’s status derived from his patron, not from his own lineage, and in an Arab tribal society, where descent conferred honor, this dependence could be humiliating. Reports, preserved in later sources, describe mawālī receiving lower pay or none, serving as infantry beside Arab cavalry, being excluded from prestigious marriages, and being denied social precedence. Some of these reports reflect later polemic, but their persistence indicates real friction. As the number of non-Arab Muslims grew, especially in Iraq and the east, the arrangement became harder to maintain. A category designed to absorb a few converts was being asked to manage many.

Historical Vocabulary
Mawlā / Mawālī

A mawlā (plural mawālī) was a person tied to another by walāʾ, the bond of clientage. The term covered freed slaves and their descendants, non-Arab converts attached to an Arab patron or tribe, and clients of families or rulers. The Umayyad caliphs themselves had mawālī. The word was also used in the reverse sense, for the patron.

Students should not translate mawlā simply as “non-Arab Muslim” or “convert,” which would erase the many freed persons and clients, or as “slave,” which it was not. Nor should they treat mawālī as a single class: a scholar in Basra and a peasant-soldier in Khurasan shared a legal category but very different lives. Its importance for this chapter lies in the pressure that the growing number of clients placed on an order built around Arab descent.

Section 24.7

Muslim Equality and Social Hierarchy

The Qurʾān teaches that human beings were made into peoples and tribes so that they might know one another, and that the most honored before God is the most pious. This principle, together with the ritual equality of believers in prayer, gave Islam a universalizing message that could attract converts of every origin. The tension examined here is not simply that “Islam preached equality but the Umayyads were prejudiced.” The historical question is more specific: how did a conquest society, organized partly around Arab military settlement and tribal precedence, adjust as Islam ceased to be confined mainly to the conquerors?

The conquest society had built several privileges into its institutions. The dīwān ranked registered Muslims by tribe and by priority in the community and paid stipends accordingly. Arab lineage carried social prestige, celebrated in poetry and preserved through genealogy. Military command and provincial office were held mostly by Arab tribal leaders and Umayyad kin. None of this was a formal doctrine of Arab supremacy, and rulers and governors sometimes promoted mawālī. But it created a structure in which an Arab Muslim usually held higher status than a non-Arab Muslim of equal piety.

Rulers had incentives to preserve this structure: the Arab military, particularly the Syrian army, was the regime’s principal instrument of power, and its stipends were financed from provincial revenue. Reforming the hierarchy risked alienating those on whom the regime relied. But leaving it in place created its own problems, since non-Arab Muslims were an increasing share of the community whose unity the caliphs proclaimed. The result was inconsistency, with a patchwork of practices differing by governor, province, and moment, rather than a coherent policy in either direction.

Section 24.8

Taxation, Conversion, and the Fiscal Problem

Taxation is the point at which conversion touched the state’s finances most directly. Chapters 20 and 22 stressed that the vocabulary of early Islamic taxation was fluid. Two terms appear repeatedly in later sources: jizya, which in its developed form was a poll tax on non-Muslim adult males, and kharāj, which came to mean a land tax. In the seventh and early eighth centuries, however, these words were used loosely, sometimes for tribute in general, and administration differed by region: papyri from Egypt show a poll-tax on non-Muslim men alongside land taxes and payments in kind, while in Iraq and Iran the arrangements built on Sasanian practice, and local intermediaries such as the dihqāns collected revenue. Students should avoid projecting the later, standardized categories back onto the Umayyad period.

The fiscal difficulty was structural. Muslim soldiers received stipends financed from the tax revenue paid by non-Muslim subjects. If non-Muslims converted, and if conversion removed the personal tax and created a claim to a stipend, the state faced a double loss. Governors under pressure to meet revenue targets, or to pay armies, therefore had reasons to limit or resist the fiscal consequences of conversion. Reports in later sources attribute such policies to particular governors. For example, the Iraqi governor al-Ḥajjāj is reported to have sent peasant converts back to their villages and to have taxed them, which would have discouraged the movement of villagers into garrison cities. Such reports are difficult to verify and may exaggerate, but they indicate the sort of measures governors were thought to have taken.

It would be wrong, however, to conclude that the Umayyad state had a single, empire-wide policy of discouraging conversion. The evidence points to variation by province, governor, and time, and to a range of motives. Governments also increased burdens on non-Muslim and Muslim peasants alike through censuses and surveys, as under Hishām, whose reign saw efforts to improve revenue collection in several provinces. The fiscal problem was real, but it was managed piecemeal, and its outcome depended on local conditions.

Section 24.9

ʿUmar II and the Problem of New Muslims, 717–720

The reign of ʿUmar ibn ʿAbd al-ʿAzīz (ʿUmar II, r. 717–720) offers a compact case study. He ruled for only about three years, but later tradition remembered him as an exceptionally pious and just ruler, and later writers tended to attribute to him reforms they admired. This means that reports about him need to be read with special caution: they combine possible historical policy with idealized memory.

The sources associate him with several measures relevant to this chapter. He is said to have ordered that recognized Muslim converts not be charged the poll tax, and to have moved toward paying converts a stipend under the same terms as others. In the east, sources describe complaints from converts in Khurasan that they were paying jizya despite their conversion and that mawālī were fighting without pay. Later tradition also credits him with restrictions on non-Muslims, though the attribution of such measures to him is disputed by historians and often reflects later conventions.

What can historians safely conclude? The reports establish that fiscal treatment of converts was a live and contested issue by the early eighth century, that Khurasan was a place where the issue was acute, and that some governors and caliphs took different positions. They do not by themselves establish that ʿUmar II abolished a system, or that his policies were consistently enforced during and after his reign. Tradition reports that some of his measures were relaxed under his successors, but the extent is uncertain. The value of the evidence lies less in proving a universal system than in revealing a contested fiscal practice and the arguments used about it.

Section 24.10

Provincial Worlds: Syria, Egypt, Iraq, and the East

The “Umayyad Empire” was never a single society. Understanding the crisis of the 740s requires treating regional difference as a causal factor, because grievances arose from the different positions of each region in the regime.

Syria

Syria was the regime’s military and dynastic core. Its army, organized in districts (ajnād) and dominated by Arab tribal groups long associated with the Umayyads, was the caliphs’ most reliable force, and its officers benefited from the connection. Syria’s population, however, remained largely Christian, and the region retained much of its late-antique administrative and religious character, as Chapters 13 and 22 described. Syrian tribal politics were divided between groups often labeled Qays and Yaman, a rivalry that later flared in the civil war.

Egypt

Egypt offers exceptionally rich documentary evidence through papyri. Letters from the governor Qurra ibn Sharīk (governor c. 709–714) to the local official at Aphrodito, and the tax and labor demands recorded there, show a provincial administration requisitioning workers and supplies for projects including mosque construction and the fleet, and the villagers’ responses, including flight to escape obligations. Later evidence records tax surveys, pressure on monastic communities, revolts by Coptic Christians in the Delta in the 720s and 730s, and settlement of Arab tribesmen in the eastern Delta under Hishām. Egypt’s administrative language moved from Greek toward Arabic around and after 700 while Coptic persisted in the countryside. Christians remained a majority for generations, and the fiscal and religious pressures faced by Egyptian villagers, and their experience of a state that was increasingly Arabic and Muslim in character, were distinct from those of Arab tribesmen in Syria or Iraq.

Iraq

Iraq had been the center of the First Fitna’s conflicts and remained a center of opposition. Its garrison cities, Kufa and Basra, contained rival Arab tribal groups and growing numbers of mawālī, and its countryside supported Aramaic- and Persian-speaking cultivators. To control it, the Umayyads installed a Syrian garrison at the new city of Wāsiṭ, founded by al-Ḥajjāj around 702, which many Iraqis resented. Iraq saw repeated revolts, including the large revolt of Ibn al-Ashʿath (700–701), which drew together Arab settlers and non-Arab supporters, Kharijite risings, and the failed revolt of Zayd ibn ʿAlī in Kufa in 740. Iraq was also the base from which the Abbasid movement organized its network.

The West and the East

In the Maghreb, Berber Muslims resented discriminatory treatment by Umayyad governors and were attracted to Kharijite ideas of egalitarian piety; their revolt of 740–742 defeated a Syrian expedition and ended direct Umayyad control over much of the region, an event examined further in Chapter 32. In the east, Khurasan and Transoxiana combined a frontier army of Arab settlers with converts and local elites, and their circumstances are the subject of the next section. Together these regions show how varied the strains on the Umayyad order were.

Section 24.11

Khurasan: A Society Made on the Frontier

Khurasan, the “land of the sunrise,” designated the northeastern territories of the former Sasanian Empire, including the cities of Marv (Merv), Nishapur, Herat, and Balkh, and stretched toward Transoxiana beyond the Oxus. It became the site of an Arab settler society that differed sharply from the garrison cities of Iraq. Traditional accounts say that in the 670s the governor Ziyād ibn Abīhi sent tens of thousands of Arab families from Basra and Kufa to settle there; the numbers are traditional and uncertain, but the movement of substantial numbers is not doubted. Unlike the garrison cities, many settlers came to live in villages and among the local population, and by the early eighth century Marv had a large Arab community.

This society was complex. The settlers were divided into tribal groups, including Tamīm, Azd, and Rabīʿa, and their rivalries followed the broader Qays–Yaman and other divisions of Arab politics. They lived alongside Iranian landholding elites (dihqāns), Sogdian merchants, local rulers in Bukhara, Samarkand, and Tokharistan, and religious communities including Zoroastrians, Buddhists, Manichaeans, and Christians of the Church of the East. Intermarriage, cultural exchange, and the adoption of local languages and customs by some settlers, and of Arabic and Islam by some locals, produced a distinctly Khurasanian society. Documents in Bactrian and Sogdian, such as the archive associated with Mount Mugh and its ruler Dēwāshtīch of Panjikent, executed around 722, show local rulers negotiating and sometimes fighting with Arab officials.

The frontier was also a source of tension. Wars in Transoxiana, especially the campaigns under Qutayba ibn Muslim (c. 705–715), extended Muslim control but were costly, and the Turgesh Turks inflicted a serious defeat on Umayyad forces in the “Defile” battle of 731. Governors of Khurasan were often appointed from Iraq or Syria and did not always share settlers’ interests, and disputes over pay, booty, and tax burdens were frequent. Later reports describe a governor around the 720s trying to encourage Sogdians to convert by promising relief from taxes, then reimposing them when revenue fell, producing resentment among new Muslims. Such reports are not fully verified, but they suggest how the fiscal problem played out on the frontier.

It is essential to avoid reducing the region’s later revolutionary role to “Persians versus Arabs.” Encyclopaedia Iranica and modern scholarship stress that dissatisfied Arab settlers were central to the movement that emerged from Khurasan; they had their own grievances, chiefly tribal rivalry, unequal access to office, and dislike of governors from outside. Converts and local elites had different grievances. That these groups could act together, at least for a time, depended on the conditions of the 740s.

Everyday Life and Social History

Ordinary Lives in a Changing Order

For most people, the transformations described in this chapter were felt as changes in the demands of the tax collector, the language of official letters, the identity of the local landlord, or the religion of a neighbor. The Egyptian papyri give some of the clearest views: farmers and craftsmen were assessed for taxes, called for labor on public works, and sometimes fled their villages. Villagers in Iraq and Khurasan, where documents are scarcer, experienced the same pressures through tax intermediaries such as the dihqāns and, in Iraq, by the movement of peasants into and out of cities.

Conversion changed daily life in small steps: a new name for a child, a household adopting Muslim prayer and dietary practice, a village building a mosque beside a church or fire temple, a daughter marrying into a Muslim family. In Muslim households, as in others, women’s work in agriculture, textiles, and domestic labor is only sparsely documented, since most sources were written by men in the administration, the army, or the scholarly class. Slavery was part of the picture, and enslaved people who were freed became the clients whose place this chapter has examined. Historians must therefore read court histories and chronicles for the experiences they mostly leave out, and supplement them with papyri, coins, archaeology, and comparison.

Section 24.12

Why the Umayyads Faced Opposition

It would be easy to write this section as a list of the regime’s faults. Historians instead ask which groups opposed it, on what grounds, and why their opposition took different forms. The answer is that several kinds of grievance existed side by side.

Iraqi resentment of Syrian predominance. Iraq’s Arab settlers saw a regime that relied on Syrian troops and Syrian officers, and had stationed a Syrian garrison in their midst. Iraqi elites felt excluded from the center of power. Tribal competition overlapped with this: Qays–Yaman and other rivalries shaped who received offices and stipends and repeatedly turned political disputes into tribal feuds, in Syria and Khurasan as well as Iraq.

Kharijite opposition (Chapter 21) rejected the legitimacy of rulers who, in their view, failed to live by Qurʾānic standards, and it attracted Arabs and non-Arabs, including many Berbers. Alid and broader pro–Ahl al-Bayt loyalties drew on the memory of Ḥusayn’s death at Karbalāʾ in 680 and looked for leadership from Muhammad’s family; they had appeared in the revolt of al-Mukhtār in the 680s, in Zayd ibn ʿAlī’s failed rising in 740, and in the activities of his son Yaḥyā in Khurasan in 743. Fiscal grievances affected non-Muslim cultivators and converts, and provincial autonomy, in the Maghreb and the east, weakened the center’s control. Pious critics, meanwhile, condemned the caliphs’ worldliness, while dynastic disputes within the Marwanid house divided the ruling family.

These constituencies disagreed with one another. Kharijites rejected the Alid claim; Arab tribal factions fought each other; converts and Arab settlers wanted different things from the state. For decades the regime had managed such opposition through a mixture of force, patronage, and the loyalty of its Syrian army. What changed in the 740s was that the regime’s own unity broke down, and the movement that eventually replaced it was one that could speak to many discontents without committing to any one of them.

Section 24.13

The Late Umayyad Crisis, 743–747

Hishām died in 743 after a long reign that had been relatively stable, despite provincial troubles. The succession that followed exposed the fragility of the dynasty. His successor al-Walīd II (r. 743–744) alienated important groups within the Umayyad family and the Syrian army, and was assassinated in April 744. His killers installed Yazīd III, who promised reforms and was associated with the Yaman faction and with more reformist religious currents, but who died after a few months. A brief succession by Ibrāhīm ibn al-Walīd followed, until Marwān ibn Muḥammad, the governor of Armenia and the northern frontier, marched south with troops from the Jazira, defeated Ibrāhīm’s forces in December 744, and took the caliphate as Marwān II. Historians call the resulting series of civil wars the “Third Fitna,” after the earlier great conflicts of the first century of Islam.

Marwān II was a capable commander. He moved his seat from Damascus to Ḥarrān in the Jazira, where his core of support lay, and spent much of 744–746 suppressing revolts in Syria itself, at Homs, in Palestine, and elsewhere. Meanwhile Iraq was in turmoil. A Kharijite leader, al-Ḍaḥḥāk ibn Qays al-Shaybānī, seized Kufa in 745 and was killed in 746, and a descendant of ʿAlī’s family, ʿAbd Allāh ibn Muʿāwiya, raised a revolt in Kufa in 744–745 and later held parts of Iran before his defeat, around 747. Marwān’s forces defeated these rebels but at great cost.

In Khurasan the crisis took the form of a collapse of Arab unity. The governor Naṣr ibn Sayyār faced rivals among the Arab settlers, including the Azdī leader al-Kirmānī, the rebel al-Ḥārith ibn Surayj, and Kharijite groups, and the Arab community of Marv was fighting itself. Marwān was too preoccupied to help. The result was that in the years just before 747 the most militarized Arab population in the east was divided against itself, while a secret organization prepared to offer an alternative. Historians such as those in the Encyclopaedia Iranica treat this civil-war crisis as a major condition for the first stage of the Abbasid Revolution. The revolution was not the cause of Umayyad weakness, but the exploitation of it.

Section 24.14

The Abbasid Daʿwa and the Hāshimiyya

The movement that would overthrow the Umayyads emerged from a family that, on its face, had no obvious claim to rule. The Abbasids descended from al-ʿAbbās, an uncle of Muhammad, and belonged to the same clan, the Banū Hāshim, as the Prophet and ʿAlī. In the early eighth century the family lived on an estate at al-Ḥumayma in southern Jordan, a site whose remains have been explored by archaeologists, and from there its head, Muḥammad ibn ʿAlī, directed a network of missionaries. Traditional accounts say that he inherited leadership of an earlier movement, the Hāshimiyya, through a bequest from Abū Hāshim ibn Muḥammad ibn al-Ḥanafiyya, a son of an Alid, who died around 717. This story is preserved in Abbasid-era sources and is contested; historians debate whether such a bequest occurred and, if so, what it meant.

The organization, known as the daʿwa, was based in Kufa and sent agents to Khurasan. Sources name leading organizers, among them Bukayr ibn Māhān in Kufa and, in Khurasan, Sulaymān ibn Kathīr al-Khuzāʿī, Lāhiz ibn Qurayẓ, and Qaḥṭaba ibn Shabīb al-Ṭāʾī, and describe twelve nuqabāʾ (representatives) directing local cells. Early activity in Khurasan was dangerous: a leading agent, Khidāsh, is reported to have been executed by the governor around 736–737. After Muḥammad ibn ʿAlī’s death in 743 leadership passed to his son Ibrāhīm, known as al-Imām.

The movement’s appeal was carefully open. Its slogan, “the chosen one from the family of Muhammad,” did not name a particular candidate, and this allowed people of different expectations to support it. Alids, who hoped for a descendant of ʿAlī, could read it one way; those who wanted a member of the wider Hāshimite house, another; those who hoped for a just ruler, a third. Some supporters may have held messianic hopes, and later traditions attached apocalyptic significance to black banners from the east. Historians must be cautious with the sources here, since accounts written after 750 present the movement as more unambiguously Abbasid from the outset than it may have been. Scholarly discussion, including in studies of the political thought of the period, stresses precisely this ambiguity.

Historical Vocabulary
Daʿwa, Hāshimiyya, and al-Riḍā min Āl Muḥammad

Daʿwa means “call” or “invitation” and refers to an organized mission that summons people to a cause. In this chapter it names the underground network that spread the Abbasid message. Hāshimiyya refers to the movement claiming the leadership of the Banū Hāshim, Muhammad’s clan; its precise origins and early doctrines are contested, and the name later came to describe the Abbasid revolutionary movement.

Al-riḍā min Āl Muḥammad (“the chosen one from the family of Muhammad”) was the movement’s deliberately open slogan. Because it named no candidate, it could appeal to Alids, other supporters of the Prophet’s family, and those seeking a just ruler. Students should not treat the slogan as evidence that the leaders had secretly intended an Abbasid caliphate from the beginning, nor as evidence that they did not. It reveals how a movement can hold a coalition together by leaving important questions unanswered.

Section 24.15

Abū Muslim and the Khurasanian Coalition

Abū Muslim, the organizer sent to Khurasan by Ibrāhīm al-Imām in about 746–747, is one of the most famous and least understood figures of the period. His origins are obscure. The sources disagree about his birthplace, ancestry, and status, and some depict him as a mawlā of the Abbasid family; his later prominence led to legends. It would therefore be a mistake to make him a simple Persian nationalist. His revolutionary army was drawn from many groups, including Arab settlers of several tribes, non-Arab Muslims, and local villagers, and the leaders who commanded its armies, such as Qaḥṭaba ibn Shabīb al-Ṭāʾī and Sulaymān ibn Kathīr al-Khuzāʿī, were Arabs.

By many accounts, Abū Muslim organized supporters in the villages around Marv, using the divisions among the Arab factions to avoid confrontation until his forces were ready. The movement drew in Arab settlers who resented the governor and their rivals, converts who hoped for fairer treatment, and villagers with local grievances. Sources indicate that its soldiers came to be identified by their region, as Khurasanians, rather than by the tribal affiliations that had divided the settlers, an important symbolic shift, though later narrative may exaggerate its novelty. This shared identity helped the coalition function despite its diversity.

Section 24.16

The Black Banners and Revolution, 747–749

In June 747 (Ramaḍān 129 AH), Abū Muslim raised the black banners at a village near Marv. Black became the movement’s color, and its use is one of the best-attested features of the revolution. By December his forces had entered Marv itself, and Naṣr ibn Sayyār, abandoned by the quarrelling settlers and unable to obtain help from Marwān II, fled westward; he died in 748. Abū Muslim had exploited the enmity between Naṣr and his rival, al-Kirmānī’s son ʿAlī, and defeated both.

The army then advanced west under Qaḥṭaba. It defeated Umayyad forces at Jurjan and at Nihawand in 748 and pressed toward Iraq, while the Umayyad governor of Iraq, Yazīd ibn ʿUmar ibn Hubayra, held Wāsiṭ. Qaḥṭaba was killed in a battle near the Euphrates in 749, and his son al-Ḥasan led the army into Kufa in the autumn of that year. In Kufa, Abū Salama, a leader of the movement, is reported to have hesitated about the succession, and sources debate whether some leaders considered an Alid candidate. Ibrāhīm al-Imām had died in prison in 749, reportedly after Marwān II arrested him. His brother Abū al-ʿAbbās was presented to the people of Kufa and proclaimed caliph in the mosque, traditionally on 28 November 749.

Why did the coalition hold long enough to succeed? Several factors worked together. The regime was divided and its armies were committed elsewhere. The Khurasanian movement had a shared enemy, and its slogan let diverse supporters imagine different outcomes. Its leaders offered concrete inducements, including pay for soldiers. And its momentum, once established, attracted further defections. But this is a story of a coalition assembled quickly, and the tensions inside it would surface later, in the period Chapter 25 examines.

Section 24.17

The Fall of Marwān II and the End of Umayyad Rule, 750

Abū al-ʿAbbās’s proclamation gave the revolution a caliph, and Marwān II moved to meet it. The decisive battle took place on 25 January 750 beside the Greater Zab, a tributary of the Tigris in northern Iraq. Abbasid forces under ʿAbd Allāh ibn ʿAlī, an uncle of Abū al-ʿAbbās, defeated Marwān’s army. Sources give very large figures for the Umayyad force, which historians treat with skepticism, but agree on the outcome: Marwān’s army broke and he fled.

He retreated through northern Iraq and Syria, but found little support. Damascus fell to Abbasid forces in April 750. Marwān continued through Palestine into Egypt, pursued by Abbasid commanders, and was killed at Būṣīr in Egypt in August 750. Accounts, including Muslim and Christian sources, also describe the pursuit and killing of members of the Umayyad family, and later traditions tell of violence against Umayyad tombs. One young Umayyad prince, ʿAbd al-Raḥmān, escaped and eventually reached the far west, an episode with major consequences described in Chapter 32.

The Umayyad caliphate had ended. Here this chapter stops. The reign of Abū al-ʿAbbās, the consolidation under his successor, the fate of the revolution’s leaders, the building of a new capital, and the transformation of the state belong to Chapter 25.

Cause and Effect

The Fall of the Umayyads

Long-term causes

A conquest society built for an Arab-Muslim minority, growing conversion among non-Arabs, unequal status for clients, fiscal strain, regional resentments toward Syrian predominance, and long-standing Kharijite and pro–Ahl al-Bayt opposition.

Short-term causes

The succession crisis after 743, assassination of al-Walīd II, the Third Fitna, Arab factional warfare in Khurasan, and the diversion of Marwān II’s forces to Syria and Iraq.

Event or transformation

An organized underground movement, offering a broad claim of legitimacy, raised an army in Khurasan and defeated the last Umayyad caliph of Damascus.

Immediate effects

The end of the Umayyad caliphate, the proclamation of Abū al-ʿAbbās, killings of Umayyad family members, and the escape of a prince to the far west.

Long-term consequences

A dynasty with strong eastern support, a shift of political weight from Syria toward Iraq and the east, and the start of a period in which non-Arab Muslims took a fuller part in public life (Chapters 25 and 29).

Historical complexity

No single cause explains the outcome. Social change did not itself topple the dynasty, and the civil war alone would not have produced a replacement without a prepared organization. The revolution combined both.
Historical Debate

Why Did the Abbasid Revolution Succeed?

The question: What social forces and conditions explain the overthrow of the Umayyads? Was the revolution chiefly an ethnic uprising of Persians and converts against Arab rule, or something more complicated?

Interpretation A — A reaction of non-Arabs against Arab rule: Influential older accounts, often associated with nineteenth-century Orientalist scholars such as Gerlof van Vloten and, with important differences, Julius Wellhausen, emphasized the grievances of mawālī and Iranian Muslims against an Arab ruling class and treated the revolution as their triumph, sometimes with a religious dimension in Shiʿi loyalty.
Interpretation B — A movement of Khurasanian Arabs and a coalition: Later scholarship, notably M. A. Shaban, argued that Arab settlers of Khurasan were the movement’s principal supporters and that mawālī played a smaller role than the older view held. Other historians, including Moshe Sharon, Elton Daniel, and Hugh Kennedy, have emphasized different elements: the religious and messianic appeal of the Hāshimite cause, Khurasan’s regional identity, the organization of the daʿwa, and the late-Umayyad civil war. Many now see a coalition, in which Arabs and non-Arabs, and different motives, combined.

Evidence: The interpretations rely on the same narrative sources, composed largely after the Abbasid victory, together with lists of participants and their origins, later tradition about Abū Muslim, coins, and comparison with other regions. They differ in how much they trust Abbasid-era narratives, how they count Arab and non-Arab supporters, and how they weigh religious ideology against social and political grievance.

Why it matters: If the revolution were essentially ethnic, the Abbasid state might be seen as a Persian restoration, an assumption that later chapters will test. If it were a coalition led by Khurasanian Arabs and shaped by the civil war, it looks more like a dynastic change made possible by a fractured elite, with non-Arabs benefiting over time. The debate also shows how the choice of sources shapes historical explanation.

Student task: Evaluate which interpretation better accounts for the evidence, identify what additional evidence, such as documentary sources from Khurasan, would help, and explain why the two positions might both contain part of the truth.

Section 24.18

What Did the Abbasid Revolution Change, and What Did It Not Yet Change?

The revolution overthrew the Umayyad dynasty and elevated a movement whose strength lay heavily in the east. It shifted political weight away from Syria and toward Iraq and Khurasan, and it brought to the center of power groups, including Khurasanian troops and many non-Arab Muslims, who had been on the margins. It also changed the language of legitimacy. Where the Umayyads had ruled through the memory of the conquests and the caliphate’s religious office, the Abbasids claimed kinship with the Prophet’s family, a claim with resonance across many of the constituencies described here.

But the revolution did not instantly abolish hierarchy, regional conflict, or the political culture of the Arab elite. Arabization and Islamization continued, unevenly, for centuries, and most subjects of the new caliphs were still not Muslim in 750. The problem of incorporating non-Arab Muslims did not vanish, and the sense of Arab privilege was contested rather than eliminated; later debates over Arab and non-Arab merit, and over the place of Persian, belong to the story that Chapters 27, 28, and 29 will tell. The coalition’s internal tensions, including those between Khurasanian leaders, Arab tribal elites, and those who had hoped for an Alid caliph, were unresolved. The Umayyad legacy also survived in the far west, and the Maghreb had already slipped from caliphal control.

The boundary of this chapter is therefore deliberate. The consolidation of the new dynasty, the foundation of Baghdad in 762, the development of the bureaucracy and court culture, and the changing imperial ideology belong to Chapter 25. Scholarly overviews likewise separate the revolution of 747–750 from the subsequent process by which Abbasid rule was actually established.

Continuity and Change Over Time

A Provincial Society in Syria or Iraq, c. 700 and c. 750

What Changed

  • Arabic was firmly established as the language of official records and was more widely used in towns, markets, and religious life
  • The number of Muslims, including non-Arab converts and clients, had grown, and mosques and Muslim institutions were more visible
  • Political loyalties had fractured through civil war, and Umayyad legitimacy had weakened
  • New groups, including the Khurasanian army, had gained political weight

What Stayed the Same

  • Most of the population still worked the land, paid taxes, and lived in villages
  • Christians, Jews, and others remained a large part of the population, with their own communal institutions
  • Tribal identity and genealogy remained important to the Arab elite
  • The state still relied on non-Muslim and local administrators and on inherited fiscal arrangements

Who experienced change most directly? Urban Muslims, clients seeking status, soldiers, and secretaries experienced the most direct shifts. Who experienced continuity? Village cultivators, whose obligations changed less than the names of their rulers. Why the pattern matters: It shows that a dramatic political revolution and a slow social transformation ran on different timetables.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that Arabization and Islamization were related but distinct, gradual, and regionally uneven processes, and that the social consequences of conquest, working over about a century, help explain why the Umayyad dynasty fell. Around 700 the caliphate was governed by an Arab-Muslim elite and increasingly administered in Arabic, but most of its subjects were neither Arabic-speaking nor Muslim. Political control, language, and religion moved on different clocks.

Arabization worked through commerce, religion, household, learning, and identity as well as through the administrative Arabicization examined in Chapter 23. It spread among non-Muslims and did not require conversion, and conversion did not require a change of language. The regional contrasts were sharp: Syria and Mesopotamia eventually became predominantly Arabic-speaking, Egypt changed more slowly and Coptic persisted for centuries, Iran became Muslim but not Arabic-speaking, and Berber languages remained widespread in the Maghreb.

Conversion is hard to study because it was usually a gradual social process and because sources record it only indirectly. Quantitative models based on naming patterns are suggestive but limited. Historians agree that most conversion occurred after the Umayyad period. Motives were many, including conviction, marriage, patronage, urban and military opportunity, enslavement and manumission, and, in particular circumstances, tax status. Popular explanations reducing conversion to tax avoidance are inadequate.

The mawālī were clients, freed persons, and converts attached to Arab patrons. They were a flexible category, not a single oppressed class, and included celebrated scholars and military commanders. But clientage created dependence in a society that honored Arab descent, and the growing numbers of non-Arab Muslims strained an order built for a conquering minority. The Qurʾānic principle that piety, not lineage, confers honor stood in tension with institutions that ranked Muslims by tribe and priority.

Taxation intensified this tension. Terminology for jizya and kharāj was fluid, and conversion threatened revenue that paid the army. Governors handled the problem piecemeal, and evidence from Egypt, Iraq, and Khurasan shows variation, not a uniform empire-wide policy. The case of ʿUmar II, known largely through later and idealizing reports, demonstrates that the treatment of converts was contested, but it cannot show what was consistently enforced.

Regional difference was a cause of the crisis, not merely background. Syria was the regime’s military core, Egypt a heavily taxed Christian majority society documented in papyri, Iraq a center of repeated opposition, and the Maghreb a region in revolt. Khurasan was a frontier society in which Arab settlers, Iranian elites, and converts lived together and in which factional strife and fiscal grievance were acute. Opposition to the Umayyads came from Iraqi resentment, tribal rivalry, Kharijites, pro–Ahl al-Bayt supporters, fiscally burdened groups, and provincial elites, and these groups often disagreed with one another.

The late Umayyad crisis of 743–747 broke the dynasty’s unity and divided the Arab community of Khurasan. Into that opening stepped the Abbasid daʿwa, an organization of the family of al-ʿAbbās that used the open slogan “the chosen one from the family of Muhammad” to gather supporters with different hopes. Abū Muslim, of obscure origins, organized a coalition of Arab settlers, converts, and villagers around Marv; the black banners were raised in 747, Kufa fell in 749, Abū al-ʿAbbās was proclaimed caliph, and Marwān II was defeated at the Greater Zab in January 750 and killed in Egypt in August.

The chapter has also emphasized historiography. Older accounts saw a Persian revolt against Arabs; more recent work stresses Khurasanian Arab settlers, the organization of the daʿwa, the ambiguity of its claim, and the civil war. The revolution changed the dynasty and the center of political gravity, but it did not immediately abolish hierarchy or complete Arabization and Islamization. Those developments, and the state the Abbasids built, are the subject of Part V.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows that the early Islamic empire was made not only by armies and caliphs but by slow social processes: people learning a language, joining a religion, negotiating their status, and paying or resisting taxes. Political revolutions are visible; the transformations beneath them often are not. By separating Arabization from Islamization, the chapter provides a tool students will use throughout the rest of the textbook, from the Persianate world of the Abbasid east to the Arabic-speaking Christian and Jewish communities of later centuries.

The chapter also reveals a recurring pattern in the history of the region: an order built on one set of assumptions is strained when social reality outgrows them. The conquest society assumed that Muslims were mostly conquerors; conversion broke that assumption. The problem of incorporating new members into a community that claimed to be universal, while preserving the privileges of its founders, reappears, in different forms, in the Abbasid, Ottoman, and modern periods.

Finally, the chapter connects regions that are often studied separately. Egyptian papyri, Iraqi garrison cities, Syrian armies, Khurasanian villages, and Berber revolts belong to one story, and the revolution that ended the Umayyads drew on all of them. It shifted political weight eastward and prepared the ground for the cosmopolitan Abbasid world, the subject of Part V. But it did not finish the process of Arabization and Islamization, which continued for centuries and shaped the identities of the modern Arab world in ways that no single dynasty could determine.
