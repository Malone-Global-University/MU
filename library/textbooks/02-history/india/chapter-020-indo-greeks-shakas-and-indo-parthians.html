Indo-Greeks, Shakas, and Indo-Parthians

Indian History: South Asia from the Indus Civilization to the Present

Chapter Opening

Imagine holding a silver coin struck in Gandhara in the second century BCE. One face shows a diademed royal portrait ringed by a Greek legend naming a king and calling him a savior. The other shows Athena, a Greek goddess of war, beside a legend in Kharoshthi script and a Prakrit language of South Asia, giving the same royal name and title again. The object is small, but it contains a whole political world: a ruler addressing Greek-speaking soldiers and settlers and South Asian subjects and merchants at the same time, on a single piece of metal, in two languages and two scripts.

Chapter 19 traced the political regionalization of the Gangetic heartland after Mauryan power collapsed, showing that South Asia had become a multipolar world of competing states rather than a single imperial system. This chapter shifts the center of gravity decisively toward the northwest: the Kabul valley, Gandhara, the Punjab, and the lower Indus, together with the mountain routes leading northward across the Hindu Kush toward Bactria and westward toward the Iranian plateau. Between roughly the third century BCE and the first century CE (with the Western Kshatrapa branch continuing through the second century CE), this region was ruled by a succession of transregional powers whose histories students usually meet under three labels: the Indo-Greeks, the Shakas (including the Western Kshatrapas), and the Indo-Parthians.

Older narratives sometimes present these rulers as successive waves of "foreign invaders" who fell upon an India that had been left exposed by Mauryan decline. This chapter argues for a different framing. The northwest was not a peripheral zone periodically overrun by outsiders. It was a historically connected region linking the Indus basin, Afghanistan, the Iranian world, Central Asia, and the Hellenistic world, and its rulers, soldiers, merchants, religious communities, languages, and artistic traditions crossed political boundaries constantly. The Indo-Greek, Shaka, and Indo-Parthian kingdoms emerged within that connected frontier. Their importance lies both in the states they founded and in the durable networks of exchange they helped to intensify.

A conceptual point runs through the entire chapter: identity is not the same thing as political geography. A ruler with a Greek name and Greek royal titles could govern South Asian subjects, issue bilingual coinage, patronize Buddhist institutions, and belong politically to a kingdom centered south of the Hindu Kush. A Shaka ruler did not remain culturally identical to steppe populations merely because later sources called him a Shaka. An Indo-Parthian king was neither simply "Indian" nor simply "Parthian." These mixed political identities are exactly what make the northwest historically important, and they resist the modern habit of sorting the ancient world into fixed national or ethnic categories, a habit Chapter 1 warned against.

The evidence for this world is distinctive and often frustrating. Very few narrative histories written by the Indo-Greeks, Shakas, or Indo-Parthians survive. Greek and Latin authors, Chinese envoys and chroniclers, later Indian literary traditions, and Buddhist texts mention these kingdoms, but usually briefly, at a distance, and with their own purposes. The richest evidence is material: coins in enormous quantity, inscriptions in Kharoshthi, Brahmi, Greek, and Sanskrit, and archaeological remains from sites such as Taxila and Ai Khanoum. This chapter therefore doubles as a lesson in how historians reconstruct political history from numismatic and epigraphic evidence when narrative sources are scarce.

The chapter is organized around the assigned sections. Section 20.1 establishes the Greco-Bactrian background north of the Hindu Kush. Sections 20.2 through 20.4 examine the Indo-Greek kingdoms, the exceptionally visible king Menander, and the ways coinage expressed kingship. Sections 20.5 through 20.7 turn to the Shakas, the Western Kshatrapas, and Indo-Parthian rule. Section 20.8 treats Gandhara as a multilingual, multireligious cultural zone, and Section 20.9 draws the argument together by presenting the northwest as a crossroads seen from many directions. Two boundaries should be kept in mind. The detailed history of the Yuezhi and the formation of the Kushan Empire belongs to Chapter 21, and the sustained contest between the Satavahanas and the Western Kshatrapas belongs to Chapter 22. This chapter explains the political and commercial world that made the Kushans possible, but it stops before telling their story.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain the origins of the Greco-Bactrian kingdom and why the Hindu Kush functioned as a passable frontier rather than a wall between Bactria and South Asia.
  2. Describe the political plurality of the Indo-Greek kingdoms and explain why their chronology and relationships among rulers remain uncertain.
  3. Distinguish the historically attested king Menander from the literary figure Milinda in later Buddhist tradition.
  4. Analyze coins as simultaneous evidence of money, political communication, sovereignty, and exchange, and identify their limitations as historical sources.
  5. Explain the term "Shaka," the movements of Shaka-linked groups, and why simple domino models of migration are inadequate.
  6. Describe the political role of the Kshatrapa title and the significance of the Western Kshatrapas in western Indian regional and commercial history.
  7. Assess the evidence for Indo-Parthian rule, and separate the historical Gondophares from later religious tradition.
  8. Characterize Gandhara as a multilingual, multireligious, transregional cultural zone in the period before mature Kushan-era art.
  9. Compare Indo-Greek, Shaka, and Indo-Parthian political formations and explain what changed and what persisted when ruling houses changed.
  10. Evaluate why the label "foreign invaders" obscures the historical processes at work in northwestern South Asia.

Historical Context

Three earlier developments frame this chapter. First, Chapter 14 showed that northwestern South Asia had long been integrated into the wider Iranian and Hellenistic worlds. Gandhara and the Indus region belonged to the Achaemenid Empire, and administrative practices, scripts, and trade connections from that period, including the Aramaic script from which Kharoshthi developed, outlasted Achaemenid rule. Alexander's campaign in the fourth century BCE then introduced Macedonian and Greek settlers, soldiers, and administrators into the region, whatever the brevity of his own presence.

Second, Chapters 15 through 18 traced the Mauryan Empire, whose rulers incorporated the northwest after Chandragupta's agreement with Seleucus I. Ashoka's edicts in Greek and Aramaic at Kandahar and in Kharoshthi at sites in the northwest confirm that the empire governed Greek-speaking and Aramaic-using populations there. Mauryan authority did not vanish overnight, but as Chapter 18 explained, provincial autonomy and fiscal and military limits eroded imperial control. Chapter 19 then showed a Gangetic and central Indian landscape of regional powers.

Third, the Hellenistic world that followed Alexander fragmented into rival kingdoms. The Seleucid Empire controlled Iran and, initially, Bactria. As Seleucid attention turned westward toward Syria and rivalries with Ptolemaic Egypt, its eastern provinces gained room for independent action. This background explains why a Greek-ruled state could appear in Bactria and later extend south. It also explains why the rulers of northwestern South Asia were closely connected to Iran and Central Asia rather than isolated within a "Mauryan aftermath."

Historians face acute source problems in this period. No Indo-Greek royal chronicle survives. The most important evidence is numismatic: thousands of coins with royal names, titles, and images. Inscriptions in Kharoshthi, Brahmi, Greek, and later Sanskrit add dated or datable donations and dedications. Greek and Roman authors, especially Polybius, Strabo, Justin, and Plutarch, and the Chinese Shiji and Hanshu provide outside glimpses. The Pali Milindapanha and Indian sources such as the Yuga Purana and Patanjali's grammatical commentary preserve later or indirect memories. Archaeological excavation at Ai Khanoum in northern Afghanistan and Taxila in the Punjab region provides a physical setting. Because these types of evidence are uneven, dates in this chapter are often approximate, and sequences of rulers are sometimes reconstructions.

Modern borders require special care here. The relevant territories lie today in Afghanistan, Pakistan, India, Uzbekistan, Tajikistan, Turkmenistan, and Iran. None of these states existed in the period examined. Terms such as Bactria, Gandhara, Arachosia, Sistan or Sakastan, the Punjab, and Sindh are used because they are historically appropriate, and modern national labels for ancient regions, such as calling Gandhara "ancient Pakistan," are avoided, as Chapter 1 advised.

Section 20.1

The Greco-Bactrian World

Bactria lies north of the Hindu Kush, in the plains and river valleys of northern Afghanistan and adjoining parts of Central Asia, watered by the Amu Darya (the ancient Oxus) and its tributaries. It was a rich agricultural and pastoral region with a long history of urban settlement, incorporated into the Achaemenid Empire and then into Alexander's conquests. Alexander's successors, and particularly the Seleucids, inherited an eastern territory populated by Iranian-speaking local communities and by Greek and Macedonian settlers and soldiers. Bactria's governors were thus positioned at the far eastern edge of the Hellenistic world, distant from the royal court in Syria.

Ancient authors, notably Justin's later summary of an earlier Latin history, describe a revolt in the mid-third century BCE in which the Greek governor Diodotus made Bactria independent. The exact date, and the relationship between the first and second rulers named Diodotus, remain debated. Diodotus's successor Euthydemus and his son Demetrius I consolidated a state that the Seleucid king Antiochus III challenged directly. Polybius reports that Antiochus besieged Bactra for two years in the last decade of the third century BCE before accepting terms with Euthydemus, and that he then crossed the Hindu Kush and renewed friendship with an Indian king named Subhagasena, collecting elephants and gifts. This is a rare narrative glimpse of routes and diplomacy connecting Bactria to the mountains and plains south of the range, and it comes from an outsider's account of a Seleucid campaign.

Material evidence complements the texts. At Ai Khanoum, a city at the confluence of the Amu Darya and the Kokcha rivers in northeastern Afghanistan, French excavations from the 1960s to 1970s uncovered a gymnasium, a theater, a palace complex with a large treasury, and a hero-shrine, alongside evidence that Greek-language culture and local Central Asian and Near Eastern building traditions existed side by side. An inscription there preserves a set of maxims associated with Delphi that a man named Clearchus had, by his own account, copied and set up, a striking sign that Greek intellectual life was being consciously maintained thousands of kilometers from the Aegean. Ai Khanoum was destroyed around 145 BCE, probably amid the political and nomadic disruptions that destabilized the Greco-Bactrian kingdom; Greek rule elsewhere in Bactria continued for roughly another decade or more, with Heliocles I conventionally regarded as the last Greek king there (c. 130 BCE). Section 20.5 returns to these disruptions.

What matters for this chapter is that Bactria's Greek kings did not remain confined north of the mountains. The Hindu Kush is a formidable barrier, but it is not a civilizational wall. Routes across its passes and along adjoining valleys connected Bactria with the Kabul valley and Gandhara. Chinese envoy Zhang Qian, who visited Bactria (Daxia) around 128 BCE, reported in the Shiji that he saw goods there that merchants said came by way of a country to the southeast usually identified with India. Whatever the details, the report suggests that commercial links across these mountains were already active. The same routes allowed Greek rulers to extend authority to Arachosia (around Kandahar, where Ashoka had earlier set up inscriptions in Greek) and to the Kabul valley and Gandhara.

Political instability in Bactria helps explain southward expansion. Justin reports rivalry between Eucratides and Demetrius, and other sources suggest that different branches of the Greek royal house competed for territory. Eucratides, ruling in the middle of the second century BCE, issued impressive coinage, including an unusually large gold piece, and his conflicts appear to have divided Greek power between rival lines. This chapter deliberately does not turn into a history of Hellenistic Central Asia. What it takes from Bactria is the explanation for why Greek-ruled states could appear in northwestern South Asia: a mobile military elite, connected to Central Asian economies, with rivalries at home and opportunities to the south.

Section 20.2

Indo-Greek Kingdoms

Greek-ruled states south of the Hindu Kush are conventionally called Indo-Greek, a modern term. Ancient South Asian authors called the Greeks Yavanas (Yonas in Prakrit), and Greek coin legends style rulers as basileis, kings. The movement into Arachosia, the Kabul valley, Gandhara, and the Punjab is generally associated with Demetrius I and his contemporaries in the early second century BCE. Some of the coins of Demetrius depict him wearing an elephant-scalp headdress, an image that recalls earlier Hellenistic royal portraiture and that some scholars connect to claims of conquest in South Asia, though the specific meaning remains interpreted rather than documented.

The essential point about the Indo-Greeks is their plurality. There was no single "Indo-Greek Empire" with a neat succession of rulers. Coins preserve dozens of royal names, including Agathocles, Pantaleon, Apollodotus I, Antimachus, Menander I, Zoilus, Lysias, Antialcidas, Philoxenus, Strato I, Hippostratus, and Hermaeus. Some of these ruled simultaneously in different regions, some were relatives, some were rivals, and a few appear to have controlled only small territories. Female royal figures are visible too: coins of Strato I were issued in the name of his mother Agathokleia, who appears to have acted as regent. Because such information comes primarily from coins and only secondarily from literary accounts, the order and relationships of the kings are reconstructed through techniques such as tracing overstruck coins, mapping hoards and finds, and comparing monograms and styles.

Historical Vocabulary
Yavana

Yavana (in Prakrit, Yona) is the South Asian word for Greeks. It derives ultimately from the name of the Ionians, which reached Indian languages through Old Persian usage. Chapters 14 and 16 encountered the Yonas in Ashoka's edicts as a population living in the northwestern frontier of his empire. In later Sanskrit and Prakrit texts and inscriptions, the term broadened, and in some contexts came to refer to peoples of the western world more generally, including Romans and others. Students should read the word according to its date and context rather than assume it names only the Indo-Greek rulers.

The word matters because it shows how South Asian observers classified outsiders and neighbors. It should not be treated as a hostile label. Inscriptions in the western Deccan, for instance, record Yavana donors to Buddhist cave sites, indicating that people identified as Yavanas took part in local religious and commercial life, a theme returned to in Chapters 22 and 25.

Geography helps explain the political picture. Greek settlements and mints were concentrated where routes and irrigated agriculture converged: the Kabul valley and Kapisa, Pushkalavati in the Peshawar basin, Taxila, and Sagala in the Punjab. Taxila offers an especially informative case. The Bhir Mound settlement had earlier served as the region's principal city under Achaemenid and Mauryan rule. In the second century BCE a new city, Sirkap, was laid out on a more regular plan across the Tamra stream, with straight streets, fortification walls, and blocks of houses and shops. Its founding is often attributed to the Indo-Greek period, although the excavated city continued to be occupied and rebuilt under Shaka, Indo-Parthian, and Kushan rulers, and archaeologists have interpreted its layers with caution.

Indian textual sources preserve indirect memories of Greek military activity. Patanjali's grammatical commentary, composed in the second century BCE according to common dating, uses an example sentence about a Yavana besieging Saketa and Madhyamika, which suggests that Greek forces were within the recollection of Gangetic writers. The Yuga Purana, a prophetic text of uncertain date, describes Yavana invasions in more dramatic terms. Such sources belong to a specific literary and moral setting rather than straightforward chronicle. Similarly, Strabo, drawing on an earlier writer, credited Greek rulers with conquering more of India than Alexander had, and reported a Greek advance reaching the region beyond the Beas. These texts are best treated as evidence that Greek power was a real presence and a subject of discussion, not as reliable maps of its limits.

The chronology of the Indo-Greeks extends from around 180 BCE to about the turn of the era, with Greek rule surviving unevenly: it disappeared from the Paropamisadae and Kabul-region sphere earlier (Hermaeus is conventionally placed around 70 BCE), while continuing in parts of the eastern Punjab until around the beginning of the Common Era, although scholars still debate the late sequence. Their decline was gradual and uneven. Some regions passed to Shaka rulers in the late second and first centuries BCE, discussed in Section 20.5, while others remained under Greek kings for longer. The fact that coin types with Greek royal names continued to be produced by later rulers indicates that Greek royal imagery retained prestige after Greek political power had waned. Chapter 21 picks up this transition as the Kushans emerged.

Section 20.3

Menander

Menander I is the best-known Indo-Greek king because he is visible in more kinds of evidence than any other. He ruled sometime in the mid-second century BCE (modern estimates range from about 165 to 130 BCE). His coins, discussed in detail in Section 20.4, are found across a wide area of the northwest and the Punjab, and are unusually abundant, which suggests a long or prosperous reign. Strabo reports, drawing on an earlier account, that Menander crossed the Hypanis (Beas) and advanced eastward. A controversial Kharoshthi inscription on the Shinkot/Bajaur reliquary, accepted as genuine by some specialists but questioned by others, appears to date a relic deposit to the reign of King Menander, which, if accepted, would imply that his rule extended into the hill regions north of Gandhara. The first-century CE Greek sailing guide known as the Periplus of the Erythraean Sea reports that at Barygaza, on the western coast, old drachmas inscribed in Greek with the names of Apollodotus and Menander were still in circulation, a testimony to the durability of Indo-Greek coinage as trade money long after these kings had died.

Menander also appears in the Greek author Plutarch, who tells a story that after Menander died in camp, several cities competed to keep his ashes and eventually divided them, an outcome Plutarch compares to the dividing of relics. Modern historians have noticed the resemblance to the Buddhist tradition of dividing the Buddha's relics, although Plutarch does not make that connection explicit, and the story comes from a moral essay rather than a biography. It shows that Menander's memory as a just and popular ruler had reached Greek-speaking literary circles, but it cannot be treated as a documented account of his death and funeral.

The most famous Menander is the literary one. The Pali text Milindapanha ("The Questions of Milinda") presents a dialogue between King Milinda, ruling at Sagala, and the Buddhist monk Nagasena, in which the king poses sharp philosophical questions about the self, rebirth, and karma, and is persuaded. Milinda is generally identified with Menander. But the text's history is layered. The Pali version that survives is a compilation that appears to have been expanded over time, and a Chinese translation of a shorter form exists, suggesting that the work developed in stages. Historians therefore separate two questions: what do contemporary sources show about Menander, and how did later Buddhist tradition remember him? The second question is illuminating in its own right. That Buddhist authors chose a Greek king as the intelligent interlocutor of a great monk shows that Yavana rulers had become part of Buddhist historical memory, and that Greek culture was thought to embody serious philosophical debate.

A number of pitfalls follow. Students should not state that Menander "converted to Buddhism" as though it were biography. What can be said is that a Greek king of Gandhara issued coinage in a form intelligible to local subjects, was remembered in Buddhist literature, and reigned in an environment where Buddhist monasteries were significant institutions. Whether he was personally a lay follower, a patron, a diplomatically inclusive king, or simply a ruler later adopted by Buddhist storytellers is exactly the question addressed in the Historical Debate below.

Historical Debate

Was Menander a Buddhist?

The question: Should Menander I be understood as a Buddhist king, as a Greek ruler who patronized or accommodated Buddhism, or as a historical figure later absorbed into Buddhist tradition without clear evidence of personal belief?

Interpretation A — Menander as a Buddhist or Buddhist-sympathizing king: Supporters note the Milindapanha, Plutarch's story of the division of Menander's ashes, an eight-spoked wheel on certain issues that some interpret as the Buddhist wheel of the law, and a reliquary inscription mentioning his reign. (The Kharoshthi title dhramika, sometimes cited in older scholarship, belongs to coinage of a later king, Menander II, and is not evidence for Menander I.) Together, they argue, these point to genuine personal or dynastic affiliation with Buddhism.
Interpretation B — Menander as a patron or a figure of memory: Skeptics respond that the Milindapanha is a later and composite text, that Plutarch does not mention Buddhism, that the wheel's Buddhist interpretation is debated and it could be a general symbol of royal authority or kingship, that his standard coin legend simply calls him king and savior, that the reliquary inscription is itself contested, and that his coinage prominently uses Greek deities such as Athena. On this view, Menander was a king who governed a Buddhist-populated region, accommodated its institutions, and was later adopted into Buddhist storytelling.

Evidence: Both interpretations rely on the same small body of coins, one inscription, and literary references. They differ in how much weight they give to a late Pali text and to interpretive readings of symbols.

Why it matters: The answer affects how historians understand Greek participation in South Asian religion and how kingship worked in a plural environment. A balanced position accepts that Menander operated within a Buddhist-influenced society and was remembered by Buddhists, while recognizing that firm claims about his personal faith exceed the evidence. The example also illustrates a wider methodological point: literary memory and contemporary evidence answer different historical questions.

Student task: List the evidence for Menander's relationship with Buddhism and sort it into contemporary and later sources. Then explain which parts of the case are strongest and which additional evidence would most improve confidence.

Section 20.4

Coins and Kingship

Indo-Greek coinage is the backbone of this chapter's evidence, and it repays close study. A coin is simultaneously money, a political statement, a symbol of sovereignty, evidence of exchange, and an archaeological object. Rulers chose what to put on their coins. Portraits, titles, deities, animals, and languages were meant to be read by the people who used the coins, and by rivals and neighbors. Greco-Bactrian rulers north of the Hindu Kush generally retained the Attic weight standard familiar across the Hellenistic world, while Indo-Greek rulers south of the mountains increasingly issued bilingual silver on the lighter so-called Indian standard (Menander I's common bilingual drachms weigh roughly 2.4 g), alongside bronze and copper denominations adapted to regional monetary practice, often with different legends and images.

Language and script are central. Coins in the Kabul valley and Gandhara frequently pair a Greek legend on one face with a legend in Kharoshthi on the other, giving the same royal name and title in a Prakrit language. Some issues of Agathocles and Pantaleon carry legends in Brahmi, showing that rulers with a presence farther east employed the script used in the Gangetic world. The titles are informative: Greek basileus (king), soter (savior), dikaios (just), nikephoros (victorious), and aniketos (unconquered) appear beside Kharoshthi forms such as maharaja, tratara (savior), and, on some later issues of a different king, dhramika. Rulers used the vocabulary of Hellenistic monarchy while adapting it into forms familiar in South Asia. Coin imagery also blends traditions: Zeus, Athena, and Heracles appear beside elephants, humped bulls, and, on some issues, deities and symbols with South Asian associations. Some coins of Agathocles are thought to depict Indian deities identified by scholars as forms associated with Vasudeva-Krishna and Balarama, although such identifications are debated.

Numismatics is also a story of discovery. In the 1830s, scholars including James Prinsep and Christian Lassen used bilingual Greek and Kharoshthi coin legends, where the Greek could be read, to decipher the Kharoshthi script. Work on Brahmi overlapped with this, and Prinsep's decisive Brahmi decipherment, using Ashokan inscriptions, culminated around 1837. Thus Indo-Greek coins were not only evidence for this chapter's history; they helped modern scholars unlock the writing of early South Asia.

Historical Vocabulary
Kharoshthi and Gandhari

Kharoshthi is a script written from right to left, developed from the Aramaic alphabet used in the Achaemenid administration of the northwest. It was employed in Gandhara and neighboring regions, on Ashokan edicts at Shahbazgarhi and Mansehra, on Indo-Greek, Shaka, Indo-Parthian, and Kushan coins, and in many inscriptions. Gandhari is the name given to the Middle Indo-Aryan (Prakrit) language most commonly written in Kharoshthi in this region. Birch-bark Buddhist manuscripts in Gandhari, discovered in modern times and dated to roughly the first century CE and later, are among the earliest surviving Buddhist manuscripts.

Students should not confuse script with language. Kharoshthi is a way of writing, and Gandhari is a language. Brahmi, by contrast, became the ancestor of most later South Asian scripts, as noted in Chapter 1. The coexistence of Greek, Kharoshthi, and Brahmi on coins and inscriptions shows how multiple writing systems shared the northwest.

Coins have limits. A coin find does not automatically prove permanent territorial control; coins travel, are hoarded, and are used long after they are minted, as the Periplus passage on old Greek coins at Barygaza shows. Portraits on coins are idealized or stylized and cannot be read as straightforward photographic likenesses, although Bactrian Greek portraits are unusually individualized. Monograms, symbols, and mint marks help to sort issues but produce rival reconstructions. And because many kings appear only through coins, historians know names but often not biographies. The sequence of rulers remains provisional, which is why this chapter refers to approximate reign dates and dwells on interpretive caution.

Section 20.5

Shaka Migrations

The next group in the northwest is usually called Shaka or Saka. The word is an ethnonym, a name for a people or set of peoples, found in Old Persian royal inscriptions as Saka, in Greek and Latin sources as Sacae or Scythians, in Chinese sources in the form Sai, and in Indian sources as Shaka. These terms overlap but are not identical, and their applications shifted. In general, they refer to Iranian-speaking pastoralist groups spread across the steppes and oases of Central Asia, including regions of the Achaemenid frontier. Historians should avoid assuming that every use of the word refers to a single unified people with a single origin.

The traditional reconstruction, based largely on the Chinese Hanshu, describes a chain of disruptions in the second century BCE. The Xiongnu are said to have defeated the Yuezhi, who moved westward and displaced the Sai from the Ili region, and some of the Sai then moved south through Central Asia toward Bactria and beyond. This account is useful but should be handled with care. It compresses a long, complex, multi-actor process into a tidy sequence of pushes, and it derives from Chinese sources describing distant events. Archaeological evidence for movements is scattered, and the picture cannot be reduced to a simple domino sequence in which one whole people neatly displaces another. Groups split, allied, and mixed, and mobile populations often included subgroups with differing affiliations. The Yuezhi story returns in detail in Chapter 21.

By the late second century BCE, Shaka-linked groups were established in Sistan, the region around the Helmand river in what is today southern Afghanistan and southeastern Iran, later known as Sakastan, or "land of the Shakas." From there, movements southward into Arachosia, the lower Indus, and Gandhara, and eastward across the Punjab, brought Shaka rulers into the political world of the Indo-Greeks. The Periplus refers to a region around the lower Indus governed from a city called Minnagara, a name that includes a South Asian word for city, connected to what scholars call Indo-Scythia.

The earliest prominent Shaka king in northwestern South Asia is Maues (or Moga), who issued coins in Gandhara and the Punjab around the late second and first centuries BCE. His coins borrow heavily from Indo-Greek designs, with Greek and Kharoshthi legends, images of Zeus, Poseidon, and Nike, and imagery such as elephants. The pattern is significant: newcomers did not sweep away existing monetary and political forms; they adapted them. Maues's successors, including Azes I, continued the practice. The Taxila copper plate of Patika, dated to year 78 of an unidentified era and issued during the reign of the Great King Moga (usually identified with Maues), records the enshrinement of Buddhist relics, another sign of how Shaka authorities took part in existing religious networks. Subordinate rulers, called kshatrapas, discussed in Section 20.6, held provincial or regional authority, including in Mathura.

A crucial lesson follows: mobile groups became rulers of settled territories, and historians should not freeze them permanently as "nomads." Shaka rulers used minting, urban centers, taxation, and patronage. Meanwhile, Indo-Greek kings did not simply disappear; coin evidence suggests overlapping rule, with Shaka kings controlling some regions while Greek kings held others, and with cases of rulers issuing coins that mimic earlier Greek types. The result was a layered political landscape rather than a clean succession, prefiguring what happened again with the Indo-Parthians and Kushans.

Section 20.6

Western Kshatrapas

Shaka-linked dynasties also established states in western India, where they are known as the Western Kshatrapas (or Western Satraps). Their history involves regions far from Gandhara: Gujarat and Saurashtra, Malwa, the coastal plain, and parts of the Deccan margin. Their capitals included Ujjain, an important city on routes between the Gangetic plain, Malwa, and the western coast. This section explains who they were and why their territories mattered. The detailed contest between the Western Kshatrapas and the Satavahanas belongs to Chapter 22.

Historical Vocabulary
Kshatrapa and Mahakshatrapa

Kshatrapa derives from an Old Persian term for the governor of a province, the origin of the Greek word satrap. In the Achaemenid Empire, a satrap governed a large province on behalf of the king. In the Shaka world, kshatrapa designated a governor or subordinate ruler, and mahakshatrapa ("great kshatrapa") designated a senior ruler, often the head of a dynasty. Over time, the titles came to mark ruling positions in their own right rather than simply delegated offices.

The title reveals continuity with Iranian administrative vocabulary, but that does not mean that Western Kshatrapa rulers stayed culturally Iranian. Their inscriptions and coins increasingly use Prakrit and, by the second century CE, Sanskrit, and their dynasties took part in local religious patronage.

Two Western Kshatrapa lines are commonly distinguished. The first is the Kshaharata line, whose best-known ruler Nahapana appears in inscriptions at Nasik and Junnar in the western Deccan, including donations made by his son-in-law Ushavadata (Rishabhadatta) to Buddhist and Brahmanical recipients. The dates given for Nahapana depend on how the regnal years in his inscriptions are read against the Saka era, and are debated, but he is generally placed in the early second century CE. The second line is associated with Chashtana and his descendants, based at Ujjain. Later Western Kshatrapa inscriptions and coins carry dates conventionally interpreted in the Saka era, whose epoch is 78 CE; the historical origin of that era remains debated. It should not be conflated with the Kushan era instituted by Kanishka, now commonly placed from about 127 CE (see Chapter 21).

The most important inscription is that of Rudradaman I at Junagadh in Gujarat, dated to the year 72 of the Saka era, approximately 150 CE. It is among the earliest long inscriptions in polished Sanskrit prose and records that Rudradaman repaired the Sudarshana lake, an embankment and water reservoir originally constructed under Mauryan authority and later improved under Ashoka's governor. In the same passage, Rudradaman lists his titles, victories, and claims about his rule. The inscription is a valuable source, but it is a royal proclamation, meant to showcase achievements. It documents irrigation infrastructure, the continuing importance of Mauryan-era works, the use of Sanskrit as a language of royal display, and the claim to have governed regions in western India and beyond, though the true extent of his control is uncertain.

Western Kshatrapa power drew on commerce. Their territory included access to ports such as Bharukaccha (Greek Barygaza), the great port on the Narmada estuary described in the Periplus, and inland routes linking the coast to Ujjain and the Gangetic plain. Their silver coinage, with a portrait on one face and a symbol on the other, developed layered legends: early issues can combine Greek or pseudo-Greek, Kharoshthi, and Brahmi, after which Kharoshthi drops away and Brahmi becomes dominant for meaningful legends, while degraded Greek-style characters can persist on obverses. Circulating coins of these dynasties have been found in large quantity, indicating a monetized economy. The intellectual environment of western India also appears to have been receptive to Greek-derived learning; the Sanskrit Yavanajataka, a text on astrology drawing on Greek material, is generally dated to the second or third century CE and is connected with western India, though its precise circumstances are debated.

Chapter 22 will show how Satavahana kings claimed victories over the Kshaharatas and how the Deccan developed its own regional system. Chapter 26 returns to the region when the Gupta ruler Chandragupta II expanded into the west. For this chapter, the point is that Shaka-linked rule extended far beyond Gandhara and lasted for centuries, transforming a title from Achaemenid administration into a durable regional political institution in western India.

Section 20.7

Indo-Parthian Rule

The third group is usually called Indo-Parthian. The name is again a modern label. Parthia was a state in the Iranian world whose Arsacid kings had become a major power to the west of Bactria in the second and first centuries BCE. The "Indo-Parthian" kings of the northwest are generally understood to have ruled independently of the central Parthian government, although their names, titles, and coin styles show connections to the Iranian world. Some scholars link the leading ruling house to a Suren clan of eastern Iran, but this is an interpretation rather than a firm fact.

The best-known ruler is Gondophares (Gondophernes), who reigned roughly in the first half of the first century CE. His coins bear Greek legends that use the title autokrator, meaning a sovereign acting by his own authority, and often the epithet soter, alongside Kharoshthi legends, and his territories seem to have included Sistan, Arachosia, and parts of Gandhara and the Punjab, with Taxila as a significant city. A Kharoshthi inscription from Takht-i-Bahi, a Buddhist monastic site near Mardan in the Peshawar region, names Gondophares and is dated to year 103 of an era. If the era is the one usually associated with the year 58 or 57 BCE, that corresponds to about 45 CE; the era's identification is debated. Successors and relatives, including Abdagases and Sases, appear in coins and later accounts, but their relationships and the extent of their authority remain uncertain.

It is important not to overstate what "Indo-Parthian rule" means. It was not a single centralized empire on the scale of the Mauryas. Coin evidence and inscriptions suggest a fluid set of kingdoms and subordinate rulers whose territories shifted. The Periplus describes the lower Indus region of the first century CE as governed by Parthian princes who were often at war among themselves, a report that agrees with the picture of political fragmentation rather than a unified empire. The Indo-Parthians also coexisted with remaining Shaka rulers and with the growing power of the Kushans, discussed in Chapter 21, who eventually incorporated much of the region.

Gondophares also appears in Christian tradition. The Acts of Thomas, a text composed in the early centuries CE, tells how the apostle Thomas traveled to India and met a king named Gundaphorus. Historians need to separate two things. Gondophares is a historically attested king, known from coins and an inscription. The Acts of Thomas is a later religious narrative whose relationship to actual events is debated, and it cannot be used as straightforward evidence for the ruler's court or for the presence of the apostle. The name in the text does show that the memory of a king called Gondophares, or a king with a similar name, reached Christian authors, a notable indication of connections between South Asia and the wider Mediterranean and Near Eastern religious world.

Archaeologically, the Indo-Parthian period is represented at Taxila, where the Sirkap layers reflect continuing urban life, and where a temple with columns near the site of Jandial has attracted debate about its builders and function, with some scholars proposing Zoroastrian or Iranian associations and others interpreting it differently. Such debates remind students that attributing buildings to particular ethnic or dynastic groups is often hazardous.

Section 20.8

Cultural Interaction in Gandhara

Gandhara, the region around Peshawar and Taxila, extending toward Swat and Bajaur, was a multilingual, multireligious, and transregional cultural zone during these centuries. This section deliberately does not develop the full history of Gandharan art. That subject, including the emergence of the Buddha image and the comparison with Mathura, belongs to Section 21.8. Here the goal is to establish the setting that made later art possible.

Languages and scripts coexisted. Greek was used on coins and in some inscriptions, and Greek names appear among donors and officials. Aramaic legacy shaped Kharoshthi. Gandhari Prakrit was widely used for administration and religion. Brahmi appeared where connections to the Gangetic world were stronger. Iranian names, such as those of Shaka and Indo-Parthian rulers, show Iranian-speaking elites. In an environment like this, individuals may have been bilingual or multilingual, and identities were flexible.

Religious evidence is especially informative. At Besnagar (Vidisha), in central India, a stone pillar inscription records that a Greek named Heliodorus, from Taxila, sent as ambassador of the king Antialcidas to a local king named Bhagabhadra, set up a Garuda pillar in honor of Vasudeva, and described himself as a devotee, a Bhagavata. The date is usually placed around 110 BCE. The inscription shows a Greek official from the northwest participating in a Vaishnava devotional tradition in central India and moving between courts, and it also documents diplomatic contact between an Indo-Greek king and a ruler in the Gangetic-Malwa region. In the Buddhist sphere, a Kharoshthi inscription from Swat records the deposit of relics by Theodoros, a meridarch (a district official, a Greek administrative title). The Taxila copper plate of Patika and the Mathura Lion Capital, a Kharoshthi inscription from Mathura that names Shaka rulers, queens including Ayasia Kamuia, and Buddhist donations, indicate that Shaka authorities also became patrons. Later, the Takht-i-Bahi inscription records a donation by a private donor, dated by the reign of Gondophares, at a monastery site; it does not say the king himself gave the gift. In short, Greek and Shaka officials and rulers appear directly in religious patronage records, while Indo-Parthian regnal dating appears in the Buddhist epigraphic environment of Takht-i-Bahi.

Urban archaeology at Taxila adds a material dimension. Sirkap's grid plan shows a city with streets, shops, courtyard houses, and religious structures, including stupa courts and shrines, suggesting a cosmopolitan population. Finds from excavations include Hellenistic-style objects, local pottery, coins from many issuers, ornaments, and Buddhist relic deposits. The Dharmarajika stupa near Taxila shows long use as a Buddhist sacred place. A relic casket from Bimaran, in Afghanistan, decorated with figures that resemble Buddhas in a Hellenistic idiom, was found with coins issued in the name of Azes (once conventionally attributed to Azes II, but now subject to revised attribution and chronology), and its date is debated, and scholars disagree about whether it belongs to the pre-Kushan or Kushan period. It illustrates why the origins of the Buddha image remain unsettled, a question left to Chapter 21.

Gandhara was also a place where manuscript culture developed. Buddhist texts on birch bark written in Gandhari and Kharoshthi, dated by scholars to the early centuries CE, show that monastic communities were producing and preserving texts in the region. These sources connect Gandhara to the developing Buddhist networks discussed in Chapters 21 and 25, including the later spread of Mahayana ideas discussed in Chapter 28.

Everyday Life and Social History

Living in a City of Many Languages

The rulers dominate the surviving evidence, but ordinary life in cities like Taxila, Pushkalavati, and Kapisa can be glimpsed through excavation. Houses of stone and mud brick opened onto narrow streets, shops lined main thoroughfares, and coins in circulation came from kings of several dynasties. A household might have used objects from local workshops alongside imported items. People handling money would have dealt with multiple coin types and weight standards, and merchants and money-changers presumably needed to know which coins were accepted where.

Farmers on irrigated plains and pastoralists in adjoining uplands supplied grain, animals, and wool, while artisans made pottery, metalwork, ornaments, and stone objects. Monasteries were important economic and social institutions that received donations and housed communities. Women appear as royal figures, such as Agathokleia and Ayasia Kamuia, and as donors in some inscriptions, but everyday women's experience is largely invisible in these sources. Historians must be careful not to assume that elite royal or donor inscriptions represent the whole population, or that a bilingual coin implies bilingual peasants.

Section 20.9

The Northwest as a Historical Crossroads

This section draws together the chapter's interpretive argument. The northwest looks like different things depending on where the observer stands. From the Gangetic plain, it could be an exposed frontier, the direction from which armies and unfamiliar rulers came. From Bactria, it was the southern extension of a Central Asian world. From the Iranian plateau, it was an eastern zone connected through Arachosia and Sistan. For merchants, it was a corridor between the Indus plains, Central Asia, and the Arabian Sea. For Buddhist communities, it was a growing network linking South Asia with Central Asia. The word crossroads captures this multiplicity better than the word frontier, which assumes one center and one edge.

Political regimes changed from Greek to Shaka to Parthian, but many features persisted across those changes. Urban sites such as Taxila continued to function. Routes across the Hindu Kush, along the Kabul river, and down the Indus continued to be used. Coinage remained a preferred instrument of royal communication, often imitating earlier Greek types. Kharoshthi remained a common script. Buddhist institutions continued to attract patronage from rulers of different backgrounds. These continuities, rather than the names of ruling houses, explain why the northwest could pass from one dynasty to another without losing its role in exchange.

Commercial connections deserve emphasis. The Periplus shows that in the first century CE, the lower Indus region and the western coast were tied to Red Sea and Indian Ocean trade, and Chapter 25 examines these networks. Overland trade tied the northwest to Bactria and beyond. The Chinese report of Zhang Qian shows that goods from beyond the Hindu Kush moved across these lands and that Han China's attention was drawn to the region. Political fragmentation did not prevent commerce; rulers who competed also depended on merchants, taxes, and mints.

Chapter 21 will show how the Kushans turned these connections into a much larger imperial system. The Indo-Greek, Shaka, and Indo-Parthian kingdoms made that possible by developing monetary systems, multilingual administration, patronage practices, and regional political models that the Kushans would inherit and expand. The Kushans were not a bolt from the blue; they came into a world already arranged around transregional rule, but that story belongs to the next chapter.

Multiple Perspectives

A Changing Frontier: Reconstructed Perspectives

A Greek-speaking soldier or settler in Bactria or the Kabul valley: Dependent on royal patronage and military service, and living amid mixed populations, such a person could see the loss of Bactria as an end of the world he knew, while the southern kingdoms offered new opportunities and a different cultural environment in which Greek identity was preserved partly through coins, names, and titles.
A merchant or money-changer in Taxila: Political change mattered mainly through its effect on taxes, safe roads, and accepted currency. Coins of different rulers circulated together, and a merchant's interest lay in dependable routes and markets, whoever held the citadel.
A Buddhist monastic community: Monasteries sought donors and protection from rulers of any background, and inscriptions show Greek and Shaka patrons and donations dated by Indo-Parthian reigns. A ruler in the Gangetic plain or the Deccan: To the rulers described in Chapters 19 and 22, the northwest and western coast were both sources of danger and partners in diplomacy and trade, as the Heliodorus inscription and the Nasik inscriptions show.
A pastoralist or farmer in the countryside: Rural communities paid taxes and supplied labor, animals, and grain to whichever authority controlled the district. The surviving evidence gives them little voice, and historians must infer their experience from settlement patterns and economic records.
Continuity and Change Over Time

When Dynasties Changed: Indo-Greek, Shaka, and Indo-Parthian Rule

What Changed

  • The ruling houses, their names, and their titles: Greek basileis gave way to Shaka mahakshatrapas and Indo-Parthian autokrators
  • Geographic centers of power, including the shift of Greek rule from Bactria to the south, and the rise of Sakastan and Ujjain as bases
  • Coin designs and legends, as Greek deity types slowly gave way to new symbols and, in the west, to Brahmi-dominant legends
  • The languages of royal display, with Sanskrit appearing in Kshatrapa inscriptions

What Stayed the Same

  • Urban centers such as Taxila, and the routes that linked them
  • The use of coinage and multiple scripts as instruments of rule and exchange
  • Royal patronage of Buddhist and other religious institutions
  • Rural agrarian and pastoral economies, and taxation as their link to the state

Who experienced change most directly? Court elites, soldiers, and officials felt political change most immediately, while villagers and many artisans experienced greater continuity. Why the pattern matters: It shows that dynastic change alone is a poor measure of historical change, and that networks and institutions often outlast the ruling houses that used them.

Cause and Effect

Why the Northwest Repeatedly Drew Transregional States

Long-term causes

The northwest's position between Central Asia, Iran, and the Indus plains, its irrigated valleys and cities, and its earlier integration into Achaemenid, Alexander's, and Mauryan systems created a region where different political and commercial networks overlapped.

Short-term causes

Seleucid weakness, rivalry within the Greco-Bactrian royal houses, and disruptions across Central Asia pushed armed groups and dynasties south of the Hindu Kush, while the fragmentation of Mauryan authority left a diverse political landscape.

Immediate effects

New kingdoms with Greek, Shaka, and Parthian associations formed, adopting existing urban centers, minting bilingual coins, and patronizing local religions.

Long-term consequences

Monetary systems, multilingual administration, and religious patronage networks prepared the ground for the Kushan Empire and intensified links among South Asia, Central Asia, Iran, and the Indian Ocean.

Historical complexity: Geography made the northwest accessible but did not determine events. Specific decisions, rivalries, and alliances, and developments in Central Asia and China, shaped outcomes, and no single cause explains why these kingdoms took the forms they did.

Section 20.10

Review and Historical Synthesis

This chapter has argued that labeling the Indo-Greeks, Shakas, and Indo-Parthians merely as foreign invaders obscures more than it explains. All three groups entered the northwest from directions that lay within a connected system of routes, and each became involved in local politics, religion, and commerce. Their states were real and important, but the most durable result of their presence was the intensification of networks that linked South Asia with Central Asia, Iran, and the wider Hellenistic and Indian Ocean worlds.

Section 20.1 established that the Greco-Bactrian kingdom arose from Seleucid fragmentation and that the Hindu Kush was a passable barrier. Section 20.2 showed that Greek-ruled states south of the mountains were plural rather than unified, and that they can be reconstructed mainly through coins. Section 20.3 used Menander to distinguish a historical king from a literary memory. Section 20.4 showed how coinage functioned as money, communication, and evidence, and how bilingual legends made political messages legible to different populations.

Sections 20.5 through 20.7 compared the succeeding political formations. Indo-Greek kings ruled through Hellenistic royal forms adapted to a South Asian environment. Shaka rulers came from mobile Iranian-speaking populations but became rulers of settled territories in the northwest and, as Western Kshatrapas, in western India, where they built a durable state and used a title inherited from Achaemenid administration. Indo-Parthian rulers, best represented by Gondophares, governed a fluid set of kingdoms connected to eastern Iran. In each case, political power was mobile, operating across a highly connected northwest, and conquest and cultural incorporation occurred together.

Section 20.8 showed Gandhara as a multilingual, multireligious cultural zone where Greek and Shaka officials and rulers appear directly in Buddhist and devotional patronage records, and Indo-Parthian regnal dating appears in the Buddhist epigraphic environment of Takht-i-Bahi. Section 20.9 argued that the northwest was a crossroads, seen differently from the Gangetic plain, Bactria, Iran, and the marketplace, and that continuities in cities, routes, coinage, and religious institutions outlasted the ruling houses.

Several caveats remain essential. Chronology is provisional, especially for the sequence of Indo-Greek kings and the era systems used in inscriptions. Much of the evidence is numismatic and epigraphic, which favors royal self-presentation. Later traditions such as the Milindapanha and the Acts of Thomas preserve valuable memories but must not be treated as contemporary reports. And modern national labels should not be projected onto these regions or the people who lived in them.

Chapter 21 builds directly on this foundation. It examines how the Yuezhi and the Kushan state emerged in Bactria and turned the connections described here into a much larger Eurasian imperial system, with Kanishka, Silk Road commerce, Kushan coinage, Buddhist patronage, and the art of Gandhara and Mathura. Chapter 22 then turns to the Deccan, where the Satavahanas developed a regional political and commercial system that also came into contact with the Western Kshatrapas introduced here.

Chapter Summary

This chapter shifted attention from the Gangetic heartland to the northwest and argued that northwestern South Asia was a historically connected crossroads rather than a peripheral zone repeatedly invaded from outside. Across roughly the third century BCE to the second century CE, Greek, Shaka, and Parthian-connected rulers governed parts of the region, and their states must be understood within networks linking the Indus basin, Afghanistan, Iran, Central Asia, and the Hellenistic world.

The background lay in Bactria, where a Greek-ruled state emerged from the Seleucid Empire in the mid-third century BCE. Evidence from Polybius, Justin, the Chinese Shiji, and the excavated city of Ai Khanoum shows a state with Greek urban institutions and connections across the Hindu Kush. The mountain range was a difficult but passable frontier, and routes linked Bactria with the Kabul valley, Gandhara, and Arachosia.

Greek-ruled states south of the mountains, conventionally called Indo-Greek, were plural rather than unified. Coins preserve dozens of royal names, and scholars reconstruct their order through overstrikes, hoards, and style. Indian and Greek literary sources supply indirect glimpses of Greek military and political activity, but the coins and inscriptions form the core evidence, which is why numismatics receives particular attention.

Menander I illustrates the interplay of different evidence. Coins, a disputed reliquary inscription, Strabo, Plutarch, and the Periplus document a widely known king, while the Pali Milindapanha preserves a later literary Milinda. The Historical Debate showed why claims that Menander was a Buddhist require care: the evidence supports a ruler operating in a Buddhist-influenced society and remembered by Buddhists, but not firm conclusions about his personal faith.

Coinage was money, political communication, and evidence. Bilingual Greek and Kharoshthi legends, titles that combined Hellenistic and South Asian vocabulary, and mixed iconography show rulers addressing multiple audiences. The decipherment of Kharoshthi in the nineteenth century through bilingual coins shows how these objects also transformed modern historical knowledge. Coins have limits: they do not prove permanent control, and portraits are stylized.

The Shakas are known through an ethnonym used in Persian, Greek, Chinese, and Indian sources. Movements of Shaka-linked groups were part of wider disruptions across Central Asia, but traditional chain-reaction accounts are simplified reconstructions. Shaka rulers such as Maues and Azes I adapted Indo-Greek monetary forms and became rulers of settled territories, with kshatrapas governing regions such as Mathura.

In western India, the Western Kshatrapas built a durable Shaka-linked state with Ujjain as a major center and with access to Barygaza and other ports. Inscriptions from Nasik, Junnar, and Junagadh, including Rudradaman's Sanskrit inscription, show a dynasty that adapted Achaemenid-derived titles into a regional institution and adopted Sanskrit for royal display. Their detailed contest with the Satavahanas belongs to Chapter 22.

Indo-Parthian rulers, notably Gondophares, governed a fluid set of kingdoms connected to eastern Iran. Coins and the Takht-i-Bahi inscription document his rule, while the Acts of Thomas preserves a later religious tradition that must be separated from the historical king. The Periplus describes political fragmentation in the lower Indus region.

Gandhara emerged as a multilingual, multireligious cultural zone. Greek, Kharoshthi, Gandhari, Brahmi, and Iranian names coexisted; the Heliodorus pillar, the Swat relic inscription of Theodoros, the Taxila copper plate of Patika, the Mathura Lion Capital, and the Takht-i-Bahi inscription (a donation dated by Gondophares's reign) show officials and rulers of diverse origins in Buddhist and devotional settings. The full history of Gandharan art belongs to Chapter 21.

The chapter concluded that the northwest is best understood as a crossroads seen from many directions. Dynasties changed, but cities, routes, coinage, multilingual administration, and religious patronage created continuities. These developments prepared the ground for the Kushan Empire, examined in Chapter 21.

Key Terms

Greco-Bactrian kingdom
The Greek-ruled state that arose in Bactria from the mid-third century BCE, the base from which Greek rule extended south of the Hindu Kush.
Indo-Greek kingdoms
A modern label for the plural Greek-ruled states of the Kabul valley, Gandhara, and the Punjab, from about 180 BCE into the early first century CE.
Hellenistic
Relating to the Greek-influenced world that followed Alexander's conquests, including the Seleucid and Greco-Bactrian states.
Yavana
The South Asian term for Greeks, derived from the name of the Ionians via Persian; later broadened to designate peoples of the western world.
Ai Khanoum
A Hellenistic city in northeastern Afghanistan whose excavation revealed Greek institutions in Bactria; destroyed around 145 BCE.
Numismatics
The study of coins, essential for reconstructing Indo-Greek, Shaka, and Indo-Parthian history where narrative sources are scarce.
Bilingual coin
A coin carrying legends in two languages or scripts, such as Greek and Kharoshthi, indicating multiple intended audiences.
Overstrike
A coin struck over an earlier coin, used by historians to establish the relative order of issuers.
Kharoshthi
A right-to-left script derived from Aramaic and used in Gandhara and the northwest for coins, inscriptions, and manuscripts.
Gandhari
The Middle Indo-Aryan language typically written in Kharoshthi in the northwest, used in administration and Buddhist manuscripts.
Basileus
The Greek word for king, used in the Greek legends of Hellenistic and Indo-Greek coins.
Maharaja
A Sanskrit and Prakrit title meaning great king, used in Kharoshthi coin legends as an equivalent of basileus.
Milindapanha
A Pali text presenting a dialogue between King Milinda, identified with Menander, and the monk Nagasena; a later, layered composition.
Menander I
The best-attested Indo-Greek king, known from coins, an inscription, classical authors, and Buddhist tradition.
Shaka (Saka)
An ethnonym for Iranian-speaking groups of Central Asia and adjoining regions, applied to rulers in the northwest and western India.
Sakastan
The region of Sistan, associated with Shaka settlement and a base for movements toward South Asia.
Kshatrapa
A governor or subordinate ruler, derived from the Old Persian term behind the word satrap.
Mahakshatrapa
"Great kshatrapa," a senior ruling title in Shaka-linked dynasties.
Western Kshatrapas
Shaka-linked dynasties of Gujarat, Malwa, and neighboring regions, including the Kshaharatas and the line of Chashtana and Rudradaman.
Indo-Parthian kingdoms
A modern label for the fluid set of kingdoms of the first century CE associated with Gondophares and connected to the Iranian world.
Autokrator
A Greek title meaning ruler acting by his own authority, used on the coins of Gondophares.
Meridarch
A Greek administrative title for a district official, attested in a Swat relic inscription.
Periplus of the Erythraean Sea
A first-century CE Greek sailing guide describing ports and trade from the Red Sea to India, including the northwest and Barygaza.
Barygaza (Bharukaccha)
A major port on the Narmada estuary in western India, linked to Ujjain and to Indian Ocean trade.
Sirkap
The planned city at Taxila associated with Indo-Greek foundation and continued occupation under later rulers.
Heliodorus pillar
A pillar inscription at Besnagar recording a Greek ambassador's devotion to Vasudeva.
Mathura Lion Capital
A Kharoshthi inscription documenting Shaka rulers, queens, and Buddhist donation at Mathura.
Gondophares
The best-known Indo-Parthian king, attested by coins and the Takht-i-Bahi inscription and remembered in the Acts of Thomas.
Crossroads
An analytical concept treating a region as a meeting point of routes and networks rather than a peripheral frontier.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it changes how the northwest appears on the historical map. Instead of a vulnerable edge that outsiders repeatedly crossed, the region emerges as a meeting place where rulers, merchants, monks, and soldiers moved among Bactria, Iran, the Indus plains, and the Indian Ocean. The same coin that circulated in a Taxila market could be found far away, and the same monastery could receive donations from a Greek official, a Shaka governor, or a donor whose gift was dated by a Parthian king's reign.

The chapter also teaches a methodological lesson. Where narrative sources are thin, historians build understanding from coins, inscriptions, and archaeology, and they must be candid about limits. Distinguishing the historical Menander from the Milinda of Buddhist tradition, or Gondophares from the king of the Acts of Thomas, illustrates how memory and evidence must be kept apart while both are valued.

The developments here shaped what followed. Monetary practices, multilingual administration, and patronage networks of the northwest were inherited by the Kushans in Chapter 21, and the Shaka-linked dynasties of western India remained important into the Gupta era of Chapters 26 and 27. Meanwhile, Chapter 22 turns to the Deccan, whose rulers dealt with the Western Kshatrapas introduced here.

Within the six-volume series, this chapter reinforces a theme from Chapter 1: South Asia was never sealed from the wider world. Political change came from and through connections, and the region's history is best understood as the history of a crossroads whose networks outlasted the dynasties that ruled them.
