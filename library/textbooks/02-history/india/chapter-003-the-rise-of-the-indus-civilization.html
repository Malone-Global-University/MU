The Rise of the Indus Civilization

Indian History: South Asia from the Indus Civilization to the Present

Chapter Opening

Sometime close to 2600 BCE, in city after city scattered across a vast stretch of the Indus and Ghaggar-Hakra plains, communities that had spent many centuries building villages, herding animals, and trading beads across hundreds of kilometers began building something new: cities laid out with striking regularity, built up on massive mudbrick platforms, supplied with some of the most sophisticated drainage systems the Bronze Age world would produce, and bound together, across a region larger than many modern countries, by a shared system of weights, a shared repertoire of craft techniques, and a still-undeciphered sign or script system stamped onto thousands of small stone seals. No inscription tells us the name any of these cities called themselves. No king's name survives. Yet the scale, consistency, and sophistication of what these communities built remain, five thousand years later, genuinely remarkable.

Chapter 2 traced the long, uneven process by which farming and pastoral communities across the greater Indus and Ghaggar-Hakra zone grew larger, more interconnected, and more materially complex, culminating in the regional Early Harappan traditions — Kot Diji, Amri, the Ravi phase, and the Hakra-associated cultures — that had taken shape by the later fourth millennium BCE. This chapter picks up that story at the threshold Chapter 2 approached but did not cross: the emergence, beginning around 2600 BCE, of the Mature Harappan urban civilization, one of the world's earliest and most extensive urban societies, and the first of South Asia's civilizations to leave behind planned cities on the scale this chapter examines.

The Indus Civilization — named, in the usual archaeological convention, for the river along whose plains its cities were first identified, though its full geographic extent reached well beyond the Indus valley itself — presents historians with a genuinely unusual combination of riches and silences. On one hand, archaeologists have recovered an enormous body of material evidence: the excavated remains of great cities including Harappa, Mohenjo-daro, and Dholavira, alongside hundreds of smaller towns and villages; a strikingly uniform system of weights and measures used across a huge geographic area; sophisticated urban planning and drainage; extensive craft production in stone, shell, metal, and ceramic; and long-distance trade connections reaching from Afghanistan to the Persian Gulf and Mesopotamia. On the other hand, the Indus script that appears on thousands of stone seals and other objects remains undeciphered, no king's name or dynastic list survives, no royal palace or unambiguous temple has been confidently identified, and historians cannot say with confidence what language the Indus peoples spoke, what specific religious beliefs they held, or how their political authority was organized.

This combination of rich material evidence and profound interpretive silence means that this chapter must proceed differently from a chapter on, say, the Mauryan Empire, where inscriptions name kings and describe policy. Here, historians must reason primarily from cities, streets, drains, bricks, weights, seals, and trade goods — asking what these objects can responsibly establish, and being equally clear about what they cannot. This is not a weakness particular to the Indus Civilization; it is, in a more extreme form, the same evidentiary challenge Chapter 1 introduced and Chapter 2 practiced at a smaller scale. This chapter asks students to extend that same disciplined, evidence-based reasoning to a much larger and more startling historical subject: an urban civilization covering, at its height, roughly one million square kilometers, with estimates varying according to how the civilization's geographic limits are defined, integrated by trade and cultural convention rather than, so far as the evidence shows, by military conquest or an obviously centralized imperial government.

The chapter's structure reflects the kind of evidence available. Rather than opening with kings and dynasties, it opens with the cities themselves — first the general character of Mature Harappan urbanism, then three of its most thoroughly excavated centers, Harappa, Mohenjo-daro, and Dholavira, each illustrating different aspects of the civilization's geographic reach and local adaptation. Students should also be alert, throughout, to a methodological point this chapter will make repeatedly: even well-worn descriptive labels used by archaeologists, such as "citadel" and "lower town," rest on interpretations that have themselves been revised as excavation and analysis have continued, and this chapter presents such labels as useful conventions rather than settled conclusions about function. It then turns to the specific achievements that make Indus cities so distinctive among Bronze Age urban societies: their planning and water management, their standardized weights and measures, their agricultural base, and their craft production and trade networks. Only in its final main section does the chapter confront directly the most difficult and most debated question the evidence raises: what kind of government, if any, organized this vast and strikingly uniform civilization, and how do historians reason about political power in the absence of any decipherable text that could tell them directly?

Students should resist two temptations as they read this chapter. The first is to assume that a civilization this materially impressive must have been governed by kings and priests on the Mesopotamian or Egyptian model, simply because that pattern is more familiar from other early urban societies; the evidence for the Indus Civilization does not straightforwardly support that assumption, and Section 3.9 examines why. The second is to conclude, from the absence of confirmed kings, temples, and deciphered texts, that the Indus Civilization must have been somehow less sophisticated or less "real" than its better-documented Bronze Age contemporaries; the archaeological evidence decisively contradicts that conclusion. The Indus cities were, by any material measure, among the most technically accomplished urban societies of the ancient world, and this chapter's task is to explain that accomplishment honestly, without either exaggerating what the evidence can prove or diminishing what it very clearly demonstrates.

By the chapter's end, students should understand not only the physical achievements of Harappa, Mohenjo-daro, and Dholavira, but also why the Indus Civilization poses such distinctive historical and methodological challenges, and how historians and archaeologists have nonetheless built a substantial, evidence-based understanding of one of the world's first great urban civilizations. Chapter 4 continues this account, turning to Harappan society, religion, writing, and the civilization's eventual transformation and regionalization after roughly 1900 BCE.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Describe the defining features of Mature Harappan urbanism and explain how it differed from the Early Harappan traditions examined in Chapter 2.
  2. Compare Harappa, Mohenjo-daro, and Dholavira, identifying shared features and significant local or regional differences among them.
  3. Explain the principles of Harappan city planning, including street layout, multi-sector urban organization (including the conventional but revised "citadel" and "lower town" labels), and construction technology.
  4. Analyze Harappan water management and drainage systems and explain their significance for understanding urban civic organization.
  5. Evaluate the evidence for a standardized system of weights and measures across the Indus zone and explain what this standardization suggests, and does not prove, about political and economic organization.
  6. Describe the agricultural base that supported Harappan cities and assess the evidence for irrigation, crop diversity, and regional agricultural variation.
  7. Trace the major craft industries of the Indus Civilization and its trade connections with Afghanistan, Central Asia, the Persian Gulf, and Mesopotamia.
  8. Assess the competing scholarly interpretations of Harappan political organization, including centralized-state, city-state, and non-state or heterarchical models.
  9. Explain why the absence of deciphered texts, confirmed royal names, and unambiguous palaces or temples limits confident claims about Harappan government and ideology.
  10. Identify the major categories of archaeological evidence used to study the Indus Civilization and evaluate their strengths and limitations.
  11. Contextualize the Indus Civilization among its Bronze Age contemporaries in Mesopotamia, Egypt, and Central Asia.

Historical Context

Chapter 2 traced the long developmental sequence — from Mehrgarh's earliest aceramic farming phase through the increasingly complex Early Harappan regional traditions of the later fourth and early third millennia BCE — that laid the economic, technological, and social foundations for the civilization this chapter examines. By around 2600 BCE, the greater Indus and Ghaggar-Hakra zone had already developed mudbrick architectural technology, craft specialization in beadwork, pottery, and early metallurgy, long-distance exchange networks reaching Afghanistan and the Arabian Sea coast, and a degree of settlement hierarchy and regional cultural coherence expressed through traditions such as Kot Diji, Amri, and the Hakra/Ravi cultures.

What happened next was not simply more of the same. Within a relatively compressed period — a matter of a few generations by some estimates, though the precise pace and mechanism of the transition remain debated among archaeologists — settlements across this zone underwent a transformation in scale, planning, and material standardization that justifies treating the Mature Harappan period as a genuinely new phase of South Asian history, distinct from the Early Harappan traditions that preceded it. Cities grew to sizes that dwarfed even the largest Early Harappan settlements. Construction adopted standardized brick proportions used with striking consistency across enormous distances. Weights conforming to a shared numerical system appear from Gujarat to the Punjab to the northern reaches of Afghanistan. Craft goods, especially finely made stone beads and seals bearing a shared sign system, circulated across this same vast area.

The geographic extent of the Mature Harappan world was, for its time, extraordinary. At its greatest extent, sites bearing the hallmarks of Indus material culture have been identified across a region stretching from the Makran coast of Baluchistan and northern Afghanistan in the west and northwest, through Punjab and Sindh, into Gujarat and coastal western India in the south, and toward the upper reaches of the Yamuna-Ganga interfluve in the east — an area considerably larger than either contemporary Egypt or the Mesopotamian city-state world, and one that included, on current estimates, well over a thousand identified settlement sites, though only a small fraction have been extensively excavated.

Historians studying this civilization face constraints introduced in Chapter 1 and Chapter 2 in a particularly acute form. The Indus script — the system of signs carved onto seals, tablets, and other objects, discussed further in Chapter 4 — remains undeciphered despite more than a century of proposed decipherments, none commanding broad scholarly consensus. No securely translated Harappan text exists. No king's name, no dynastic sequence, no royal proclamation, no religious scripture, and no legal code have been recovered and confirmed. This means that virtually everything this chapter can establish about Harappan cities, economy, and society must be inferred from material remains: excavated architecture, artifacts, skeletal evidence, and comparative analysis with better-documented contemporary civilizations in Mesopotamia and Egypt, used cautiously and only where genuinely illuminating rather than as a template imposed uncritically onto Indus evidence.

This chapter, accordingly, proceeds primarily through the cities and their material culture, reserving the more explicitly interpretive questions of society, religion, and script for Chapter 4, while addressing directly, in Section 3.9, the single most debated interpretive question this material evidence raises: what kind of political authority, if any, organized and maintained the remarkable uniformity this chapter documents.

Section 3.1

The Mature Harappan Transformation

Archaeologists mark the beginning of the Mature Harappan, or "Integration Era," phase of the Indus Civilization at approximately 2600 BCE, the point at which the regional Early Harappan traditions examined in Chapter 2 gave way, across an enormous geographic area, to a strikingly uniform urban culture. The word "Mature" here signals a contrast with the preceding "Early Harappan" phase, not a value judgment about earlier communities; it identifies a specific archaeological horizon characterized by particular, identifiable features that appear together, with unusual consistency, across sites separated by hundreds of kilometers.

Several features distinguish this Mature Harappan horizon from what came before. Settlements grew dramatically in size, with the largest urban centers — Mohenjo-daro, Harappa, Rakhigarhi, Ganweriwala, and Dholavira among them — reaching populations that some estimates place in the tens of thousands, though such population estimates for ancient cities carry substantial uncertainty and should be treated as informed approximation rather than precise fact. Cities were laid out according to shared planning principles, discussed fully in Section 3.5, including grid-oriented street systems, one or more elevated mounds conventionally described as a "citadel" alongside more extensive lower urban areas, and sophisticated drainage infrastructure. As Section 3.5 explains more fully, this citadel-and-lower-town description, while a useful and widely used descriptive convention, has been substantially complicated by more recent excavation, which has found domestic and industrial activity within the elevated sectors and multiple distinct mounds within what earlier scholarship treated as a single "lower town." Construction employed standardized fired and mudbrick with a consistent ratio of length to width to thickness used across the entire Indus zone, a level of standardization that has no obvious parallel among contemporary Bronze Age urban traditions in Mesopotamia or Egypt. A shared system of cubical stone weights, examined in Section 3.6, circulated with remarkable numerical consistency from Gujarat to the Indus-Punjab region and into Afghanistan. Craft production, especially in stone beadwork, shell-working, ceramics, and metallurgy, reached new levels of technical sophistication and scale, supplying both local consumption and long-distance exchange. And a system of carved stone seals bearing short inscriptions in the still-undeciphered Indus script, together with recurring animal and symbolic imagery, appears at sites across this same vast territory, examined further in Chapter 4.

What makes this transformation historically striking is not any single feature in isolation — grid-planned cities, standardized weights, and craft specialization each have partial parallels in other early urban societies — but rather the combination of scale, consistency, and geographic extent with which these features appear together across the Indus world. A brick made in Gujarat and a brick made near the Afghan border conform to a strikingly similar set of proportions. A stone weight found at a Punjab site and one found at a Gujarat site fall within the same numerical series. This degree of standardization across such a vast area, in a period before any confirmed centralized imperial administration comparable to later South Asian or Mesopotamian states, is one of the central historical puzzles this chapter and Section 3.9 in particular will examine.

Historians and archaeologists generally treat the shift from Early Harappan regional diversity to Mature Harappan standardization as a genuine, historically significant transformation — sometimes described, cautiously, as a process of "integration" — while resisting the idea that it occurred instantaneously or that it erased all regional variation. As Sections 3.2 through 3.4 will show through the specific cases of Harappa, Mohenjo-daro, and Dholavira, considerable local adaptation persisted even within this broadly shared cultural framework, particularly in construction materials suited to local geology, in the specific arrangement of civic and residential space, and in the relative emphasis given to features such as water storage, fortification, or craft production. The Mature Harappan world, in other words, achieved a genuinely unusual degree of shared material culture without becoming culturally or architecturally identical from one region to another — a pattern this chapter will return to throughout.

Section 3.2

Harappa

Harappa, situated on the Ravi River in present-day Punjab, Pakistan, holds a special place in the history of South Asian archaeology: it was the first of the great Indus sites to attract substantial modern archaeological investigation, and it consequently lent its name to the entire civilization and to the archaeological terms "Harappan" and "Mature Harappan" used throughout this textbook. The site had been noted by earlier surveyors, including Alexander Cunningham in the nineteenth century, without recognition of its true age or significance; systematic excavation began under Daya Ram Sahni in the early 1920s. Recognition that Harappa, together with Mohenjo-daro, belonged to a previously unknown Bronze Age civilization emerged from comparison of discoveries at both sites and was publicly announced by John Marshall in 1924. Excavations continuing intermittently, and with increasingly modern methods, through recent decades, revealed a large urban site with elevated mounds, more extensive lower urban areas, cemeteries, and craft-production areas, though the site suffered significant damage in earlier centuries from brick-robbing, including reported nineteenth-century removal of Harappan bricks for use as railway ballast — a reminder that the physical survival of ancient sites depends heavily on later historical circumstances entirely outside the control of the ancient builders themselves.

As Chapter 2 noted, Harappa's occupation sequence extends well back into the Early Harappan period, with a Ravi-phase occupation, depending on the chronological scheme used, beginning as early as 3700 or as late as 3300 BCE, followed by a Kot Diji-phase occupation from roughly 2800 to 2600 BCE, before the site's Mature Harappan transformation. This long, continuous occupation sequence makes Harappa an unusually valuable site for tracing the actual transition from Early Harappan regional culture to Mature Harappan urbanism at a single location, rather than inferring that transition only by comparing separate sites.

At its Mature Harappan height, Harappa's western elevated mound, raised on a substantial mudbrick platform and conventionally described as its "citadel," was enclosed in part by fortification walls constructed of mudbrick faced with fired brick, a construction technique requiring considerable coordinated labor and resource mobilization; excavation has shown, however, that this elevated sector itself contained residential and craft activity rather than functioning solely as an administrative or elite precinct. The city's more extensive lower urban areas, comprising several distinct mounds rather than a single undifferentiated "lower town," extended across a wide area to the north and east, organized, as at other major Harappan cities, along a broadly grid-oriented street pattern with narrower lanes branching from wider thoroughfares. Excavators identified areas associated with craft production, including evidence for bead-making, shell-working, and pottery manufacture, alongside residential architecture built predominantly of standardized mudbrick, occasionally supplemented by fired brick in structures requiring greater durability, such as drains and wells.

Harappa's cemeteries have provided important evidence for mortuary practice, including burials oriented in a generally consistent manner and accompanied by grave goods such as pottery vessels, ornaments, and, in some cases, mirrors and other personal items, evidence examined further in Chapter 4's treatment of Harappan society and belief. As at Mehrgarh, discussed in Chapter 2, historians must exercise caution in inferring specific religious beliefs from burial patterns alone, though the consistency of certain practices across Harappan cemeteries suggests shared cultural norms surrounding death across a considerable geographic area.

Harappa also preserved important evidence relevant to writing and administration: numerous inscribed seals, sealings (impressions made by pressing a seal into clay, often used to mark or secure goods), and inscribed pottery have been recovered from the site, contributing significantly to the corpus of Indus script evidence that Chapter 4 examines in detail. The presence of sealings at Harappa, sometimes bearing the impression of packaging materials such as cord or cloth on their reverse side, provides valuable evidence that seals were used in the movement or storage of goods, a point taken up further in Section 3.8's discussion of trade and craft specialization.

Section 3.3

Mohenjo-daro

Mohenjo-daro, on the Indus River in present-day Sindh, Pakistan, is among the largest and most extensively excavated Mature Harappan cities, and its scale and preservation have made it, in many respects, the civilization's most iconic urban center, though students should remember that it is only one among many major Harappan cities and should not be treated as though it were somehow more representative or more important than Harappa, Rakhigarhi, Ganweriwala, or Dholavira simply because it is the most famous.

Mohenjo-daro's layout is conventionally described in terms of a broad organizational pattern shared with other major Mature Harappan cities: an elevated western mound, built on a substantial platform of mudbrick fill and traditionally labeled the "citadel," and more extensive eastern urban areas, often described collectively as the "lower town," organized along a grid of main streets running roughly north-south and east-west, with narrower residential lanes branching off these principal thoroughfares. Older archaeological literature commonly treated this as a straightforward division between a western administrative or elite sector and an eastern common-residential sector. Modern excavation has substantially complicated that binary model: the elevated western mound itself contained domestic occupation and industrial activity rather than functioning purely as an administrative or ceremonial precinct, the surrounding wall and gateway once proposed by early twentieth-century excavators have not been accepted by many later scholars in the form originally proposed, and the eastern urban area consists of several separate mounds that may represent distinct neighborhoods rather than a single unified "lower town." This textbook retains "citadel mound" and "lower town" as conventional descriptive labels because they remain in wide use, but students should understand that the social or political function once assumed for this division cannot simply be taken for granted. Estimates of the site's peak population vary considerably among scholars, generally ranging from the low tens of thousands to somewhat higher figures, though, as with all ancient population estimates, these numbers should be treated as approximate rather than precise.

The citadel mound at Mohenjo-daro contains some of the site's most distinctive structures. The Great Bath, a large, carefully constructed and waterproofed rectangular tank built of finely fitted baked brick set in gypsum mortar, with a bitumen waterproofing layer, is among the best-known structures at the site. Its careful construction, its position on the citadel, and the presence of surrounding rooms and a well suggest to most archaeologists that it served some form of ritual, ceremonial, or communal bathing function, comparable in general concept, though not necessarily in specific meaning, to ritual bathing practices attested in various forms throughout later South Asian religious history; however, historians must be careful not to assume that the Great Bath's function mirrored any specific later Hindu, Buddhist, or Jain ritual practice, since no textual evidence survives to confirm such a connection, and the interpretive leap from "possibly ritual" to "confirmed precursor of a later specific tradition" would exceed what the evidence supports.

Nearby on the citadel, excavators identified a large pillared hall, sometimes described as an assembly hall, and a substantial structure long labeled the "Granary" by early excavators, on the assumption that its raised platform and internal compartmented structure were designed for grain storage. This identification, once treated as a near-certainty in older textbook accounts, is now considered by many archaeologists to be less secure than earlier scholarship assumed; alternative interpretations have proposed uses ranging from a warehouse for varied goods to a platform supporting a now-vanished superstructure of a different function altogether. This textbook presents the traditional "Granary" label cautiously, as a widely used but contested identification rather than a settled fact, consistent with this textbook's broader commitment to distinguishing confident interpretation from informed speculation.

Mohenjo-daro's lower town preserves extensive residential architecture, typically organized around internal courtyards, with many houses equipped with private wells and bathing platforms connected to the city's remarkable drainage network, examined fully in Section 3.5. The uniformity of basic house construction across much of the lower town, combined with variation in house size and the number of rooms, has led some archaeologists to suggest a population with differentiated wealth or status but without the extreme architectural contrasts — grand palaces set against minimal housing — that characterize many other early urban civilizations, a point taken up further in Section 3.9's discussion of Harappan political organization.

Excavations at Mohenjo-daro have also recovered a substantial corpus of seals, figurines, pottery, and craft objects, including the well-known small bronze figure sometimes described as a "dancing girl," a soapstone seal depicting a horned figure surrounded by animals sometimes discussed in connection with later South Asian religious iconography, and numerous terracotta figurines whose precise function and meaning remain debated. This textbook addresses these objects and their contested interpretations more fully in Chapter 4's treatment of Harappan religion and belief, since confident claims about their religious significance depend on interpretive arguments that extend beyond this chapter's primary focus on urban planning, economy, and political organization.

Section 3.4

Dholavira and Regional Urbanism

Dholavira, located on Khadir Island in the Rann of Kutch, Gujarat, in present-day India, illustrates both the geographic reach of Mature Harappan urbanism and the significant regional adaptation that occurred within the broader shared cultural tradition examined in Section 3.1. Unlike Harappa and Mohenjo-daro, which were built predominantly of mudbrick with baked-brick facing in specific structural contexts, Dholavira made extensive use of dressed stone in its construction, reflecting the different geological resources available in its semi-arid Gujarat setting.

Dholavira's urban plan follows a distinctive tripartite division, with a heavily fortified citadel, a "middle town," and a lower town, each bounded by substantial stone and mudbrick walls, arranged within an outer city wall enclosing the settlement as a whole. This more elaborately subdivided urban plan differs from the conventional descriptive organization often applied to Mohenjo-daro and Harappa, while those cities themselves also possessed more complex multi-mound urban arrangements than the older citadel/lower-town binary implied, illustrating that Mature Harappan urban planning, while broadly consistent in its underlying principles, did not impose a single rigid template on every city.

The site's most distinctive feature is its sophisticated water-management system, discussed further in Section 3.5, comprising a series of large stone-cut reservoirs connected by channels designed to capture and store seasonal rainfall and runoff from nearby streams, an adaptation of clear importance in a region with limited and highly seasonal rainfall. This reservoir system represents one of the most elaborate water-management achievements documented anywhere in the Bronze Age world, and it demonstrates that Harappan urban planners adapted their general principles of civic water management, examined city-wide in Section 3.5, to the specific environmental demands of the arid Kutch region rather than applying a single undifferentiated design everywhere.

Dholavira has also yielded one of the most celebrated examples of the Indus sign/script system: a large inscription, sometimes described as a signboard, composed of ten large sign-shaped objects, found in association with the city's North Gate and widely reconstructed as elements of a prominent signboard or display, probably mounted on a perishable backing that has not survived. While this inscription is often illustrated in popular accounts of the Indus Civilization because of its unusual size and prominent original context, it remains, like all other Indus inscriptions, undeciphered, and its content cannot currently be read with confidence, a point examined further in Chapter 4.

Excavated craft-production areas at Dholavira, including evidence for shell-working, bead-making, and metalworking, along with substantial storage facilities and evidence of long-distance trade contact, indicate that the city functioned as a significant regional economic center, likely benefiting from its position along maritime and overland routes connecting the Gujarat coast to other parts of the Indus world and, potentially, to more distant trading partners across the Arabian Sea, a theme developed further in Section 3.8. Taken together, Dholavira's stone construction, distinctive tripartite plan, elaborate reservoir system, and prominent inscription illustrate a central theme of this chapter: the Mature Harappan world combined extraordinary shared conventions in weights, brick proportions, and craft techniques with genuine regional creativity in how individual cities were built, organized, and adapted to their local environments.

Section 3.5

City Planning and Water Management

Among the most distinctive achievements of Mature Harappan civilization is the degree of deliberate planning visible in its cities, and, closely related, the sophistication of its approach to water supply, drainage, and waste management. These features recur, with local variation, across Harappa, Mohenjo-daro, Dholavira, and other extensively excavated sites, and they represent some of the clearest material evidence for coordinated civic organization anywhere in the Bronze Age world.

Major Harappan cities are conventionally described as dividing their urban space into a raised "citadel" area and a larger "lower town," though, as Sections 3.2 and 3.3 explained, this description is now understood as a useful but imperfect convention rather than a precise account of function, since elevated sectors contained ordinary domestic and industrial activity and "lower town" areas often comprised multiple distinct mounds. As Dholavira illustrates, this basic descriptive division could also be elaborated into additional subdivisions at some cities. Streets within the lower town were typically laid out along a broadly grid-oriented pattern, with wider main thoroughfares intersected by narrower lanes, an arrangement that facilitated both movement through the city and the installation of covered drains along street margins. Mudbrick was widely used for platforms, walls, and much residential construction, while baked (fired) brick was also important — used especially extensively at Mohenjo-daro, in both domestic and public architecture, and not confined to any single building type — and was particularly favored for wells, drains, bathing surfaces, and other structures requiring durability or resistance to moisture, reflecting a sensible allocation of the greater labor and fuel costs associated with brick-firing to the applications where it mattered most.

Perhaps the single most striking feature of Harappan urban planning is its drainage infrastructure. Many houses in cities such as Mohenjo-daro were equipped with private wells and bathing areas, and, in some cases, structures that archaeologists interpret as latrines, though such identifications remain interpretive rather than certain in every instance. Household wastewater typically entered a network of covered brick drains running beneath or alongside streets, channeling it toward settling tanks, soak pits, or larger drainage channels; the ultimate termination of many of these drains — whether they emptied beyond the city's edge or were absorbed locally — cannot now be securely reconstructed in every case. These drains were regularly built with sumps or catch-pits designed to trap solid waste for periodic removal, indicating not only sophisticated initial construction but also an evident system of ongoing civic maintenance. This drainage infrastructure is among the most extensive and systematic known from the Bronze Age world, and it is frequently cited by historians of ancient technology as one of the Indus Civilization's most distinctive contributions to the history of urban engineering.

Water storage and management extended beyond individual houses to city-wide systems. At Mohenjo-daro, numerous wells distributed throughout the lower town provided widespread access to water, supplementing the citadel's more monumental water features such as the Great Bath. At Dholavira, as Section 3.4 described, an elaborate system of stone-cut reservoirs, connected by channels and supplied by seasonal rainfall and stream runoff, addressed the particular water-scarcity challenges of the arid Kutch environment through large-scale storage rather than the well-based approach more typical of cities situated directly on the Indus floodplain. This variation illustrates an important general point: Harappan urban planners appear to have applied a shared set of underlying engineering principles — careful attention to water supply, waste removal, and civic infrastructure — while adapting specific solutions to local hydrology and available construction materials.

The consistency of Harappan brick proportions across this enormous geographic area deserves particular attention. Excavated bricks from sites separated by many hundreds of kilometers conform, with only minor variation, to a standardized ratio of length to width to thickness of approximately 4:2:1, a proportion well suited to stable, interlocking brick-laying and one that appears far too consistent across such distances to be coincidental. This standardization, alongside the standardized weight system examined in Section 3.6, constitutes some of the clearest material evidence for shared technical convention across the Mature Harappan world, and it raises directly the interpretive questions about political and administrative organization that Section 3.9 addresses.

At the same time, historians should resist over-interpreting this planning as evidence of rigid uniformity or centrally dictated urban design in every detail. Excavations reveal considerable variation in house size, room arrangement, and neighborhood density even within single cities, and, as noted above, Dholavira's more elaborately subdivided tripartite plan differs meaningfully from the urban arrangements documented at Mohenjo-daro and Harappa. Harappan city planning is best understood as the widespread application of shared engineering knowledge and civic priorities, expressed through locally adapted solutions, rather than as the imposition of an identical blueprint everywhere.

Section 3.6

Standardized Weights, Measures, and Production

Among all the evidence for material standardization across the Mature Harappan world, the civilization's system of weights provides perhaps the most compelling and most thoroughly studied example. Excavations at Harappan sites across the entire geographic range of the civilization — from Gujarat in the south to the Indus-Punjab core to sites in Afghanistan in the northwest — have recovered thousands of small cubical, and occasionally spherical or truncated-spherical, stone weights, most commonly fashioned from chert, that conform to a shared binary-based numerical series.

The smallest weights in this series follow a doubling pattern — 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, and 64 units of a base weight generally estimated at approximately 0.86 to 0.87 grams, though estimates vary somewhat depending on the specific set of excavated weights analyzed — before shifting, at higher values, to a decimal-based progression of 160, 200, 320, 640, and higher multiples. This combination of binary and decimal organization within a single coherent system, replicated with only minor local variation across such an enormous geographic area, represents an extraordinary achievement of shared technical and administrative convention. Weights of the smaller denominations, suitable for weighing valuable goods such as beads, shell, or precious materials, are especially common, while larger weights, presumably used for bulkier commodities, are somewhat less frequently recovered but follow the same underlying system.

The consistency of this weight system across such a vast area invites careful interpretation. It does not, by itself, prove the existence of a single centralized political authority capable of enforcing weight standards through law or administrative decree, since shared commercial convention can also emerge and spread through trade networks, craft-guild practice, or widely shared cultural consensus without requiring a unified state to impose it — a possibility examined further in Section 3.9. What the weight system does demonstrate clearly is that merchants, craftspeople, and communities across an enormous portion of the Indus world shared a common framework for measuring value and quantity, a framework stable and widespread enough to facilitate trade and exchange across very long distances with confidence that a given weight meant the same thing wherever it was used.

Standardization extended beyond weights to other aspects of craft production. As Section 3.5 noted, brick proportions were remarkably consistent across the Indus zone. Similarly, evidence from craft workshops indicates standardized techniques in areas such as bead-drilling, shell-cutting, and certain categories of pottery production, discussed further in Section 3.8, suggesting that craft knowledge itself, and not merely the tools used to measure finished goods, circulated and was shared across considerable distances, whether through the movement of craftspeople themselves, the training of apprentices in widely shared techniques, or some combination of both.

Linear measurement also shows evidence of standardization, most notably in a small number of recovered graduated scales, including an ivory scale from Lothal marked with fine, evenly spaced divisions, and a fragment of a shell scale from Mohenjo-daro, both suggesting a standardized system of linear measurement used in construction and craft production, complementing the weight-based system used for measuring mass. Taken together, this body of evidence — weights, brick proportions, craft techniques, and linear measures — constitutes some of the strongest material evidence available anywhere in the Bronze Age world for widespread, sustained technical standardization across an urban civilization's full geographic extent, and it forms an essential foundation for the debate over Harappan political organization examined in Section 3.9.

Primary Evidence Window

The Harappan Weight System

Evidence type: Excavated cubical stone weights, primarily chert · Distribution: Sites across the entire Mature Harappan geographic range, from Gujarat to Afghanistan · Recovered through: Systematic excavation at numerous Harappan sites over more than a century, with comparative metrological analysis conducted by multiple independent researchers

Context: Because no Harappan legal or administrative text survives to explain how weights were regulated, historians must reconstruct the weight system entirely through the comparative measurement and statistical analysis of thousands of recovered physical weights. Multiple independent studies, conducted using weights excavated at different sites and by different archaeological teams over many decades, have converged on broadly similar conclusions about the system's binary-to-decimal structure, which strengthens confidence in the basic pattern even though certain details, such as the precise value of the base unit, remain subject to ongoing refinement.

Description of the evidence: Cubical weights recovered from Harappan sites cluster around a base unit of approximately 0.86–0.87 grams, with smaller weights following a doubling series (1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64) and larger weights following a decimal-based progression (160, 200, 320, 640, and higher multiples), replicated with only minor local variation across sites separated by well over a thousand kilometers.

Analysis: This evidence demonstrates a genuinely shared technical convention across an enormous area, but it cannot, by itself, establish who created or enforced this standard, whether enforcement was centralized or decentralized, or what specific goods were most commonly weighed at any given site. Historians must therefore combine this metrological evidence with other categories of evidence — craft-production contexts, trade goods, and settlement patterns — to build a fuller, though still necessarily incomplete, picture of Harappan economic organization.

Section 3.7

Agriculture and the Urban Food Supply

No urban civilization of Mohenjo-daro's or Harappa's scale could have existed without a substantial and reliable agricultural base capable of supporting dense non-farming populations, including craftspeople, traders, religious specialists, and whatever administrative personnel oversaw the civic functions examined in Sections 3.5 and 3.6. Reconstructing Harappan agriculture depends chiefly on archaeobotanical evidence — recovered seeds, chaff, and plant impressions — supplemented by analysis of agricultural tools, storage facilities, and, more cautiously, comparison with better-documented later South Asian farming practices adapted to similar environments.

Harappan agriculture built directly on the crop and animal base established during the Early Harappan period examined in Chapter 2: wheat and barley remained staple cereal crops across much of the Indus zone, cultivated on the fertile alluvial soils renewed annually by seasonal flooding of the Indus and its tributaries, a pattern of flood-recession agriculture that did not necessarily require large-scale artificial irrigation works of the kind associated with, for example, ancient Mesopotamian canal systems, though small-scale water management, including possible use of wells and local channels, likely supplemented natural flooding in some areas. Sesame and various pulses (legumes) supplemented the cereal staples, adding valuable protein and oil to the diet.

Wheat and barley remained major staples across much of the Indus zone, but the Mature Harappan agricultural base diversified regionally: rice is attested at some eastern Harappan settlements, while millets and other summer crops became particularly important in Gujarat and other areas suited to monsoonal cultivation, reflecting the incorporation of crops better adapted to more easterly and southerly rainfall patterns than those of the northwestern borderlands. Rice and millets should be understood as significant regional contributions to a diversifying agricultural system rather than as staples of equal civilization-wide importance to wheat and barley. This regional dietary diversity is nonetheless consistent with this chapter's broader theme of a civilization that combined shared overarching cultural and technical conventions with genuine local and regional adaptation.

Livestock remained economically important throughout the Mature Harappan period. Cattle, whose local domestication history Chapter 2 traced, provided milk, traction for plowing and cartage, and, eventually, meat, and cattle imagery appears prominently on Harappan seals, discussed further in Chapter 4. Sheep and goats continued to be herded for meat, milk, and wool or hair, while evidence for domesticated water buffalo also appears during this period, an animal well suited to the wetter conditions of parts of the Indus floodplain and eastern Harappan zone.

The storage and redistribution of agricultural surplus remains one of the more debated aspects of Harappan economic organization, closely connected to the interpretive questions examined in Section 3.9. As Section 3.3 noted, the large compartmented structure at Mohenjo-daro traditionally labeled a "Granary" has an uncertain function, and no unambiguous, universally agreed-upon large-scale state granary has been confidently identified at any Harappan city, in contrast to the well-documented centralized grain-storage and redistribution systems attested in contemporary Mesopotamian temple and palace economies. This absence does not necessarily mean Harappan cities lacked organized agricultural storage; it may instead indicate that such storage was organized at a more local, household, or neighborhood level rather than through large centralized state granaries, a possibility consistent with the more decentralized models of Harappan political organization discussed in Section 3.9.

Transportation of agricultural goods within and between settlements likely relied on a combination of riverine transport, well suited to the Indus and its tributaries, and overland cartage, attested by terracotta models of carts with solid wheels recovered from multiple Harappan sites, indicating the use of animal-drawn wheeled vehicles, almost certainly drawn by cattle, for moving goods across the countryside and into urban centers. This transportation infrastructure, though far less monumental than the drainage systems examined in Section 3.5, was nonetheless essential to sustaining the urban populations this chapter has described.

Section 3.8

Craft Specialists and Long-Distance Trade

Building on the craft traditions traced through Mehrgarh and the Early Harappan sites examined in Chapter 2, Mature Harappan craft production reached a level of scale, specialization, and technical sophistication that placed the Indus Civilization among the most productive craft economies of the Bronze Age world. Excavated workshop areas at cities including Harappa, Mohenjo-daro, Dholavira, Lothal, and Chanhudaro reveal concentrated, often neighborhood-scale production of specific goods, suggesting genuine craft specialization organized, in many cases, by particular households or workshops rather than by individual households producing everything they needed.

Stone bead production reached extraordinary technical heights during this period. Harappan craftspeople produced beads from carnelian, agate, and other semi-precious stones using sophisticated techniques, including the heat-treatment of carnelian to achieve a desired reddish color and the use of specialized drills, likely tipped with extremely hard materials, capable of boring long, straight perforations through beads of considerable length — a technical achievement that some modern experimental archaeologists have found genuinely difficult to replicate using only the tools and materials available to Harappan craftspeople. Long carnelian beads of Harappan manufacture have been recovered at sites in Mesopotamia, providing direct material evidence of trade connections examined further below.

Shell-working, particularly using the shells of marine mollusks harvested along the Gujarat and Makran coasts, produced bangles, ladles, inlay, and other ornamental objects, with major shell-working centers identified at coastal or near-coastal sites such as Lothal and Nageshwar, and finished shell products distributed inland to cities such as Mohenjo-daro and Harappa, demonstrating an internal Harappan exchange network connecting coastal raw-material sources to inland urban consumers.

Metallurgy advanced considerably beyond the early copper-working traced at Mehrgarh in Chapter 2. Harappan metalworkers produced copper and bronze tools, weapons, vessels, and ornaments, working with copper likely sourced from Rajasthan and possibly from more distant sources including Oman, and with tin, necessary for producing true bronze, likely obtained through long-distance exchange networks reaching well beyond the Indus zone itself, since tin sources within South Asia are limited. Faience, a glazed, quartz-based ceramic material requiring considerable technical skill to produce, was used for beads, small figurines, and vessels, representing another area of specialized craft achievement.

Pottery production, building on the increasingly sophisticated ceramic traditions traced in Chapter 2, reached new levels of standardization during the Mature Harappan period, with a recognizable repertoire of forms, including distinctive black-on-red painted designs, produced at multiple centers across the Indus zone using broadly shared stylistic and technical conventions, another dimension of the material standardization examined throughout this chapter.

The Indus Civilization's trade connections extended considerably beyond its own geographic borders. To the northwest, connections with Afghanistan and Central Asia are associated with materials including lapis lazuli, sourced from the Badakhshan region. Shortughai, a Harappan-associated settlement in northern Afghanistan often interpreted as a trading outpost, lay near routes leading toward Badakhshan and other Central Asian resource zones, including sources of lapis lazuli; its precise role in the procurement of particular commodities, including tin, remains debated, since a documented Harappan presence at the site does not by itself establish exactly which materials moved through it or in what quantities. To the west and southwest, maritime trade connected the Indus coast to the Persian Gulf, Oman, and, ultimately, Mesopotamia. Mesopotamian cuneiform texts of the mid-third millennium BCE onward refer to a distant trading partner named Meluhha, generally identified by most scholars, though not with absolute unanimity, with the Indus region or its wider trading world; these texts describe Meluhhan traders, goods including carnelian, ivory, and various woods, and even, in a small number of references, individuals identified as being "of Meluhha" resident in Mesopotamian cities, suggesting some degree of established, ongoing commercial relationship rather than merely occasional, indirect contact.

Archaeological evidence corroborates this textual record: Indus-style carnelian beads, seals of a distinctive "Persian Gulf" or "Dilmun" style associated with intermediary trading communities in the Gulf region, and other Harappan-associated objects have been recovered at Mesopotamian sites, while Mesopotamian and Gulf materials appear, though less abundantly, at Indus sites, indicating exchange in both directions rather than a one-way flow of goods. This evidence situates the Indus Civilization firmly within a wider Bronze Age exchange network connecting South Asia, the Iranian plateau, the Persian Gulf, and Mesopotamia — a theme that anticipates, on a smaller and more regionally contained scale, the transregional connections Chapter 1 emphasized as a recurring feature of South Asian history across every period this textbook examines.

Section 3.9

Government without Known Kings

Having examined the cities, planning, standardization, agriculture, and trade of the Mature Harappan world, this chapter now confronts directly its most debated interpretive question: what kind of political authority organized and sustained this remarkable civilization? Students accustomed to studying ancient Egypt, with its well-documented pharaohs, or Mesopotamia, with its named kings, law codes, and administrative archives, may find the Indus evidence disorienting. No confirmed royal name, no securely identified palace, no unambiguous temple dedicated to a named deity, and no deciphered administrative or legal document has been recovered from any Mature Harappan site. This absence is a persistent pattern across every major excavated Harappan city, although preservation, incomplete excavation, and the still-undeciphered sign system remain important limitations that cannot be entirely ruled out as contributing factors. Even allowing for these limitations, the consistency of the pattern across so many extensively excavated sites requires historians to reason carefully about what kind of political organization is, and is not, consistent with the material evidence actually available.

Several competing scholarly models attempt to explain this evidence, and this textbook presents them as genuine, unresolved alternatives rather than favoring one as settled fact.

One model proposes a unified territorial state, governed from one or more major centers such as Mohenjo-daro and Harappa, capable of enforcing the striking standardization in weights, brick proportions, and craft techniques examined in Sections 3.5 through 3.8 across the entire Indus zone. Proponents of this view point to the sheer scale and consistency of standardization as evidence that some form of centralized authority, whether a single unified state or a small number of allied or hierarchically related centers, must have existed to establish and maintain such uniform conventions across so vast an area over so many centuries. Some versions of this model propose that Mohenjo-daro and Harappa may have functioned as twin or rotating capitals, though this remains speculative.

A second model proposes a network of independent or loosely federated city-states, each governing its own immediate hinterland while participating in a shared cultural and economic system that produced standardized weights, bricks, and craft techniques through sustained trade contact, shared craft-training traditions, and widespread cultural consensus rather than through centralized political enforcement. Proponents of this view note the absence of any securely identified imperial capital, the absence of evidence for a standing military capable of enforcing unified rule across such a vast territory, and the genuine regional variation documented in this chapter, particularly at Dholavira, as evidence against a single centralized state.

A third model, sometimes described as a heterarchical or non-state model, proposes that Harappan society may have been organized through multiple, overlapping sources of authority and social integration — perhaps including merchant associations, craft-based communities, religious or ritual specialists, and kinship or lineage-based structures — without any single dominant political hierarchy of the kind associated with kingship in Mesopotamia or Egypt. Proponents of this view point to the notable absence of grand palaces, the relatively modest scale of the largest identified residential structures compared to Mesopotamian or Egyptian royal architecture, and the absence of large-scale royal tombs or grave goods indicating dramatic status differentiation, as evidence that Harappan society may have distributed authority more broadly than a single royal or priestly elite.

Each model draws on genuine evidence, and each faces genuine difficulties. The centralized-state model struggles to identify unambiguous evidence of a ruling elite, a royal residence, or an administrative bureaucracy of the kind well documented in contemporary Mesopotamia. The city-state and heterarchical models struggle to explain how such extraordinarily consistent standardization in weights and brick proportions could have emerged and persisted for centuries across such an enormous area without some form of coordinating authority or, at minimum, an extremely robust and widely shared cultural convention functioning in place of formal political enforcement. Some archaeologists have proposed intermediate positions, suggesting, for example, that political authority may have been organized regionally, with several major centers each governing a substantial hinterland, while broader cultural and economic integration across the whole Indus zone was maintained primarily through trade networks and shared craft and religious traditions rather than through a single unified government.

This textbook does not resolve this debate, because the evidence currently available does not permit confident resolution. What can be said with reasonable confidence is this: whatever political system organized the Mature Harappan world, it evidently did not require, or did not produce, the kind of monumental, individually glorified kingship attested by Egyptian pyramids and pharaonic inscriptions or Mesopotamian royal palaces and dedicatory texts. Harappan civic investment appears concentrated instead in shared infrastructure — drainage, water storage, fortification, and standardized weights and measures — that benefited urban populations broadly rather than celebrating individual rulers. Whether this reflects a fundamentally different, perhaps more collective or corporate, form of political organization, or simply reflects the loss or as-yet-undiscovered nature of royal records and monuments that once existed, remains one of the most significant open questions in the study of the ancient world, and students should approach popular claims of certainty on this question, in either direction, with appropriate skepticism.

Section 3.10

Review and Historical Synthesis

This chapter has examined the emergence and character of the Mature Harappan urban civilization, beginning around 2600 BCE, through its great cities, its planning and engineering achievements, its economic foundations, and its unresolved political organization. Several threads developed separately across the preceding nine sections converge here into a single interpretive foundation for Chapter 4.

The urban-transformation argument of Section 3.1 established that the shift from Early Harappan regional traditions to Mature Harappan civilization represented a genuine, historically significant transformation in scale, planning, and material standardization, even though it built directly on the agricultural, craft, and settlement foundations traced in Chapter 2 rather than emerging from nothing. The comparative case studies of Sections 3.2 through 3.4 — Harappa, Mohenjo-daro, and Dholavira — demonstrated that this transformation produced genuine shared conventions across an enormous geographic area while still permitting substantial local adaptation in construction material, urban subdivision, and civic emphasis, a pattern of unity-with-variation that recurs throughout this chapter's treatment of planning, standardization, agriculture, and trade.

The infrastructure argument of Section 3.5 established that Harappan cities achieved an exceptionally high level of civic planning and water management, including some of the most extensive and systematic drainage systems known from the Bronze Age world, expressed through grid-oriented streets, standardized brick construction, and sophisticated drainage systems maintained through evident ongoing civic effort. The standardization argument of Section 3.6 extended this theme through the civilization's remarkably consistent weight system, brick proportions, and craft techniques, evidence that constitutes some of the strongest material proof available anywhere in the ancient world for sustained technical convention across a vast urban civilization, while also, importantly, not by itself resolving the question of how that convention was established or enforced.

The economic argument of Sections 3.7 and 3.8 established the agricultural and craft-and-trade foundations that made Harappan urbanism possible: an agricultural base centered on wheat and barley across much of the Indus zone, supplemented regionally by rice, millets, pulses, sesame, and managed livestock, adapted to different regional environments across the Indus zone, together with highly specialized craft industries in stone beadwork, shell-working, metallurgy, and ceramics, sustained by long-distance trade networks reaching Afghanistan, the Persian Gulf, and Mesopotamia, documented independently by both archaeological finds and, in the specific case of Meluhha, Mesopotamian textual evidence.

The political-organization argument of Section 3.9 established this chapter's central methodological lesson: extraordinary material evidence does not always resolve every important historical question, and honest historical writing must sometimes present genuine, unresolved scholarly debate rather than manufacturing false certainty. The absence of confirmed kings, palaces, and deciphered texts does not diminish the Indus Civilization's genuine historical significance; it requires historians to reason with unusual care from material evidence alone, exactly as Chapter 2 required for the pre-literate societies that preceded Harappan urbanism, though now applied to a civilization of far greater scale and sophistication.

Chapter 4 builds directly on this foundation, turning to Harappan society, religion, and the undeciphered Indus script examined only briefly in this chapter, together with the civilization's external connections to Mesopotamia and the Persian Gulf world and its gradual transformation, regionalization, and eventual decline as an integrated urban system after approximately 1900 BCE. Students should carry forward from this chapter a central lesson that will recur throughout this textbook: material sophistication and political or religious legibility to modern historians are not the same thing, and a civilization's silence on certain questions is not evidence of its insignificance.

Historical Debate

Was There a Harappan State?

The question: Did the Mature Harappan civilization possess a centralized political authority capable of enforcing standardized weights, brick proportions, and craft conventions across its vast territory, or did this standardization emerge and persist through decentralized trade networks, shared cultural convention, and regionally organized authority without any single unifying government?

Interpretation A — A centralized or closely integrated state: The extraordinary consistency of weights, brick proportions, and craft techniques across a territory larger than many modern countries is difficult to explain without some form of coordinating authority capable of establishing and enforcing shared standards over many generations. Even if no single "emperor" ruled the entire zone, some scholars propose a small number of major regional centers, possibly including Mohenjo-daro and Harappa, exercising authority over extensive hinterlands and coordinating with one another to maintain shared conventions.
Interpretation B — Decentralized cities integrated by trade and shared culture: No palace, royal tomb, dynastic inscription, or standing army has been confidently identified at any Harappan site, in sharp contrast to contemporary Mesopotamia and Egypt. The genuine regional variation visible at Dholavira, combined with the absence of dramatic architectural or mortuary evidence for a powerful ruling elite, suggests that Harappan cities may have functioned as largely independent or loosely connected communities, unified primarily through extensive trade networks, shared craft-training traditions, and widely diffused cultural and religious practice rather than through centralized political command.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on the same body of evidence — standardized weights and bricks, extensive trade networks, the absence of confirmed royal architecture, and regional variation among excavated cities — but weigh the significance of standardization versus the absence of monumental royal evidence differently.

Why it matters: This textbook presents both interpretations, along with intermediate heterarchical models, as genuinely unresolved, because the current archaeological and textual evidence does not permit confident resolution. This uncertainty is itself an important lesson about the limits of material evidence, however abundant, when it is not accompanied by decipherable texts.

Student task: Using the evidence presented in Sections 3.5, 3.6, and 3.9, construct an argument for each interpretation, and then explain what specific new archaeological discovery would most strengthen or weaken each position.

Historical Vocabulary
Urbanism and Standardization

Urbanism refers to a pattern of settlement characterized by large, dense population centers with specialized economic functions, distinguished from the smaller villages and towns examined in Chapter 2 by scale, planning, craft specialization, and typically by monumental or civic architecture. Standardization refers to the consistent replication of a particular form, proportion, or measurement — such as a brick's dimensions or a weight's mass — across many examples and, in the Harappan case, across a very wide geographic area.

Archaeologists treat standardization as significant evidence because it is unlikely to occur by chance; when thousands of independently manufactured objects across hundreds of kilometers conform to the same proportions or values, some shared knowledge, convention, or authority must explain that consistency, even if the precise mechanism — political enforcement, trade-network convention, or widely shared craft training — remains debated, as Section 3.9 discusses.

Historical Vocabulary
Seals and the Indus Sign System

Harappan seals, typically carved from steatite (soapstone) and often fired to harden them, generally combine a short inscription in the undeciphered Indus sign system with a carved image, most commonly an animal such as a humped bull ("unicorn" bull in the conventional archaeological description of a bull shown in profile with a single visible horn), water buffalo, elephant, or composite mythological creature. When pressed into soft clay, a seal produced a sealing, sometimes preserving the impression of cord, cloth, or other packaging material on its reverse, indicating that sealings were used to mark, secure, or authenticate goods or containers.

This textbook uses the term "sign system" alongside "script" for the Indus inscriptions because scholars have not reached consensus on whether these signs represent a full writing system capable of recording spoken language in the same sense as cuneiform or Egyptian hieroglyphics, or whether they instead functioned as a more limited system of symbols, such as clan or lineage marks, without directly transcribing language. Chapter 4 examines this question, and the broader evidence for Harappan writing, in greater depth.

A trader engaged in long-distance exchange: Seals bearing standardized inscriptions and sealings marked with the impressions of packaging materials suggest individuals responsible for authenticating and moving goods across considerable distances, whether by river, overland cart, or maritime vessel, connecting Harappan cities to distant markets in Afghanistan, the Persian Gulf, and Mesopotamia.
A farmer supplying an urban center: The scale of Harappan cities required a substantial agricultural hinterland; a farming household producing wheat, barley, or, in eastern regions, rice and millet, alongside managed cattle, sheep, or goats, would have supplied part of the surplus that sustained specialized urban craftspeople, traders, and whatever administrative or religious personnel organized civic life.
Continuity and Change Over Time

From Village to City

What Changed

  • Settlement scale increased dramatically, from Early Harappan villages and towns to cities housing, by some estimates, tens of thousands of people
  • Construction shifted toward standardized brick proportions and large-scale civic infrastructure, including drainage systems and, at some sites, elevated platforms and reservoirs
  • Craft production intensified and diversified, reaching new levels of technical sophistication in beadwork, shell-working, metallurgy, and faience
  • Long-distance trade expanded to include sustained, documented contact with the Persian Gulf and Mesopotamia, evidenced independently by both archaeology and Mesopotamian texts
  • A shared sign system, seals, and an extremely consistent weight system spread across an enormous geographic area

What Stayed the Same

  • The core agricultural base of wheat, barley, and managed livestock, established during the Early Harappan and earlier periods, remained fundamental to the urban economy
  • Mudbrick remained the dominant construction material, with fired brick reserved for specific high-value applications, continuing a technology traced back to Mehrgarh in Chapter 2
  • Regional variation persisted even within the broadly shared Mature Harappan cultural framework, as Dholavira's distinctive stone construction and reservoir system demonstrate
  • Craft specialization and long-distance exchange, already developing during the Early Harappan period, continued to expand along established patterns rather than beginning anew

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both change and continuity reinforces this textbook's recurring lesson that the rise of Indus civilization was a genuine transformation built upon, rather than disconnected from, the long developmental process traced in Chapter 2.

Cause and Effect

Why Cities Emerged around 2600 BCE

Long-term causes

Many centuries of agricultural development, craft specialization, and expanding exchange networks, traced through Mehrgarh and the Early Harappan traditions in Chapter 2, created the economic surplus and technical foundation necessary for urban life.

Short-term causes

Growing settlement size, increasing craft standardization, and intensifying regional exchange during the later Early Harappan period appear to have reached a threshold at which coordinated large-scale urban construction and shared technical convention became both possible and advantageous.

Effect

Cities such as Harappa, Mohenjo-daro, and Dholavira emerged with sophisticated planning, drainage, standardized weights and brick proportions, and expanded craft production and trade, sustaining dense urban populations across a vast geographic area.

This textbook's response

This chapter treats Mature Harappan urbanism as the outcome of long developmental processes traced in Chapter 2, while devoting Section 3.9 to the genuinely unresolved question of what political mechanism, if any, coordinated and sustained this transformation.

Historical complexity: The precise mechanism and pace of the Early-to-Mature Harappan transition remain debated among archaeologists; some evidence suggests a relatively rapid transformation over a few generations, while other evidence suggests a more gradual, uneven process varying by site, cautioning against a single simple narrative of sudden urban "invention."

Everyday Life and Social History

Daily Life in a Standardized Urban World

For an ordinary resident of Mohenjo-daro or Harappa, daily life would have unfolded within a civic environment of unusual consistency by the standards of the ancient world: streets laid out along a predictable grid, houses built to broadly similar proportions using standardized brick, and, for many households, access to private wells, bathing platforms, and a functioning drainage system connecting to the wider city. Food likely combined wheat- or barley-based staples with pulses, dairy and meat from managed livestock, and, depending on region, rice or millet, prepared and stored using the standardized ceramic vessels recovered in such abundance from Harappan sites.

Work likely varied considerably by neighborhood and household: some residents were engaged in agriculture in the city's surrounding hinterland, others in specialized craft production such as bead-making, shell-working, or metalworking, and others, plausibly, in trade, transportation, or whatever administrative or religious functions organized civic life, though, as Section 3.9 emphasized, historians cannot confidently reconstruct the specific social or political hierarchy that organized this labor. What the material evidence does support is a picture of urban life that, while certainly marked by some variation in house size and access to resources, does not show the extreme architectural contrasts between grand palatial residences and minimal housing documented in many other early urban civilizations, suggesting a society organized, at least materially, somewhat differently from the sharply stratified palace-and-temple economies of contemporary Mesopotamia.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the emergence, around 2600 BCE, of the Mature Harappan urban civilization, tracing its transformation from the Early Harappan regional traditions examined in Chapter 2 into one of the ancient world's most extensive and materially standardized urban societies. It has argued throughout that this transformation, however dramatic in its material results, built directly on many centuries of prior agricultural, craft, and settlement development rather than emerging suddenly or without antecedent.

The chapter examined three of the civilization's most thoroughly excavated cities: Harappa, the type-site that gave the civilization its name and preserves an unusually continuous occupation sequence from Early Harappan through Mature Harappan times; Mohenjo-daro, among the largest and most extensively studied Harappan cities, notable for its Great Bath, its contested "Granary," and its extensive residential architecture; and Dholavira, whose stone construction, distinctive tripartite urban plan, and elaborate reservoir system illustrate significant regional adaptation within the broader shared Harappan cultural tradition.

The chapter then examined the specific achievements that distinguish Mature Harappan civilization among its Bronze Age contemporaries: sophisticated city planning conventionally described in terms of an elevated "citadel" and a more extensive "lower town," though modern excavation has significantly complicated this once-tidy binary model by revealing domestic and industrial activity within elevated sectors and multiple distinct mounds within areas once treated as a single lower town; grid-oriented streets and drainage and water-management infrastructure among the most advanced known from the Bronze Age world, adapted to local environmental conditions; a strikingly consistent binary-to-decimal weight system, standardized brick proportions, and shared craft techniques replicated across an area larger than many modern countries; an agricultural base centered on wheat and barley and diversified regionally by rice, millets, pulses, sesame, and managed livestock, adapted to different environments across the Indus zone; and highly specialized craft industries in stone beadwork, shell-working, metallurgy, and ceramics, sustained by long-distance trade networks reaching Afghanistan, the Persian Gulf, and Mesopotamia, corroborated by both archaeological finds and Mesopotamian textual references to Meluhha.

The chapter's most significant interpretive discussion concerned Harappan political organization. Faced with an absence of confirmed royal names, palaces, or deciphered administrative texts, historians have proposed several competing models — a centralized or closely integrated territorial state, a network of independent or loosely federated city-states, and a heterarchical or non-state model distributing authority across multiple institutions — each drawing on genuine evidence and each facing genuine interpretive difficulties. This textbook has presented these models as an unresolved scholarly debate rather than adopting one as settled fact, using this uncertainty to reinforce a central methodological lesson: abundant material evidence does not automatically resolve every important historical question, particularly in the absence of decipherable texts.

Throughout, this chapter has emphasized that the Indus Civilization achieved genuine technical and civic sophistication — in planning, engineering, craft production, and long-distance trade — without leaving behind the kind of monumental, individually glorified kingship well documented in contemporary Egypt and Mesopotamia, a pattern that should not be read as evidence of lesser achievement but rather as evidence that early urban civilizations could organize political and social life in genuinely different ways. This lesson prepares students directly for Chapter 4, which turns to Harappan society, religion, the undeciphered Indus script, and the civilization's eventual transformation and regionalization after approximately 1900 BCE.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Mature Harappan
The urban phase of the Indus Civilization, beginning around 2600 BCE, characterized by large planned cities, standardized weights and brick proportions, and extensive craft production and trade.
Integration Era
An alternative archaeological term for the Mature Harappan period, emphasizing the widespread cultural and material integration achieved across the Indus zone during this phase.
Citadel mound
A conventional archaeological label for an elevated mound found at many Harappan cities, often built on a substantial mudbrick platform; excavation has shown these mounds also contained domestic and industrial activity, so the term should not be taken to imply a purely administrative or elite function.
Lower town
A conventional archaeological label for the more extensive urban areas of a Harappan city beyond its elevated "citadel" mound, typically organized along a grid-oriented street plan; at sites such as Mohenjo-daro this area is now understood to comprise several distinct mounds rather than a single unified sector.
Standardization
The consistent replication of a form, proportion, or measurement across many examples and, in the Harappan case, across a vast geographic area; among the strongest evidence for shared technical convention in the Indus world.
Baked brick
Fired mudbrick, used selectively in Harappan construction for structures requiring durability or moisture resistance, such as wells, drains, and bathing platforms.
Great Bath
A large, carefully constructed and waterproofed tank on the citadel mound at Mohenjo-daro, widely interpreted as having had ritual or communal significance.
"Granary" (contested term)
A large compartmented structure at Mohenjo-daro traditionally identified as a grain-storage facility, though this identification is now considered uncertain by many archaeologists.
Cubical weight system
The Harappan system of cubical stone weights following a binary-to-decimal numerical progression, replicated with striking consistency across the Indus zone.
Steatite seal
A carved stone (soapstone) object, typically combining an inscription in the undeciphered Indus sign system with an animal or symbolic image, used to produce impressions in clay.
Sealing
An impression made by pressing a seal into soft clay, sometimes preserving the impression of packaging materials, providing evidence for the use of seals in marking or securing goods.
Sign system
The still-undeciphered set of signs found on Harappan seals and other objects; scholars debate whether it constitutes a full writing system or a more limited symbolic system.
Reservoir system (Dholavira)
A network of large stone-cut water-storage tanks and channels at Dholavira, designed to capture seasonal rainfall and stream runoff in an arid environment.
Craft specialization
The organization of production so that particular households or workshops focus on specific goods, evidenced at Harappan sites by concentrated workshop areas for beadwork, shell-working, and metallurgy.
Carnelian bead
A semi-precious stone bead, often heat-treated to achieve a reddish color, representing one of the most technically sophisticated and widely traded Harappan craft products.
Faience
A glazed, quartz-based ceramic material used by Harappan craftspeople for beads, figurines, and vessels, requiring considerable technical skill to produce.
Meluhha
A name appearing in Mesopotamian cuneiform texts, generally identified by most scholars with the Indus region or its wider trading world.
Heterarchy
A model of social or political organization in which authority is distributed across multiple, overlapping institutions rather than concentrated in a single hierarchical structure; one proposed model for Harappan political organization.
City-state model
A model of Harappan political organization proposing independent or loosely federated cities, each governing its own hinterland, integrated primarily through trade and shared culture rather than centralized government.
Settlement hierarchy
Differences in size and function among settlements within a region, used by archaeologists to infer economic or administrative relationships among Harappan cities, towns, and villages.
Bronze Age
An archaeological period defined by the widespread use of bronze for tools and weapons; the Indus Civilization is generally classified within the wider Bronze Age world of contemporary Mesopotamia and Egypt.
Harappa
Major Mature Harappan city on the Ravi River in Punjab, the type-site that gave the civilization its name, examined in Section 3.2.
Mohenjo-daro
Major Mature Harappan city on the Indus River in Sindh, notable for its scale, planning, and drainage, examined in Section 3.3.
Dholavira
Major Mature Harappan city in Kutch, Gujarat, notable for its stone construction and reservoir system, examined in Section 3.4.
Lothal
Harappan site in coastal Gujarat notable for craft workshops and a large brick structure sometimes interpreted as a dockyard, though this identification remains debated.
Shortughai
A Harappan-associated settlement in northern Afghanistan, often interpreted as a trading outpost near routes toward Badakhshan and Central Asian resource zones; its precise role in procuring specific commodities remains debated.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has asked students to grapple with a genuinely unusual historical situation: a civilization whose material achievements are extraordinarily well documented and whose political and religious life remains, in crucial respects, unknown. Learning to hold both of these facts at once — genuine admiration for Harappan engineering, craft, and economic organization, alongside genuine humility about what cannot yet be known regarding Harappan kingship, law, or belief — is precisely the kind of disciplined historical thinking this textbook asks students to practice throughout.

The Mature Harappan world examined in this chapter did not emerge from nothing; it emerged from long regional developmental processes traced in Chapter 2, including the farming communities represented by Mehrgarh and the increasingly complex Early Harappan traditions of the greater Indus zone. Yet what it achieved — cities planned with striking consistency across a vast territory, drainage systems among the most advanced known from the Bronze Age world, a shared weight system spanning well over a thousand kilometers, and trade networks reaching Mesopotamia — represents a genuine historical transformation, one of the earliest and most remarkable urban achievements anywhere in the ancient world.

Students who understand that material sophistication does not always translate into interpretive clarity, that historians must sometimes present genuine scholarly disagreement rather than manufactured certainty, and that a civilization's silence about its kings and gods does not diminish the significance of what it built, are well prepared to encounter Chapter 4's continued examination of Harappan society, religion, writing, and the civilization's eventual transformation after 1900 BCE — a transformation that, like the rise examined in this chapter, unfolded gradually and unevenly rather than through sudden collapse.

Chapter 4 now turns to that fuller social and religious portrait, and to the long process of change that followed the urban world this chapter has described.
