Part VII — Regional Kingdoms and the Early Medieval Order
35

Rajput Polities and the Changing Northwest

Indian History: South Asia from the Indus Civilization to the Present

Chapter Opening

On a hill above the Khajuraho temples, and in the court poems of Malwa and Ajmer, kings of the tenth and twelfth centuries told remarkably similar stories about themselves. They were descended from the sun or the moon, or from a hero who had risen from a sacrificial fire. They were warriors, protectors of the cow and the Brahmin, and masters of impregnable hills. These stories were not neutral history. They were instruments of political self-definition, composed by courts that had recently risen from subordinate positions and needed to explain why they now ruled in their own right.

Chapter 34 showed how the Gurjara-Pratiharas, Palas, and Rashtrakutas contested Kannauj, and how their imperial systems depended on subordinate rulers whose loyalty could be won but not guaranteed. This chapter asks what those subordinate houses became. Across Rajasthan, Malwa, Bundelkhand, and the northwestern plains, families that had begun as military servants, local magnates, or governors of frontier districts built territorial kingdoms of their own. By the eleventh and twelfth centuries, several of them, among them the Chahamanas (Chauhans), Chandellas, and Paramaras, ruled substantial states with fortified capitals, monumental temples, and sophisticated courts.

Later history gave many of these ruling houses a collective name: Rajput. The word carries heavy weight. In popular memory, Rajputs are a single warrior people of ancient lineage, defined by honor, valor, and resistance to conquest. This chapter treats that image as a historical product to be explained rather than a starting point. “Rajput” as a broad social and political category developed gradually, and the chapter's central argument is that Rajput political identity emerged from the regionalized political world of early medieval northern and western India. Warrior lineages converted military service, local landed power, dynastic genealogy, marriage alliances, fortified centers, and royal patronage into claims of sovereignty.

The Chahamanas, Chandellas, and Paramaras were therefore not remnants of a single ancient Rajput nation. They were products of a long process of state formation, and each had its own political trajectory, resources, rivals, and culture. Later memories, especially bardic traditions, colonial writings, and modern heritage narratives, often transformed complex medieval histories into simpler heroic stories. Recovering the earlier history requires separating contemporary evidence from what came afterward.

The evidence is rich but demands care. Inscriptions on stone and copper record genealogies, grants, and victories. Sanskrit court epics such as the Navasahasankacharita and the Prithviraja Vijaya shaped, and were shaped by, royal self-presentation. Temples, lakes, and forts survive as material evidence of royal ambition. Persian chronicles, written mostly after these kingdoms met Turkic armies, offer an outside and often hostile view. Much later bardic compilations, such as the Prithviraj Raso, preserve a powerful literary memory but are unreliable guides to the twelfth century.

This chapter is bounded by its neighbors. Chapter 34 owns the Pratiharas as an imperial dynasty and the Kannauj contest. Chapter 36 owns Kashmir and the Himalayan states. Chapter 37 owns the broader analysis of land grants, temple economies, and the feudalism debate. And Chapter 39 owns Mahmud of Ghazni, Muhammad of Ghor, and the Battles of Tarain. The chapter therefore brings the Chauhan kingdom to the eve of the Ghurid confrontation and then stops, pointing forward.

Historical Context

The political landscape of this chapter emerged from the regionalization described in Chapters 31 through 34. After Harsha, northern India was a field of competing regional kingdoms. The Gurjara-Pratiharas rose from Rajasthan and Malwa to dominate Kannauj in the ninth century, while the Rashtrakutas intervened from the Deccan and the Palas from the east. All three governed through subordinate rulers (samantas) who attended court, provided troops, and received honors. When Pratihara supremacy weakened in the tenth century, the subordinate houses stood on their own regional foundations.

The geography is varied. The Aravalli range runs from Gujarat toward Delhi, dividing the arid Thar to the west from the better-watered lands to the east. Rajasthan includes desert, hills, salt lakes such as Sambhar, and rivers such as the Luni, Banas, and Chambal. Malwa is a plateau of fertile black soils between the Aravallis, the Vindhyas, and the Narmada, linking Gujarat's ports with the Ganges plain. Bundelkhand, to the east, is a rocky, forested region on the Vindhya fringe drained by rivers such as the Betwa and Ken. These regions differ in resources, but all lie along trade corridors and frontier zones, and all offered defensible hills and strong points.

Much of the political vocabulary was inherited. Kings used titles such as maharajadhiraja and paramabhattaraka, issued land grants to Brahmins and temples, and patronized court poets who composed Sanskrit genealogies. Earlier inscriptions, including those of the Pala and Pratihara courts, list officials called rajaputras, a term that appears to have meant a prince or high-ranking noble, not an ethnic group. Understanding how the word moved from rank to community is part of the chapter's task.

Evidence from this period comes from inscriptions, copper-plate grants, court epics, temple and fort remains, coins, and, from the eleventh century onward, Persian accounts. Each has limits. Inscriptions glorify patrons and claim victories that may not have occurred or had lasting effects. Genealogies were constructed to serve present purposes. Persian sources were written by authors with their own audiences and, in most cases, after conflict had begun. Later traditions, especially bardic compilations and colonial-era writings, stand at a further remove. Dates for most rulers are approximate and sometimes disputed, and this chapter uses “c.” accordingly.

Finally, northwestern India was connected to a wider world. Merchants moved goods between Gujarat's ports and the Ganges plain. Horses came from the northwest and across the Arabian Sea. Turkic and Afghan powers were consolidating in Ghazni and later Ghur, as Chapter 39 will describe. The Rajasthan-Malwa-Bundelkhand states of this chapter were therefore regional powers within a changing northwestern frontier, not an isolated or unchanging world.

Section 35.1

The Problem of Rajput Origins

Historical Vocabulary
Rajput and Rajaputra

Rajaputra (“king's son”) is a Sanskrit term found in inscriptions of the early medieval period, including Pala and Pratihara charters, where it denotes a prince or a member of the ruling aristocracy, often in lists of officials. Rajput is the later vernacular and Persian form. In the later medieval period it came to denote a broad community of warrior and landholding families claiming Kshatriya status.

The shift from rank to community was gradual and is part of what this chapter explains. The term should not be used for the tenth and eleventh centuries as though it already named a single people.

The question “Where did the Rajputs come from?” has long been debated, and the debate shows how historical interpretation is shaped by assumptions. The nineteenth-century writer James Tod, drawing on bardic and genealogical traditions, presented Rajputs as the heirs of ancient Kshatriyas, and also suggested possible Scythian links. Later British scholars, notably Vincent Smith, argued that many Rajput clans descended from foreign groups, such as the Hunas and Gurjaras, who entered northwestern India in the fifth and sixth centuries and were gradually absorbed into Hindu society as Kshatriyas. Indian historians, among them G. H. Ojha, countered with arguments for continuous Kshatriya descent, and D. R. Bhandarkar proposed Brahmin origins for some lineages.

Each of these explanations rests on a shared assumption: that a single origin must be found for the whole category. Modern scholarship has increasingly challenged that assumption. A prominent line of interpretation, associated with the historian B. D. Chattopadhyaya's work on early medieval Rajasthan, looks instead at process. It emphasizes how local chiefs, military families, and landholders, drawn from diverse backgrounds, gained political and economic power, acquired genealogies, and secured recognition as Kshatriyas, a process that formed part of wider early medieval state formation.

On this view, “origin” is the wrong question for most lineages. The better question is how particular families rose, which resources they commanded, which alliances they made, and how they presented themselves. The evidence cannot settle the ancestry of every clan, and for most it is silent. What can be shown is that the dynasties of this chapter rose in specific regional contexts, from specific political positions, over the course of several centuries.

Historical Debate

Where Did the Rajputs Come From?

The question: Are Rajput clans descended from an ancient Kshatriya class, from foreign groups absorbed into Indian society, or from a variety of local groups who acquired Kshatriya status through political success?

Interpretation A — Descent from older groups (Kshatriya or foreign): Traditional and some colonial-era accounts sought a single ancestral stock, either the ancient solar and lunar Kshatriyas of the epics or foreign peoples such as the Hunas, Gurjaras, and Scythians who entered the northwest. Evidence offered includes dynastic genealogies, the appearance of Gurjara and Huna groups in early inscriptions, and the political prominence of the Gurjara-Pratiharas.
Interpretation B — Process of state formation and status acquisition: More recent scholarship emphasizes that families of varied backgrounds, including local chiefs, military servants, and landholders, rose through service and conquest, then adopted Kshatriya identity through genealogy, ritual, marriage, and patronage. Evidence includes the diversity of early inscriptional claims, the contested and changing character of genealogies, the use of rajaputra as a rank, and the gradual spread of the word “Rajput” as a community label.

Evidence: Both interpretations use the same inscriptions, literary genealogies, and later traditions. They differ over whether the foreign-origin claims reflect actual descent or later assimilation language, and over whether genealogical statements preserve memory or invention. For most clans, direct evidence of ancestry does not survive.

Why it matters: The question affects how the category “Rajput” is used. If it names a single people, then medieval politics looks like the actions of a unified nation. If it names a process, then political history is about diverse states and the construction of identity. This textbook adopts the process view as better supported while acknowledging that questions about particular lineages remain open.

Section 35.2

From Warrior Lineage to Rajput Identity

If origin is not the most useful question, then the formation of identity is. Several processes worked together across centuries. Military service was the first. Rulers needed commanders and garrison holders, and rewarded them with land, revenue rights, and honors. Over generations, such families came to hold territories as hereditary possessions. Landholding followed: control of villages and their revenue gave families the resources to support followers, build forts, and make grants of their own.

Political office and title came next. Inscriptions record families moving from the rank of samanta to mahasamanta and then to the imperial titles of maharajadhiraja and paramabhattaraka, a progression that signaled and helped produce independence. At the same time, families acquired and elaborated genealogies tracing descent from the sun, moon, or heroes of the epics, a topic examined in the next section. Marriage alliances tied rising families to established ones, spreading and reinforcing status claims.

The result was a distinction that historians insist on: between lineage claims and historically demonstrable origins. A dynasty's inscription might say that its founder arose from a sacrificial fire or was born in the solar line. These statements tell historians what the dynasty wished to be and how it wished to be seen. They do not establish who the founder actually was. Conversely, the first appearance of a family in the record, often as a subordinate in someone else's charter, tells us about its position then, but not about its earlier history.

Identity continued to change after the period of this chapter. Persian sources from the thirteenth century onward use the term Rajput for Hindu warrior and landholding groups, and the Mughal period (Chapters 50 through 56) saw further redefinition as Rajput rulers entered imperial service. The historian Dirk Kolff has shown that, in later centuries, Rajput status could be open to people who gained military and political success, which reinforces the view that the identity was a social process. For the tenth to twelfth centuries, historians therefore speak of “Rajput” cautiously, as a retrospective category for dynasties and lineages whose descendants later fell within that community.

Section 35.3

Genealogy, Kshatriya Status, and the Agnikula Tradition

Historical Vocabulary
Agnikula

Agnikula (“fire lineage”) refers to a tradition that certain warrior families originated from a sacrificial fire-pit (agnikunda). Its earliest literary form, associated with the Paramaras of Malwa, tells of a hero produced from a fire-pit on Mount Abu. Later traditions, especially the Prithviraj Raso in its later versions, extended the story to four clans, among them the Paramaras, Chahamanas, Chaulukyas (Solankis), and Pratiharas.

Historians treat the tradition as a political and literary construction, not as an account of biological ancestry. The idea that all four clans shared a single fire origin is later than the earliest evidence.

Genealogy was central to royal legitimacy. Early medieval courts valued two main models of descent: the solar line (Suryavamsha), descending from the sun and the epic hero Rama, and the lunar line (Chandravamsha), descending from the moon and the heroes of the Mahabharata. The Chandellas, for example, claimed lunar descent. Such claims linked dynasties to a heroic past and secured recognition as Kshatriyas. They also had practical uses, since sovereignty over land and subordinates was easier to defend if a king's ancestors could be shown to have been kings.

The Agnikula story belongs to this tradition of legitimizing narratives. It is notable because it is both early and specific, appearing in a court poem composed for a Paramara king, and because later writers extended it into a larger scheme. The fact that the early version names only the Paramaras, and that its extension to other clans comes later, is evidence that the story grew in the telling. Other houses used different stories. Some Chahamana inscriptions trace their line to an ancestral founder who appeared at Shakambhari, and later Chahamana works give still other versions.

Primary Source Window

A Court Poem and a Later Heroic Tradition: The Paramara Fire-Pit Story

Source types: A Sanskrit court epic (mahakavya) and a later heroic poem · Primary example: Padmagupta's Navasahasankacharita, composed c. 1005 at the Paramara court of Sindhuraja · Compared with: the later Prithviraj Raso tradition

Context: Padmagupta, a court poet, wrote a poem about the exploits of Sindhuraja, also called Navasahasanka, and included an account of the dynasty's origin. Because it was composed for the king, it presents the dynasty as having been created for a heroic purpose. Some early Paramara records, by contrast, associate the family with service to Rashtrakuta rulers, which suggests the family had a subordinate past that the origin story does not mention.

Paraphrase of content: The poem relates that the sage Vasishtha, whose cow had been seized by the sage Vishvamitra at Mount Abu, performed a sacrifice, and a warrior emerged from the fire-pit who recovered the cow. The sage gave him the name Paramara, interpreted as “slayer of enemies.” The line of Malwa's kings is then traced from this hero. The Prithviraj Raso tradition, composed and reworked centuries later, expands the fire-pit story into a creation of several warrior clans, tying their origins to a shared sacrifice and a shared purpose of defending sacred order.

Analysis: The earlier text is evidence for how a king's court presented his dynasty in about 1005. It is not evidence for the actual origin of the Paramaras, and it contradicts the picture of subordinate beginnings found elsewhere. The later tradition is evidence for how the story grew, and for the way a regional origin myth became a pan-Rajput narrative. Neither text allows historians to reconstruct the ancestry of the families it describes. Both show that genealogy was an active political tool.

Questions for students:

  1. What does the earlier poem say about the Paramara origin, and who commissioned it?
  2. Why would a dynasty prefer a heroic fire-pit origin to an account of subordinate service?
  3. What can historians learn from comparing the early and later versions?
  4. What can historians not establish about actual ancestry from either text?
Section 35.4

From Samantas to Sovereigns: State Formation after Pratihara Supremacy

Chapter 34 explained how Pratihara and Pala power depended on subordinate rulers. This section asks how some of those subordinates turned their positions into kingdoms. The Chahamanas emerged within the Pratihara political sphere. The Harsha inscription of 973 traces their genealogy back to Guvaka I, whom some scholars have identified as a feudatory associated with Nagabhata II, although the relevant passage has been interpreted differently. They later reached independence and imperial titles. The Chandellas begin as rulers in the Bundelkhand region under Pratihara overlordship, and tradition credits Chandella Harsha with helping a Pratihara king regain Kannauj after the Rashtrakuta attack. The Paramaras began under Rashtrakuta, not Pratihara, overlords. In each case, a change in the fortunes of the overlord, whether Indra III's attack on Kannauj or the later Rashtrakuta decline, opened space for the subordinate.

The pattern had several features. First, the families accumulated land and followers in defensible regions. Second, they used the rivalries among larger powers to shift allegiance, to withhold tribute, or to claim titles. Third, they secured legitimacy through genealogy, temple building, and grants to Brahmins and religious institutions. Fourth, they formed marriage alliances with neighboring houses, building networks that could support them in conflicts. Finally, they built or captured forts that served as capitals and refuges.

State formation here did not mean the construction of centralized bureaucracies. Rulers governed through hereditary kinsmen, local chiefs, and officials who often held land. Authority was layered: a king might claim the titles of a universal sovereign while depending on a ring of subordinates who in turn had their own followers. This was the same structure the Pratiharas had used, now reproduced on a smaller regional scale. The new states were therefore heirs to the Pratihara political method as well as beneficiaries of Pratihara decline.

Cause and Effect

Why Subordinate Houses Became Sovereign Dynasties

Long-term causes

Regional resource bases and defensible territories; political structures built on subordinate rulers; inherited conventions of royal titles, grants, and genealogy.

Short-term causes

Weakening of Pratihara and Rashtrakuta overlords after the early tenth century; succession disputes; opportunities to shift allegiance.

Immediate effects

Assumption of imperial titles; construction of capitals and temples; new marriage alliances and rivalries among neighboring houses.

Long-term consequences

A multipolar northwest and center of competing kingdoms, each with regional identity, which later faced Turkic armies one at a time.

Historical complexity: No single factor explains the outcome. Individual leaders, local conditions, and chance mattered, and not every subordinate house succeeded. Many remained local magnates or disappeared from the record.

Section 35.5

Rajasthan: Environment, Routes, and the Geography of Power

Rajasthan's politics cannot be understood without its environment. The Aravalli range, one of the oldest mountain systems in India, runs southwest to northeast and divides the state into two broad zones. West and north of it lie the Thar and the semi-arid plains, with low rainfall and scarce water. East and south of it, rainfall is better, and rivers such as the Banas and Chambal support agriculture. Water sources, whether lakes, wells, tanks, or rivers, were limited and valuable, and the control of them gave political power.

Resources were uneven. Salt from the lake at Sambhar was a significant commodity, and the Shakambhari region took its name, and its goddess, from the area. The Aravallis contained copper, lead, and other metals, and the hills offered natural strong points. Pastoral populations and mobile warrior groups used the arid zones, and horse breeding and trade were important. Agriculture was strongest along rivers and in the eastern plains, but pastoralism and trade were often more important to the western economy.

Routes mattered as well. Overland trade connected Gujarat's ports, such as Cambay, with the Ganges plain, and passed through Rajasthan and Malwa. Roads also connected Sindh and the Punjab with the interior, and these were used by merchants, armies, and pilgrims. The Pushkar lake and Mount Abu were pilgrimage and ritual centers. A king who controlled a pass, a lake, a fort, and a stretch of road could tax caravans, extract tribute, and support an army. This geography encouraged many small and medium states instead of one large one, and it favored defensible capitals.

Section 35.6

Forts, Capitals, and the Making of Regional States

Historical Vocabulary
Durga

Durga is the Sanskrit term for a fort or fortified place, and one of the seven elements of the state in classical Indian political thought. Treatises distinguished types, including hill forts (parvata), water forts, forest forts, and desert forts, each suited to a particular terrain.

In this chapter the word is used for fortified centers that housed rulers, garrisons, stores, and water supplies, and that served as symbols of sovereignty.

Forts were central to the political life of these states, and should be understood as institutions rather than decoration. A fort provided a refuge in war and a base for controlling surrounding districts. It housed garrisons, granaries, treasuries, wells and tanks, and often temples and palaces. Its position on a hill or along a route allowed rulers to control movement and collect dues. Possession of a famous fort carried prestige: Kalinjar in Bundelkhand, Gwalior, Mandu in Malwa, and the hill at Ajmer were names that rulers boasted of holding.

Capitals did not always coincide with forts. Dhara, the Paramara capital, was a city on the Malwa plain, while the nearby hill fort of Mandu offered refuge. Ajmer was founded as a fortified city, according to Chahamana tradition, by a king named Ajayaraja, and its hill fortress dominated the plain. Mahoba and Kalinjar served the Chandellas in complementary ways, one as a city with great lakes and the other as a hill fortress.

It is important not to turn forts into an unchanging Rajput tradition. Many forts were older than the dynasties that held them and passed among rulers repeatedly. The idea of a hill fort as the essential expression of Rajput identity belongs in part to later memory and to the later Mughal and princely-state periods. In the tenth to twelfth centuries, forts were components of a broader strategy, which included field armies, alliances, and trade control.

Multiple Perspectives

A Fortified Hill and Its Neighbors

Note: these perspectives are analytical reconstructions based on the kinds of evidence discussed in this chapter, not quotations.

The ruling house: A fort secured the dynasty's person, treasury, and prestige. Holding it was a statement of sovereignty, and losing it could end a reign, which is why inscriptions emphasize captured and defended strongholds.
Garrison troops and local chiefs: Service in a fort meant land grants, stipends, or honors, and tied families to the dynasty. Chiefs who held outlying posts could also use them as bases for independence.
Peasants and artisans of the district: A fort was both protection and burden. Villagers might shelter nearby in danger, but they also supplied grain, labor, and taxes, and bore the cost of campaigns and sieges. The sources, written for courts, rarely record their views.
Merchants and caravan traders: A fort that controlled a route offered security but also exacted tolls. Traders favored stable rulers who protected roads and sometimes financed them.
Rival rulers and invading armies: For besiegers, a fort was an obstacle that demanded time, supplies, and engineering. Many sieges ended through negotiation, tribute, or withdrawal rather than conquest.
Section 35.7

The Gurjara-Pratihara Legacy

The successor houses inherited a political and cultural toolkit from the Pratihara world. They inherited titles: the imperial formulas of maharajadhiraja and parameshvara, which smaller kings now claimed. They inherited a royal Sanskrit culture, with courts that supported poets, grammarians, and dramatists, such as Rajashekhara at the Pratihara court, whose works circulated across the region. They also inherited the method of ruling through subordinate chiefs, discussed in Chapter 34.

Military organization continued as well. The Pratiharas were known for cavalry, and their successors maintained mounted forces alongside elephants and infantry. Coinage reflected continuity: coin types circulated in the region that drew on earlier models, and later Chahamana coins used the bull-and-horseman design common across northwestern India, a type that outlived the dynasties that issued it. Finally, the idea of Kannauj as the center of imperial overlordship survived. Later dynasties, including the Gahadavalas (Chapter 34), claimed it, and several of the houses in this chapter had campaigned there.

The legacy also included memory and ambition. The Chahamanas, Chandellas, and Paramaras each aspired to wider sovereignty, and each competed with others for it. None created a successor empire on the Pratihara scale, in part because the Pratihara experience had shown how hard that was. This chapter treats them as regional powers that were conscious of belonging to a larger political world but were not aiming to reconstruct a single empire.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From Pratihara Subordinates to Sovereign Kingdoms

What Changed

  • Subordinate houses became independent dynasties with their own capitals and titles
  • Political power moved from a single imperial center to a set of competing regional courts
  • Temples, lakes, and forts became the dynasties' own monuments, marking their sovereignty
  • Genealogies were developed to establish Kshatriya standing

What Stayed the Same

  • Rule through subordinates and layered authority
  • Reliance on land grants, Sanskrit courts, and imperial titles
  • The importance of cavalry, elephants, and defensible places
  • The regional resource bases of western and central India

Who experienced change most directly? Courts and military families, whose status and titles changed. Who experienced continuity? Most cultivators and artisans, who continued to pay revenue and supply labor under new masters. Why it matters: It shows that the breakup of an empire changed the political map more than it changed the structure of power.

Section 35.8

The Rise of the Chahamanas (Chauhans)

The Chahamanas, later called Chauhans, first appear in the record as a regional lineage around Shakambhari, the area of the Sambhar salt lake in eastern Rajasthan. Their early history relies heavily on later inscriptions and literary works that trace the dynasty back several centuries, to figures whose historicity is uncertain. The stronger evidence begins in the ninth and tenth centuries, when Chahamana rulers appear as subordinates of Pratihara kings. The Harsha inscription of 973, issued in the reign of Vigraharaja II, is one of the most informative early records, listing earlier rulers and recording victories and temple patronage.

The dynasty's rise was gradual. Control of the salt lake gave it a valuable resource, and its location between the desert, the Aravalli hills, and the plains toward Delhi and Malwa gave it strategic reach. Over the next two centuries the Chahamanas fought with neighbors, including the Tomaras of Delhi region, the Chaulukyas of Gujarat, and Paramara and Guhila rulers, in conflicts that shifted in outcome. The shift of the main seat from Shakambhari to Ajmer, associated by tradition with the king Ajayaraja in the early twelfth century, provided a stronger, more defensible capital on a hill above the plain.

The decisive expansion came under Arnoraja and, above all, Vigraharaja IV (Visaladeva, c. 1150–1164). Arnoraja faced conflict with the Chaulukya king Kumarapala of Gujarat, which Chaulukya sources present as a Chaulukya victory. Vigraharaja IV extended Chahamana power eastward and northward. The Bijolia inscription credits him with the conquest of Delhi from the Tomaras, while his inscription on the Ashokan Topra/Siwalik pillar claims wider supremacy between the Vindhyas and the Himalayas. He is also remembered as a patron of letters: he is credited with a Sanskrit play, the Harakeli Nataka, and plays inscribed on stone slabs were later found at Ajmer, where a structure later converted into a mosque has long been associated with a Sanskrit school. The claims in his inscriptions of ruling a vast territory are, like all royal claims, partly rhetorical, but the shift of Chahamana power from a regional base to a kingdom with interests across northwestern India is clear.

The Chahamanas thus illustrate the process described earlier in the chapter. A family that began as a subordinate in a regional landscape controlled a valuable resource, moved to a defensible capital, used war and marriage to expand, and presented itself in the language of royal sovereignty. They should not be called a Rajput state in the sense of a unified Rajput polity. They were one of several regional kingdoms that competed with each other as much as with outsiders.

Section 35.9

Ajmer, Delhi, and Prithviraj III

Prithviraj III, who came to the throne around 1177 as a young ruler, has become one of the best-known figures in Indian historical memory, far more than the surviving record of his reign justifies. The historically recoverable Prithviraj ruled a kingdom centered on Ajmer, with Delhi as a northern frontier possession, and spent much of his reign in conflicts with neighboring Indian kingdoms. He initially ruled with the guidance of his mother, Karpuradevi, a Kalachuri princess, and faced challenges from relatives, including a cousin who rebelled and was suppressed.

His campaigns included a conflict with the Chaulukyas of Gujarat under Bhima II, which appears to have ended in an arrangement rather than decisive victory. His best-attested military action against another Indian power was the invasion of the Chandella kingdom of Jejakabhukti in 1182 to 1183, recorded in inscriptions at Madanpur. The Chandella ruler Paramardi was defeated in the field but was not annexed, and the kingdom survived. The episode shows a northwestern India in which regional kings were fighting each other, and in which gaining prestige and tribute, not permanent conquest, was often the goal.

For Prithviraj's court, a major contemporary source is the Sanskrit poem Prithviraja Vijaya, composed around 1191 to 1193 by a court poet named Jayanaka, which survives incompletely and was discovered in Kashmir in the nineteenth century. It celebrates the king and his ancestors and must be read as court panegyric. For the Ghurid side, Persian chroniclers such as Hasan Nizami and Minhaj Siraj wrote accounts that mention the Chahamana ruler, though the details and tone differ. These materials will be examined in Chapter 39, which owns the Ghurid conquests and the Battles of Tarain.

This chapter therefore ends Prithviraj's story at the threshold. By around 1190 the Chahamana kingdom stretched from Ajmer to the Delhi region and bordered the expanding Ghurid power in the Punjab. A major confrontation was coming, and its outcome would reshape the northwest. But the narrative of that confrontation belongs to Chapter 39. What this chapter emphasizes is that Prithviraj was one regional king among several, not the leader of a united Hindu resistance, a framing that belongs to later memory.

Evidence and Memory

Prithviraj III: What Survives, and What Was Added

Near-contemporary evidence: inscriptions of Chahamana and Chandella kingdoms, including those at Madanpur; the Prithviraja Vijaya, a court poem composed during or just after his reign; coins; and Persian chronicles written by authors on the Ghurid side. These sources agree that Prithviraj ruled from Ajmer, fought neighboring kingdoms, and faced the Ghurids. They disagree about details and emphasis, and each reflects its author's patron.

Later memory: the Prithviraj Raso, a long Hindi-Brajbhasha-Rajasthani heroic poem attributed to the court poet Chand Bardai, is the main source of the popular image. It developed over centuries, in multiple versions, and contains episodes that do not match the historical record, including the romantic abduction of a princess and a dramatic dying revenge. Its chronology and genealogy are unreliable for the twelfth century.

Why the distinction matters: the poem is real historical evidence, but for the society that composed and reworked it, not for Prithviraj's own time. A historian can use it to study how later communities imagined a heroic past, while relying on inscriptions and near-contemporary texts to reconstruct the reign itself.

Section 35.10

The Chandellas of Jejakabhukti

The Chandellas ruled Jejakabhukti, the region of Bundelkhand, from the ninth century into the early thirteenth. They claimed lunar descent, and some historians have proposed that their origins lay among local groups of the region, though the question remains open. The first rulers appear as subordinates of the Pratiharas. Chandella Harsha (early tenth century) is credited in later inscriptions with aiding a Pratihara king, and his successor Yashovarman (c. 925–950) established the dynasty as an independent power. His Khajuraho inscription, dated around 954, claims victories over neighbors and records the building of a Vishnu temple, the Lakshmana temple, which housed a prized image. The inscription records that the image had reached him through a chain of earlier rulers, an indication of how highly such objects were valued as marks of legitimacy.

Dhanga (c. 950–1002), Yashovarman's son, ruled for some fifty years and presided over the dynasty's consolidation. Inscriptions credit him with extending the kingdom and with patronage of temples, including the Vishvanatha temple at Khajuraho. Muslim chroniclers also associate Chandella and other Indian rulers with resistance to early Ghaznavid raids, though the details are uncertain. Vidyadhara (c. 1017–1029) is the best-known later king. He faced Mahmud of Ghazni in a campaign around 1019–20, but no permanent Chandella territory was conquered. Mahmud returned in 1022, proceeded through Gwalior, and besieged Kalinjar; Persian accounts describe the campaign as ending in an arrangement involving tribute or gifts rather than permanent occupation of the fortress, and the Chandellas remained in control of their land. These events belong, in their wider setting, to Chapter 39.

Later kings of the eleventh and twelfth centuries, among them Kirtivarman and Madanavarman, ruled during a period of competition with neighbors, notably the Kalachuris of Tripuri and the Chaulukyas, and built lakes and fortifications. Kirtivarman's court saw the performance of the philosophical allegory Prabodhachandrodaya. Paramardi (c. 1165–1203), the last major Chandella ruler, was defeated by Prithviraj III in 1182 to 1183, as described in the previous section. In the early thirteenth century, Turkic forces captured Kalinjar, a development that Chapter 40 takes up.

The Chandellas therefore illustrate the regional state at its most durable. For some three centuries they held a territory based on the fortress of Kalinjar, the city of Mahoba, and the surrounding agricultural districts, surviving rivals and early Turkic incursions. Their political story should not be reduced to the monuments for which they are famous, but those monuments are an important part of how they governed.

Section 35.11

Khajuraho, Kingship, and Chandella Royal Patronage

The temples of Khajuraho, now a World Heritage site, were built mainly between the middle of the tenth century and the middle of the eleventh. The group includes the Lakshmana temple (c. 954), the Vishvanatha temple, the Kandariya Mahadeva temple (c. 1025–1050), and Jain temples such as the Parshvanatha temple. They are noted for their tall curving towers, platforms, and extensive sculpture, which includes divine images, celestial figures, and scenes of daily life and, in some cases, erotic imagery.

As royal patronage, the temples declared Chandella sovereignty. A king who built a great temple displayed wealth, command of labor and materials, and piety, and linked his dynasty to the gods. Inscriptions record the dedication of images and the names of rulers. The temples were also centers of religious and social life, and housed priests, musicians, dancers, and servants supported by endowments. The combination of Shaiva, Vaishnava, and Jain foundations shows that the court patronized several traditions, and that wealthy Jain donors and officials were part of the Khajuraho community. The group should be read as evidence of religious plurality supported by royal and elite patronage.

The meaning of the sculpture is debated. Explanations have included tantric ritual, the symbolism of auspiciousness and fertility, political and cosmic metaphors, and courtly aesthetic traditions, and there is no consensus on a single interpretation. Historians stress that the imagery appears within a larger program of religious and ornamental sculpture and that nineteenth-century and later reactions often reflect the viewers' own expectations. This chapter does not enter the broader analysis of temple-centered economies, land grants, and institutional wealth, which Chapter 37 examines comparatively. For the Chandellas, the important point is that monumental building was part of statecraft, and that Khajuraho offers material evidence for a court culture that would otherwise be known mainly through inscriptions.

Section 35.12

The Paramaras of Malwa

The Paramaras ruled Malwa, the plateau of central-western India, from about the ninth or tenth century to the early fourteenth. Their earliest history is obscure. Records suggest an early association with the Rashtrakutas, whom they served, and their independent rise is usually dated to the middle of the tenth century. The Harsola copper plates of 949 belong to Siyaka II (also called Harsha), who is credited by later inscriptions and literary works with attacking the Rashtrakuta capital around 972 to 973, an event that signaled the end of Paramara subordination and contributed to Rashtrakuta decline (Chapter 31).

Malwa's location made it strategically and economically valuable. Fertile black soils supported agriculture, and the plateau lay on the routes between Gujarat's ports and the Ganges plain. It was also a contested frontier among the Chaulukyas of Gujarat, the Kalachuris, the Chandellas, the Rashtrakutas and their successors the Chalukyas of Kalyani, and the Chahamanas. The Paramaras' capital was Dhara, with Ujjain and later Mandu as important centers.

Vakpati Munja (c. 972–997) expanded the kingdom and was known as a patron of poets, including Padmagupta and Dhananjaya, and as a builder of reservoirs. He campaigned against the Chalukyas of Kalyani repeatedly, but was defeated and killed by Tailapa II around 995 to 997, a reminder of the risks of overreach. His successor Sindhuraja (c. 997–1010) restored the kingdom and was the subject of the Navasahasankacharita.

After the reign of Bhoja (next section), the kingdom was weakened by defeat and recovery. Udayaditya (c. 1070s–1080s) revived Paramara power, as the Udaipur prashasti at Vidisha records. In the 1130s, the Chaulukya king Jayasimha Siddharaja conquered Malwa, and the Paramara line survived in diminished form, with later rulers regaining territory at times. The Delhi Sultanate absorbed Malwa in the early fourteenth century, as Chapter 41 describes.

Everyday Life and Social History

Tanks, Fields, and Courts in the Early Medieval Northwest

Most people in these kingdoms were cultivators and herders. In Malwa and the plains of Bundelkhand, farmers grew grains and pulses and depended on rain and, where available, tanks and wells. Rulers and local elites built reservoirs, among them the Munjasagara associated with Munja and the Madanasagara at Mahoba, which stored monsoon water for irrigation and drinking. These works are recorded in inscriptions as acts of royal merit, but they also served practical needs. In drier Rajasthan, pastoralism and caravan trade were central, and salt and metals moved along trade routes.

Towns held artisans, merchants, priests, and officials, and temple complexes supported musicians, dancers, and craft specialists. Jain merchants and officials appear in inscriptions as donors. Women appear in the record as royal consorts and mothers who sometimes ruled as regents, as Karpuradevi did for Prithviraj, and as donors of images and tanks. Evidence for the lives of cultivators, laborers, and the poor is scarce and almost entirely indirect, preserved in grant records that list villages and obligations but rarely describe the people who lived there.

Section 35.13

Bhoja and the Ideal of the Scholar-King

Bhoja (c. 1010–1055) is the most celebrated Paramara king and one of the most famous rulers of early medieval India. Contemporary and near-contemporary evidence shows a vigorous ruler who fought on several fronts. Inscriptions and court literature claim victories over the Chaulukyas of Gujarat, the Kalachuris, and rulers in the Konkan and elsewhere, though the claims are partly rhetorical and results were mixed. Opponents recorded successes against him. In his later years Malwa faced a coalition of neighbors, and Bhoja died around 1055 during a period of military difficulty. The kingdom was then plundered and weakened.

Bhoja's cultural reputation is larger than his military record. He is credited with authorship or sponsorship of a great number of Sanskrit works on poetics, grammar, architecture, medicine, and statecraft, including the Sarasvatikanthabharana and the Shringaraprakasha on literary theory, the Samaranganasutradhara on architecture, and the Yuktikalpataru. Scholars debate how much he wrote himself and how much was produced by court scholars under his name. He also is associated with building works at Dhara and the reservoir known as Bhojsagar near Bhopal, and with a temple at Bhojpur, though attributions are sometimes traditional.

The later Bhoja of legend is larger still. Medieval and later story collections, including the early-fourteenth-century Jain Prabandhachintamani, present him as a wise and generous scholar-king surrounded by poets, and attach to him a host of anecdotes, some involving earlier poets such as Kalidasa who lived centuries before. The “Raja Bhoja” of folklore is therefore partly a composite figure. The historian can accept that Bhoja was a real king with real accomplishments and patronage, and that his court was a center of Sanskrit learning, while recognizing that the accumulation of stories reflects later admiration. The ideal of the scholar-king was powerful because it fused learning and sovereignty, offering a model of kingship that other courts, including those of the Chandellas and Chahamanas, also valued.

Bhoja's name also appears in modern disputes, including over a historic structure at Dhara associated with a Sanskrit school and now the subject of religious and legal controversy. These debates belong to the study of memory and heritage rather than to the reconstruction of eleventh-century history.

Section 35.14

Warfare: Cavalry, Elephants, Forts, Raiding, and Siege

Armies in this period combined several arms. Cavalry was increasingly important, and horses had to be imported or bred in suitable grazing regions, since the climate of much of India is not ideal for horse breeding. Horses came overland from the northwest and by sea from Arabia and Iran, and rulers who controlled the routes had an advantage. Elephants, drawn from the forests of central and eastern India, were prestigious and effective in some settings but costly to maintain and vulnerable to terrain and missiles. Infantry, including archers and swordsmen, made up the bulk of most forces, and contingents were provided by subordinate chiefs, who brought their own followers.

Campaigning had seasonal and logistical limits. The monsoon made movement difficult in many regions, and armies depended on local grain, fodder, and water along their route. Distance from a ruler's core made supply and reinforcement slow, which helps explain why conquests were so often temporary. Raiding for plunder, tribute, and captured animals was a regular form of warfare, and a victorious king often accepted submission and gifts and then withdrew. Sieges of forts could be prolonged and costly, and many ended in negotiation, a ransom, or a truce.

Political thought of the period, including treatises descending from the Arthashastra tradition and the works of Bhoja and other court authors, discussed the “circle of states,” the use of diplomacy, spies, and alliances, and the classification of forts and armies. Practice followed this pragmatic tradition: rulers made and broke alliances, married into rival families, deceived and bribed, and sought advantage. Heroic poetry glorified valor and sacrifice, and certain ideals of conduct mattered to courts, but it would be a mistake to treat warfare in these kingdoms as ritualized combat governed solely by honor. They were no more and no less pragmatic than their Turkic opponents, who also used diplomacy, marriage, and negotiation. The limits on holding conquered territory, which Chapter 34 showed for the imperial dynasties, applied equally to these regional states.

Section 35.15

Kingship, Kinship, Marriage, Honor, and Political Culture

Political culture in these courts rested on a set of related ideas. Kingship was understood as a divinely sanctioned office exercised by a Kshatriya, whose duty was to protect subjects, uphold social order, support Brahmins and temples, and defend his realm. Royal titles emphasized universality even when real authority was local. Courts supported poets and scholars who praised kings and recorded their deeds, and the king's reputation (yashas) was both a political asset and a goal.

Kinship and marriage structured power. Dynasties were organized around lineages, in which brothers, sons, and cousins held offices and territories and competed for succession, and rivalries within a family could be as dangerous as external wars. Marriage alliances connected houses: Prithviraj's mother came from the Kalachuri dynasty, and other unions linked Paramaras, Chaulukyas, and Chandellas. Alliances secured peace, support, and prestige, though they did not prevent war. Women of royal families appear as regents, donors, and symbols of lineage honor, but their own voices rarely survive.

Ideals of valor and honor were real and shaped how courts represented themselves. Praise of a king's courage, the glorification of death in battle, and loyalty to a lord appear throughout the poetry. Historians distinguish these ideals from practice. Loyalty could be conditional, defections occurred, and heroic language coexisted with deception and negotiation. Heroic literature should not be equated with battlefield reporting. “Rajput political culture,” in the sense used by later writers, was therefore a set of values and practices that developed over time and that scholars analyze as historical products rather than as the permanent character of a people.

Section 35.16

Rajputs in Later Historical Memory

Memory of these dynasties was shaped by several later traditions. Bardic and genealogical traditions preserved stories, often in vernacular languages, through hereditary poets and genealogists, and these accounts were revised over time to suit the needs of patrons. The Prithviraj Raso is the most famous product. Regional traditions also preserved epics about the Chandella-era wars, such as the oral Alha-Khanda cycle of Mahoba, which remains popular in northern India. These are valuable evidence for how communities remembered the past, but they cannot be used as direct evidence for the eleventh or twelfth centuries.

Medieval and early modern chronicles added further layers. Persian histories, written for Turkic and Mughal patrons, described Indian rulers from outside, and Sanskrit and Jain compilations such as the Prabandhachintamani preserved anecdotes. During the Mughal period (Chapters 50 through 56), Rajput houses became part of imperial service, and that experience again reshaped identity and memory.

The colonial period had a major effect. James Tod's Annals and Antiquities of Rajasthan (1829 to 1832), based on bardic and genealogical material and his own experience, presented Rajput society in terms drawn from European feudalism and portrayed chivalric honor as its defining quality. His work was influential and produced a lasting image, although it also reflected the assumptions and needs of the colonial official who wrote it. The British later classified Rajputs as a “martial race” (Chapter 73) and administered Rajasthan through the princely states of the Rajputana Agency (Chapter 71).

In the modern period, Rajput history has been used in heritage tourism, in regional and caste politics, in nationalist narratives of resistance to invasion, and in film and popular literature. Disputes over how these pasts should be portrayed continue. A historian's task is to treat these later uses as historically important phenomena, as evidence for how societies construct identity, without letting them substitute for the evidence of the period. Chapter 111 returns to the question of historical memory more broadly.

Section 35.17

Review and Historical Synthesis

The synthesis question is: how did military lineages become territorial dynasties, and when is it historically useful to call those dynasties “Rajput”? The chapter supplies the elements of an answer.

The first part of the answer lies in Chapter 34's result. Pratihara, Rashtrakuta, and Pala empires were built on layered authority in which subordinate families held land, commanded followers, and could shift allegiance. When imperial power weakened, those families had resources, positions, and models of rule at hand. Regional geography reinforced the outcome: defensible hills, scarce water, trade routes, and distinct plateaus favored multiple states with fortified centers.

The second part concerns legitimacy. Rising families made themselves sovereign dynasties through titles, genealogies, temples, lakes, grants, and marriages. The claims they made, to solar, lunar, or fire-born descent, were not records of ancestry but instruments of statecraft. The Chahamanas, Chandellas, and Paramaras pursued these strategies in different environments with different results, and their differences matter as much as what they shared.

The third part concerns the term itself. “Rajput” is useful for the dynasties of this chapter as a retrospective label for lineages whose descendants later belonged to a broad community of warrior landholders, but it is misleading if it implies a single people with a fixed identity and a shared political program in the eleventh and twelfth centuries. It is also misleading when later memory, especially heroic literature and colonial description, is treated as direct evidence. The better historical practice is to describe these dynasties by their own names, regions, and records, and to treat the later category as a subject of study.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined state formation, warrior lineages, and political culture in Rajasthan, Malwa, Bundelkhand, and the northwestern plains between about the ninth and twelfth centuries. Its central argument is that Rajput political identity emerged gradually from the regionalized political world of early medieval northern and western India, not from a single ancient people.

The chapter began with the problem of origins. Traditional, colonial, and nationalist accounts sought a single ancestry for the Rajputs, whether ancient Kshatriya, foreign, or Brahmin. Recent scholarship emphasizes processes by which families of diverse background acquired land, followers, titles, genealogy, and Kshatriya standing. The term rajaputra originally denoted rank, and “Rajput” as a community label developed later.

Genealogies were instruments of legitimacy. Claims of solar, lunar, and fire-born descent, including the Agnikula story first attested for the Paramaras, were political and literary constructions. The Primary Source Window compared the early court poem with later heroic tradition and showed how a regional origin story grew into a larger narrative.

Chapter 34's analysis of subordinate rulers provided the bridge. As Pratihara and Rashtrakuta power weakened, subordinate houses used their landholdings, followings, strategic positions, and marriage alliances to claim sovereignty. Rajasthan's geography, with its Aravalli ridge, scarce water, salt, and trade routes, and the plateaus of Malwa and Bundelkhand, shaped where these states formed. Forts were institutions of power and symbols of sovereignty, but they should not be treated as timeless Rajput traditions.

The Chahamanas rose from Shakambhari to Ajmer and the Delhi region, reaching their height under Vigraharaja IV and Prithviraj III. The chapter treated Prithviraj as one regional king among several and brought his story to the eve of the Ghurid confrontation, leaving the Tarain battles to Chapter 39. It also distinguished the evidence for his reign from the later image created by the Prithviraj Raso.

The Chandellas of Jejakabhukti held Bundelkhand for some three centuries and built Khajuraho. Their temples served as royal statements and as evidence of a plural religious world. The Paramaras of Malwa rose from Rashtrakuta subordination, reached their height under Bhoja, and then faced reverses and conquest by neighbors. Bhoja was both a real ruler and, in later memory, an ideal scholar-king.

Thematic sections examined warfare and political culture. Armies combined cavalry, elephants, and infantry, campaigns were limited by logistics, and raiding and negotiation were common. Kingship, kinship, and marriage structured politics, and ideals of honor coexisted with pragmatic diplomacy. These practices were not unique to Rajput courts.

The final sections turned to memory. Bardic traditions, Persian chronicles, Jain compilations, Tod's colonial synthesis, and modern heritage and political uses all shaped the image of the Rajputs. These traditions are important historical evidence for their own periods but are not transparent evidence for the eleventh and twelfth centuries. The chapter closed by arguing that “Rajput” is useful as a retrospective category for a set of dynasties but misleading if it implies a unified people or program.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how one of the most familiar categories in South Asian history was made. The warrior dynasties of Rajasthan, Malwa, and Bundelkhand were real states with real achievements, and the later image of the Rajputs grew from their histories, but the two are not identical. Learning to separate them is a model for historical method.

The chapter also shows how empires leave their structures behind. The Pratihara system of layered authority and subordinate chiefs did not disappear when the empire did. It produced the regional kingdoms that followed, which in turn shaped the northwestern frontier that Turkic armies would meet. Chapters 39 through 41 take up that encounter, and Chapters 50 through 56 examine how Rajput houses entered the Mughal order.

Finally, the chapter shows that memory is a historical force. Genealogies, heroic poems, colonial synthesis, and modern heritage politics each reshaped the past in ways that mattered to their own times. Treating those shaping forces as subjects of study, as the final chapter of the series will do on a larger scale, helps students understand both the period and the many ways it has been used since.
