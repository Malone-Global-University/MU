Part IV — The Mauryan Age
18

The End of the Mauryan Empire and Its Legacies

Indian History: South Asia from the Indus Civilization to the Present

Chapter Opening

The Mauryan Empire depended on more than kings. Revenue had to move from fields to treasuries, orders from Pataliputra to provinces, officials across roads and river routes, and military force toward regions that resisted central commands. Chapter 17 showed how difficult such coordination could be even when imperial authority was strong. After Ashoka's death, the central question was whether his successors could continue to sustain those connections.

In the Barabar and Nagarjuni hills of present-day Bihar, a few kilometers from the old Magadhan heartland, visitors can still enter small caves cut into hard granite and finished inside with a mirror-like polish. Some of these caves were donated to ascetics of the Ajivika community, and inscriptions record the gifts. Several date to the reign of Ashoka. Others, at Nagarjuni, name a different Mauryan king: Dasharatha. These modest chambers are among the most valuable pieces of evidence for the subject of this chapter, because they show that Mauryan kings were still issuing inscriptions, still patronizing religious communities, and still using the imperial idiom of royal donation after Ashoka had died. Whatever happened to the Mauryan Empire, it did not vanish overnight.

Chapters 15 through 17 traced the rise of the Mauryan state under Chandragupta and Bindusara, the transformation of Mauryan kingship under Ashoka, and the economic, social, and administrative foundations of imperial power. This chapter completes Part IV by asking what happened next. Its answer is more complicated than the familiar textbook sentence that the empire "declined after Ashoka." The evidence for the end of the empire is much thinner than the evidence for its height. Ashoka speaks to historians in his own inscriptions; his successors mostly do not. Historians must reconstruct the fragmentation of Mauryan authority from later dynastic lists, later Buddhist, Jain, and Brahmanical literature, a small number of inscriptions, archaeological evidence, and the appearance of new regional powers in the centuries that followed.

This chapter's central argument is that Mauryan imperial authority fragmented for several interacting reasons rather than one decisive cause. Succession after Ashoka appears to have been less secure. Regional control was uneven to begin with and became weaker. The fiscal, military, and administrative demands of governing a vast and unevenly integrated realm became harder to meet. The dynasty formally ended around 185 BCE with the overthrow of its last king by his own commander, but the end of a dynasty was not the end of early historic society. Fields continued to be cultivated, cities and trade routes continued to function, and Buddhist, Jain, Brahmanical, Ajivika, and other religious communities continued to flourish under new and often regional rulers.

The chapter is also about legacy, and here careful chronology is essential. Some things that later generations attached to the Mauryas were genuinely Mauryan: the polished stone pillars, the Barabar caves, the inscriptional tradition, and the founding of monuments that later grew far larger. Other things were later accretions, such as the enlargement of the Great Stupa at Sanchi, the story of 84,000 Ashokan stupas, and modern claims that the Mauryas created the first unified India. Sorting the third-century-BCE achievement from the much larger legacy that later societies constructed is one of the chapter's principal tasks.

The chapter proceeds in three movements. Sections 18.1 through 18.5 examine the fragmentation of Mauryan rule: the poorly documented reigns after Ashoka, the problems of succession, provincial autonomy, fiscal and military limits, and the Shunga overthrow. Sections 18.6 and 18.7 turn to Mauryan art, architecture, and monumental Buddhism, distinguishing what the Mauryas built from what later patrons added. Sections 18.8 and 18.9 examine the later career of the Mauryan example, first as a model of imperial kingship and then in historical memory, from ancient literary traditions through the decipherment of Ashoka's inscriptions to modern national symbolism. Section 18.10 synthesizes end, continuity, and legacy and prepares the transition to Part V.

Throughout, students should notice a shift in the character of the evidence. Chapter 16 could rely on inscriptions in the ruler's own voice. This chapter must often work from later reconstruction, and it asks students to be explicit about what is contemporary, what is later, and what is inference. That distinction is not a technicality. It is the difference between describing what the sources say and describing what historians can responsibly infer from them.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why the chronology and succession of Mauryan rulers after Ashoka are much less secure than those of Chandragupta and Ashoka.
  2. Distinguish contemporary inscriptional evidence, such as the Nagarjuni cave inscriptions of Dasharatha, from later literary reconstruction of the end of the dynasty.
  3. Analyze problems of succession as one factor in the fragmentation of Mauryan authority without reducing that fragmentation to the character of individual rulers.
  4. Assess how the unevenness of Mauryan control across core, corridors, and frontiers shaped the pattern of regional autonomy.
  5. Evaluate fiscal, logistical, and military explanations for fragmentation as reasoned reconstruction rather than documented fact, and explain why older explanations blaming Ashoka's Buddhism outrun the evidence.
  6. Narrate the overthrow of Brihadratha by Pushyamitra as a later literary tradition, and identify what the surviving accounts can and cannot establish.
  7. Describe the principal features of Mauryan monumental art and architecture and explain what made them politically innovative.
  8. Trace the development of the stupa at Sanchi from Mauryan foundation through post-Mauryan enlargement to later embellishment.
  9. Compare institutional continuity with remembered precedent in assessing the Mauryan influence on later South Asian states.
  10. Interpret the layers of Mauryan historical memory, from ancient Buddhist, Jain, Brahmanical, and Greek traditions to modern rediscovery and national symbolism.
  11. Distinguish the end of a dynasty from the collapse of society and economy, and explain why the difference matters.
  12. Synthesize what ended, what continued, and what survived as legacy after the Mauryas.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did Mauryan imperial authority fragment after Ashoka, and which political, religious, artistic, and historical legacies actually survived the end of the dynasty?
  2. What can historians securely say about Mauryan rule after Ashoka, and why does the evidence become so much thinner?
  3. Why is "weak successors" an unsatisfying explanation for imperial fragmentation?
  4. How did the geographically uneven character of Mauryan control shape the way the empire came apart?
  5. What fiscal and logistical demands did a large territorial empire impose, and why must any argument about a Mauryan fiscal crisis be labeled reconstruction?
  6. How should historians treat later accounts of the overthrow of Brihadratha and the alleged persecutions of Pushyamitra?
  7. Which Mauryan monuments and artistic forms were genuinely Mauryan, and which were later transformations of Mauryan foundations?
  8. Did later rulers inherit Mauryan institutions, or did they adopt Mauryan precedent selectively?
  9. How has the memory of the Mauryas, and especially of Ashoka, been created, lost, recovered, and reused across two millennia?

Historical Context

By the reign of Ashoka, the Mauryan state was the largest political formation South Asia had yet seen. Chapter 15 traced how Chandragupta Maurya, after the fall of the Nandas, built a territorial empire centered on Pataliputra, defeated Seleucus I in a settlement that secured the northwest, and organized an army and revenue system able to support imperial ambitions. Bindusara extended that inheritance. Chapter 16 examined Ashoka, whose conquest of Kalinga, adoption of dhamma as a public ethic, and extraordinary corpus of inscriptions transformed Mauryan kingship and left the best documentary record of any early South Asian ruler.

Chapter 17 then moved beneath the level of dynastic narrative to ask how such an empire worked. Its answer was that imperial power rested on chains of connection: agricultural surplus and revenue, officials and provincial centers, roads and rivers, armies, and communication between capital and periphery. Chapter 17 also emphasized that these chains were geographically uneven. The Gangetic core was densely administered; major corridors linking it to the northwest, the Deccan, and the coast were reasonably well integrated; frontier and forest zones were governed more lightly, and some peoples were incorporated on negotiated or partial terms. An empire of this kind was powerful but also dependent on the constant renewal of the relationships that kept it together.

The centuries around the end of the Mauryan dynasty were, in the wider world, a period of significant political movement. To the northwest, the Seleucid Empire and its successor Greco-Bactrian state were reorganizing Central Asian power, a process that would soon bring new rulers into the Indus region and is examined in Chapter 20. In the Deccan and the far south, older regional societies, examined in Chapters 8, 22, and 23, were developing their own political forms. In the Ganges valley, the second urbanization discussed in Chapter 9 had created cities and networks of trade that outlived any particular dynasty.

This chapter's evidentiary situation is unusual and should be kept in mind throughout. Ashoka's own inscriptions document his reign; the Greek accounts of Megasthenes, preserved only in fragments quoted by later writers, illuminate Chandragupta's court. But for the period after Ashoka the record narrows sharply. The principal sources are the Puranas, Sanskrit narrative and dynastic texts compiled centuries later; Buddhist works such as the Ashokavadana and the Sri Lankan chronicles; Jain traditions; a small number of contemporary inscriptions, notably those of Dasharatha; and archaeological evidence. These sources disagree with one another, and none is a contemporary court chronicle of the dynasty's decline. Historians therefore treat statements about the end of the empire as reconstructions, some more secure than others.

Section 18.1

The Mauryas after Ashoka

Chapter 17 ended with a question: what happens when the center can no longer sustain the flows of revenue, officials, information, and force that hold an empire together? The question is easy to pose and hard to answer for the Mauryas, because the evidence for the decades after Ashoka is much weaker than the evidence for his reign. Ashoka's death is conventionally placed around 232 BCE, but even that date rests on a chronology reconstructed from a mixture of Greek synchronisms, Buddhist tradition, and Puranic reign lengths. The traditional end of the dynasty around 185 BCE leaves roughly half a century, and the Puranas assign the whole Mauryan dynasty a duration of roughly a hundred and thirty-odd years. Within that span, historians must fill in what happened between two well-documented reigns and a violent, poorly documented ending.

The literary sources give names, but they do not agree. Puranic dynastic lists preserve sequences of Mauryan kings after Ashoka, but the names, order, and reign lengths vary among manuscripts and among Puranas. Buddhist traditions, especially the Ashokavadana, associate the succession with other figures, and Jain traditions emphasize a ruler called Samprati, portrayed as a grandson of Ashoka and a generous patron of Jainism. Some of these names may refer to the same person under different forms; some may refer to rulers of parts of the empire rather than the whole; some may reflect the later concerns of the traditions that preserved them. A neat, single line of successors, confidently presented, would misrepresent the evidence. This textbook therefore does not offer one.

One post-Ashokan king does emerge in contemporary evidence: Dasharatha. Three short inscriptions in caves at the Nagarjuni hills, close to the Barabar caves that Ashoka had earlier donated, record that Dasharatha gave the caves to Ajivika ascetics. These inscriptions are examined in this chapter's Primary Source Window. For now, their importance is threefold. They are contemporary documents rather than later literary reconstructions. They show that a king of the Mauryan line was still issuing inscriptions and making royal grants in the same region and in the same manner as his predecessor. And they show that Mauryan patronage of religious communities, including one that stood outside the Buddhist tradition with which Ashoka is most associated, continued after Ashoka. The Mauryan court after Ashoka was not simply a shrinking version of a Buddhist court.

Historians have also considered the possibility that Mauryan rule after Ashoka was not always exercised by a single king over the whole realm. Some scholars have suggested that different members of the dynasty may have held different parts of the empire, perhaps simultaneously, and that this may account for some of the conflicts in the king lists. This suggestion is plausible but not proven, since it is a reconstruction designed to reconcile inconsistent traditions rather than a fact recorded in any contemporary text. The safest statement is that the Mauryan political order after Ashoka is poorly documented, that several rulers are named in later sources, that one of them, Dasharatha, is attested in inscriptions, and that the unity of the empire under a single powerful ruler cannot be assumed for the entire period.

This evidentiary limit shapes everything that follows. Chapter 16 could explain what Ashoka intended because he told his subjects in his own words. Chapter 18 cannot say what Ashoka's successors intended, feared, or decided. It can only ask what the surviving pattern of evidence suggests about structural conditions: how authority was transferred, how regional control changed, what the empire cost to maintain, and how the dynasty finally ended. The controlling question for this section is therefore modest and important: what can historians securely say about Mauryan rule after Ashoka, and why does the evidence become so much thinner?

Historical Vocabulary
Vamsa: Dynastic Lists and Their Uses

A vamsa (literally "lineage") is a genealogical or dynastic list, common in the Puranas and in Buddhist and Jain chronicle traditions. Such lists give the names of kings in sequence, sometimes with reign lengths. They are indispensable to reconstructing early Indian political chronology, but they were not compiled as neutral archives. They were assembled by authors with religious, dynastic, or literary purposes, often centuries after the reigns they describe, and they were copied and revised over time.

For the Mauryas, the Puranic vamsa lists provide a rough framework but disagree in details, and Buddhist and Jain lists follow different priorities. Historians therefore treat them as sources to be cross-checked against inscriptions, coins, and Greek synchronisms, and not as a secure register of who ruled when. Where contemporary inscriptions, such as Ashoka's or Dasharatha's, survive, they take precedence over later lists.

Section 18.2

Problems of Succession

Any large monarchy must repeatedly solve the problem of transferring authority. The Mauryas had done so at least twice, from Chandragupta to Bindusara and from Bindusara to Ashoka, though the second transition is traditionally associated with conflict among Bindusara's sons, a story preserved in later Buddhist sources. Chapter 15 showed that Bindusara's reign demonstrated something important: the empire could survive a change of ruler. This section asks why repeated succession appears to have become more difficult afterward.

It is tempting to answer with a simple biographical explanation: a great king was followed by weaker descendants, and the empire declined as a result. This explanation is common in older textbooks, and it should be resisted. It cannot be demonstrated, because the personalities, abilities, and policies of Ashoka's successors are almost entirely unknown. It also mistakes a structural problem for a personal one. An empire organized around the personal authority of a monarch, supported by a court and a set of provincial officials appointed by and answerable to that monarch, was vulnerable to any weakening of the ruler's capacity to command, whatever the reason.

Several structural factors plausibly made succession harder. First, dynastic uncertainty may have been increased by the size of the royal family and the possibility of multiple claimants. The later traditions preserve stories of rivalry, including in Ashoka's own accession, and no fixed rule of succession is securely attested. Second, the empire may have been divided, formally or informally, among members of the dynasty, a possibility discussed in Section 18.1. Third, Ashoka's reign had created an unusually visible form of royal authority, communicated through inscriptions, officials such as the dhamma-mahamattas examined in Chapter 16, and public monuments. Rulers with shorter reigns or weaker networks may have been less able to sustain that direct, personal projection of authority across distant provinces. This last point is inference rather than documented fact, but it fits what Chapter 17 established about the demands of imperial coordination.

Shorter reigns and more frequent transitions would have magnified each of these problems. Each succession was a moment when provincial officials could reconsider their loyalties, when rivals within the court could mobilize supporters, and when the flow of revenue and information to the capital could be disrupted. A dynasty could survive one such moment; a series of them, especially in a state that lacked institutional mechanisms to make succession routine, could gradually erode the sense that the center was the indispensable source of authority.

The formulation that best fits the evidence is therefore this: a large empire required repeated successful transfers of authority. The evidence suggests that Mauryan succession after Ashoka did not reproduce the same level of centralized cohesion, but the personalities and abilities of individual successors are poorly known. Dynastic instability was a real and probable factor, but it was one element in a larger set of pressures, not an explanation that stands alone.

Section 18.3

Provincial Autonomy

Chapter 17 argued that Mauryan authority was uneven geographically. It follows that fragmentation was probably not a moment in which a uniformly administered empire shattered on a single day. Students should instead imagine graduated levels of authority: a densely administered core around Pataliputra and the Gangetic plain; provincial centers such as Taxila, Ujjain, Tosali, Suvarnagiri, and others through which the emperor ruled distant regions; the major corridors of roads and rivers connecting these centers to the core; and peripheral and frontier zones where imperial claims were lighter and local societies retained considerable autonomy.

As central authority weakened, that graduated structure gave several ways for the empire to loosen. Provincial governors, who in the Mauryan system often included royal princes or trusted officials, could act with more independence, particularly if the ruler at the center was less secure. Local elites and pre-existing regional political structures could reassert themselves. Distant regions could stop sending resources or obeying commands without any formal declaration of independence. And the corridors that sustained communication could become harder to maintain, so that the effective reach of the center contracted from the edges inward.

The evidence for regional change is scattered and often later. In the northwest, the Greek historian Polybius reports that the Seleucid king Antiochus III, around 206 BCE, crossed the Hindu Kush and renewed friendship with an Indian king named Subhagasena. Whoever this king was, the report suggests that the region beyond the Hindu Kush was by then politically distinct from Pataliputra, and it anticipates the Greco-Bactrian and Indo-Greek presence examined in Chapter 20. In the east and the Deccan, later inscriptions attest regional rulers whose political worlds are explored in Chapters 19 and 22, and the Kalinga region, conquered by Ashoka, later appears with its own rulers. Yet historians must resist assuming that every later regional kingdom was a Mauryan province that rebelled. Many later states emerged from older local polities, from new military leadership, from migrations, or from combinations of these, and treating them all as offshoots of Mauryan governance would impose a neat pattern on a messy record.

The distinction that most needs emphasis is between loss of dynastic sovereignty and continuity of local society and economy. The Mauryan state was an overlay on a landscape of villages, towns, guilds, religious communities, and regional elites, many of which predated it and all of which outlasted it. For a farmer paying a share of the harvest, a weaver selling cloth in a market town, a merchant moving goods along a river route, or a monk residing in a monastery supported by lay donors, the weakening of a distant imperial court might have meant a change in who collected revenue or who offered protection, rather than the end of daily economic and religious life. This is an important correction to the older language of "fall," which can suggest that the whole civilization went down with the dynasty.

Section 18.4

Fiscal and Military Limits

This is the chapter's strongest exercise in causal analysis, and also the one that demands the most caution. Chapter 17 established what an empire required: revenue to pay for administration, officials to collect revenue and enforce orders, transport to move goods and information, an army to enforce claims and defend frontiers, and communication to keep all of these connected. This section reverses the chain and asks what would happen if any link weakened.

A large territorial empire imposed permanent costs: maintaining a standing army and garrisons, paying officials, keeping roads and river routes open, supporting a court, funding royal projects, and provisioning the capital. Revenue depended on agriculture, trade, and other resources spread over an enormous geographic area, and collecting it required an administrative apparatus that had to reach into provinces and villages. If succession problems or provincial autonomy reduced the amount of revenue reaching the center, or the extent to which provincial officials obeyed the center, the capacity to pay soldiers and officials would also decline, which in turn would reduce the center's capacity to compel obedience.

It is essential to state clearly what this argument does not claim. Older explanations blamed the decline on Ashoka's Buddhism: on the expense of his stupa building and donations, on the alleged pacifism that supposedly weakened the army, or on dhamma itself. These formulas outrun the evidence. Chapter 16 showed that Ashoka never abolished coercion. His inscriptions retain the authority to punish, warn forest peoples of the state's power, and maintain the army; his renunciation of conquest by war applied to his own aggression, not to the instruments of the state. There is no evidence that his patronage bankrupted the empire. The idea of a treasury drained by pious generosity derives mainly from legends in the Ashokavadana, in which ministers try to restrict the emperor's gifts to the Buddhist community. Historians treat this as edifying narrative rather than financial history, and no surviving Mauryan document provides a budget that would show a fiscal crisis at all.

What survives, therefore, is a plausible structural explanation rather than a documented one. The geographic scale of the empire created persistent fiscal and logistical demands. Those demands were manageable when the center was strong, when its officials were loyal, and when its army was paid and supplied. They became harder to meet when authority was contested, when provincial elites held back resources, or when new external pressures, such as those emerging in the northwest, required military attention. This is reasoning from the structure of the empire, informed by Chapter 17, and this textbook labels it as reconstruction. It is compatible with the thin evidence we have, but it cannot be proven from it.

The advantage of a structural argument is that it explains how several modest problems could accumulate. A single problem, such as a disputed succession, would have been survivable. But a disputed succession might reduce provincial compliance, reduced compliance might reduce revenue, reduced revenue might weaken the army, and a weaker army might make further compliance harder to enforce. Such a self-reinforcing sequence does not need a dramatic catastrophe. It needs only the gradual erosion of the connections that Chapter 17 identified as the empire's foundation.

Cause and Effect

A Feedback Loop of Weakening Authority (Reconstruction)

Long-term causes

A territory too large and too diverse to be governed uniformly; uneven integration among core, corridors, and frontiers; dependence on the personal authority of the ruler and on officials appointed by the center.

Short-term causes

Uncertain succession and shorter or contested reigns after Ashoka; possible division of authority within the dynasty; new geopolitical pressures, especially in the northwest.

Transformation

Weaker central authority reduced compliance and extraction; lower revenue reduced military and administrative capacity; reduced capacity encouraged greater regional autonomy; regional autonomy weakened the center further.

Long-term consequences

The end of Mauryan dynastic rule around 185 BCE, the rise of regional and transregional powers examined in Chapters 19 through 22, and the survival of Mauryan monuments and memory.

Historical complexity: No source documents this loop directly. It is a reconstruction of how the structural features described in Chapter 17 would have interacted. Its value is to explain how several modest pressures could reinforce one another rather than to name a single cause. Students should also notice what is missing from the loop: any claim that dhamma, pacifism, or Buddhist patronage caused the decline.

Section 18.5

The Shunga Overthrow

The dynasty's formal end is conventionally dated to around 185 BCE. Later literary tradition says that the last Mauryan king, Brihadratha, was killed by his commander-in-chief Pushyamitra, who then established the Shunga dynasty. The Puranas preserve the basic tradition. A more vivid account appears in the Harshacharita, a seventh-century CE biography of the ruler Harsha composed by Banabhatta, which describes the king being killed while reviewing his troops. That source was written roughly eight hundred years after the event. It is evidence that the story was known in early medieval literary circles, not a contemporary account of the killing.

Historians therefore treat the overthrow as historically probable in outline, since multiple later traditions agree that a Mauryan king was replaced by a military commander named Pushyamitra, but as poorly documented in detail. What can be said is limited. The last Mauryan king ruled from a base that by then was largely confined to the Gangetic heartland and its immediate surroundings; his commander held significant military authority; and the transfer of power was violent and dynastic. It is not possible to reconstruct the political conditions surrounding the coup, or to say whether the Mauryan king exercised effective control over much of the empire at the time, from any contemporary source.

The event nonetheless illustrates a point that Chapter 17 anticipated. In a state that depended on a professional army and on the loyalty of its commanders, the military officer who controlled that army was a potential rival to the ruler. The overthrow of Brihadratha by his own senapati was not a foreign conquest and not a popular uprising; it was an internal seizure of power by someone who already held a position at the heart of the system. It ended Mauryan rule in the Gangetic core. It did not, by itself, end the regional political diversity that had already emerged elsewhere.

Students should also know what this section does not address. Later Buddhist traditions, notably the Ashokavadana and related texts, portray Pushyamitra as a persecutor of Buddhists. The older scholarly formula summarizing this tradition holds that Pushyamitra overthrew a Buddhist dynasty and launched a Brahmanical reaction against Buddhism. The evidence for such a systematic anti-Buddhist program is highly contested, and major Buddhist monuments continued to receive substantial patronage in the post-Mauryan period, as Section 18.7 shows. The debate over Pushyamitra's religious policy belongs primarily with the Shunga state itself, and Chapter 19 examines it there. The assigned scope of this chapter is limited to the formal end of Mauryan rule; Pushyamitra's government, patronage, and political world are the subject of the next chapter.

Primary Source Window

Dasharatha's Cave Inscriptions at Nagarjuni, later third century BCE

Creator: Dasharatha, a Mauryan king after Ashoka, in his own name and title · Date: after Ashoka's death, approximately later third century BCE (exact regnal dates uncertain) · Location: Nagarjuni hills, near the Barabar hills, in present-day Bihar · Type: Short royal inscriptions in the Brahmi script and a Prakrit language, recording a donation of rock-cut caves to a religious community

Context: Three caves in the Nagarjuni hills carry inscriptions naming Dasharatha and recording that he gave the caves to Ajivika ascetics for their residence. The Ajivikas, examined in Chapter 12, were an ascetic community distinct from Buddhists and Jains. Ashoka had earlier donated caves at nearby Barabar to the same community. Because these inscriptions carry the king's name, are cut into the caves themselves, and were not altered afterward, they are among the best contemporary evidence for Mauryan royal activity after Ashoka.

Paraphrase: The inscriptions record that King Dasharatha, using a royal epithet reminiscent of the one Ashoka used in his own edicts, gave these caves to the Ajivika ascetics, and they express the intention that the donation should endure as long as the sun and moon. The inscriptions are brief and do not describe any military, fiscal, or political events.
Later literary account (paraphrase, for comparison): Banabhatta's seventh-century Harshacharita reports that Pushyamitra, the commander of the Mauryan army, killed the Mauryan king Brihadratha, described as weak in fulfilling his vows, during a military review. Similar traditions appear in the Puranas.

Analysis: The two sources illustrate opposite strengths. The Nagarjuni inscriptions are contemporary, precisely located, and unedited, but they say almost nothing about politics: they show that a Mauryan king was making donations, not how much territory he controlled or how he ruled. The Harshacharita and Puranic accounts narrate the dynasty's violent ending in vivid terms, but they were composed centuries later, reflect later literary purposes, and cannot be verified from contemporary evidence. Historians therefore use the inscriptions to establish that Mauryan royal patronage and inscription-making persisted after Ashoka, and use the later literature to suggest, with caution, how the dynasty ended. Neither source alone can explain why the empire fragmented.

Questions for students:

  1. What does this source say or show?
  2. Who created it and for what possible purpose?
  3. What can historians learn from it about Mauryan rule after Ashoka?
  4. What can historians not establish from this source alone, and how does the later literary account help or fail to fill that gap?
Section 18.6

Mauryan Art and Architecture

With this section the chapter pivots from fragmentation to legacy. The Mauryan Empire's political unity did not last, but some of what it built did. The most distinctive Mauryan monuments are the Ashokan monolithic pillars: tall, tapering shafts of polished sandstone, each carved from a single block, topped with sculpted capitals of animals such as lions, bulls, and elephants, and in many cases inscribed with Ashoka's edicts. Such pillars stood at Sarnath, Lauriya Nandangarh, Rampurva, Sanchi, and other sites, many of them associated with Buddhist places of pilgrimage or with routes across the Gangetic plain. The stone was quarried in the region of Chunar near Varanasi, then shaped, polished, and transported over long distances, a feat that required specialized labor, planning, and considerable resources.

The Sarnath Lion Capital is the best-known example. It consists of four lions seated back to back above a circular abacus decorated with a wheel, an elephant, a bull, a horse, and a lion in relief, all resting on a lotus base. The capital originally crowned a pillar in the area where the Buddha is traditionally said to have first taught. It is a masterpiece of stone sculpture, and it illustrates the combination of naturalistic animal representation, geometric composition, and polished finish that characterizes the best Mauryan work. Its later adoption as the emblem of the Republic of India is discussed in Section 18.9.

Mauryan architecture was not limited to pillars. The Barabar and Nagarjuni caves are among the earliest surviving rock-cut chambers in South Asia. Their interiors, cut into hard granite, are finished with a remarkable polish, and the Lomas Rishi cave carries a façade imitating the wooden architecture that presumably preceded it, a reminder that stone construction was in part a translation of earlier building traditions. At Pataliputra, excavations have revealed traces of timber palisades and the remains of a large pillared hall at Kumrahar, evidence consistent with the descriptions of the capital preserved in Greek and later Chinese accounts, though the reconstruction of the imperial city from these remains is partial and debated.

One analytical point deserves emphasis. The lustrous surface known as "Mauryan polish" should not be presented as though every polished stone object automatically came from Ashoka's workshops. Polished stone appears on objects in other contexts and periods, and identifying an object as Mauryan requires archaeological context, inscription, style, or a combination of these. Studying the polish, the quarrying, and the transport of the monoliths tells historians about technical sophistication and organization, but it does not license assigning every polished fragment to a single royal patron.

What, then, made Mauryan monumental art politically innovative? The best answer is that it placed royal authority and royal messages into durable public landscapes on a scale not previously documented in South Asia. Earlier states had used wooden buildings, earthen ramparts, and coinage to express power. The Mauryas used stone, polished to a high finish, carved with symbols, and inscribed with texts addressed to the population. A pillar was at once a piece of technical achievement, a religious and cosmological symbol, a marker of place, and a vehicle for royal speech. That combination did not depend on any specific dynasty to remain meaningful, which is one reason the monuments outlasted the state that made them.

Historical Vocabulary
"Mauryan Polish"

"Mauryan polish" is the modern name for the glossy, mirror-like surface finish found on Ashokan pillars, on the interior walls of the Barabar caves, and on some other Mauryan-period stone objects. It was achieved through careful abrasion of fine-grained stone, and its exact method has been discussed by modern researchers.

The term is useful for describing a technique but potentially misleading when used as a dating shortcut. Historians caution that not every polished stone surface is automatically Mauryan, and that identifying a work as Mauryan requires supporting evidence beyond finish alone.

Section 18.7

Stupas and Monumental Buddhism

This section requires particular chronological discipline. Ashoka is strongly associated in Buddhist tradition with the patronage of stupas, hemispherical mounds containing relics of the Buddha, and later Buddhist texts magnify the scale of this patronage. The Ashokavadana and the Sri Lankan chronicles describe Ashoka opening earlier relic deposits and redistributing the relics into 84,000 stupas across the world. This number is symbolic and should not be taken literally. It expresses the belief, held in later Buddhist communities, that Ashoka was the ideal royal patron. Archaeology has found Buddhist monuments and inscriptions associated with Ashoka's reign, but nothing that supports a count remotely approaching this figure.

Sanchi, in central India near Vidisha, is the ideal case study because it demonstrates both Mauryan foundation and post-Mauryan transformation. According to the historical description used for the site's World Heritage listing, the Great Stupa at Sanchi has an Ashokan foundation, but it was enlarged and encased in stone in the Shunga period, with additional structures added later, and the famous ornamental gateways are later still. The visible stupa a visitor sees today is therefore not Ashoka's monument. It is a later, larger structure built around and over an earlier Mauryan core. Students should learn to read the site in layers: Mauryan nucleus, post-Mauryan enlargement, later embellishment.

Sanchi also illustrates who paid for such monuments. Inscriptions on the railings and gateways at Sanchi and at other early Buddhist sites record donations by individuals from a wide range of social positions: monks and nuns, merchants, guild members, craftspeople, householders, and women. Royal patronage mattered, but so did the generosity of many others. This is a crucial correction to the idea that Ashoka single-handedly created monumental Buddhism. He was a transformative patron within a developing tradition, one whose monasteries, relic cults, pilgrimages, and donor communities were already growing and which continued to grow after the Mauryas had gone.

The stupa was also a religious institution with wider meaning. It was a place where relics, understood as extensions of the Buddha's presence, could be honored, and around which devotional practices of circumambulation and offering developed. Stupas became nodes in networks that linked monastic communities, lay donors, pilgrims, and traders, an arrangement examined further in Chapter 25 on Buddhist networks. The Mauryan contribution was to help make relic veneration and monumental construction part of the visible landscape of Buddhism, and to associate Buddhist practice with royal patronage in a way that later kings would remember and emulate, as Section 18.8 discusses.

The chronology matters for a further reason. The fact that the great expansion of Sanchi occurred in the Shunga period undermines any simple story of a Shunga anti-Buddhist reaction. Whatever the religious policies of individual rulers, the archaeological evidence shows major Buddhist building activity continuing in the post-Mauryan centuries, and Chapter 19 takes up the implications. Students should also avoid claiming that Ashoka invented the stupa; the form belongs to earlier funerary and commemorative traditions, and its later development involved many patrons.

Historical Vocabulary
Stupa

A stupa is a solid, usually dome-shaped monument, often enclosing a relic chamber, that became one of the central forms of Buddhist religious architecture. Stupas contained bodily relics of the Buddha or revered teachers, or objects associated with them, and functioned as places of devotion, pilgrimage, and donation.

A stupa could be built in stages, with later builders enlarging, encasing, or adding railings and gateways to an earlier core. For this reason, a stupa's visible form cannot be assumed to date from its foundation, and historians distinguish carefully among building phases.

Section 18.8

Imperial Models after the Mauryas

Did later South Asian rulers simply copy the Mauryas? The answer is no. But Mauryan precedents mattered, and understanding how they mattered requires distinguishing two things that are easy to confuse. Institutional continuity means that a later state actually inherited and operated the administrative structures of an earlier one. Remembered precedent means that later rulers knew of, admired, or invoked an earlier ruler or model, without necessarily reproducing its institutions. For the Mauryas, the evidence for the second is considerably stronger than the evidence for the first.

Several legacies can be identified. The Mauryas demonstrated the possibility of a territorial monarchy on a very large scale, embracing the Gangetic plain and reaching far beyond it. They advanced universalizing claims of kingship, expressed most clearly in Ashoka's assertion that all people were his children. They connected royal power to public works and welfare, including roads, wells, rest houses, and medical provision, as Chapters 16 and 17 discussed. They used inscriptions as instruments of rule, a practice that later dynasties would develop extensively, as Chapter 26 shows for the Guptas and Chapter 33 for the Cholas. They fostered a public moral ideology in which the king was responsible for the ethical conduct of his subjects. And they established a relationship between kingship and religious patronage that later rulers, Buddhist and otherwise, would adapt.

What later states did not do was inherit the Mauryan state as a functioning machine. The Shunga, Kanva, Satavahana, Kushan, Gupta, and other later polities developed under conditions very different from those of the third century BCE, with different economic bases, different military technologies, different religious environments, and different regional structures. Some later rulers explicitly recalled Mauryan predecessors, and later administrative vocabulary shows echoes of earlier practice, but the evidence does not support a continuous institutional chain. It is misleading to imagine a sequence in which the Mauryas led to the Guptas, the Guptas to the Mughals, and the Mughals to modern India. That kind of linear story imposes false continuity and treats the past as a rehearsal for the present.

A better formulation is that the Mauryas became one available model among many for imagining extensive kingship, but later states selectively adapted rather than simply inherited Mauryan institutions. The Mauryan example offered later rulers a vocabulary of empire and a set of memorable images. Which parts of that vocabulary were used, and how, depended on the political needs of each new dynasty. Chapters 19 through 22 will show the regional states that succeeded the Mauryas building their own political forms, and the later imperial systems examined in Volumes I through III each drew on a wider range of models than the Mauryan one alone.

Section 18.9

Maurya in Historical Memory

How the Mauryas have been remembered is itself a historical subject, and it has several layers. The first layer consists of ancient literary memories. Buddhist tradition preserved a powerful image of Ashoka as a righteous king who converted after war, built stupas, and supported the sangha; this image, developed in the Ashokavadana and the Sri Lankan chronicles, traveled to Sri Lanka, Southeast Asia, and East Asia, and formed part of the way Buddhist communities conceived the ideal ruler. Chapter 24 examines Ashoka's place in Sri Lankan tradition, including the story of his son Mahinda. Jain traditions remembered Chandragupta and Samprati as supporters of Jainism. Puranic tradition recorded the Mauryas within lists of royal dynasties, and the Sanskrit tradition preserved the story of Chandragupta's rise. Greek and Roman authors, working from Megasthenes and others, remembered "Sandrocottus" as an Indian king who had made peace with Seleucus.

The second layer is archaeological and epigraphic rediscovery. Over the centuries the script of Ashoka's inscriptions passed out of general knowledge, and the pillars stood in the landscape as objects of curiosity whose texts could not be read. In the fourteenth century, the Delhi sultan Firuz Shah Tughlaq had two Ashokan pillars moved to Delhi, and contemporary accounts record his interest in them and in the puzzle of their unreadable writing, a point discussed further in Chapter 42. Not until the nineteenth century did scholars decipher Brahmi, a breakthrough associated with James Prinsep in 1837, which made Ashoka's edicts readable again. The inscriptions referred to a king styled "Devanampiya Piyadasi," and the identification of this figure with Ashoka drew on Sri Lankan chronicle traditions and was later confirmed by an edict discovered at Maski that used the name Ashoka directly. From this recovery, historians reconstructed Mauryan chronology and Ashoka's importance in a new way.

The third layer is modern historical memory. Ashoka became an important figure in Indian nationalist thought, celebrated as a ruler who combined imperial power with ethical governance. The Ashoka Chakra, the wheel from the abacus of the Sarnath capital, appears on the flag of India adopted in 1947, and the Sarnath Lion Capital was adopted as the state emblem in 1950. School narratives often present the Mauryas as India's "first empire" or "first unifier." These uses are historically significant as evidence of how modern societies use the past. They are not, however, statements about ancient realities. The Mauryan Empire was an ancient imperial polity that held much of South Asia, but it was not a nation-state, it did not include the whole of the region, and it did not create modern India.

The chapter also asks students to challenge a popular claim: that Ashoka was forgotten until the British rediscovered him. The fuller picture is more interesting. Ashoka's inscriptions and their meaning were lost or transformed in many places, and his identity as the author of the pillar edicts was forgotten. But Buddhist textual traditions in South and Southeast Asia preserved powerful memories of Ashoka as a model king. What the nineteenth century recovered was the voice of a historical Ashoka speaking in his own inscriptions, which sometimes matched, sometimes complicated, and sometimes contradicted the stories that traditions had preserved. Modern scholars therefore have to hold together two Ashokas: the historical ruler known from his edicts and the remembered ruler of literary tradition.

Section 18.10

Review and Historical Synthesis

This chapter has asked why Mauryan imperial authority fragmented after Ashoka and which political, religious, artistic, and historical legacies survived the end of the dynasty. The answer requires separating three things that popular accounts tend to run together: what ended, what continued, and what survived as legacy.

What ended? Mauryan dynastic rule ended, conventionally around 185 BCE, with the killing of the last king by his commander. The centralized sovereignty that Pataliputra had exercised across the earlier imperial sphere had by then largely dissolved, gradually rather than suddenly. And Ashoka's particular inscriptional and dhamma program, which Chapter 16 examined as a distinctive experiment in public royal ethics, did not continue as such. Section 18.1 showed that the evidence for this process is thin, that the succession after Ashoka is poorly known, and that Dasharatha's inscriptions give us a rare contemporary glimpse of continued royal activity. Sections 18.2 through 18.4 argued that fragmentation is best understood as the interaction of several factors: less secure succession, weakening regional control, and fiscal, military, and logistical demands that became harder to meet when authority was contested. The feedback loop introduced in Section 18.4 is a reconstruction, not a documented sequence, and it explicitly excludes explanations that blame Ashoka's Buddhism.

What continued? Agriculture, cities, trade, and crafts continued, as Section 18.3 emphasized. Religious communities, Buddhist, Jain, Brahmanical, Ajivika, and others, continued to receive patronage, and the enlargement of Sanchi in the post-Mauryan centuries shows Buddhist institutions expanding rather than collapsing. Regional states grew in importance. Some administrative practices and long-distance networks persisted, and the trade routes that Chapter 17 described did not stop in 185 BCE. The end of the dynasty was not the end of early historic South Asian society.

What survived as legacy? Ashokan inscriptions and monuments survived in the landscape. The artistic forms of Mauryan sculpture and stonework, especially the animal capital and polished surface, left a mark on later art, a subject Chapters 21 and 29 revisit. Memory of Ashoka survived in Buddhist traditions and was recovered in new form in the nineteenth century. The Mauryan example of extensive kingship, public moral ideology, and religious patronage remained available to later rulers, as Section 18.8 argued, though as precedent rather than inherited machinery. Section 18.9 showed that the memory of the Mauryas has been constructed in layers and continues to be used in the present.

The final synthesis should reject a single-cause collapse. The Mauryan Empire fragmented because the political connections holding together a vast and unevenly integrated realm became harder to reproduce after Ashoka. Succession problems, regional autonomy, fiscal and military demands, and changing political conditions reinforced one another. Yet the end of the dynasty was not the end of early historic South Asian society. Regional economies, religious institutions, cities, and exchange networks continued, while Mauryan monuments and memories acquired new meanings over centuries.

The end of Mauryan dynastic rule did not end the political, economic, or religious history that the empire had connected. Instead, authority became increasingly regional. New rulers competed for the Gangetic heartland, northwestern South Asia became tied to new transregional powers, and Buddhist, Brahmanical, Jain, commercial, and artistic networks continued across changing political boundaries. Chapter 19 begins this post-Mauryan world with the Shungas, Kanvas, and the emergence of a more multipolar political order.

Historical Debate

Why Did the Mauryan Empire Fragment?

The question: Historians have proposed many explanations for the end of Mauryan rule. Which factors were most important, and how should they be weighed against the thin evidence?

Interpretation A — Dynastic and political explanations: Some historians emphasize succession difficulties, court rivalries, and the possible division of the realm among members of the royal family, culminating in the coup by a military commander. On this view, the empire's dependence on the personal authority of the king made it vulnerable to any weakening of the monarchy.
Interpretation B — Structural and administrative explanations: Others emphasize the difficulty of administering a very large, unevenly integrated territory with the technologies of communication and transport available, the fiscal and logistical demands of maintaining an army and bureaucracy, and the persistence of regional political structures that reasserted themselves when central control loosened. Some studies also emphasize changing geopolitical conditions, especially in the northwest.
Older explanations now largely rejected: Earlier scholarship sometimes blamed Ashoka's Buddhism, pacifism, or expensive patronage. These explanations depend on the assumption that dhamma weakened the state's coercive capacity, a claim unsupported by Ashoka's own inscriptions and by the evidence that Mauryan rule continued for decades afterward.

Evidence: All the interpretations rely on the same limited body of evidence: later dynastic lists, later literature, a few inscriptions such as Dasharatha's, archaeological patterns, and the later emergence of regional states. None rests on a contemporary account of the decline, and none is directly confirmed by a Mauryan fiscal record.

Why it matters: The choice among explanations affects how historians understand the character of the empire. If the cause was primarily personal or dynastic, the empire looks like a durable structure undone by bad luck. If the cause was structural, it looks like an ambitious state whose scale outran its means of integration. This textbook treats the explanations as interacting rather than competing: succession problems, regional autonomy, fiscal and military demands, and external pressures reinforced one another.

Student task: Choose two of the factors above and explain how each could have made the other worse. Then identify one additional kind of evidence, such as more inscriptions or excavated administrative records, that would help historians decide among the explanations.

Multiple Perspectives

After Ashoka: Reconstructed Perspectives

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical illustrations grounded in the patterns discussed in this chapter. They are not quotations or accounts from any named historical individual.

A court official after Ashoka: Such an official depended on the authority of the king and on the flow of information and revenue between capital and provinces. Uncertain successions would have created pressure to choose which claimant or faction to support, with real consequences for career and safety.
A provincial governor or regional elite: A governor at a distant provincial center might have found that orders from the capital were harder to enforce and that local support mattered more than imperial favor. For regional elites, weaker central authority could mean greater autonomy and new opportunities to consolidate local power.
An Ajivika ascetic receiving a cave from Dasharatha: For a community outside the Buddhist and Jain traditions, royal cave donations meant secure lodging and public recognition. The donation indicates that the Mauryan court's patronage extended beyond the tradition most associated with Ashoka.
A Buddhist donor at an expanding stupa: A lay donor, monk, or nun contributing to a growing site such as Sanchi participated in a devotional life that did not depend on any particular dynasty. Donor inscriptions show that such patronage came from many social groups.
A military officer under the late Mauryas: An army commander held a position of power at the center of the state. Loyalty to the king could conflict with ambition, and the tradition of Brihadratha's overthrow suggests that such commanders could become rivals.
A merchant whose routes continue despite political fragmentation: Merchants needed safe routes, markets, and predictable local authorities more than any specific dynasty. Political fragmentation might raise the number of tolls or authorities to negotiate but did not necessarily stop trade.
Continuity and Change Over Time

Ashokan Empire, Post-Ashokan Mauryan Rule, and the Early Post-Mauryan World

What Changed

  • Central authority weakened and eventually ended in the Gangetic heartland
  • Regional sovereignty increased, and new dynasties emerged
  • Ashoka's public inscriptional program of dhamma was not continued in the same form
  • Mauryan-founded monuments were enlarged and transformed by new patrons

What Stayed the Same

  • Agriculture, villages, and cities continued to function
  • Long-distance and regional trade continued
  • Buddhist, Jain, Brahmanical, and Ajivika communities continued to receive patronage
  • Regional elites and older local political structures persisted
  • Royal patronage of religious communities remained a characteristic form of kingship

Who experienced change most directly? Courts, provincial officials, and military commanders felt political change most directly. Who experienced greater continuity? Cultivators, artisans, merchants, and religious communities more often experienced continuity in daily life, though they would have felt any change in who collected revenue and offered protection.

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing the pattern prevents the equation of dynastic collapse with social or economic collapse.

Everyday Life and Social History

Did Ordinary Life Collapse When the Empire Ended?

The evidence suggests that it did not. The Mauryan state was superimposed on villages, guilds, market towns, and religious communities that had their own institutions. A village headman, a potter, a weaver, or a boatman on the Ganges did not depend on the Mauryan court for daily work. The tax collector might have changed, the local strongman might have become more important, and the roads might have been less secure or more heavily tolled, but ploughing, sowing, harvesting, crafts, and worship went on. Donor inscriptions at Buddhist sites, coins circulating in regional markets, and continuity in town life at sites across the Gangetic plain all point to sustained local activity.

This does not mean that political change had no effect. Conflict at the center could produce violence and disruption in particular places, changing taxation could hurt or help particular groups, and the reorientation of trade routes could favor some towns over others. But dynastic collapse and social or economic collapse are not synonymous, and the historian's task is to identify who was affected, how, and in what places. Because most surviving evidence for this period comes from royal, religious, and elite sources, the lives of ordinary people must be inferred from archaeology and from indirect evidence, and any confident statement about their experience should be labeled accordingly.

Chapter Summary

This chapter completed Part IV by examining the fragmentation of Mauryan imperial authority after Ashoka and by separating the empire's genuine third-century-BCE achievements from the much larger legacy later generations attached to it. Its central argument was that Mauryan authority fragmented for several interacting reasons rather than one decisive cause, and that the end of the dynasty was not the end of early historic South Asian society.

The chapter began with the poorly documented decades after Ashoka's death around 232 BCE. It showed that Puranic, Buddhist, and Jain traditions preserve conflicting lists of successors, that a neat, certain succession list would misrepresent the evidence, and that Dasharatha is the one post-Ashokan king known from contemporary inscriptions, through his cave donations to the Ajivikas at Nagarjuni. It also emphasized the general evidentiary shift from the abundant, ruler-authored documentation of Ashoka's reign to the thin and later documentation of the dynasty's end.

Section 18.2 examined problems of succession, distinguishing dynastic instability from the unsupported claim that weak kings destroyed the empire. It argued that a large empire required repeated successful transfers of authority, that Mauryan succession after Ashoka appears not to have reproduced the same centralized cohesion, and that the abilities of individual successors are too poorly known to serve as an explanation.

Section 18.3 built on Chapter 17's account of uneven integration. It described Mauryan authority as graduated from core to provincial centers to corridors to frontiers, and argued that fragmentation probably proceeded unevenly rather than in a single break. It cautioned against treating every later regional kingdom as a Mauryan province in revolt, and it distinguished loss of dynastic sovereignty from continuity of local society and economy.

Section 18.4 offered the chapter's principal causal analysis. It described the fiscal, logistical, and military demands of governing a vast territory and proposed, as reconstruction rather than documented fact, a feedback loop in which weaker central authority reduced compliance and revenue, which reduced capacity and encouraged regional autonomy. It rejected older claims that Ashoka's Buddhism, pacifism, or generosity bankrupted or disarmed the empire.

Section 18.5 narrated the dynasty's formal end around 185 BCE, when, according to later literary tradition, the last Mauryan king Brihadratha was killed by his commander Pushyamitra, who founded the Shunga dynasty. It identified this narrative as later and unverified in detail, and it reserved the debate over Pushyamitra's religious policy for Chapter 19.

Sections 18.6 and 18.7 turned to legacy in art and religion. They examined Ashokan pillars, the Sarnath Lion Capital, the Barabar caves, and the technical achievement of polished stone, and argued that Mauryan monuments were politically innovative in placing royal messages into durable public landscapes. They showed, through Sanchi, that Mauryan foundations were later enlarged and embellished, that the figure of 84,000 stupas is symbolic, and that Ashoka was a transformative patron within a developing Buddhist tradition rather than the inventor of monumental Buddhism.

Section 18.8 addressed the question of whether later rulers copied the Mauryas. It distinguished institutional continuity from remembered precedent, identified the Mauryan legacies of large-scale territorial monarchy, universalizing claims, public works, inscriptions, moral ideology, and religious patronage, and argued that later states selectively adapted Mauryan models rather than inheriting Mauryan institutions.

Section 18.9 traced the layers of Mauryan memory: ancient Buddhist, Jain, Brahmanical, and Greek traditions; the loss of the meaning of the inscriptions and their recovery through the nineteenth-century decipherment of Brahmi; and modern national symbolism, including the Ashoka Chakra and the Sarnath Lion Capital. It rejected both the claim that Ashoka was entirely forgotten until the nineteenth century and the claim that the Mauryas created modern India.

Section 18.10 synthesized what ended, what continued, and what survived as legacy, and it closed Part IV by handing the narrative to Chapter 19, where authority becomes increasingly regional and new rulers compete in the Gangetic heartland and beyond. The chapter's methodological lesson is that the evidence for decline is much weaker than the evidence for Ashoka's reign, and that responsible history makes that difference visible.

Pushyamitra
Mauryan military commander who, according to later tradition, founded the Shunga dynasty; his state is treated in Chapter 19.
Provincial autonomy
The condition in which regional governors or elites act with substantial independence from a central authority, without necessarily formally rejecting it.
Sovereignty
The recognized supreme authority to govern a territory; loss of dynastic sovereignty is distinct from social or economic collapse.
Core and periphery
An analytical distinction between densely administered central regions and more lightly governed outer zones of an empire.
Fiscal capacity
A state's ability to collect and spend revenue to support administration and military forces.
Logistics
The organization of transport, supply, and communication needed to move officials, goods, information, and troops.
Feedback loop
A causal pattern in which an effect reinforces its own cause, as when weaker authority reduces resources that would restore authority.
Senapati
Commander of an army; Pushyamitra is described in later tradition as the Mauryan senapati.
Monolithic pillar
A pillar carved from a single piece of stone, as in the Ashokan pillars of polished sandstone.
Mauryan polish
A glossy surface finish characteristic of Ashokan pillars and the Barabar caves, not a sufficient criterion for dating an object as Mauryan.
Sarnath Lion Capital
An Ashokan pillar capital of four lions back to back above a decorated abacus, later adopted as the emblem of the Republic of India.
Barabar caves
Granite rock-cut caves in Bihar with polished interiors, donated to the Ajivikas under Ashoka; the nearby Nagarjuni caves carry Dasharatha's donations.
Ajivika
A distinct ascetic community discussed in Chapter 12, recipients of Mauryan cave donations.
Stupa
A solid dome-shaped Buddhist monument, often enclosing relics, that could be enlarged and embellished across centuries.
Relic
A bodily remain or object associated with the Buddha or a revered teacher, venerated as an extension of his presence.
Encasing
The practice of enlarging a stupa by building a new outer layer around an earlier core, as at Sanchi.
84,000 stupas
A symbolic number in Buddhist tradition for the stupas Ashoka is said to have built; not to be taken literally.
Brahmi
The script of Ashoka's inscriptions in most of the empire, deciphered in the nineteenth century.
Decipherment
The scholarly process by which an unreadable script becomes readable, in this case restoring Ashoka's inscriptions to historical use.
Imperial model
A remembered pattern of rulership that later states could adapt, distinct from a directly inherited institution.
Institutional continuity
The actual inheritance and operation of an earlier state's administrative structures by a later state.
Remembered precedent
The knowledge, admiration, or invocation of an earlier ruler or model without necessarily reproducing its institutions.
Historical memory
The ways societies remember, transform, and use the past, distinct from the historical reconstruction of what happened.
Teleology
Explaining the past as though it were directed toward a known later outcome, such as a supposed line from the Mauryas to modern India.
Ashoka Chakra
The wheel motif from the abacus of the Sarnath capital, adopted in 1947 for the flag of India.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how historians reason when the evidence gets thin. The Mauryan Empire fragmented, but no Mauryan document explains why. Historians must reason from structure, from later tradition, from scattered inscriptions, and from the archaeological record, and must say plainly which parts of their account are reconstruction. That habit of separating evidence from inference is a skill that later chapters will require again and again, especially where sources are later, partial, or politically charged.

The chapter also reveals a larger pattern in South Asian history: large empires did not simply rise and fall, and their ends did not erase the societies they had connected. Farmers, artisans, merchants, monks, and regional elites continued to live and work as central authority receded. The Mauryan experience anticipates the later cycles of consolidation and regionalization that recur in the histories of the Guptas, the Delhi Sultanate, the Mughals, and the eighteenth-century successor states, each with different institutions and conditions but with comparable questions about what holds a large political system together.

The Mauryan legacy is equally instructive because it is a legacy of monuments and memory more than of continuing institutions. Ashoka's pillars, the Barabar caves, and the foundation of Sanchi outlasted the dynasty, were altered by later patrons, and were reinterpreted by later societies. The historical Ashoka recovered in the nineteenth century and the remembered Ashoka of Buddhist tradition are not identical, and the modern symbol on a flag or a state seal is a third thing again. Students who learn to distinguish these layers will be better equipped to approach the many other cases, from temples to empires to national heroes, where the past is both studied and used.

Chapter 19 now turns to the world after the Mauryas, beginning with the Shungas and Kanvas in the Gangetic heartland and continuing through the more multipolar political order that followed. Part V will show that the end of the Mauryan Empire opened, rather than closed, a period of remarkable political, commercial, and religious creativity across early historic South Asia and the connected ancient world.
