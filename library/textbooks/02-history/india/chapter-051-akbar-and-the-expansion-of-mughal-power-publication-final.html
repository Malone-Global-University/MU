Akbar and the Expansion of Mughal Power

Indian History: South Asia from the Indus Civilization to the Present

Chapter Opening

In 1556 a thirteen-year-old was proclaimed emperor in a camp near Kalanaur, ruling little more than a contested corridor between Kabul and Delhi. By 1605 Akbar's empire stretched from Kabul to Bengal and toward the Deccan.

This chapter traces how conquest, negotiated alliance, control of military appointments and the distribution of revenue produced a durable but unevenly controlled state. It continues Chapter 50 and reserves court structure, provincial and revenue administration, and religious policy for Chapter 52. Evidence includes Abu'l-Fazl's official Akbarnama, Bada'uni's critical history and Nizam al-Din Ahmad's Tabaqat-i Akbari, each requiring source criticism.

Akbar's achievements must therefore be understood without assuming that later Mughal successes were predetermined in 1556. The story connects battlefields to revenue assignments, military command to household alliances, and the claims of a universal emperor to the continuing authority of regional rulers. Students should ask not only where the empire advanced but who paid for the advances, which communities negotiated with its officers, and how the meaning of sovereignty changed from one frontier to another.

Learning Objectives

Students should be able to:

  1. Explain why Mughal survival in 1556 was uncertain.
  2. Analyze the relationship between conquest and political integration.
  3. Distinguish conquest from effective pacification.
  4. Evaluate the range of Rajput relations with the Mughals.
  5. Trace campaigns in Gujarat, Bengal, the northwest and the Deccan.
  6. Describe mansabdari and jagirdari as evolving institutions.

Guiding Historical Questions

  1. Why was Mughal survival in 1556 uncertain, and what changed by 1560?
  2. How did Akbar prevent commanders from becoming regional rulers?
  3. Was Rajput relations a single policy or a range of responses?
  4. Why did conquering a capital not equal controlling a region?
  5. How did the northwest frontier differ from the Gangetic plain?
  6. What did the Deccan campaigns achieve and not achieve?
  7. How did mansabdari and jagirdari support expansion?

Historical Context

Babur's victories of 1526 and 1527 gave the Mughals a foothold in northern India, but Humayun lost it to Sher Shah Sur in 1540 and regained it only in 1555 (Chapter 50). The Sur state had built roads, a coinage and revenue practices that the Mughals inherited, while Afghan commanders, Rajput houses and regional sultanates remained powerful. Chapter 51 traces Akbar's consolidation; Chapter 52 examines governance and ideology.

Division I: Accession, Panipat, and the Struggle for Survival

Section 51.1

Humayun's Death, Akbar's Accession, and the Crisis of 1556

Humayun died in January 1556 after falling on the stairs of his library at Delhi, barely six months after recovering the city from the Surs. His death was initially kept quiet. Akbar, born in 1542 during his father's exile, was in the Punjab with Bairam Khan, campaigning against Sikandar Sur, and was enthroned at Kalanaur in February 1556.

The inheritance was precarious. Mughal control covered Delhi, Agra and parts of the Punjab, while Kabul was held separately and the Gangetic plain, Rajasthan, Malwa, Gujarat and Bengal lay beyond reach. Bairam Khan, a long-serving Turkoman commander, acted as regent (vakil), so authority rested on a guardian's command of the army rather than on institutions.

Our evidence for these years is largely Persian court history, above all Abu'l-Fazl's Akbarnama, written decades later to present the reign as destined to succeed, along with Nizam al-Din Ahmad's Tabaqat-i Akbari and Bada'uni's more critical account. Details of the enthronement, such as the hastily prepared platform at Kalanaur, vary by source and should be read in that light.

The immediate problem was not only enemies but timing. Humayun's death was initially concealed, and the young emperor's authority was untested. Mughal officers had to decide whether to follow a boy, a regent or their own ambitions, and the dynasty's survival depended on that decision.

The difference between a dynastic proclamation and actual obedience explains the first months of the reign. The boy possessed a celebrated Timurid name, but his father's recovery of the Indo-Gangetic heartland was very recent. Many officers had served several rulers and were accustomed to negotiating their obligations. News of Humayun's death threatened to encourage defections, especially because Afghan competitors could mobilize older networks inherited from the Lodis and Surs. The proclamation at Kalanaur therefore needed to be followed by a demonstration that Akbar's army could retain its cohesion, hold the Punjab approaches, and support the restoration of imperial authority farther south. In the political vocabulary of the period, sovereignty was not secured by an accession ceremony alone: the sovereign needed commanders capable of keeping the roads, treasuries, and fortified posts on which his claims depended.

Kalanaur also illustrates the importance of mobile government. Early Mughal sovereignty often traveled with the household, military camp, standards, and officials who authenticated appointments. Holding a capital mattered, but a field army near the Punjab could preserve the dynasty even if Delhi was lost temporarily. The regency created an institutional paradox. Bairam Khan had experience, allies, and authority to compel obedience, yet those same assets made officers suspect that he could monopolize patronage. Akbar depended on him while simultaneously needing the allegiance of commanders who regarded the boy as a legitimate ruler in his own right. Later court histories present a providential beginning; their language should not obscure the practical uncertainty evident in rival claimants' campaigns and quickly changing military allegiances.

The court's composition helps illuminate the political choices available to its members. Soldiers originating in Iran, Central Asia, and Afghanistan expected compensation, while old families attached to Humayun wished to protect precedence; Indian recruits and local garrison officers weighed what a new sovereign might offer. Women of the Timurid household helped preserve dynastic relationships and legitimacy, though the chronicles seldom give them equal narrative space. Merchants and cultivators had a more immediate concern: whether the next campaigning season would bring requisitions or stable tax collection. Thus the accession was simultaneously a crisis of succession, military command, finance, and public order. The immediate achievements of 1556 deserve recognition because their eventual success could not have been assumed by anyone living through the crisis.

One further difficulty was chronological: imperial news traveled slowly compared with the speed at which rival commanders could exploit uncertainty. The delayed announcement of a ruler's death was a customary expedient, not proof that his successors already controlled the country. An appointment or proclamation had to be carried to particular towns, read in public settings, and accepted by local military officers. Those who delayed recognition might be awaiting reports from another prince. In this environment a regent's first task was to coordinate intelligence and prevent contradictory messages from dissolving the fragile coalition around the throne. The source traditions are exceptionally interested in auspicious dates and royal ceremonies; the historian must supplement that emphasis with questions about communications and the actual movement of troops.

Section 51.2

Hemu, the Sur Successor States, and the Recapture of Delhi

Hemu came from a non-noble background and rose through administrative and supply service under the Sur rulers, eventually commanding armies for Adil Shah Sur. Contemporary Persian chronicles mostly portray him as an upstart, so their hostility must be weighed when judging his abilities.

In October 1556 Hemu defeated the Mughal governor Tardi Beg near Delhi and entered the city. He took the title Vikramaditya, claiming kingship in his own name. His success showed that Mughal rule was recent and reversible, and that the Sur successor states still commanded significant resources.

Hemu's rise matters because it complicates the picture of a simple Mughal-Afghan contest. A man from a commercial and administrative background could command Afghan armies, which shows how fluid loyalty and rank were in the Sur successor states. Persian chroniclers, hostile to him, give limited information about his early life.

When Hemu advanced on Delhi, the Mughal governor Tardi Beg withdrew rather than fight to the end. Bairam Khan blamed him for abandoning the city and had him killed, a decision that signalled the regent's willingness to discipline powerful officers and also revealed how quickly Mughal control could unravel.

Hemu's career exposes the limits of histories organized exclusively around reigning dynasties. His advancement through transport, provisioning, and administrative service involved skills essential to campaigning: obtaining food, mobilizing animals, managing communications, and coordinating personnel. Such abilities could make a nonhereditary servant indispensable to an Afghan court. The Sur successor states were divided among rival claimants after Islam Shah's death, and military entrepreneurs could seek high office by winning battles even when they lacked dynastic descent. Hemu's command under Muhammad Adil Shah Sur combined service to a nominal ruler with growing personal power. His armies drew on soldiers whose political allegiance cannot be reduced to any single religious identity. Afghan factional divisions also meant that a victory for Hemu could imperil some Afghan nobles while benefiting others.

Capturing Agra and then Delhi altered the strategic balance. These cities offered revenue, stores, weapons, prestige, and control of routes through the upper Gangetic plain. Yet holding them required agreements with the garrisons and local chiefs who actually controlled nearby crossings and grain markets. Claims about Hemu's royal title and coronation depend upon chroniclers with strong political interests, and their exact ceremonial descriptions are not equally secure. What is beyond dispute is that his success posed a genuine alternative to the fragile Mughal restoration. The Mughals could not simply wait for rebellion to fade; they faced a mobilized army able to turn the prestige of Delhi against their young ruler.

Persian court writers frequently present Hemu's rise as an unnatural reversal of hierarchy, making his commercial background an insult rather than an explanatory fact. The historian should instead investigate how wartime states promoted expertise, why Afghan officers accepted a commander without their lineage, and how victory created its own claim to legitimacy. Tardi Beg's retreat and subsequent killing reveal the corresponding Mughal anxiety. Bairam Khan interpreted the loss of Delhi as a threat requiring discipline, but killing an officer also risked frightening others whose service was indispensable. The choice to fight rather than retreat toward Kabul rested on assessments of the Punjab army, Hemu's operational position, and the costs of abandoning the symbolic center of the restored dynasty. Panipat therefore followed a complex crisis, not a simple encounter between predetermined winners and losers.

Section 51.3

The Second Battle of Panipat, 5 November 1556

The armies met at Panipat on 5 November 1556, the same plain where Babur had won in 1526. Hemu's force was larger and included many elephants, but the Mughal vanguard and flanks pressed it effectively. Hemu was reportedly struck by an arrow and his army lost cohesion when he fell.

He was brought before Akbar and Bairam Khan and killed. Sources disagree about who struck the blow, and some later accounts reflect the interests of those telling them. The victory reopened Delhi and Agra, but it removed one commander, not Afghan power as a whole.

Later Mughal court narratives place the young emperor at some distance from the main fighting. The Mughal vanguard under Ali Quli Khan Shaibani is credited with capturing Hemu's artillery before the main armies fully engaged, and the capture of the guns helped turn the battle, though details differ between sources.

The execution of the captive Hemu has been interpreted both as a justified wartime act and as a deliberate demonstration of strength. Chroniclers present it as proof of Akbar's early resolve, while later writers question whether the story reflects the legitimating purposes of the official history.

Historians disagree about how decisive the battle was. Some stress that it removed the strongest Afghan commander and reopened the capital, while others note that survival also depended on the years that followed. The disagreement concerns weighting, since sources agree on the battle's outcome.

Battle narratives require particular scrutiny because the official historian knew the outcome and used it to demonstrate Akbar's royal fortune. The Mughal advance guard's seizure of enemy guns before the main encounter may have deprived Hemu of an important resource, but armies were not automatically defeated when a single formation lost its artillery. Hemu commanded forces accustomed to fighting in northern India and included cavalry as well as heavily equipped elephants. The elephants offered shock power and elevated positions for their commanders, while also creating difficulties if frightened or deprived of leadership. The Mughal commanders benefited from coordinated mounted movements and the ability to exploit disorder once the opposing command structure was disrupted. The engagement was therefore an interaction of preparation, reconnaissance, morale, and an unexpected injury, rather than a demonstration that one weapon or group possessed inherent superiority.

Hemu's incapacitation presents a classic source problem. Court histories present the arrow wound as a providential turning point, and later retellings disagree on whether Akbar, Bairam Khan, or another official personally killed the wounded captive. The distinction matters because the story was repeatedly used to portray the emperor either as a devout conqueror or as a ruler displaying mercy. A responsible narrative can establish the defeat and execution while refusing to endorse an exact dramatic gesture unsupported by independent witnesses. The visual imagery of the youthful ruler, captive rival, and battlefield has often overshadowed the participation of cavalrymen, elephant handlers, scouts, and soldiers whose experiences shaped the actual result.

Panipat did not end the Sur political world. Sikandar Sur remained an opponent; Afghan commanders held positions across the plains; and the Mughals still needed to recover forts and roads. Nor did the battle establish the administrative institutions that would later characterize Akbar's reign. Its immediate effect was to restore access to the central cities and to validate the regency's decision to stand and fight. Its long-term significance emerged only because subsequent campaigns, appointments, and political bargains prevented another collapse. This distinction between decisive battlefield outcomes and conditional strategic consequences is important for interpreting the later Mughal victories at Chittor, Rajmahal, and Ahmadnagar. In each instance contemporaries could celebrate conquest while provincial officers continued negotiating what conquest meant on the ground.

The battle can also be examined through its material consequences. Artillery taken before an engagement might be valuable because guns required skilled crews, transport animals, ammunition, and secure ground, not simply because their firing power was extraordinary. Elephants helped commanders oversee troops but also concentrated the risks of command in a visible person. The moment Hemu was wounded, officers needed a means to convey orders to formations that may have been separated by dust and confusion. The surviving accounts are too partial to reconstruct every movement with certainty, yet the interplay between visibility, communications, and leadership explains how a force that was still capable of fighting could become disorganized rapidly. Such analysis is more convincing than assigning all responsibility to providence or courage.

Section 51.4

Defeating the Remaining Sur Claimants

Over the next years Mughal commanders pursued the remaining Sur claimants. Sikandar Sur surrendered at Mankot in 1557 and received a grant of territory, a reminder that defeated opponents were often absorbed. Adil Shah Sur died in battle in Bengal in 1557.

Even so, Gwalior, Jaunpur and much of the eastern Gangetic region remained contested, and Afghan officers continued to hold local power. Control during these years meant garrisons, roads and forts more than territorial administration.

Mankot, a hill fortress in the Siwalik region, held Sikandar Sur for months in a siege before his surrender. His terms, which gave him a territorial assignment, show that the Mughals preferred negotiated absorption of a defeated rival to prolonged fighting in difficult terrain.

Meanwhile, Mughal forces took Ajmer, Gwalior and Jaunpur in the late 1550s through campaigns whose pace depended on the availability of troops and the loyalty of local garrisons. Many Afghan commanders retained local power, so restored control meant a network of garrisons rather than uniform administration.

The siege of Mankot illustrates an early form of Mughal statecraft: isolating a rival in a difficult fortress until submission became more attractive than continued resistance. Mountain fortresses constrained cavalry, protected stores, and allowed defenders to choose when to negotiate. A large army surrounding such a place faced the expense of feeding horses and men without easy access to decisive battle. Agreements involving grants, safe conduct, or service were not signs of weakness; they were methods of converting military pressure into political settlement. Nonetheless the bargain had limits. A defeated claimant might renounce a title while followers continued to exercise influence in surrounding districts, and the emperor needed to ensure that the negotiated terms did not finance renewed revolt.

The eastern campaigns were still more demanding. Control over Delhi did not automatically open a secure road through Jaunpur, the middle Ganges, and Bihar. Fortified towns depended upon regional landholders and commanders, many of whom had served the Surs and possessed family links to competing Afghan groups. Imperial detachments could occupy strategic towns without having the officials or information needed to assess and collect revenue across surrounding villages. Periodic campaigns therefore alternated with negotiations and appointments, creating a map of uneven control. Some Afghan leaders entered service, others retired to their home regions, and still others moved toward the eastern frontier where distance, rivers, and allied households offered protection.

The distinction between surrender and incorporation should be made concrete. A grant promised at court could require enforcement by a governor; a newly appointed officer needed money to pay his retainers; and a defeated fort-holder could remain a local mediator because no replacement commanded the same relationships. Even the capture of Gwalior and Ajmer was part of a prolonged process rather than an instantaneous transformation of political society. The achievements of Akbar's first years were thus cumulative: the recovery of strategic centers, weakening of alternative dynastic claims, and gradual formation of an officer corps that could carry orders beyond the main imperial camp. Later campaigns became possible because these limited early successes secured routes, supplies, and a political nucleus.

Section 51.5

Bairam Khan's Regency: Military Recovery and Political Authority

Bairam Khan directed military operations and distributed offices and revenue assignments. He favored followers from his own network, and he was reported to be Shia in a court where many leading nobles were Sunni, a distinction that sharpened rivalries even if religion was not the only cause.

His regency stabilized the dynasty but concentrated patronage in one man. Turani nobles, Akbar's household women and ambitious officers increasingly saw the regent as an obstacle to their own advancement, while Akbar grew into adulthood.

Bairam Khan's regency can be read as military necessity, since the dynasty needed an experienced commander, but also as an experiment in delegated power. He promoted officers of his own circle and influenced appointments to religious and judicial posts, which angered competing groups at court.

The tension was structural. A regent strong enough to win battles was also strong enough to control the emperor's access to patronage. This pattern, in which military success created potential rivals to the throne, would shape much of Akbar's later policy toward his nobles.

A regency is more than a guardian acting for an underage monarch. It determines who may speak in the sovereign's name, distribute rank, confirm grants, lead armies, and decide which petitioners receive an audience. Bairam Khan's title and prestige gave him access to each of these channels. Officers whose careers had depended on Humayun could resent being displaced by those close to the regent, while men promoted by Bairam saw his position as the best protection of their own status. This conflict was not reducible to a clean division between Sunni and Shia nobles. Religious affiliation mattered in particular appointments and polemics, but kinship, personal service, military performance, and access to resources frequently crossed confessional lines.

The regent's military successes made the political dilemma sharper. On the one hand, Bairam could persuade hesitant soldiers that the restored dynasty was capable of rewarding loyalty. On the other, the same patronage power enabled critics to depict him as a rival center of authority. The most revealing evidence is often the disposition of offices and the movement of commanders, rather than hostile anecdotes about temperament. Household members had different kinds of influence: the emperor's mother and foster relations could communicate access and favor, while senior military men commanded soldiers. These channels intersected in court politics but should not be confused with a single faction acting with one consistent program.

Akbar's gradual assertion of independence also concerned the public meaning of monarchy. If all important decisions appeared to come from his guardian, nobles might continue to bargain with the regent instead of the emperor. If the boy dismissed the regent too abruptly, the resulting rupture might destroy the command structure that had saved the dynasty at Panipat. The transition therefore depended on timing, alternative networks, and a credible guarantee that Bairam's former followers would not all be punished. Later narratives emphasize the emperor's precocious judgment, but the structural question was how to transfer an army's obedience from an effective minister to a sovereign who sought personal control without losing experienced servants.

Section 51.6

The End of the Regency and Akbar's Emergence

In 1560 Akbar, nearing eighteen, dismissed Bairam Khan and ordered him to go on the pilgrimage to Mecca. Bairam Khan instead rebelled briefly, submitted, and was assassinated in Gujarat in 1561 by an Afghan with a personal grievance.

Influence then passed to Maham Anga and her son Adham Khan. In 1562 Adham Khan killed a senior minister, and Akbar had him punished with death. The episode helped establish that no guardian or powerful household would command the emperor.

Akbar's removal of Bairam Khan was prepared with the support of figures in the harem and among nobles who resented the regent's dominance. After his dismissal Bairam Khan rebelled near Jalandhar but was defeated and pardoned, and was assassinated in Gujarat by an Afghan whose father had died in the earlier fighting. His death shows the personal grievances that war left behind.

The brief ascendancy of Maham Anga's circle ended when Adham Khan killed the minister Ataga Khan in 1562 and Akbar ordered his own punishment. The incident is often taken as the point at which Akbar visibly stopped delegating control, though historians caution that the consolidation of his authority continued for years afterward.

The dismissal of Bairam Khan in 1560 should not be treated as an overnight transformation into absolute monarchy. Akbar had reached an age when military nobles expected him to exercise direct authority, but his government still depended on powerful foster kin and household patrons. The invitation to perform the pilgrimage provided a face-saving route for a former guardian to leave political command. Bairam's brief rebellion demonstrated that the settlement was fragile. His defeat and pardon offered a means to end the dispute without treating all his adherents as permanent enemies; his subsequent assassination by an Afghan with a grievance arose from the violent consequences of earlier campaigns rather than a simple imperial execution.

Maham Anga and the people associated with her occupied influential positions because household service created proximity to the emperor, but it did not amount to a legally organized regency with the same foundation as Bairam's. The killing of Ataga Khan by Adham Khan in 1562 showed how intensely access to royal patronage was contested. Akbar's order to execute Adham Khan announced that intimate family connections could not shield an offender from the ruler's coercive authority. The dramatic setting later made the incident useful for stories of Akbar's personal courage. Its political importance lay in establishing limits for a foster family whose prestige might otherwise interfere with officers directly answerable to the emperor.

The longer transition continued through military and administrative decisions. Akbar cultivated new nobles, took a more visible role in campaigning, and sought information from officers outside any single established household. The personal authority of the monarch expanded, but it still needed institutions for managing appointments and collecting resources. The political culture of Timur and Humayun had allowed very large followings to attach themselves to individual princes and commanders. Akbar's emerging solution was not to abolish these relationships but to make careers depend increasingly on imperial recognition and reassignment. The campaigns against Malwa, rebellious Uzbeks, and regional rulers were therefore part of the same process as the end of the regency: each tested whether the emperor could command soldiers and distribute rewards without permanent dependence on a powerful intermediary.

Division II: Military Consolidation

Section 51.7

The Mughal Nobility and the Problems of Centralized Command

The Mughal nobility was not a single group. Turani officers, often of Central Asian background, and Irani officers, many from the Safavid world, were joined by Afghans and Indian-born Muslims. Each group had networks and competed for office and revenue.

The difficulty for Akbar was that conquest rewarded commanders with provinces and followers. Without checks, a successful general could behave as an independent ruler, which is why many of the military crises of the 1560s are better understood as contests over command than as isolated rebellions.

Akbar's method was to balance groups against one another rather than to rely on one. Turani officers, often holding senior commands, were counterweighed by Iranis, Afghans, Indian-born Muslims and, increasingly, Rajputs, whose service gave the emperor allies not tied to Central Asian networks.

The family and clan ties of some officers, including those linked to Ataga Khan and Maham Anga, show that rivalries were as much about household politics as about ethnic origin. Historians therefore caution against treating 'Turani' or 'Irani' as fixed political parties.

The imperial nobility must be understood as a working arrangement, not a set of fixed ethnic parties. Terms such as Turani and Irani pointed toward networks of origin, language, migration, and patronage; their members were divided by family rivalries and did not automatically vote or fight together. Afghan officers also differed according to their recent political histories, and many Indian Muslims had served earlier sultanates or regional courts. The incorporation of Rajput ruling houses added soldiers and legitimacy of a different sort. For the emperor, diversity could prevent any one group from monopolizing military office, but only if rank and reward remained credible to all. Balanced recruitment was therefore a problem of institutional design: a ruler had to maintain competition without creating universal distrust.

Command and compensation were inseparable. A provincial officer was expected to defend territory, suppress disturbances, provision troops, and remit or account for revenues. But the economic resources he supervised could help him create an independent following. His relatives and clients might occupy forts, collect rents, and exercise local justice with little immediate oversight. When the court reassigned an officer, it had to ensure that the successor could actually take possession. The administrative challenge lay in separating a man's imperial appointment from any claim that his district belonged personally to his lineage. Transfers, inspections, and ranks all attempted to solve this problem, though frequent transfers could undermine knowledge of local conditions.

Much of Akbar's political life was devoted to making military service legible to the throne. Names, ranks, horses, obligations, and salaries had to be recorded and verified, while the monarch also needed discretion to reward exceptional performance. Excessive standardization might alienate commanders accustomed to personal negotiation, but unchecked patronage could reproduce the centrifugal politics of Humayun's reign. Akbar experimented with both personal trust and administrative regularity, an interaction later visible in the mansab system. The political objective was durable command of a large, composite army, rather than cultural uniformity among the men who staffed it.

Section 51.8

The Conquest of Malwa, 1561–1562

The campaign against Baz Bahadur, ruler of Malwa, began in 1561 under Adham Khan and Pir Muhammad. Mughal forces took Sarangpur, and later chroniclers describe the killing of captives and the seizure of Baz Bahadur's household. Baz Bahadur recovered the region briefly before Mughal forces under Abdullah Khan Uzbek retook it in 1562.

Malwa illustrates that victory in the field did not produce governance. Local landholders, rival commanders and uncertain revenue meant control was repeatedly contested. Baz Bahadur himself later entered Mughal service.

Chroniclers, particularly the critical Bada'uni, report atrocities by Adham Khan's forces at Sarangpur, and accounts say Akbar rode there himself to rebuke his commander. The later legend of Baz Bahadur and the singer Rupmati belongs largely to subsequent tradition and should be distinguished from contemporary evidence.

Malwa lay on routes to Gujarat and the Deccan, which made it strategically valuable. But holding it required resolving disputes among officers, relations with local zamindars and the collection of revenue, tasks the first conquests did not accomplish.

Malwa occupied a strategic hinge between the northern plains, Gujarat, and the Deccan. It possessed fertile districts, market towns, and roads that could connect the emperor's heartland to western trade. The former Malwa Sultanate had been weakened by regional wars and the political ambitions of powerful neighbors, making Baz Bahadur's regime vulnerable to an expansionist court. Yet the defeat of his army did not eliminate his local prestige. His supporters could retreat, regroup, and rely on chiefs who knew the terrain better than newly arrived Mughal officers. The campaign thereby demonstrates the difference between overthrowing a sovereign and reconstructing his state's networks of taxation, military obligation, and personal loyalty.

The behavior of imperial commanders mattered nearly as much as opposition from Baz Bahadur. Contemporary and later Persian narratives describe harsh conduct by Adham Khan and Pir Muhammad, including killings and seizure of property. These accounts require scrutiny: Bada'uni was often critical of powerful men, and Abu'l-Fazl could use a commander's misconduct to emphasize the emperor's superior justice. Nevertheless, the political issue was real. Victorious officers sought to appropriate movable wealth and control local appointments; Akbar wanted conquests to strengthen his treasury and royal reputation rather than establish independent military fiefdoms. The emperor's response to reports of atrocities was therefore not merely a moral tableau but part of the contest over who had authority to dispose of conquered resources.

Malwa's subsequent history complicates any simple year-of-conquest label. Baz Bahadur recovered ground temporarily, and governors had to suppress recurrent challenges. The music and cultural patronage associated with his court later became entangled with legends about Rupmati; such traditions offer evidence about memory, not always reliable detail about the campaign. When Baz Bahadur eventually entered Mughal service, accommodation permitted a formerly hostile ruler to retain status without reconstructing his independent kingdom. In this respect Malwa foreshadowed Rajput incorporation while differing from it: regional power could be subdued through repeated warfare, personal submission, and integration into an imperial career structure. The social effects extended beyond rulers as local communities adapted to requisitions, changing tax collectors, and new demands on travel routes.

The question of accountability deserves attention. When a commander accused of brutality retained important followers, the emperor's rebuke could not automatically undo the damage or restore confiscated property. Civilian losses and displaced households could persist long after a court chronicle concluded its account of royal justice. The military occupation of Malwa therefore tested not only Akbar's capacity to discipline his officers but his government's ability to protect trade, collect revenue predictably, and offer local elites reasons to cooperate. A ruler might win political prestige by denouncing a subordinate's actions while still depending on that subordinate's soldiery. This tension recurred in later Mughal campaigns and complicates idealized stories that treat imperial disapproval as equivalent to effective protection.

Section 51.9

Gondwana, Rani Durgavati, and the Campaign of 1564

Garha-Katanga in the central Narmada region was governed after the death of its ruler by Rani Durgavati as regent for her son Vir Narayan. In 1564 Asaf Khan, the Mughal governor at Kara, invaded.

Durgavati fought at the head of her forces and died in the engagement; her son was killed when Chauragarh fell. The campaign gave the Mughals access to a region with forts and reputed wealth, and it showed that women's regencies could be both military and political leadership, though Mughal chroniclers present them through their own lens.

Garha-Katanga lay among forests and hills inhabited by Gond communities, with a ruling house that had built forts such as Chauragarh. The region offered wealth in cattle, treasure and revenue, which attracted Mughal attention, and the campaign was led by a provincial governor on the frontier rather than by the emperor.

Accounts say Durgavati, wounded in the battle, took her own life to avoid capture. Afterward, a Gond ruler of the family was permitted to hold a reduced territory under Mughal supremacy, an arrangement that later chroniclers describe differently. The episode illustrates how conquest could end in partial restoration.

The state of Garha-Katanga was neither a marginal wilderness nor a political vacuum. It was a significant regional kingdom whose rulers commanded forts, agricultural settlements, forest resources, and networks of Gond and other local communities. Rani Durgavati had political authority as regent for Vir Narayan, but such regency required alliances with commanders and chiefs rather than merely a hereditary claim. The state's capacity to move troops and defend difficult terrain suggests institutions that imperial Persian accounts only partly reveal. Their focus on the Mughal campaign can obscure the governing experience, court politics, and regional economic systems of the people the invaders encountered.

Asaf Khan's expedition of 1564 was undertaken by a powerful imperial officer operating from the northern frontier. The terrain around the Narmada valley offered defensible approaches and routes to strongholds, but it also created supply difficulties for a cavalry-centered army. Accounts differ in the details of the engagement and emphasize Durgavati's personal participation; they commonly describe her death after being wounded, reportedly by her own hand to avoid capture. Such passages should be read for the ways chroniclers represented female rulership and honorable resistance as well as for their basic account of military defeat. Vir Narayan's death in the subsequent fighting further destabilized the ruling household, while the seizure of treasures and elephants had immediate fiscal attractions for the conquerors.

Imperial power did not simply replace all regional authority. Later arrangements left elements of the local ruling lineage and chiefs in possession of territory under changed terms. This was typical of expansion into environments where the state lacked enough officers, soldiers, or information to govern each valley directly. The campaign also raises a methodological issue: a court historian may describe a region mainly in terms of the wealth extracted from it, while local communities experienced the event as a violent disruption of succession and land control. A full textbook account must acknowledge both. Garha-Katanga reveals not only the reach of Akbar's commanders but the continuing importance of indigenous political formations within the geography of the expanding empire.

Section 51.10

The Uzbek and Other Noble Rebellions, 1562–1567

Between about 1562 and 1567 the powerful Uzbek commanders, including Ali Quli Khan Zaman and Abdullah Khan, rose against Akbar. Their grievances included reward, rank and autonomy in their governorships.

Akbar answered with campaigns, pardons and reassignments. The suppression of Ali Quli Khan's revolt in 1567 removed a major rival power group, but he also showed a pattern of forgiveness when it served the empire's interests.

The Uzbek revolts unfolded in stages. Abdullah Khan rebelled in Malwa in the mid-1560s and fled to Gujarat, while Ali Quli Khan Zaman and his kinsmen rebelled in the east. Akbar pardoned them once and campaigned against them again when they rose a second time, in alliance with a threat from Kabul.

Ali Quli Khan was killed in battle in 1567 near Allahabad. The revolts show a repeated pattern: large provincial commands made rebellion possible, and Akbar's strategy mixed leniency with force, a combination that later shaped how he treated defeated opponents.

The Uzbek rebellions emerged from the same military networks that had helped preserve the empire. Officers such as Ali Quli Khan Zaman and Abdullah Khan had personal followings and provincial power bases, and the rewards of conquest furnished them with the means to resist an increasingly assertive court. Their designation as Uzbeks describes connections and reputations, not a coherent national opposition. Individual rebels made alliances opportunistically, while others of similar background remained loyal. For the emperor, a provincial revolt was especially dangerous if it coincided with pressure from Kabul or a regional ruler, because the resources needed to suppress one enemy could encourage another to move.

The sequence was uneven. Abdullah Khan's difficulty with the court in Malwa developed separately from troubles in the eastern provinces. Ali Quli Khan and his associates had previously held high positions, received forgiveness, and returned to rebellion. Akbar moved between military pressure, promises of pardon, and attempts to divide insurgents. This policy could be criticized as inconsistency, but a ruler lacking the capacity to destroy every opponent had strong reasons to reward timely surrender. The greatest risk was making forgiveness seem automatic: rivals might conclude that they could rebel without serious consequence. The emperor's personal movement toward the eastern theatre signaled that the throne would contest independent command even when the insurgents had been longtime imperial servants.

The suppression of the eastern rebels in 1567 damaged the power of a specific group, not every form of aristocratic autonomy. Noble households still controlled retainers and enjoyed local contacts. The lasting achievement was to make overt disobedience more costly and to show that royal intervention could reach provinces some distance from the capital. In the background stood another problem: the army's records, revenue assignments, and transfers were not yet sufficiently regular to allow the emperor to supervise everything from a single office. The conflicts of the 1560s help explain why later reforms attempted to make military obligations measurable and commanders dependent on imperial appointment. They also remind the reader that institutions often grow from crises rather than from a complete design announced at the start of a reign.

Section 51.11

Imperial Warfare: Recruitment, Artillery, Cavalry, Fortresses, and Supply

Mughal armies were composite. Cavalry formed the core, supported by infantry, matchlockmen, artillery, elephants and engineers. They drew on manpower from Central Asia, Iran, Afghanistan and many Indian regions.

Sieges required mining, covered approaches and heavy guns, and campaigns depended on grain, fodder and transport. Seasonality mattered: monsoon rains slowed movement and rivers could halt armies. Warfare was therefore as much about logistics and bargaining as about battle.

Akbar took an active interest in gunnery. Artillery and matchlocks became more prominent, supported by craftsmen who cast cannon and made powder, while heavy guns were used mainly in sieges and were slow to move. Cavalry remained central in the field.

Provisioning depended on merchants, notably itinerant grain carriers, together with state-organized supply and boat transport on rivers. A campaign could fail simply because an army outran its supplies, and the movement of armies often damaged the lands through which they passed, affecting local cultivators.

A Mughal campaign was a moving social and economic organism. Cavalry provided speed, scouting, pursuit, and much battlefield shock, but horses depended on fodder and constant replacement. Artillery offered different advantages in open engagements and fortification warfare; heavy siege pieces could be effective yet slow and expensive to move. Matchlockmen, archers, pioneers, boatmen, builders, and engineers performed tasks that narrative chronicles frequently assigned little glory. The imperial camp could contain merchants, artisans, money handlers, cooks, grooms, and families alongside soldiers. Its demand for grain and animals shaped prices in surrounding markets, and its passage could burden local farmers regardless of whether an army called itself their protector.

The empire's geographical range created several kinds of logistics. On the upper Ganges, transport by river could move grain and men, though monsoon floods made crossings unpredictable. In Rajasthan, water availability, rock fortresses, and arid approaches constrained large armies. In Bengal, cavalry could move poorly through waterways where boats and local pilots were decisive. In mountain valleys near Kabul, supply lines depended on passes, seasonal conditions, and negotiation with local groups. The same military organization that overran an open plain might struggle to secure a distant hill post. Administrative integration therefore depended partly on learning to employ different regional skills rather than merely sending more imperial cavalry.

Fortress warfare illustrates this labor-intensive character of conquest. Covered approaches, trenches, galleries, mines, and artillery platforms required skilled workers and protection against defenders' counterattacks. The siege of Chittor brought the emperor's engineers into the center of the narrative; the siege of Asirgarh decades later demonstrated that formidable positions could still resist even a mature imperial government. Commanders had to judge whether assault, starvation, bribery, or negotiated surrender offered the best result. Over time the court's improved ability to mobilize specialists and financing widened the possible scale of military operations. It did not remove the fundamental vulnerability of campaigns to disease, seasonal weather, overloaded supply routes, and resistance organized around difficult terrain.

The financial organization of an army was also tied to credit. Merchants and bankers could advance money or deliver provisions against promises of future payment, while officers sometimes drew on projected receipts from revenue assignments. Military supplies therefore moved through markets that were vulnerable to changing prices, insecurity, and disputes over repayment. A siege lasting several months might exhaust an official's immediate cash even when the emperor possessed great wealth elsewhere. The scale of a campaign consequently depended on money transfer, contract enforcement, and the willingness of transport communities to participate. These institutions belonged to the wider South Asian economy, not solely to the Mughal state, and their successful use was an important but rarely celebrated component of imperial warfare.

Division III: Rajput Alliances, Conquest, and Resistance

Section 51.12

Rajput States and the Political Geography of Rajasthan

Rajasthan was not a unified political space. Mewar, ruled from Chittor by the Sisodiya house, claimed high prestige; Amber, Marwar, Bikaner, Jaisalmer, Bundi and others were separate houses with rival claims, internal succession disputes and differing terrain, from fortified hills to desert. Their rulers did not share a single position toward the Mughals.

Mughal records often use 'Rajput' as a convenient label for these ruling lineages. Students should remember that the label covers varied clans, and that decisions to resist or submit usually reflected local rivalries and security calculations rather than a collective Rajput policy.

Mewar's Sisodiya house held unusual prestige, but its position was not uniquely dominant in practice. Marwar had been disrupted by Sher Shah's occupation in the 1540s and by internal succession disputes, and Mughal forces occupied Jodhpur in the mid-1560s, though a claimant, Rao Chandrasen, kept up resistance from the hills for years.

Amber, Bikaner and Jaisalmer sat closer to the plains or desert routes. Each house assessed Mughal power against its immediate rivals, so submission could be a way to gain imperial backing in a local succession dispute as much as an acknowledgment of Mughal supremacy.

Rajasthan comprised political landscapes that varied from the eastern approaches near Agra to the western deserts and the mountain strongholds of Mewar. Its ruling houses had long cultivated genealogies, ritual precedence, and alliances with neighboring powers, but those identities did not produce a single Rajput foreign policy. For example, a ruler threatened by relatives could see Akbar's backing as a path to survival, whereas a house enjoying strong legitimacy in defensible terrain might regard imperial service as a diminution of sovereignty. Geography affected those calculations. Amber lay close to vital routes toward Ajmer, while Chittor dominated approaches into Mewar and connections with Malwa. The Aravalli hills provided refuges and routes for smaller forces that imperial cavalry could not easily dominate.

The history of Marwar supplies another warning against treating regional courts as unified actors. Rival heirs and chiefs struggled over Jodhpur and adjacent territories, while the consequences of earlier interventions by Sher Shah continued to influence local politics. Rao Chandrasen resisted Mughal domination despite the willingness of others to seek imperial assistance. In Mewar, the Sisodiya house possessed a reputation for refusing subordination, but even its commanders had to negotiate with local groups and withstand pressures from rival Rajput families. The emperor could exploit disputes, yet his own alliances sometimes generated new local tensions because favoring one claimant disadvantaged another. Submission was therefore not merely a change of diplomatic allegiance; it could reorder property, succession, and the balance of power within a ruling lineage.

The social category Rajput had overlapping genealogical, military, and political meanings that changed with context. Persian historians used general labels while sometimes distinguishing particular clans with great care. The regional history must also include Bhil communities, service groups, traders, religious institutions, and agrarian populations who were not simply extensions of the ruling houses. Their support might determine whether a ruler could move supplies, obtain intelligence, or survive in hill country. Alliances and resistance consequently developed through relations at several levels: the emperor and a raja, a raja and his rival kinsman, and a ruling household and the people who sustained its armies. This complexity explains why a single policy of conciliation could not produce identical results across Rajasthan.

Section 51.13

The Amber Alliance and Rajput Service at Court

In early 1562, while Akbar was travelling to the shrine at Ajmer, Raja Bharmal of Amber, under pressure from a rival, came to meet him and offered service and a daughter in marriage. The alliance gave Bharmal imperial support and Akbar a reliable partner on the route between Agra and Ajmer.

Bharmal's son Bhagwant Das and grandson Man Singh became leading commanders, and Man Singh later governed Kabul, Bihar and Bengal. Later traditions embellish the marriage, so the details of the bride's identity should be treated cautiously, but the political result is well attested.

Tradition and many historians identify a daughter of Bharmal as the mother of Prince Salim, though accounts differ on names and details. Whatever the exact genealogy, marriage ties between the Amber and Mughal houses were durable and politically important.

Later marriages, including Salim's marriage to a daughter of Bhagwant Das, deepened the link. The alliance gave the Kachhwaha house a strong position at court and gave Akbar loyal commanders with followings drawn from Rajasthan, a recruiting pool distinct from the Turani and Irani groups.

Raja Bharmal's approach to Akbar in 1562 arose from a concrete political dilemma, not simply from admiration for the emperor. The Amber court faced pressure from competing claimants and neighboring rulers, and imperial patronage offered a military guarantee. For Akbar, a reliable partner on the Ajmer route could secure movement across Rajasthan without demanding an expensive siege of every local fort. Marriage alliance made the relationship difficult to reverse, since household and dynastic ties could continue beyond the lifetime of a single commander. The daughter of Bharmal who married Akbar is sometimes identified by names familiar from later storytelling; the contemporary evidence does not justify repeating all those names as certain. The important facts are the existence of the marriage, its political significance, and the careers that followed.

Bhagwant Das and Man Singh illustrate how an allied ruling family could become an imperial military resource. Their campaigns took them far beyond Amber, including the northwest and the eastern provinces. Man Singh's command of forces drawn from different political communities shows that Mughal service could enhance rather than erase the political standing of an established regional house. Such officers had an incentive to preserve the empire because their careers and assignments depended on its authority. Conversely the court benefited from recruits who were not tied to earlier rivalries among Central Asian nobles. This was a practical method of changing the composition of the armed elite, though it did not mean the emperor regarded all Rajput rulers in the same way.

The costs and asymmetries deserve equal attention. An alliance might require military expeditions against other Rajput houses, raising questions of kinship, loyalty, and local honor. It also gave one house an advantage over neighbors that had not accepted the emperor. Women participating in dynastic marriages occupied a politically significant position but are not well represented by their own surviving accounts. Court historians tend to describe them through genealogy and marriage arrangements, not personal choice. The Amber relationship thus demonstrates imperial integration as a negotiated and unequal arrangement that nevertheless created mutual advantages. It should neither be romanticized as effortless tolerance nor caricatured as a surrender without agency by the regional court.

Section 51.14

The Political Mechanics of Rajput–Mughal Alliances

Alliances were negotiated. A ruler acknowledged Mughal sovereignty, sent troops, attended court and often arranged a marriage; in return he received a rank, honors and, usually, his hereditary territory (watan) as a jagir. The Mughals did not require him to abandon his religion or customs.

This was not full absorption. Rajput houses retained local power and followings, and they used imperial rank to advance against rivals inside their own lineages. The arrangement served both sides, but it also tied the emperor to the interests of particular houses.

Alliances changed the status of both parties. For the Mughals, marriage with Rajput houses meant that Rajput relatives could appear at court; for the Rajputs, imperial rank brought wealth, honor and leverage over rivals. Rajput wives are recorded as retaining their own religious practices within the palace.

Not all houses benefited equally. Those who joined early generally gained more favorable terms than those who held out. This explains why historians describe Rajput relations as a spectrum of responses and caution against reading them as a single policy of conciliation or conquest.

The mechanics of alliance turned recognition of a distant emperor into durable exchanges. A raja or prince might receive a mansab, honors, marriage connections, and confirmed possession of ancestral territory; in return the court expected attendance, military contingents, and assistance in suppressing rebellions. These obligations could be enforced by appointments outside the ruler's home district, where success depended on imperial resources and cooperation with other nobles. Service in Gujarat, Kabul, or Bengal gave an allied house prestige and new income while binding its leading members to a wider state. Imperial recognition of hereditary territory as a watan jagir had special political importance because it acknowledged continuing local legitimacy even under a new sovereign.

Allied rulers did not become identical to ordinary transferred officers. They could still command local loyalties, preside over established customary practices, and negotiate the balance between imperial orders and inherited privileges. That autonomy made them useful intermediaries: the crown did not need to recreate every village relationship from Delhi or Agra. It also created friction if a local chief interpreted an imperial demand as an infringement on hereditary rights. A raja's participation in the army of the emperor should therefore not be mistaken for the disappearance of his own polity. The administrative vocabulary of rank could conceal a complex arrangement in which different forms of sovereignty coexisted uneasily.

The alliance system also had a military rationale. Rajput commanders brought cavalry, knowledge of fortresses, regional communications, and networks of soldiers accustomed to difficult terrain. In imperial campaigns they served alongside Iranis, Turanis, and other officers, creating the possibility of cross-group rivalries as well as professional cooperation. The court could use promotions to prevent particular commanders from becoming indispensable, but rewarding one house too generously might provoke competitors. The success of the policy is best judged not by the number of marriages or submissions alone but by the willingness of allied elites to continue service during distant campaigns and dynastic disputes. The cases of Amber, Mewar, and Marwar demonstrate why political incorporation must be evaluated individually rather than through a single formula.

Section 51.15

The Siege and Conquest of Chittor, 1567–1568

Udai Singh II of Mewar refused submission, and Chittor controlled routes toward Malwa and Gujarat. Akbar besieged the fortress from October 1567 with siege-works and mines. Jaimal, a Rathor commander of Merta, led the defence and was killed during the siege, as was Patta of Kailwa.

The fort fell in February 1568. Defenders died fighting and women are reported to have committed jauhar. Abu'l-Fazl's imperial account reports the killing of nearly 30,000 people after the fall, including noncombatants among those the chronicler portrays as participants in resistance. The precise number is not independently established, but the large-scale killing cannot be dismissed as merely rhetorical. The imperial chronicle attempts to justify the violence; Rajput traditions commemorate the defenders and preserve different memories of the siege.

Siege operations at Chittor involved covered approaches (sabats) and mines packed with powder, and the explosion of mines caused heavy casualties on both sides. Traditions credit Akbar himself with shooting Jaimal with a matchlock, though the details come from accounts written to praise the emperor.

Later traditions honor Jaimal and Patta, and some later travellers record that statues commemorated them in the Mughal capital. Such memorialization shows how the conquest entered both Mughal and Rajput memory, each for its own purposes. Mewar's court meanwhile continued from other centers, such as the hill capital founded by Udai Singh.

Chittor was central to Mewar's political memory and strategically significant to routes connecting the imperial heartland with Malwa and Gujarat. Its defenses allowed a smaller force to resist a larger army, but they required supplies and disciplined coordination within the fortress. The siege initiated in 1567 involved not merely artillery bombardment but the painstaking construction of covered passages, mining works, and positions from which attackers could approach the wall. Both soldiers and laborers faced danger from counterattacks and accidental explosions. Akbar's personal supervision impressed the official chroniclers, who presented the operations as a demonstration of engineering skill and sovereign resolve. A full account should distinguish such royal self-representation from the experience of defenders forced to decide how long stores and fortifications might withstand pressure.

Udai Singh's withdrawal from the fortress and the continued defense by leaders including Jaimal and Patta have attracted sharply divergent interpretations. Imperial histories emphasize royal victory; Rajput memory celebrates defenders who chose resistance under extreme conditions. Some dramatic details, including Akbar's reported shot at Jaimal, come through narratives designed to glorify particular actors and deserve attribution rather than unqualified repetition. The reported jauhar is likewise an event to discuss seriously and cautiously, without turning women's experiences into a mere decorative symbol of martial honor. The categories of people inside Chittor included warriors, their families, religious personnel, workers, and villagers who may have sought shelter. Their interests and choices are much less accessible than those of commanders.

The aftermath is historically indispensable. Abu'l-Fazl's account reports large-scale killing, giving a figure of approximately thirty thousand and describing an order for general massacre. That figure should be identified as a chronicler's total, not a verified census, but the passage itself cannot be dismissed as harmless rhetorical excess. The official justification associates peasants and other inhabitants with resistance, thereby showing that imperial violence could extend far beyond professional soldiers. This is a reminder that state formation involved coercion against civilians and that accounts celebrating imperial achievement can still preserve evidence damaging to an idealized portrait of the ruler. Students should compare the official narrative with what can be reconstructed from regional traditions while recognizing that later commemorations cannot be treated automatically as eyewitness records.

The conquest weakened an important rival but did not end Mewar as a political community. The Sisodiya court and its networks survived outside the fort, and resistance continued in mountain territory. Chittor demonstrates a distinction present throughout Akbar's reign: a fortress could fall, its inhabitants suffer enormously, and the surrounding society still refuse long-term incorporation. The memorial history of Jaimal and Patta and the enduring significance of Chittor subsequently shaped both Rajput political identity and representations of Mughal conquest. The episode should therefore be analyzed at three levels: the military operation, the devastating social consequences, and the incomplete political outcome.

The treatment of historical evidence at Chittor should also include the distinction between counting and describing violence. A court chronicler who uses a round figure may be expressing scale and political meaning rather than reporting the results of an enumerated survey. That limitation does not justify rejecting the claim that substantial killing occurred, particularly when the account itself describes an order for massacre. Conversely, the historian should not extrapolate a precise demographic loss from a rhetorically charged number without independent evidence about the population sheltered inside the fort. The evidence also invites inquiry into responsibility: orders issued from the top, decisions by officers, and the circumstances of a prolonged assault all shaped the outcome. A rigorous account can preserve the event's gravity while being explicit about what the sources cannot measure.

Ranthambore, 1569, and Mughal Influence in Rajasthan

Ranthambore, a major fortress in eastern Rajasthan, was held by Surjan Hada. In 1569 the fort was besieged and Surjan negotiated submission through Bhagwant Das, receiving honors and a post in imperial service. Kalinjar also fell that year.

Ranthambore was one of the strongest fortresses of Rajasthan, and its surrender in 1569 was arranged by negotiation after pressure. The Hada ruler's entry into service gave Mughal officers a model of how a defeated ruler could become an imperial commander.

Kalinjar in central India also fell in 1569, and in Marwar, Rao Chandrasen continued to resist until his death in 1581. The partial results of the early 1570s show that submissions were often formal while local power continued, and that Mughal influence in Rajasthan was strongest along main routes and in the plains.

Ranthambore required a different combination of siege pressure and diplomacy. Its defenses in eastern Rajasthan made rapid storming costly, while the emperor's recent success at Chittor showed the likely consequences of prolonged defiance. Surjan Hada had to weigh the survival of his household and followers against the symbolic costs of surrendering a major fortress. Negotiation through trusted intermediaries enabled both sides to avoid further losses while creating a route into imperial service for the former defender. The outcome helps explain why Mughal expansion cannot be measured only by battles: the threat of force, the availability of credible honors, and the expectation that ancestral privileges might be preserved could make submission a calculated political choice.

The empire also sought control of the wider network of strongholds and roads. Kalinjar's submission in 1569 was significant for communications between the northern plain and central India. The interactions with Bikaner, Jaisalmer, and rulers at Nagaur around 1570 showed that not every successful negotiation required an immediate campaign. A court with close routes to the imperial center might receive protection quickly; a ruler whose fortifications guarded key communications could negotiate differently. Under the other, the defeated ruler became part of the imperial hierarchy while retaining some inherited standing. Akbar often favored the second because it cost less and brought experienced regional leaders into service, but the result was not necessarily harmonious. Local officers had to reconcile imperial revenue demands with established rights, and kinship disputes persisted. This was political integration through negotiated subordination, not the creation of a uniform centralized government. Its relative durability depended on predictable rewards and an ability to adjudicate conflicts before they escalated into renewed war.

Maharana Pratap and the Battle of Haldighati, 1576

On 18 June 1576 Man Singh's imperial army met Maharana Pratap, Udai Singh's son, at Haldighati in the Aravalli hills. Pratap's forces included Rajput contingents, Bhil archers and Afghan allies, such as Hakim Khan Sur. The fighting was fierce and the Mughal army held the field.

Pratap, however, withdrew and the Mughals did not capture him or hold the hills. Accounts differ on the size of the armies and on the conduct of Man Singh, and later traditions treat the battle as a moral victory for Pratap. A tactical Mughal advantage did not produce strategic control.

Man Singh's army included Mughal officers, Rajput contingents and artillery, while Pratap's force relied on mobility in hilly country. Chroniclers present the battle as a Mughal victory, while Rajput traditions emphasize Pratap's bravery and the loyalty of his supporters, including Bhil archers and the Afghan leader Hakim Khan Sur.

Bada'uni, who was present, gives an account in which Mughal troops faced a hard fight. Accounts also say the emperor was displeased that Man Singh did not pursue Pratap, a claim that reflects imperial suspicion of divided loyalties among Rajput commanders. Later folk traditions of Pratap's horse and other heroic episodes are not supported by contemporary documents.

Source criticism is essential for Haldighati. Abu'l-Fazl and Bada'uni give differing emphases, and Rajput bardic and later traditions elaborate heroic episodes that contemporary documents do not support. The battle's significance rests less on its details than on the fact that Mewar's resistance continued afterward.

Haldighati emerged from a failure to reach an acceptable political settlement between the Mughal court and the Sisodiya ruler. Man Singh commanded the imperial army, a striking demonstration that Rajput officers could lead campaigns against other Rajput states. Maharana Pratap, newly established as ruler of Mewar after 1572, relied on supporters familiar with the Aravalli terrain and on allies whose participation complicated any religious interpretation of the war. Reports of Hakim Khan Sur and Bhil participation show that resistance assembled across several communities, though later accounts differ over numbers and individual exploits. The battlefield was confined by surrounding hills, influencing the ability of cavalry to maneuver and the importance of controlling approaches and exits.

Bada'uni's account is valuable partly because he was present with the imperial side. Yet being an eyewitness did not grant him access to every position or to the motives of the opposing commanders. Official histories often turn the battle into a narrative of royal authority even when the emperor himself was not directing every movement. Later Rajput literature emphasizes Pratap's endurance and famous horse Chetak, but its heroic details must be distinguished from evidence of the military outcome. The Mughals retained the field, and Pratap escaped. Neither a claim of complete Rajput battlefield victory nor a claim that the empire achieved permanent control is consistent with the subsequent campaigns.

The operational aftermath matters more than a single verdict of victory or defeat. Imperial commanders could occupy selected posts and disrupt communications, but small forces operating from forests, hills, and local strongholds continued to threaten supply lines. Pratap's survival preserved a political center around which resistance could reorganize. The Mughals possessed superior capacity to deploy large armies, yet maintaining a garrison in difficult country consumed provisions and depended upon cooperation from surrounding communities. Haldighati is therefore an ideal case for distinguishing tactical success from strategic pacification and for assessing why a militarily stronger state did not automatically establish the consent or administrative reach necessary to govern its opponents' homeland.

The battle also shows the danger of reading sixteenth-century conflict through later nationalist categories. Imperial service did not make Man Singh less Rajput, and resistance did not create a single transregional Rajput movement. Participants acted in dynastic, local, personal, and military contexts that were not identical to those of modern nation-states. Their subsequent memories have become politically influential, and a textbook should explain that reception without allowing later symbolism to substitute for the historical evidence.

A geographical study helps clarify why the political contest outlived the battle. Imperial troops approaching Haldighati depended on routes that could be observed and harassed by defenders familiar with the hills. Occupying a road junction was different from controlling the paths through which people moved between settlements and upland refuges. If garrisons lacked local support, an enemy could survive by avoiding a conventional showdown and striking against isolated supplies. The tactical verdict of the battle should accordingly be linked to later evidence of who could collect dues, protect travelers, and maintain armed posts in the region. The ability of Pratap's supporters to rebuild local authority after 1576 offers stronger evidence of continued resistance than heroic stories alone.

Section 51.19

Gujarat's Political Economy and the Case for Conquest

Gujarat combined fertile agricultural zones with major ports such as Cambay and Surat, a strong textile industry and access to Red Sea and Persian Gulf trade. After the death of Sultan Bahadur Shah in 1537, the Gujarat Sultanate declined into rule by nobles around weak sultans, notably Muzaffar Shah III.

Some Gujarati nobles invited Mughal intervention in rivalries with one another, and Akbar had strategic and economic reasons to accept. Control of Gujarat would bring customs revenue, access to maritime commerce and removal of a refuge for Mughal rebels such as the Mirza kinsmen.

Gujarat's commercial economy tied inland producers to coastal merchants, many of them Gujarati Hindu and Jain traders, Muslim shipowners and Armenian, Persian and Arab visitors. Cotton cloth, indigo and other goods flowed to the Red Sea, the Persian Gulf and beyond, and the pilgrim traffic to Mecca left from its ports.

For the Mughals the province offered a supply of revenue and a route to the sea, but also a risk. Its ports were exposed to Portuguese power, and its wealth could support a rival if left in unstable hands.

Gujarat was valuable because it connected productive inland districts with the oceanic commercial circuits of the Arabian Sea. Ports such as Surat and Cambay linked merchants to the Red Sea, Persian Gulf, East Africa, and other Indian Ocean destinations; textiles, indigo, grain, and a wide range of manufactured goods moved alongside imported metals and horses. The imperial court could obtain revenue not only from fields but also from customs, commercial protection, and relationships with merchant financiers. Control of the province would thus expand the resources available for additional wars. Yet maritime prosperity did not make Gujarat politically simple. Its sultanate had been weakened by factional competition, and regional commanders often regarded a nominal sovereign as someone to be manipulated or replaced.

The Mughals entered an existing world of rival noble households and local jurisdictions. Some chiefs sought outside intervention for their immediate benefit, and the Mirza descendants of the Timurid world operated as alternative centers of legitimacy. Their presence made Gujarat both an opportunity and a threat to Akbar: conquered cities could enlarge the treasury, while a politically independent western kingdom could give refuge to imperial rebels. The Mughal advance also drew the court closer to Portuguese naval power, but Portuguese control was concentrated in particular coastal positions and maritime operations, not a comprehensive occupation of Gujarati land. Inland merchants and Muslim, Hindu, and Jain commercial networks retained major influence over the flow of goods.

The political challenge after conquest was to retain the cooperation of local elites without allowing them to reconstitute an independent sultanate. Port customs required skilled officers and merchants willing to declare transactions; agricultural revenue required landlords, village records, and the physical ability to defend markets. Rapid conquest could drive revenue away through flight or concealment if communities distrusted the new government. Gujarat therefore provides an unusually clear case of the relationship between trade, military security, and imperial finance. Its significance should not be reduced to a vague claim that maritime wealth simply passed into Akbar's possession after the taking of a capital.

Akbar's Gujarat Campaigns, 1572–1573

Akbar left Fatehpur Sikri in 1572 and entered Ahmadabad in November, receiving the submission of many nobles. Muzaffar Shah III was taken, and the Mughals then besieged Surat, which fell in early 1573.

The early Gujarat campaign included Akbar's defeat of Ibrahim Husain Mirza near Sarnal in late 1572, before Surat fell in early 1573. These events must be distinguished from the later uprising: after Akbar returned north, Mirza Muhammad Husain and Ikhtiyar-ul-Mulk besieged the imperial garrison in Ahmadabad. Akbar made his celebrated rapid return march in 1573, defeated the rebels near Ahmadabad, and temporarily secured Mughal authority in the province. Accounts praise the speed of this march, but military victory still required prolonged political consolidation.

The two Gujarat campaigns must be distinguished: Akbar's initial conquest included the fighting at Sarnal in December 1572, whereas his celebrated rapid return from the north confronted the renewed rebellion near Ahmadabad in 1573. Their different timing and political circumstances matter when assessing imperial control, and figures for the armies in court histories require caution.

Akbar's Gujarat campaigns took place in distinct stages that must not be collapsed into one celebrated dash across India. The first intervention in 1572 secured important places and confronted the factional weakness of Muzaffar Shah III's regime. The battle at Sarnal, associated with Akbar's initial operations, belongs to December 1572. The capture and submission of cities did not end the war: important commanders and Timurid Mirza rivals retained armies, and Surat required additional campaigning. An army crossing into Gujarat had to secure movement between Ahmedabad, the port approaches, and the routes back toward Rajasthan. The sheer length of those connections gave opponents opportunities to recover ground after the emperor departed.

The rapid return in 1573 became one of Akbar's most celebrated feats of mobility. Hearing that rebellion threatened the new settlement around Ahmedabad, he traveled west with a selected mounted force and moved quickly against rebel concentrations. Imperial historians emphasize his energy and daring, while a strategic reading asks what made such movement possible: prior knowledge of roads, available remounts, local intelligence, and the certainty that subordinate forces could follow. It was a fast campaign against a renewed rebellion, not simply a repeat of the battle at Sarnal. This separation matters because otherwise the text obscures the continuing difficulty of occupying and governing the conquered province.

Speed could produce tactical surprise but did not by itself establish an enduring administration. A smaller mounted column might defeat an enemy caught unprepared, while longer-term security required larger forces, garrisons, reassigned officers, and agreements with landholders. Akbar's personal participation demonstrated his ability to intervene beyond the northern heartland; the subsequent work of governors mattered just as much. The official history celebrates the emperor's exceptional qualities, but the historian should also investigate the networks of soldiers, guides, suppliers, and informants that enabled his decisions to become military action. Gujarat thus expanded imperial resources while exposing dependence on effective local institutions.

The westward expedition also shows how royal mobility was a political resource. Akbar's rapid appearance could reassure loyal officials who feared being abandoned, discourage undecided chiefs from joining rebels, and expose insurgents who had counted on the emperor's absence. This psychological effect depended upon the credibility established by earlier successes. Yet it could also encourage an excessive focus on the monarch's personal energy: once he returned north, provincial officers still had to maintain garrisons and make arrangements with local leaders. The strategic question was therefore whether a dramatic campaign could be converted into routine obedience. Gujarat's repeated disturbances provide the evidence needed to answer that question.

Section 51.21

Incorporating Gujarat

Gujarat proved hard to hold. Rebellions broke out in the 1580s, including one led by the escaped Muzaffar Shah III in 1583–1584, and had to be suppressed by Mughal commanders. Local landholders, merchants and port elites bargained over revenue and security.

Maritime trade also meant dealing with the Portuguese, whose patrols and sea-pass (cartaz) requirements asserted coercive control over parts of regional maritime commerce. Akbar met Portuguese representatives, but the Mughals did not challenge them by sea, so imperial power on the coast remained limited.

Gujarat's administration required reconciling Mughal officers and local elites. The Mughals kept many Gujarati officials in place and drew on local knowledge of revenue and trade, while appointing imperial governors. Rebellions in the 1580s, including those linked to Sultan Muzaffar III's attempts to regain power, show that incorporation was slow.

Portuguese fleets and coastal strongholds exerted considerable maritime leverage, but the Portuguese did not govern the entire Gujarati coast or its inland markets. Mughal authorities and Portuguese officials negotiated over passes and the safety of ships, and Akbar interacted with Portuguese representatives at his court, but the empire did not build a navy comparable to that of its Indian Ocean rivals.

Holding Gujarat required the court to govern ports, towns, agrarian districts, and fortresses through officers whose loyalty could not be taken for granted. When Muzaffar Shah III escaped and renewed resistance in the 1580s, the persistence of former sultanate networks became clear. An old royal name could mobilize followers even after the empire had taken the principal capital. Governors therefore used force and bargaining, while the court reassigned commanders when their behavior threatened local stability. Merchants sought dependable tolls and secure routes more than constant changes of sovereign; excessive requisition or unpredictable customs would drive commerce to alternative ports or private channels.

The Portuguese added an external dimension, particularly to the management of shipping and pilgrimage routes. Their fleets and fortified coastal posts could impose restrictions or extract payments, but the Mughal state controlled important inland markets and gradually exercised influence through Gujarat's existing merchant communities. Neither power possessed the means to regulate every vessel. The cartaz was a Portuguese sea-pass requirement within a contested maritime regime; actual enforcement varied, and merchants continued to employ multiple routes and political intermediaries. Diplomatic contact and occasional conflict reflected the different strengths of land-based imperial authority and seaborne military leverage.

Commercial integration also required attention to social institutions. Hindu and Jain merchants, Muslim trading houses, brokers, moneychangers, and suppliers of credit moved goods and funds through networks established long before conquest. The emperor could benefit from these systems without inventing them. At the same time local zamindars, village officials, and military households had to reconcile provincial demands with older obligations. The result was not a single moment when Gujarat became uniformly Mughal. It was a continuing process of ensuring that revenue, trade, and military command reinforced rather than undermined one another. The province's eventual contribution to imperial power depended on maintaining these arrangements across repeated disturbances.

Bihar and Daud Karrani, 1574–1575

Daud Khan Karrani, an Afghan ruler of Bengal and Bihar, challenged Mughal authority in the early 1570s. Akbar took the field himself in 1574, advancing to Patna and Hajipur, while Daud withdrew.

Mughal forces pursued Daud into Bengal and Odisha. After a defeat at Tukaroi in March 1575, Daud made a settlement that left him with Odisha and acknowledged Mughal supremacy, but it did not hold, and scholars note that details of the treaty vary between sources.

Bihar and Bengal were governed by Afghan rulers whose power rested on the support of Afghan chiefs and on control of river transport. The Mughals had earlier claimed the region through their governor at Jaunpur, but Daud's challenge forced the emperor to take the field.

Akbar's advance down the Ganges used boats alongside land forces. Patna, a commercial center, became a base. The campaign shows how river geography shaped the eastern theatre, where waterways were routes of supply and obstacles to movement.

The eastern campaign shows the importance of logistics and rivers in imperial planning. Persian sources emphasize the emperor's leadership, while the decisions of commanders in the field, who negotiated and quarreled, often shaped the outcome.

The eastern expansion proceeded through a political environment shaped by Afghan military settlement and river-based regional economies. Daud Khan Karrani inherited power in Bengal and Bihar as the last major representative of a ruling line that maintained links to the Sur and Lodi past. His court could seek cooperation from zamindars, urban officials, and Afghan military chiefs, but these interests were not fully united. Mughal intervention had strategic significance because the eastern Gangetic plain offered fertile land, transport routes, and access to the Bengal delta. For Akbar, advancing toward Patna involved major logistical choices: rivers could transport soldiers and supplies, but also obstruct large mounted armies and enable defenders to destroy crossings.

The 1574 campaign was not simply a march from one city to another. Akbar's movement toward Patna demonstrated that imperial resources could be concentrated in a distant theater while commanders managed the parallel requirements of provisioning and garrisoning captured locations. Daud's withdrawal preserved an army even as he yielded territory; Mughal forces then faced the problem of pursuit through districts where local loyalty was uncertain. The victory at Tukaroi in 1575 and the resulting settlement did not settle the issue of sovereignty. Accounts differ over terms and the division of authority, but the broader significance is clear: an agreement reached after battle might remain ineffective when both sides retained resources and doubted the other's intentions.

The eastern campaign also reveals the importance of institutions not always visible in campaign chronicles. A military officer could seize tax records or announce the appointment of a governor, yet effective collection depended on intermediaries embedded in villages and markets. Revenue was affected by seasonal water levels, grain movement, and the cost of keeping soldiers in remote posts. If an Afghan chief could retain a following across the river networks, his political influence might survive loss of a formal capital. The conflict therefore persisted because neither battlefield victory nor treaty solved the basic problem of turning a complex region into a reliable imperial province.

Section 51.23

Rajmahal, 1576, and the Conquest of Bengal

Mughal commander Munim Khan died in late 1575 amid an epidemic at the Bengal capital, and Daud broke the peace. At Rajmahal in July 1576 the Mughal forces under Khan-i Jahan Husain Quli Khan defeated and captured him, and he was executed.

This ended Karrani rule and formally brought Bengal into the empire. But Afghan officers, local chiefs and the delta environment meant that the province remained to be conquered administratively, a process lasting decades.

After the Tukaroi settlement, Mughal commanders left, and the epidemic that struck the Bengal capital reduced their numbers. Daud's breach of the settlement brought a Mughal counterattack, culminating at Rajmahal, a site commanding a narrow passage between the river and the hills.

The victory opened the province to Mughal governors, but many Afghan officers continued to hold local power. Some entered Mughal service, others retreated eastwards, and the result was a long process of conciliation and conflict rather than a clean conquest.

The Battle of Rajmahal in 1576 marked the destruction of the principal Karrani claim to sovereign rule, but not the disappearance of Afghan political power. Daud returned to resistance after the earlier settlement broke down; Mughal commanders fought to suppress him, and his capture and execution brought a definitive end to his personal dynasty. The court could now claim Bengal as an imperial province and appoint officers to govern it. Such a claim mattered greatly in the language of sovereignty. Coinage, official appointments, and recorded victories marked the end of one regime and the formal establishment of another. Yet military victory was only the first stage of establishing a fiscal and administrative presence in an enormous river basin.

Bengal's political geography frustrated attempts to make Delhi's victory immediate and complete. Waterways connected markets and estates, while marshes and changing channels made many areas inaccessible to a large cavalry army. Regional chiefs operated from fortified settlements and could move by boat, striking isolated detachments. A governor might dominate a few important ports and urban centers but still lack the local boatmen, navigators, and informants needed to enforce orders in the countryside. Nor were all enemies simply remnants of Daud's army: rival houses, landholding chiefs, and local military communities had distinct interests and could shift between cooperation and opposition.

The effect of the conquest must therefore be described at two scales. At the imperial level, Rajmahal eliminated a major royal competitor and allowed the court to redirect resources to consolidating new territory. At the regional level, the following decades remained a history of incomplete political incorporation. This was not a failure of the battlefield victory; it was an indication that sovereignty over a highly differentiated landscape required new arrangements for transportation, military service, and revenue. Later Mughal achievements in eastern Bengal should not be backdated to the reign of Akbar merely because a chronicle used the word conquered.

The delta shows how geography shaped the boundary of imperial power.

The Baro-Bhuiyans, 'twelve lords' in the usual translation, were not a single organized state. They were a set of chiefs whose holdings, often reached by boat, gave them local strength. Accounts credit Isa Khan with leadership, but their alliances shifted over time.

Mughal campaigns used riverine forces and fortified outposts. The delta's marshes and creeks neutralised cavalry, and officers often preferred negotiation. Over time many chiefs entered imperial service, but the region's integration was a gradual process stretching well into the seventeenth century.

The Baro-Bhuiyans are best understood as a constellation of influential regional chiefs rather than a permanent alliance of exactly twelve identical members. The designation reflected regional political conventions, and the identity and relative strength of individual leaders varied. Isa Khan's authority in Bhati rested on military and landholding networks suited to the delta. Fortifications, boats, river crossings, and relationships with cultivators and local chiefs gave his forces mobility where large cavalry formations could not operate efficiently. Afghan and other commanders could cooperate with him because resistance to a distant governor served their immediate interests, but his coalition was not necessarily united by an overarching program of independence.

Campaigns against Isa Khan show the limits of formal provincial titles. A governor named by Akbar might control a recognized capital, but the emperor's decree did not cause boatmen in a distant creek to obey him. Mughal forces needed fleets, pilots, local allies, and suitable seasonal conditions. The capture of one stronghold might open access to an adjacent waterway while leaving an entire cluster of fortified settlements beyond reach. Man Singh's efforts in the 1590s illustrate both the empire's capacity to deploy experienced commanders and the persistence of local armed power. Accounts of negotiations or temporary understandings should not be transformed into a categorical statement that Isa Khan permanently submitted before his death in 1599.

The struggle also reshaped society. Fighting could interrupt cultivation and river commerce, but it simultaneously made the labor of boatbuilders, navigators, and grain suppliers indispensable. Local chiefs depended on the surplus of villages and on the ability to protect or coerce merchants who crossed their domains. The region's eventual incorporation under later Mughal rulers followed further wars and administrative reorganization, not a single decree issued after Rajmahal. Bengal thus illustrates the chapter's larger theme in unusually concrete terms: a state could have the resources to defeat a king and still lack the organizational capacity to rule every environment that king had claimed.

The term Baro-Bhuiyan itself requires care. The label is conventionally translated as twelve chiefs, but historical usage does not necessarily identify a single fixed roster operating simultaneously throughout the region. It can refer to overlapping groups of locally powerful landholders whose strength shifted as rivers changed course and political alliances were remade. Some had Afghan backgrounds; others derived standing from different local traditions. Describing them as an independent confederation with permanent membership would distort their flexible politics. The appropriate focus is on the capacity of particular chiefs to finance armed boats, defend fortified settlements, and negotiate with appointed Mughal governors while retaining local authority.

Odisha and the Eastern Coast

Afghans under leaders such as Qutlu Khan Lohani held Odisha after the collapse of Karrani power. Man Singh led Mughal forces against them around 1590–1592, gaining nominal control of the province.

Subsequent Afghan uprisings and the survival of local rajas, including the Gajapati line at Puri, show that conquest did not amount to uniform administration. Odisha was effectively an extended frontier of Bengal rather than a settled province by 1600.

Odisha had its own political and religious centers, including the Jagannath temple at Puri, and its rulers and landholders had long traditions of local authority. The Afghan power established there after Karrani collapse added to a mixed political landscape.

Man Singh's campaigns brought a settlement with local rulers and Afghan leaders, but renewed fighting followed. The Mughal presence in Odisha was therefore a combination of military occupation and agreements with local powers, and its administration took shape slowly.

Odisha occupied an intermediate position between Bengal's river world and the coastal and inland routes leading toward the Deccan. Control of the province meant influencing pilgrimage centers, agricultural settlements, ports, and military corridors. After the weakening of the Karrani power, Afghan chiefs and local rulers continued to contend over authority. Some could negotiate with Mughal officials, while others relied on existing fortresses or alliances. The campaigns associated with Man Singh around 1590–1592 were important in extending imperial claims, but the sequence of local resistance makes a single annexation date a poor guide to the region's politics.

The continuing importance of the Gajapati line and institutions around Puri demonstrates that a newly conquering empire did not automatically displace every form of regional legitimacy. Temples, pilgrimage routes, local landholding rights, and hereditary offices connected communities to rulers whose authority predated Mughal intervention. Imperial officials could benefit from these institutions by securing alliances and revenue, yet coercive attempts to change them could provoke opposition. The coexistence of local and provincial forms of authority was a practical necessity in places where the court could not supervise everyday arrangements directly.

A comparison with Gujarat clarifies the diversity of eastern expansion. Gujarat's ports exposed the empire to oceanic trade and Portuguese fleets; Odisha's coastal districts were connected to commerce but also to the political and agrarian geography of the eastern seaboard. Bengal depended on control of waterways; Odisha involved both deltaic and overland approaches. In each case success required more than a siege or surrender. It required knowing which chiefs controlled routes, how merchants moved resources, and which officials could be trusted to mediate with local societies. Akbar's expanding empire consequently became a patchwork of different governing settlements rather than a uniform territory created by a common military procedure.

Section 51.26

The Great Rebellions of 1580–1582

Between 1580 and 1582 serious revolts broke out in Bengal, Bihar and parts of the east, led by commanders such as Masum Khan Farankhudi and Baba Khan Qaqshal. Grievances included changes in assignments and the branding of horses, reduced allowances and unease about the emperor's religious policies, which are examined in Chapter 52.

Some rebels sought support from religious scholars and invoked rival claims to legitimacy, including on behalf of Akbar's half-brother in Kabul. Akbar responded with military pressure, selective pardons and the reassignment of officers, avoiding collapse but showing the strain the new system placed on the nobility.

The sources for these revolts, especially Abu'l-Fazl and Bada'uni, interpret them differently, the first as disloyalty and the second as reaction to policies he disliked. Historians therefore treat motives as contested.

In Bengal the revolt began in 1580 when officers resisting the new regulations on assignments and horse verification killed the provincial finance officer, Muzaffar Khan Turbati. Some religious scholars issued legal opinions declaring Akbar's actions unlawful, which gave rebels a language of legitimacy. Imperial troops under Todar Mal and others restored order over the next two years, but unrest in Bihar and Bengal showed how new controls on rank and revenue could provoke the officers they targeted.

The rebellions of 1580–1582 threatened the political settlement Akbar had constructed over the previous two decades. They were geographically dispersed and involved officers with different grievances, but their coincidence exposed vulnerabilities in the imperial system. Changes in revenue assignments, the inspection of military obligations, and tensions over the treatment of established officeholders created discontent among influential nobles. Disputes about the emperor's religious conduct and the status of orthodox scholars complicated that discontent without explaining every rebellion. Some insurgents invoked the legitimacy of Mirza Hakim in Kabul, hoping to attach their regional grievances to an alternative Timurid claimant. Such gestures demonstrate that succession politics remained important despite Akbar's success in building a more centralized army.

Eastern provinces were especially vulnerable because distance and riverine geography made rapid suppression difficult. Officials appointed to govern Bihar or Bengal relied on networks of subordinate officers who could change allegiance; rebel commanders might seize arsenals, revenues, and local strongholds before the imperial center could concentrate a response. The court faced a choice between crushing all opponents and offering settlements that might split the coalition. Akbar's strategy combined military campaigns, selective pardons, removal of particular leaders, and redirection of trusted officers. The cost was high because troops employed in restoring order could not simultaneously be used for expansion or frontier defense.

The underlying institutional problem was not simply the disloyalty of individual nobles. An empire that compensated commanders through revenue assignments had to distinguish the rights of appointment from the practical power of local collection. A rebellious officer could often mobilize peasants' payments and retainers in his district; central regulations about rank or horse verification did not automatically overcome that control. The rebellions therefore helped shape later efforts to standardize service while also revealing the limits of standardization. Their suppression restored a degree of cohesion, but the most important lesson was that stronger imperial authority required credible material incentives, not only punishment and moral declarations of obedience.

Section 51.27

Mirza Hakim, Kabul, and the Defense of the Northwest

Muhammad Hakim Mirza, Akbar's half-brother, ruled Kabul as a semi-independent prince. In 1581 he advanced toward Lahore, encouraged by dissident nobles. Akbar marched to Kabul and Hakim submitted, remaining in place.

Hakim died in 1585 and Kabul was incorporated under Mughal governors. Its control mattered because it linked India to Central Asia and Iran, and because it protected the northwest from raiders and rival dynasties. Akbar then based himself at Lahore for years.

Hakim's challenge was dangerous because it combined a rival claimant, Central Asian connections and discontented nobles. His death in 1585 removed the threat without a battle.

Akbar reached Kabul in August 1581, and Hakim, whose army had dissolved, submitted. Akbar left him in place rather than incorporate Kabul, a sign of caution about the costs of direct administration. After Hakim's death in 1585, Man Singh was posted to the region, and Akbar moved his court to Lahore, from where he could oversee the northwest for much of the next decade.

Kabul linked the Mughal dynasty to its Central Asian origins and to the strategic approaches of the Hindu Kush. Humayun's descendants retained competing claims to influence there, and Muhammad Hakim's relative autonomy exposed the incomplete centralization of the imperial household. His challenge toward Lahore in 1581 drew significance from the rebellious climate in eastern India: an alternative Timurid prince could offer nobles a legitimate name under which to resist the emperor. Akbar's march toward Kabul therefore addressed a problem of dynastic authority as much as a threat to the Punjab frontier. A ruler secure in Delhi but vulnerable to a rival prince in Kabul could never assume that the empire's military elite would remain politically unified.

Kabul itself had limited agrarian resources compared with the Gangetic heartland, but its markets, horse trade, and access to the passes made it valuable. The court needed to control communications to both Central Asia and Iran, where the Uzbeks and Safavids followed policies that might affect the security of the Mughal northwest. A military advance through the mountains involved supplies, negotiations with local inhabitants, and the maintenance of garrisons along an extended route. The emperor could demonstrate his authority by marching there, yet it was neither necessary nor immediately practical to remove every inherited local relationship. Hakim remained in his position after the confrontation, showing that the court still employed negotiated accommodation when a dynastic rival could not be eliminated cheaply.

Hakim's death in 1585 made a more direct Mughal appointment regime possible. Akbar's long presence in Lahore in the following years demonstrates that the northwest remained a center of strategic concern rather than an incidental frontier. Its importance included communication with Kashmir, the hill regions, and Kandahar. The history of Kabul also reveals how the empire's governing arrangements differed from its earlier Timurid pattern: a prince's quasi-hereditary appanage increasingly gave way to provincial officers whose standing depended on the emperor. The transition was not instantaneous, and the continued importance of Afghan and mountain communities limited what an appointment from the capital could accomplish.

The communications between Kabul and Lahore had symbolic as well as military value. A claimant who controlled the passes could receive envoys, recruit troops, and interfere with the horse routes that supplied imperial cavalry. This helped make Mirza Hakim's position more consequential than his immediate income might suggest. Akbar's decision to march toward Kabul was therefore a demonstration of capacity to operate across the mountains while retaining authority in the northern plains. The settlement also reveals the persistence of dynastic bargaining: direct annexation was not the only method available, and the emperor could preserve a relative's dignity while making the cost of open rebellion more visible. That compromise reduced immediate conflict but did not permanently remove the possibility of rival claims to sovereignty.

Section 51.28

Afghan Frontier Resistance, the Yusufzais, and the Roshanis

The mountainous country between Kabul and the Indus was home to Yusufzai and other Afghan groups, with local leaders and strong tribal traditions. The Roshani movement, led by Bayazid Ansari and his successors, combined religious teaching with political resistance.

Campaigns in 1585–1586 were costly. In February 1586 Birbal, the court favorite, died with a large detachment in an ambush in the Malandarai pass, one of the heaviest Mughal losses of the reign. The Mughals secured the main routes but never fully controlled the hills.

The Malandarai disaster of 1586 is a reminder that frontier warfare in the Swat and Bajaur hills favored defenders. Control rested on forts and negotiation with local leaders, not on occupation of whole valleys.

The Roshani movement, founded by Bayazid Ansari, attracted Afghan followers with a mix of religious teaching and resistance to outside rule. After Bayazid's death in 1585, his son Jalala continued the movement. Mughal officers such as Zain Khan Koka campaigned in Swat and Bajaur, and Raja Todar Mal also took part, but the hills could be crossed only along a few passes, where ambushes were easy.

The Yusufzai country and the adjoining valleys could not be governed using the same military methods that were effective on the northern plains. Its communities organized authority through local leadership, kinship networks, and access to pasture and cultivated valleys. Mountain passes allowed small forces to choose favorable positions for ambush, while imperial troops often had to advance in difficult columns with little room to maneuver. To a Mughal commander, the security of a road might be the immediate objective; to local inhabitants, that same road could bring outside taxation, garrisons, and interference in political autonomy. Conflicting interests therefore persisted even when negotiations seemed to promise peace.

The Roshani movement associated with Bayazid Ansari and his successors combined religious ideas with struggles over authority among Afghan communities. It would be misleading to treat all frontier resistance as the work of one religious movement, or all communities in the mountains as a single tribe acting together. Some groups cooperated with imperial officers, while others sought to resist or exploit the conflicts for their own advantage. The loss of Birbal and many soldiers in the Malandarai pass in 1586 brought home the seriousness of the challenge to the imperial center. Courtly accounts of that disaster reveal concern over military leadership and the inability of conventional formations to anticipate an attack in narrow terrain.

Even a major punitive expedition could destroy villages or seize hostages without establishing continuous government. The costs of permanent garrisons included provisioning and the risk that soldiers unfamiliar with the local environment would be isolated. Akbar's administration concentrated on securing principal communications and negotiating with selected chiefs, while considerable autonomy remained in difficult regions. The frontier should not be described as pacified simply because a chronicle reports the destruction of one resistance group. It was an evolving relationship among imperial commanders, regional political actors, and religious communities, with continuing consequences for the distribution of military resources.

Chronicles also encourage a false contrast between a disciplined empire and disordered mountain tribes. Frontier communities possessed their own arrangements for settling disputes, mobilizing armed men, and negotiating access to routes; they were not simply people living outside politics. Imperial officers sometimes relied on selected chiefs as guides or intermediaries, then found that agreements made with one group did not bind its rivals. The resulting uncertainty was structural. A Mughal governor could not assume that a treaty concluded in one valley applied to another, and local communities could not assume that a new imperial commander would honor his predecessor's promises. This explains the recurrent pattern of temporary settlements followed by renewed campaigns.

Section 51.29

Kashmir and the Conquest of the Valley, 1585–1588

Kashmir was ruled by the Chak dynasty. Mughal pressure began in 1585, and Yusuf Shah Chak negotiated through intermediaries in 1586, but the arrangement collapsed amid factional opposition. Mughal forces then entered the valley, and Yusuf Shah was later detained and given a rank in imperial service.

Resistance under other Chak leaders continued before the valley was incorporated by about 1588. Akbar visited in 1589, and Kashmir's shawls, saffron and routes to Central Asia added to Mughal resources.

The sequence is difficult to establish: Yusuf Shah Chak's submission and detention in 1586 and Yaqub Shah Chak's continued resistance are recorded differently by Persian and Kashmiri sources, and Akbar's visit of 1589 is dated from the Persian chronicles.

Kashmir lay behind snowbound passes, which limited the season and size of a campaign. Mughal forces entered the valley via the passes of the Pir Panjal range. The Chak dynasty was divided between factions, and some Kashmiri nobles negotiated while others resisted, so the valley's incorporation was more a political settlement and military pressure than a single conquest.

Kashmiri chronicles and Persian sources give differing accounts of the Chak resistance. Historians therefore reconstruct the conquest by comparing the two traditions and note that the sequence of agreements, rejections and military action is not fully settled.

Kashmir's geography made it a highly valued but difficult target. Enclosed mountain approaches, valleys, lakes, and difficult seasonal routes limited the operations of large armies while allowing local rulers to defend access points. The Chak dynasty governed a politically divided kingdom that retained its own court and cultural traditions. Mughal interest arose from the strategic relationship between Kashmir and the routes connecting the Punjab to the upper Indus and Central Asia, as well as the region's recognized products and symbolic prestige. But the ability to send an army across a pass did not ensure that local nobles would accept a permanent imperial governor.

Yusuf Shah Chak's negotiations in 1586 are among the episodes requiring careful comparison between imperial and regional narratives. Accounts differ over the terms of submission and the circumstances of his detention. An official chronicle may represent the emperor as receiving rightful allegiance, whereas Kashmir-centered histories can emphasize deception and the loss of sovereignty. The historian need not flatten these into a false choice between flawless imperial legality and uniform local innocence. The evidence permits analysis of the asymmetry between a powerful state conducting negotiations and a regional ruler with diminishing options. Continued resistance by other Chak leaders confirms that control of Yusuf Shah did not amount to an uncontested settlement.

The subsequent military presence and Akbar's visit in 1589 marked a significant extension of imperial influence. Kashmir's shawl production, saffron, agriculture, and crafts were integrated into wider political and commercial relationships, though their existence long predated the Mughals. Imperial supervision still depended on people able to traverse difficult mountain routes and administer towns and villages during seasons of limited access. The conquest demonstrates the mixed methods of expansion—diplomacy, detention, warfare, appointment, and cultivation of local elites—and cautions against equating a line drawn on an imperial map with equal enforcement in every valley.

Section 51.30

Sindh, the Lower Indus, and Baluchistan

Lower Sindh was held by the Tarkhan rulers, including Mirza Jani Beg. The region combined river corridors, desert and coastal ports, and was difficult to reach. From 1590 to 1592 Abd al-Rahim, the Khan-i Khanan, campaigned against Jani Beg, whose resistance ended in negotiated submission in 1592; Jani Beg subsequently entered imperial service.

Mughal authority then extended toward Thatta and the western coast, and in subsequent years over parts of Baluchistan. Control was strongest along rivers and routes, and weaker among pastoral and tribal groups.

The Mughals had already absorbed northern Sindh earlier, and the campaign against Jani Beg's lower Sindh ran through 1592, ending in his negotiated surrender.

Jani Beg Tarkhan held the Lower Indus around Thatta and Sehwan, with a river fleet that made direct assault difficult. Abd al-Rahim's campaign combined river operations with sieges and lasted from 1590 into 1592. Jani Beg's negotiated surrender that year brought him into imperial service, and the Lower Indus became part of the empire, though local chiefs retained influence in the countryside.

The lower Indus corridor presented a different combination of opportunities and obstacles from Kashmir. River navigation connected towns and agricultural districts, while the surrounding deserts and coastal approaches made overland communication difficult. The Tarkhan dynasty held authority around Thatta and other settlements through local military households and relations with merchant and agrarian communities. For the Mughal court, control of Sindh promised access to river routes, commercial centers, and the western approaches to the Arabian Sea. It also had strategic importance because the Indus basin linked the Punjab to territories that might provide alternative routes toward Kandahar and the Deccan.

Abd al-Rahim Khan-i Khanan's campaign unfolded across 1590–1592 and should not be reduced to a victory in 1590 or a single surrender in 1591. Imperial officers needed to use boats and secure supplies, while Mirza Jani Beg could rely on regional knowledge and the defensive possibilities offered by the river. Warfare and negotiations alternated as the costs of continued resistance mounted. By 1592 the Tarkhan ruler had accepted a settlement and entered the wider political orbit of Mughal service. This arrangement did not erase local lineages; it redefined the conditions under which regional rulers might retain status and resources.

The broader category Baluchistan needs similar caution. The court could extend influence over specified forts and routes without establishing intensive control over every pastoral community or desert district. A map showing imperial borders is therefore more reliable evidence of official claims than of detailed administration. In the lower Indus the emperor's government depended on landholders, shipmen, traders, and local officials whose cooperation made communications workable. Sindh illustrates negotiated incorporation after a costly campaign, and it helps explain why the Mughal northwest remained a network of corridors, towns, and bargaining relationships rather than a consistently governed territorial block.

Kandahar in 1595 and Mughal–Safavid Diplomacy

Kandahar, a fortress city between the Mughal, Safavid and Uzbek spheres, was held by a Safavid governor, Muzaffar Husain Mirza. In 1595 he handed it over to the Mughal commander Shah Beg Khan, after tensions with the Safavid court, and entered Mughal service.

The transfer gave the Mughals the gateway to Iran. Akbar kept formal relations with the Safavids, and the episode shows that frontier expansion depended on diplomacy and defections as well as arms. Kandahar would remain contested in the seventeenth century (Chapter 53).

Kandahar's incorporation in 1595 combined diplomacy, a defecting governor and military pressure, and is better described as de facto transfer than as conquest.

The Safavids, preoccupied with Uzbek pressure in the northeast, did not respond militarily to the loss of Kandahar. Muzaffar Husain Mirza, the Safavid governor, entered Mughal service, and Akbar retained diplomatic contact with Shah Abbas I. Kandahar remained a disputed strategic prize in the seventeenth century.

Kandahar had repeatedly passed between Timurid and Safavid spheres because of its exceptional position on routes connecting India, Iran, and Central Asia. Its possession affected trade, military movement, and the credibility of claims to the former territories of Babur and Humayun. The Safavid governor Muzaffar Husain Mirza's transfer of the city to Mughal authority in 1595 emerged from relations at the Iranian court and from the governor's own calculations. It was not primarily the result of Akbar storming an impregnable fortress. This distinction is important because it shows diplomatic opportunity working alongside the empire's wider military reputation. A governor who feared enemies within his existing state might seek safety, honor, and reward in service to a neighboring ruler.

For Akbar, acquiring Kandahar was a strategic gain that nevertheless demanded continued investment. The city had to be provisioned, fortified, and connected to Kabul and the Punjab. Its administration required an officer corps familiar with military frontier life, as well as arrangements with surrounding communities whose loyalties could shift in response to regional political pressures. The Uzbeks in Central Asia and Safavid rulers in Iran remained factors in decisions made at the Mughal court. Even when direct relations between Delhi and Isfahan were cordial, the local circumstances of a frontier governor could produce abrupt changes in control.

Kandahar's history also highlights the difference between an empire of roads and an empire of uniformly governed territory. The state might hold the key fortress and secure the principal caravan route while exercising limited authority over areas farther from the garrison. The transfer of 1595 formed part of the broader northwestern consolidation after 1585, but it did not settle the frontier permanently. Later Mughal–Safavid struggles, reserved for subsequent chapters, should be foreshadowed only to clarify that this acquisition was reversible. Its immediate significance lies in the successful use of military prestige and negotiated defection to secure a strategically important gateway without a prolonged siege.

Institutional Comparison: Two Frontiers

Northwest: mountain passes, tribal autonomy, diplomacy with Safavids and Uzbeks. Bengal: rivers, landholding chiefs, Afghan networks. In both, control extended along routes and towns more than across the whole territory.

Military geography shaped institutional choice. In the northwest the crown prioritized mountain passes, the security of caravan routes, and diplomacy among competing states and local chiefs. Bengal required boats, regional pilots, and bargaining with fortified landholders. Both frontiers demanded reliable garrisons, but the types of service and knowledge required were different. A commander accomplished little by copying cavalry tactics from the plains into every environment.

The documentary limits also differed. Court histories celebrate the actions of imperial governors but often know comparatively little about the internal deliberations of mountain communities or riverine confederacies. A comparison should measure effective control in routes and towns, the regularity of tribute and revenue, the longevity of negotiated settlements, and the costs of repeated campaigns—not only the date when a court historian declares a province conquered.

The Deccan Sultanates and Akbar's Southern Strategy

The Deccan was divided among several sultanates: Ahmadnagar, Bijapur and Golconda, with Khandesh and Berar on the northern edge. These states were linked by war, marriage and religious differences, and often allied or fought one another and Vijayanagara (Chapter 45 and Chapter 46).

In 1591 Akbar sent embassies to Khandesh, Ahmadnagar, Bijapur and Golconda demanding acknowledgment of Mughal supremacy. Khandesh's ruler offered nominal acknowledgment, but the Mughal conquest of Khandesh and Asirgarh was not accomplished until 1600–1601; the other courts refused or made limited gestures. Mughal motives combined strategic security, control of the routes to the southern coast, and the argument that a universal emperor should receive recognition.

Akbar's envoys in 1591, among them the poet Faizi, were received differently. Khandesh's ruler gave a nominal acknowledgment of Mughal supremacy, but this did not amount to incorporation, which came only with the capture of Asirgarh in 1601. Ahmadnagar, Bijapur and Golconda received envoys but did not accept subordination.

The Deccan sultanates were rivals as well as neighbors, and their courts included both Deccani and foreign-born elites, so a united response to Mughal demands was difficult. Akbar's southern policy drew on strategic reasons, such as controlling the routes through Khandesh, and on ideas of universal kingship that made the refusal of the southern sultans a challenge.

The Deccan should be approached as a region of established states with long political histories, not as an empty southern frontier awaiting Mughal occupation. Ahmadnagar, Bijapur, Golconda, and their neighbors possessed military establishments, revenue systems, cavalry networks, fortified capitals, commercial links, and factions among Deccani and foreign-born nobles. Their relations were shaped by competition over territory, dynastic marriages, access to ports, and earlier conflicts involving Vijayanagara. Akbar's northern empire had expanded toward Malwa, Gujarat, and Khandesh, placing its armies closer to the Deccan's routes and political controversies. Pressure from the north therefore altered an already competitive system rather than introducing interstate conflict for the first time.

The embassies of 1591 sought recognition of Mughal supremacy and tested the readiness of southern rulers to accept an unequal diplomatic relationship. A formal acknowledgment could allow a Deccan government to continue governing its own territory while accepting a distant emperor's greater rank. Refusal could justify intervention in imperial rhetoric, but military decisions also depended on the vulnerability of a particular state, the availability of allies, and the costs of campaigning. Khandesh's willingness to negotiate at this stage did not mean that the imperial government already possessed effective control of its most important fortress, Asirgarh. Ahmadnagar's contested succession subsequently offered a more immediate opportunity to intervene.

Deccan campaigning differed from warfare in the northern plains. The plateau contained major fortresses, river systems, forested approaches, and routes affected by monsoon weather. Cavalry could operate effectively in open terrain but might be trapped by prolonged sieges and uncertain supply. The Mughal government needed experienced commanders who could work with local political groups, yet officers sent from the north sometimes competed with one another over credit and resources. The imperial argument for universal sovereignty thus encountered practical limitations imposed by geography, rival states, and the autonomy of regional military households. Understanding these constraints is necessary to explain why northern Mughal strength did not produce the easy annexation of the entire Deccan.

The crisis at Ahmadnagar grew from disputes within the Nizam Shahi ruling house and among powerful nobles. It allowed the dynasty to survive, at least temporarily, while giving Akbar a strategically valuable territorial claim. Chand Bibi's decisions have sometimes been presented as either heroic resistance or betrayal; such binary interpretations disregard the problem of preserving a court and population when continued siege could bring catastrophic losses. The surrounding states and factions interpreted the agreement differently, making renewed conflict likely. The dilemma was not specific to women rulers, although gender shaped how later narratives praised or condemned her. A textbook should analyze her decisions through the competing demands of dynastic survival, military capacity, and external pressure.

Deccani nobles resented the concession, and Mughal commanders quarreled over its enforcement and over military leadership. Fighting resumed around 1597, with a Mughal victory against a Deccani coalition, but this did not secure Ahmadnagar itself.

The renewed fighting of 1597 involved regional allies who had their own strategic interests. Bijapur and Golconda did not wish the Mughal advance to create a dominant northern power on their immediate frontier, yet neither was prepared to sacrifice all its resources to preserve Ahmadnagar. Deccani contingents could join an anti-Mughal coalition while individual nobles remained open to separate negotiation with the imperial camp. These divisions helped the empire establish a foothold without creating a stable southern settlement. The campaigns also exposed tensions among Mughal commanders, who competed over responsibility for losses and the rewards of victory.

Berar illustrates why an agreement about sovereignty must be separated from its fiscal effects. The empire needed officials to assess land and collect dues, while local military households could resist displacement or conceal revenue. Soldiers garrisoned in the province created costs before substantial income was secure. Each additional post required ammunition, animals, grain, and reliable communication. The strategic value of the territory was undeniable because it opened routes farther south, but its immediate fiscal contribution could be less than optimistic court accounts suggested. This tension between territorial acquisition and the costs of controlling a hostile district continued to shape the Deccan campaigns until the end of Akbar's reign.

Section 51.35

The Renewed Campaign against Ahmadnagar, 1597–1600

Prince Daniyal and Abd al-Rahim led renewed campaigns, though poor coordination and rivalry weakened Mughal efforts. Chand Bibi was killed by her own troops in 1600, after being accused of negotiating with the Mughals. Ahmadnagar fort fell in August 1600, and the child Bahadur was sent to imperial custody.

The sultanate, however, did not end. Deccani nobles installed another Nizam Shahi claimant, and leaders such as Malik Ambar later rebuilt resistance (Chapter 53). The seizure of the fort should not be confused with the end of the state.

Chand Bibi was killed in 1600 amid accusations among the defenders that she was negotiating with the Mughals. The Mughal army took the fort in August 1600, and the child sultan Bahadur was sent into custody at Gwalior.

Capturing the fort did not end the Nizam Shahi state. Deccani nobles installed another claimant, Murtaza Nizam Shah II, and leaders including Malik Ambar organized resistance. The Mughal gain was an important fortress and a strong claim, not complete sovereignty.

The renewed struggle for Ahmadnagar after 1597 exposed the fragility of both the local regency and the imperial command structure. Mughal officers believed that further pressure could remove the remaining obstacles to occupation, while Deccan factions alternated between resistance, accommodation, and support for rival claimants. The besieging army needed to protect long supply lines and hold previously acquired positions in Berar. Chand Bibi's effort to preserve the dynasty depended on maintaining cooperation among nobles who differed sharply over whether negotiations with the Mughals were acceptable. The political crisis inside the capital therefore directly affected the military outcome.

Chand Bibi's killing by members of her own side in 1600 is one of the reign's starkest examples of how rumors of betrayal could destroy a regency. The precise sequence of events is filtered through hostile and celebratory sources, but the broad result is clear: factional violence weakened the defense, the fort fell, and the child sultan entered Mughal custody. The takeover of the fortress in August 1600 was a considerable achievement, yet the victorious commanders inherited a damaged political environment rather than an obedient province. Other Nizam Shahi claimants continued to attract allegiance, and regional military leaders sought bases from which resistance might be renewed.

The career of Malik Ambar, whose greatest achievements belong to a later period, demonstrates the longer-term limits of the conquest. The fall of a capital did not destroy the agricultural, military, or diplomatic networks through which a regional state could be reconstructed elsewhere. Mughal officers holding Ahmadnagar required garrisons and revenue assignments, while rival leaders could exploit the plateau and relationships with neighboring sultanates. Students should therefore distinguish three outcomes: the capture of a particular fortress, the imprisonment of a particular claimant, and the continued existence of a political community capable of resisting imperial control. Conflating these would exaggerate Akbar's success and obscure why his successors remained embroiled in Deccan warfare.

The capture of an imperial hostage or a young dynastic claimant could stabilize an immediate settlement while making future resistance more likely. Relatives and officers who did not accept the arrangement could assert the rights of an alternative member of the ruling house, drawing on memories of earlier independence and local support. The Mughals therefore confronted political continuity even after a successful siege. In such circumstances a new provincial governor had to decide which local officials to retain, how to secure supplies, and whether to recognize established land rights. The danger was that attempts to remove everyone associated with the defeated dynasty might destroy the practical networks required to govern the conquered districts.

Section 51.36

Khandesh, Asirgarh, and the Southern Frontier

Akbar went south himself in 1599, establishing his headquarters at Burhanpur. The fortress of Asirgarh in Khandesh, one of the strongest in the region, controlled routes into the Deccan. It was besieged from 1600 and surrendered in January 1601.

The conquest completed Mughal control of Khandesh and gave access to Berar and Ahmadnagar. The three conquered areas were organized as provinces under Prince Daniyal and officers appointed from the court, but they had few settled revenue resources and relied on military force.

Asirgarh, in Khandesh, was one of the strongest fortresses of the region and controlled the route between the Narmada and the Deccan. Akbar's siege began in 1600 and lasted months, with accounts describing both military pressure and negotiation. The garrison surrendered in January 1601, and the Khandesh ruler Miran Bahadur Khan was taken into custody.

With Asirgarh's fall Khandesh was incorporated, in contrast to the nominal acknowledgment of 1591. The conquest gave the Mughals a secure base for operations toward Berar and Ahmadnagar.

Khandesh occupied the northern approaches to the Deccan, where major fortresses controlled routes from Malwa and the Tapti valley. Its ruling line had previously acknowledged Mughal superiority in diplomatic terms, but this relationship did not equal surrender of the entire state. Miran Bahadur Khan's position at Asirgarh represented continuing local sovereignty supported by a formidable defensive work. Akbar's movement to the region in 1599 and the subsequent operations around Asirgarh were therefore attempts to convert nominal precedence into effective command of a strategic gateway. The emperor's personal arrival demonstrated the importance attached to the southern frontier and the difficulties subordinate officers had encountered there.

Asirgarh's defenses, supplies, and approaches required a prolonged operation. Later sources discuss disease, negotiation, and accusations of bribery or deception in different ways; these should not be reduced to a single unverified dramatic explanation for the surrender. The fortress fell in January 1601, giving the empire command of a crucial position. Yet the experience confirmed that even an immensely powerful state could be delayed by a local ruler occupying difficult terrain. The military and political solution involved both siege pressure and negotiations affecting the future status of the defeated household.

The incorporation of Khandesh, together with footholds in Berar and Ahmadnagar, extended the map of Mughal provincial authority. The creation or reorganization of administrative jurisdictions should not be confused with uniform revenue collection. Garrison costs were immediate, while secure receipts from disputed countryside took time to establish. Prince Daniyal and other commanders needed to manage the new positions in a setting where Deccan political houses remained active. The conquest gave the Mughals a southern base from which to influence future conflicts, but it also committed the state to defending a long, expensive frontier. That mixed result is essential to a balanced evaluation of Akbar's late reign.

The chronology of southern consolidation should also be separated from the celebratory language of a successful siege. The surrender of Asirgarh opened a strategic gateway, but establishing reliable movement from Burhanpur into the plateau required officials who could protect convoys, arrange fodder, and negotiate passage with neighboring chiefs. A garrison could enforce an imperial order near the walls while the surrounding countryside remained politically disputed. Nor did the presence of a prince in command guarantee close supervision by the emperor of every military action. The late Deccan acquisitions expanded the range of choices available to the next generation of Mughal rulers, but those choices came with a durable commitment to finance and defend distant positions. Their cost was part of the inheritance of 1605.

Section 51.37

The Limits of Akbar's Deccan Expansion

Mughal gains in the south were real but limited. Campaign costs were high, commanders disagreed, and local Deccani nobles retained forts and followings. Bijapur and Golconda remained independent, and Ahmadnagar's resistance revived.

Akbar left the Deccan in 1601 to deal with Prince Salim's challenge in the north. His death in 1605 left the frontier unfinished, and later emperors had to take up the Deccan problem again, as Chapters 53 and 54 show.

The conquered Deccan territories were organized as provinces under Prince Daniyal, with officers drawn from the court. However, the fiscal resources of these areas were limited, and troops needed supplies from the north. Revenue collection also depended on bargains with local landholders.

Several limits explain why the frontier remained unsettled: distance from the capital, long supply lines, rivalry among Mughal commanders, the continuing independence of Bijapur and Golconda, and the revival of Ahmadnagar's resistance. Later chapters trace how these problems shaped seventeenth-century policy.

The limits of Deccan expansion arose from several causes that operated together. The plateau contained strong fortified centers, dispersed political loyalties, and armies able to exploit the distances separating Mughal garrisons. Ahmadnagar's surviving factions could still call upon the resources of villages, mobile cavalry, and diplomatic contacts with Bijapur or Golconda. The empire possessed the fiscal resources to mount great expeditions, but those resources were not unlimited. Money committed to a southern siege could not be used simultaneously to secure every northwest route or suppress rebellion in Bengal. Territorial conquest was thus a continuing negotiation between imperial ambition and the costs imposed by regional geography.

Military coordination presented another problem. Princes and senior nobles commanded overlapping forces, competed for recognition, and sometimes resisted each other's orders. An emperor at a distance had to balance reliance on powerful commanders against the danger that a provincial army might develop a separate political center. The institutional reforms that strengthened imperial service in northern India offered partial solutions, but they could not erase rivalry or replace logistical competence. Commanders who gained a fortress still faced the demands of supplying it, organizing adjacent districts, and securing a loyal local administrative network. The very success of the conquest created new expenses and responsibilities.

The imperial position in 1605 should be described as an important but incomplete advance. Mughal power extended into Khandesh and selected territories of Berar and Ahmadnagar; it had not absorbed Bijapur or Golconda, nor had it extinguished the Nizam Shahi political tradition. Later emperors inherited both strategic advantages and persistent vulnerabilities from these acquisitions. This is why the Deccan belongs centrally in a chapter on Akbar's expansion even though most of its subsequent wars are reserved for later chapters. It reveals the border between what the imperial army could seize and what the state could govern sustainably, and it exposes the tendency of official court narratives to make conquest appear more final than events justified.

Section 51.38

From Personal Following to Centralized Imperial Command

In the Timurid and Central Asian tradition that Babur and Humayun inherited, commanders often led personal followings who owed loyalty to them as much as to the ruler. This worked in conquest but made control of provinces difficult, as the rebellions of the 1560s showed.

Akbar's response was gradual. He sought to make officers answerable directly to the emperor, through appointment, promotion, transfer and rewards. The aim was not to destroy followings but to ensure that rank and income came from the throne.

Earlier Mughal and Central Asian practice made commanders leaders of their own followers. Akbar did not abolish personal followings; he tried to place them within an imperial hierarchy in which appointments, ranks and assignments were granted by the emperor. The experience of Bairam Khan, the Uzbek revolts and the 1580–82 revolts shaped this effort.

The transition from personal military followings to imperial command was not a process in which armed retainers suddenly disappeared. A noble's followers remained important because they supplied experienced cavalrymen, officers, and administrative agents who trusted their patron. The emperor's problem was to make that patron's career contingent on recognition by the court rather than on a permanent claim to local territory. Numerical ranks, periodically reassigned responsibilities, and inspection of military commitments offered ways to define what was owed. These techniques developed from the needs of an expanding empire whose officers served far from their own regional bases. The ultimate aim was not impersonal modern bureaucracy but a more reliable monarchy capable of rewarding and disciplining military elites.

The policy required access to information. Officials had to know who commanded particular units, which troopers were present, where horses came from, and what revenues could pay them. Each piece of information could be contested. A commander might exaggerate the strength of his following or retain the proceeds of a district that the court considered assigned revenue. A locally influential chief could claim that inherited rights were exempt from ordinary transfer. Imperial regulations were therefore political instruments, not merely neutral administrative paperwork. By establishing a common language of rank and service, Akbar hoped to negotiate with many different noble groups on terms in which the crown was the ultimate source of recognition.

The practical results were mixed. Centralized appointment encouraged a composite elite and reduced the likelihood that a single older faction could control the army. Yet a ruler could not simply move every officer at will without losing local expertise or causing discontent. Transfers involved families, soldiers, and credit relationships, and disputes over their compensation could provoke rebellion. The stability of Akbar's system depended on balancing royal discretion with expectations that service would be rewarded. Its institutional development was gradual, shaped by the crises of the 1560s and 1580s, rather than by a fully articulated constitutional plan from the beginning of the reign.

Continuity and Change Over Time: From Followings to Service

What changed: officers became ranked servants tied to imperial appointment. What continued: personal loyalty, factions and regional power. Who experienced change: the high nobility and subordinate rulers. Who saw continuity: cultivators and local landholders. Why it matters: it explains Mughal durability without exaggerating centralization.

Section 51.39

The Origins and Evolution of the Mansabdari System

The mansabdari system ranked imperial officers by numerical grades. Rank determined status, salary and the military force an officer had to maintain. It emerged piece by piece, with reorganizations in the 1570s and further refinement later in the reign, and historians debate its precise stages.

Officers included Turanis, Iranis, Rajputs, Afghans and Indian Muslims, and appointment, promotion and demotion lay with the emperor. The system tied thousands of military men to the throne, giving Akbar leverage that earlier rulers lacked. Its mature form should not be projected back to 1556.

Historians date the main reorganization of ranks to the 1570s, when the emperor reviewed appointments alongside measures for horse verification. The A'in-i Akbari describes a ladder of ranks, but it reflects the system at a later stage, and scholars disagree over how closely it matches earlier practice. The system also coexisted with continued grants of cash salaries and with hereditary claims to rank by some families.

Promotion and demotion depended on the emperor's judgment, on military performance and on court politics. Rank gave status as well as pay, so it affected an officer's place in ceremony and in the nobility.

Historians disagree about the pace and purpose of the system's development. Some emphasize deliberate design by Akbar and his advisers, while others see gradual response to specific problems such as fraud in musters. The Ain's picture of a complete ladder likely reflects the system's mature stage.

The mansab system is best explained as an evolving mechanism for arranging rank, remuneration, and service. The word mansab refers to a position or grade, but its meaning in Mughal practice was created through specific schedules, appointments, and obligations. Abu'l-Fazl's Ain-i Akbari presents a developed portrait of a court that had learned to classify military and administrative servants in numerical orders. That source is invaluable, yet historians warn that it should not be treated as a photograph of the institutions as they existed in 1556. Reform was incremental, and the relationship between personal precedence and the number of men to be maintained changed during the reign. Published research by Shireen Moosvi specifically examines this evolution rather than assuming it was fixed at one moment.

A rank connected an officer to the emperor's hierarchy. It could indicate his ceremonial standing, authorize payment, and convey responsibility for a defined military following. Promotion signaled confidence, while demotion or reassignment communicated the monarch's power to regulate service. The numerical grades created a scale on which an Irani noble, a Rajput commander, or an Indian-born official might be compared without implying that each had identical ancestry or local power. At the same time the grade had to be translated into real money and actual horses, which were more difficult to secure than an official figure written in a register.

The system also made it possible to distribute political status across a growing number of regional elites. A raja could retain ancestral authority while receiving imperial rank; a military newcomer might rise through success in a frontier campaign. The breadth of recruitment served imperial interests but produced new competition. Court officials prepared lists, certified appointments, and recorded obligations, giving the emperor tools for supervision. Such documents were not immune to manipulation. Historians reconstruct the system by comparing the normative language of the Ain with narrative evidence, career histories, and later administrative records. The safest conclusion is that a standardized hierarchy had become crucial by 1605, while the exact chronology of its several technical features requires more cautious language.

Historical reconstruction is complicated by the nature of the surviving records. Lists of ranks describe sanctioned positions, while narrative histories highlight conspicuous promotions, demotions, and military failures. Neither type alone tells us exactly how many trained cavalrymen stood ready on a particular day or how much money an officer actually received. Normative schedules were subject to adjustment, arrears, and exceptions. This difference between regulation and practice should inform the student's reading of the Ain-i Akbari. Abu'l-Fazl's precise tables are an extraordinary administrative source, but their precision can be misleading if converted without qualification into a claim that every district and commander complied uniformly.

Historical Vocabulary: Mansab

A mansab was a numerical rank defining an officer's status, pay and military obligation. It evolved over the reign and was not a fixed hereditary title.

Section 51.40

Zat, Sawar, Cavalry Verification, and Military Service

Over time the system distinguished two measures: zat, an officer's personal rank, and sawar, the number of mounted troopers he had to keep, which was often lower than the zat. This dual rank is generally dated to the 1590s.

Enforcement relied on the branding of horses (dagh), written descriptions of troopers and musters before imperial officials. These controls aimed to prevent fraud, such as hiring borrowed horses and men for inspection, but evasion remained widespread.

The distinction between zat and sawar is generally associated with the 1590s, and historians debate its precise date and purpose. Its effect was to separate personal standing from the cavalry actually maintained, and officers could be given a high zat with a lower sawar rank.

Verification relied on descriptive rolls of men (chehra) and the branding of horses (dagh). Officers sometimes used borrowed horses and men at musters, so the regulations were repeatedly reissued. Enforcement depended on honest inspection, which was uneven.

The distinction between zat and sawar demonstrates why technical administrative terminology must be explained historically rather than used as an unexplained label. In the mature system, zat denoted an officer's personal standing and was used in assessing his own remuneration, while sawar was associated with obligations to maintain mounted men and the financial provision assigned for that purpose. The familiar pairing was not necessarily applied in the same form at every stage of Akbar's reign. Evidence from the Ain and subsequent administrative practice allows historians to reconstruct a complex evolution, with scholarly disagreement over dates, categories, and the sequence of modifications. A textbook should show the reader the mature distinction without claiming an exact year for its introduction where the scholarship remains divided.

Horse branding, described by the term dagh, and descriptive identification of personnel, often called chehra, attempted to prevent fraudulent musters. A commander paid to provide a particular body of soldiers had an incentive to present the same horse more than once, to exaggerate numbers, or to replace trained men with cheaper substitutes. Branding and description reduced those possibilities in principle but required inspectors, reliable records, and some degree of separation between the interests of the officer and those verifying his men. These measures were adapted from earlier military practices as well as developed further by Mughal administrators; they should not be credited uncritically to a single imperial innovation.

The connection to warfare was immediate. Cavalry cost money for mounts, fodder, equipment, and replacement animals, while pay delays could undermine morale or lead troops to plunder. Standards for inspection helped the crown compare obligations across nobles serving in different provinces. But nominal rank could not guarantee operational readiness in Bengal's waterways or the Aravalli hills. The state still needed skilled commanders, local recruits, engineers, and seasonal planning. Technical systems thus strengthened imperial capacity precisely because they made resources more predictable, not because they eliminated the judgement and political negotiation necessary to employ those resources.

Section 51.41

Jagirdari and the Financing of Imperial Service

Officers were largely paid not in cash but with assignments of revenue (jagirs) from designated territories, calculated against their salaries. A jagir conferred the right to collect state revenue, not ownership of the land.

Jagirs were transferred regularly, which limited the roots officers could grow in any one place but also encouraged short-term extraction from cultivators. Some lands were kept as crown territory (khalisa). Detailed revenue assessment belongs to Chapter 52.

Jagirs were assigned by calculating expected revenue against an officer's salary, which sometimes proved inaccurate. Officers had an incentive to collect as much as possible during a short tenure, with consequences for cultivators and for local landholders.

Some territory was held as khalisa to provide revenue directly to the treasury, and the balance between khalisa and jagir lands changed over time. Chapter 52 examines assessment methods and provincial administration.

Jagirs were not uniform. Their value, location and duration varied with an officer's rank and court standing, and officers with poor assignments could be disadvantaged. The assignment system therefore shaped factional politics as well as finance.

The jagir was a revenue assignment used to compensate imperial servants, not an estate owned outright in the modern sense. An officer might receive the right to collect or receive specified revenues from particular districts in lieu of cash salary, subject to the terms and duration of his appointment. The government could transfer the assignment, and local landholders retained their own claims and relationships within the same territory. Distinguishing jagirdar from zamindar matters: the former held a state-sanctioned claim linked to service, while the latter could possess inherited rights and local authority that did not vanish when the assignment changed. Confusing the two makes Mughal agrarian politics appear much more straightforward than it was.

The fiscal advantage was that the empire could remunerate a large military elite without transporting an equivalent amount of cash to every garrison. But the arrangement created friction between nominal assessment and actual collections. A newly appointed jagirdar might not know the villages or existing obligations, while local officials and chiefs could dispute the amount demanded. Officers who expected to be moved after a short tenure had reasons to collect rapidly, even at the expense of long-term cultivation. Frequent transfer might prevent the rise of hereditary provincial lords, yet it could also weaken the incentives for protecting agricultural productivity. Some assignments produced less than their recorded value, requiring negotiation or supplementary support from the court.

The crown's interests were not identical to those of every officer. The emperor wanted sufficient military readiness, dependable tax flows, and manageable rural relations; a jagirdar wanted to finance his household and troops; cultivators wanted to preserve the resources needed for the next harvest. Local chiefs could mediate or exploit these competing demands. The deeper mechanics of revenue measurement and fiscal administration belong to Chapter 52, but the political-military logic of jagirdari is indispensable here. Without it, readers cannot understand how a geographically extensive army was funded or why noble rebellions so often concerned postings, assignments, and the right to control revenues from particular districts.

The use of revenue assignments also created different forms of political knowledge. An imperial accountant might know a district through registers, estimated yields, and recorded obligations. A local chief knew which villages had suffered crop failure, which families had enforceable customary rights, and which routes could safely be used by collectors. Neither perspective was complete. Successful administration depended on cooperation between them, while conflict could provoke resistance or concealment. This helps explain why a central effort to standardize service did not automatically produce standardized agrarian practice. Chapter 52 will examine the technical assessment systems; here the key point is that military finance linked elite careers to the uneven and socially contested extraction of rural resources.

Section 51.42

Building a Composite Imperial Nobility

The nobility Akbar assembled was diverse. Turani and Irani officers formed large groups, Indian-born Muslims and Afghans were present, and Rajputs, most prominently of the Kachhwaha house, became a significant group among high-ranking officers. Hindu officials such as Todar Mal also held major posts.

Diversity brought both strength and tension. Group identities, patronage networks and competition for offices shaped factions, and promotion was not equally open to all. Integration meant shared service to the emperor, not erasure of differences.

The nobility's composition reflected the empire's expansion. Rajput and Afghan officers joined as territories were conquered, while Turani and Irani officers continued to arrive from the west. Indian-born Muslims and Hindu administrators held important civil and financial posts.

Group identity did not predict political behavior. Officers from the same background could be rivals, while officers of different backgrounds could be allies, and marriage, patronage and personal loyalty crossed group lines.

A composite nobility was one of the empire's principal political achievements. Akbar recruited from populations with differing historical ties to the Timurid dynasty, including Central Asian and Iranian migrants, Afghan families, established Indian Muslim lineages, and Rajput ruling houses. These groups did not become socially identical; distinctions of household prestige, language, marriage, religion, and military background persisted. Nevertheless membership in the imperial service offered a common framework for rank, honors, and advancement. The crown could compare officers through appointments and use the distribution of patronage to prevent any one network from holding the entire military establishment hostage.

The incorporation of regional elites served more than a symbolic ideal of inclusiveness. Rajput commanders supplied armies and local legitimacy, while Indian Muslim officials brought knowledge of older sultanate institutions, and Iranian and Central Asian migrants continued to provide diplomatic and military connections to the wider Persianate world. A particular noble might accumulate influence because he could cross these networks and govern provinces far from his place of origin. Such officers were valuable to the emperor but could also develop followers capable of supporting rebellion or succession campaigns. Imperial promotion therefore had to create loyalty without conceding an independent right to territory or military office.

Social mobility remained selective. Elite birth, wealth, military experience, and access to patronage influenced advancement; the existence of an integrated nobility did not make the state broadly egalitarian. Women of high-ranking households participated in marriage alliances and property arrangements, but chronicles devoted disproportionately little attention to their agency. Ordinary soldiers and rural communities often experienced the composite empire through tax demands and military recruitment rather than through the prestigious language of court service. A balanced explanation should acknowledge the stabilizing achievements of Akbar's recruitment policy while explaining its uneven effects and the continuing hierarchy within the political system.

Section 51.43

Regional Rulers, Zamindars, and Negotiated Imperial Authority

The Mughals ruled through many subordinate rulers and local landholders (zamindars) who retained forts, followings and customary rights. Rajput and other rulers held hereditary lands (watan jagirs) and provided troops and loyalty.

Authority was negotiated. Local figures used Mughal rank to strengthen their own positions, while the empire relied on them for taxes, order and soldiers. This explains why imperial sovereignty was strong at the center and varied across regions.

Subordinate rulers and zamindars were an essential part of the empire's military manpower. A chief might hold his watan as a jagir, serve with his troops and receive rank, while retaining customary authority over his own people.

This arrangement allowed Mughal authority to reach regions that officials could not directly administer. It also meant imperial control depended on the cooperation of local elites, who could shift their loyalty if their interests were threatened.

Local rulers and zamindars formed a crucial layer between the court and the agrarian world. Their authority might derive from hereditary rights, armed followers, control of forts, revenue collection, or recognition within a particular community. The empire could not realistically remove all of them without destroying much of the information and local mediation needed for effective government. Instead it negotiated various degrees of subordination: recognition of rank, confirmation of inherited territory, payment of dues, obligations to supply military contingents, or acceptance of an imperial governor nearby. Such arrangements could be formalized through an appointment or remain practical bargains shaped by relative military power.

The concept of watan jagir captures one important mechanism in Rajput relations, but similar complexities appeared elsewhere under different local terminology. A ruler might be a subordinate within an imperial hierarchy and still exercise considerable autonomy over everyday governance at home. A zamindar could pay assessed revenue while controlling armed retainers and intervening in local disputes. The degree of imperial authority thus varied across regions and sometimes across neighboring estates. In the Bengal delta, access to fleets and waterways allowed chiefs to defy an appointed provincial administration; in Rajasthan, inherited legitimacy and hill fortresses could sustain resistance even after an imperial battlefield success.

This system created both resilience and vulnerability. Recognizing local power reduced the cost of conquest and allowed the court to build coalitions with regional societies. But the continuation of strong local lineages meant that imperial governors might confront disputes over succession, rights, or military obligations. The state often had to negotiate rather than simply enforce. The durability of Akbar's empire rested in part on accepting such asymmetry and creating incentives for participation, not on achieving the literal elimination of every alternative authority. Students should therefore avoid defining centralized government as direct administrative control of every village; the political integration achieved under Akbar was real, but its methods were negotiated and regionally differentiated.

The difference between allegiance and practical authority could be visible even within one village. The imperial government might claim sovereignty, a jagirdar might possess an assigned fiscal interest, a zamindar might hold hereditary collection rights, and local headmen might exercise everyday influence over cultivation and dispute resolution. These relationships could cooperate or compete. Treating only the emperor's decree as historically real would erase most of the arrangements through which state demands reached rural households. For this reason the history of expansion needs to follow appointments down to their social consequences, even when the available sources make those consequences difficult to reconstruct with precision.

Watan

A watan was a hereditary home territory of a ruler or chief. Mughal officers from ruling houses often held their watan as a jagir, which let the empire work through existing lords while binding them to service.

Section 51.44

Factional Politics and the Limits of Centralization

Court factions competed for offices, commands and jagirs, and personal ties to the emperor and princes mattered a great deal. Disputes between commanders weakened campaigns, as in the Deccan.

The system never fully overcame the tension between assignments made by the center and the regional influence of officers and local elites. Its flexibility helped Akbar manage conquest, yet it left problems that his successors inherited.

Factions formed around princes, powerful ministers and commanders, and competition was often over the control of provinces and military resources. The Deccan campaigns showed how rivalry among Mughal commanders could undermine operations.

Akbar balanced groups, rotated appointments and intervened personally, which sustained his authority but made the system dependent on his judgment. The same system would have to function under later emperors with different political skills.

Factional politics did not disappear when the emperor created a more standardized hierarchy of rank. Noble households competed for appointments, access to the ruler, military honors, and favorable revenue assignments. Rivalries could follow lines of origin, kinship, marriage, or earlier service, but these categories overlapped and rarely created stable blocs. The emperor gained leverage by promoting officers from different backgrounds and rotating major commands. Yet an officer who believed that promised rewards were withheld might conclude that rebellion or a rival prince offered a better future. The state therefore required not only formal procedures but continuing political judgement in distributing prestige and resources.

Provincial campaigns reveal how such competition affected military success. In Gujarat and the Deccan, disagreements among commanders could delay operations, encourage enemies, and burden soldiers with prolonged campaigning. Even when the imperial state won, excessive private enrichment by officers might undermine local cooperation and reduce the treasury's receipts. At the same time the emperor depended on initiative: commanders far from court had to negotiate with regional rulers or move troops before instructions could arrive. Overly rigid control would have limited that flexibility. Akbar's practice sought a difficult balance between delegated authority and personal supervision, with different outcomes in different military theaters.

The elite system also depended heavily on the emperor's capacity to arbitrate disputes. His long reign enabled officers to build expectations that their service would be judged and rewarded, but the same dependence on a powerful sovereign raised questions about succession. If a prince or group of nobles could persuade colleagues that imperial favor would be redistributed, loyalty might shift rapidly. This helps connect the institutional analysis to Salim's late-reign challenge without anticipating the entire politics of Jahangir's subsequent government. The mature system made Mughal rule more durable than it had been in 1556, but it did not abolish the personal and dynastic foundations of early modern monarchy.

Section 51.45

Geography, Ecology, and the Limits of Imperial Expansion

Environment shaped every campaign. The Gangetic plain favored cavalry and large armies, while Rajasthan's hills and desert favored defenders. Bengal's rivers and creeks limited horses and favored boats, and the Himalayan and Hindu Kush passes made supply difficult.

The monsoon halted movement across much of the subcontinent for months, and sieges of hill forts required patience and resources. Agricultural wealth, in turn, determined where armies could be fed. Geography did not determine outcomes, but it set limits that commanders had to negotiate.

Environmental factors also affected state finances. Fertile river plains produced the revenue that paid officers, while arid zones and hill country offered less, and the cost of garrisons in frontier regions often exceeded local income. Expansion into regions with less productive land therefore increased military costs more than it increased revenue.

The map of Mughal expansion conceals environments whose military requirements differed radically. The Indo-Gangetic plains supplied grain, roads, and towns that could support mounted campaigning; Rajasthan required water and access to fortresses; Bengal required vessels and pilots; Kashmir and the Afghan frontier demanded mountain logistics; and the Deccan imposed long supply lines across a plateau with formidable strongholds. A dynasty could become more powerful in aggregate without becoming equally effective in every region. The ability to move grain and horses was frequently a more reliable predictor of military success than a ruler's declaration of sovereignty over a territory. Seasonal weather further complicated the issue, since monsoon rains altered river crossings and roads while mountain snow affected northern passes.

Ecology interacted with fiscal administration. Rich agricultural zones might finance military operations elsewhere, but revenues were mediated by local landholders, collectors, and markets rather than transferred automatically into an imperial treasury. A distant garrison could cost more than it collected, especially when hostilities disrupted cultivation or trade. The court had to determine whether a province's strategic position justified temporary financial loss. Control of a port or road could have value disproportionate to its immediate land revenue, while a contested valley might require repeated expeditions to protect a single route. These calculations help explain why the empire expanded in some directions while facing long periods of resistance in others.

Regional social relationships were another element of geography. A local chief who possessed knowledge of water sources, mountain paths, or river channels could be indispensable to a governor even if he formally ranked below imperial commanders. Nominally subordinate communities could retain substantial bargaining leverage. The territorial expansion visible by 1605 was therefore the result of solving many distinct local problems rather than applying one universal technique. The chapter's map studies should encourage readers to compare accessibility, communication, seasonality, and the persistence of political identities, not merely to color regions according to dates of conquest.

Section 51.46

The Mughal Empire in 1605: Territorial Reach and Uneven Sovereignty

By 1605 Mughal claims extended from Kabul and Kashmir to Bengal and Odisha, and from the Punjab and Sindh to Gujarat and into Khandesh, Berar and parts of Ahmadnagar. Provinces were organized under governors and officials, but effective control varied.

Mewar, the Bengal delta, the Afghan frontier hills and the Deccan show the gap between claimed and effective authority. Independent rulers, including Bijapur, Golconda and remaining Ahmadnagar claimants, survived beyond the frontier. The empire was a large, uneven structure maintained through force, alliance and negotiation, not a uniformly governed territory.

Historians describe the empire of 1605 as a hierarchy of control. Core provinces of the Gangetic plain and Punjab were administered by imperial officials, while peripheral regions were governed by negotiated arrangements with local chiefs. The administrative divisions of the empire were not uniform, and the Deccan provinces existed as claims backed by garrisons.

The phrase 'uneven sovereignty' describes a range of relationships, from direct administration to tribute-paying rulers to areas where Mughal officials rarely penetrated. The difference between claimed and effective authority is central to understanding how empires of this period worked.

By 1605 Akbar's empire included a far wider and more economically diverse territory than that inherited from Humayun. Its armies had defeated rival claimants in the north, integrated powerful Rajput houses, conquered major commercial and agricultural regions, secured significant northwestern positions, and gained southern footholds. Imperial appointments and ranked military service gave this enlarged realm a means of coordinating officers drawn from many backgrounds. These were substantial achievements, and they should not be obscured by the fact that resistance continued. Yet the geographic scale of a political claim must be distinguished from the degree of day-to-day control exercised in each district.

The contrast among provinces was pronounced. In the central Gangetic heartland the crown possessed established fiscal routes and more consistent garrison networks. In Gujarat, urban commerce and merchant communities presented possibilities for revenue but also required stable local agreements. Bengal's eastern waterways continued to support chiefs beyond routine provincial control. In Mewar the Sisodiya court remained independent of a full settlement with the emperor. The northwest contained principal Mughal fortresses alongside mountain groups retaining substantial autonomy. In the Deccan the conquest of Ahmadnagar fort and Asirgarh yielded strategic gains while much of the southern political landscape remained outside stable imperial administration.

The empire was not a modern unitary state administering homogenous citizens behind fixed borders. Its reach consisted of graded relations—direct occupation, tributary acknowledgment, service arrangements, revenue claims, and intermittent military influence. These relations were not inherently ineffective: they allowed the monarchy to govern great distances with fewer resources than direct occupation would require. They were vulnerable when local intermediaries withdrew cooperation or when a succession crisis weakened imperial authority. Akbar's achievement was to make this differentiated arrangement more durable and more capable of financing further campaigns, not to eliminate the diverse political orders from which it had been assembled.

A more exact assessment of Akbar's achievement can therefore use several indicators rather than a single territorial area: how reliably governors were appointed and replaced, whether military officers obeyed summons, whether principal roads were safe, which local rulers supplied contingents, and whether revenue could be collected without extraordinary force. The evidence permits firmer conclusions about some regions than others. Imperial chronicles document campaigns and formal appointments especially well, but may be less revealing about everyday rural compliance. When later scholars produce maps of the empire, those maps should be read as interpretive syntheses of varied historical evidence rather than as direct reproductions of a single contemporary administrative boundary survey.

Section 51.47

Akbar's Final Years, Prince Salim, and the Problem of Succession

Akbar's later years were troubled by the conduct of his sons. Murad died in 1599 and Daniyal in 1605, both after histories of excessive drinking. Salim, the eldest, grew impatient, and in the early 1600s he set up a rival court at Allahabad and acted as an independent ruler.

In 1602 Abu'l-Fazl, summoned from the Deccan, was killed on the road by Bir Singh Bundela, an act widely attributed to Salim's instigation. Akbar was reconciled with his son in 1604. Factions at court discussed alternatives, including Salim's son Khusrau. Akbar died in October 1605. Salim's accession as Jahangir is treated in Chapter 53.

Salim's relations with his father deteriorated after he was sent against Mewar in 1599 and instead went to Allahabad. By 1602 he acted as an independent prince, and the killing of Abu'l-Fazl by Bir Singh Bundela is widely attributed to his encouragement, though the sources reflect partisan interests.

Reconciliation followed in 1603–1604 through the intercession of senior women of the family, and the death of Akbar's mother in 1604 added to the strain. At Akbar's death in October 1605, a faction at court had favored Salim's son Khusrau, but Salim became emperor as Jahangir. Chapter 53 examines his reign.

The final years of Akbar's reign exposed the unresolved political problem of a powerful monarchy: the succession remained a decision contested among princes, household networks, and leading nobles. Prince Salim had established a substantial political following and increasingly sought recognition independent of his father. His conduct around the turn of the century, including tension over the eastern provinces and control of followers, worried the emperor because a prince with resources could become a rival power center before a formal accession. Akbar's other sons experienced difficulties of their own, and the deaths of Murad in 1599 and Daniyal in 1605 narrowed the field. The political significance of these deaths is clearer than any simplified story that Salim was always the uncontested heir.

The killing of Abu'l-Fazl in 1602 by Bir Singh Deo Bundela, acting in circumstances associated with Salim's opposition, showed the deadly stakes of succession politics. Abu'l-Fazl was more than an official chronicler: he was a trusted adviser whose presence could influence the emperor's understanding of the prince. His removal was therefore both a personal loss and a political event. The imperial court's response and the prince's relationship with senior women of the dynasty figured in efforts to restore communication. Accounts differ about the exact sequence of reconciliation, and later narratives sometimes provide a more orderly resolution than the evidence warrants. Akbar's mother Hamida Banu Begum died in 1604, another loss within the central household.

Some nobles considered Salim's son Khusrau as an alternative, indicating that the mature Mughal elite still retained the ability to influence succession. The court was not an impersonal electoral institution, but neither was the choice of the next ruler simply a private matter involving two relatives. Officers weighed military security, future appointments, and their relations with each contender. Akbar's death in October 1605 brought these tensions to a point. Salim succeeded as Jahangir, whose subsequent reign belongs to Chapter 53. The closing episode completes the chapter's central argument: Akbar had created a more resilient system of conquest and service, but personal monarchy and powerful political households continued to shape its future.

Section 51.48

Review and Historical Synthesis

This chapter has shown how a dynasty with a precarious hold on Delhi in 1556 became the ruler of an empire stretching from Kabul to Bengal by 1605. Expansion was achieved through campaigns, but also through alliances with Rajput houses, regional chiefs and defeated opponents, and through institutions that tied officers to the emperor.

Conquest was not pacification. Haldighati, the Bengal delta, the Afghan hills and the Deccan show how resistance persisted after victories. The mansabdari and jagirdari systems, evolving across the reign, helped control a composite nobility, but they could not eliminate faction or regional power. Akbar's empire proved more durable than Babur's and Humayun's because of this combination, yet it passed unfinished problems of succession, frontiers and local autonomy to his successors. Chapter 52 turns to the governing system and ideology that sustained it.

The history of Akbar's reign should be read as a series of connected experiments in the formation of imperial power. The empire survived the emergency of 1556 because experienced officers retained a functioning army, but it became more durable only when Akbar could exercise authority over those officers and extend their cooperation to new communities. The campaigns of Malwa and Gondwana, the conflict with rebellious nobles, and the alliance with Amber show how military command and political integration developed together. His government strengthened its control of major routes and treasuries while learning that the elimination of a rival ruler did not necessarily eliminate the networks on which that ruler depended.

Regional campaigns expose different forms of conquest. Chittor demonstrates the terrible human costs of imperial expansion and the persistence of resistance after the fall of a celebrated fortress. Haldighati illustrates how a tactical outcome can differ from the subsequent strategic position. Gujarat's trade made it valuable but also required bargaining with commercial communities and confronting foreign maritime power. The defeat of Daud Karrani at Rajmahal ended a dynasty while leaving many riverine chiefs independent of routine provincial administration. The northwest combined forts, diplomacy, and military operations in mountains where large armies could not reliably compel every community. In the Deccan, Akbar's late campaigns secured major positions without extinguishing the states and political traditions that continued to resist.

The developing mansab and jagir arrangements gave the empire tools to coordinate a diverse military elite across these regions. They connected status, compensation, and service to imperial appointments, but their complexity and evolution resist a simple invention narrative. The system had roots in earlier Sultanate and Timurid practices while acquiring new levels of standardization and scope under Akbar. It also created incentives for overcollection and continuing conflict between central demands and local rights. A composite nobility strengthened the court, yet factional competition and disputed succession remained possible. Imperial power in 1605 was therefore both materially stronger and politically more differentiated than it had been at Akbar's accession.

The final historical judgment should avoid two oversimplifications. Akbar was not merely a benevolent organizer who expanded through consent, for his wars caused destruction and his armies employed severe violence. Nor was Mughal power only a sequence of conquests imposed by force, because negotiated subordination and the continuing autonomy of regional elites were central to its operation. The achievement was an adaptive, hierarchical imperial order able to command resources across a vast region, yet always dependent on the social and political relationships that connected provincial officers to local society. The next chapter investigates its mature administrative and ideological foundations in detail; this chapter has shown why those institutions became necessary and what problems they were meant to address.

Chapter Summary

Chapter 51 has argued that Akbar's empire was built less by a sequence of victories than by the interaction of conquest, negotiation and institutions. In 1556 the dynasty held a narrow corridor around Delhi and Agra, and its survival depended on whether Mughal officers would follow a thirteen-year-old and his regent.

The victory at Panipat over Hemu on 5 November 1556 reopened Delhi and Agra but did not end Afghan power. Over the following years Mughal forces absorbed Sur remnants, and Akbar ended Bairam Khan's regency in 1560 and executed Adham Khan in 1562, establishing personal rule.

The campaigns in Malwa and Gondwana and the Uzbek revolts exposed the central problem of command: successful generals could become autonomous rulers. Akbar's responses combined pardon, transfer, and force, and the problem returned in the revolts of 1580–1582.

Relations with Rajput houses ranged across a spectrum. Amber's alliance of 1562 brought loyal commanders; Ranthambore negotiated; Chittor fell in 1568 after a siege that, by Abu'l-Fazl's own account, ended in the killing of nearly 30,000 people; Mewar resisted for decades after Haldighati.

Gujarat, Bihar and Bengal brought wealth and access to the sea and the eastern rivers. Yet the Gujarat risings, the survival of Afghan and local power after Rajmahal in 1576, and Isa Khan's resistance in the delta show that taking a capital did not secure the countryside.

In the northwest, Kabul, Kashmir, Sindh and Kandahar were added by force, defection and diplomacy between 1585 and 1595, while the Yusufzai and Roshani hills stayed largely autonomous. The frontier differed from the Gangetic plain in terrain, political structure and cost.

The Deccan campaigns, in which Chand Bibi defended Ahmadnagar in 1595–1596, ended with the capture of the fort in 1600 and Asirgarh in 1601. These gains did not extinguish the Nizam Shahi state or bring Bijapur and Golconda under control.

Mansabdari and jagirdari, evolving across the reign, organized military and political service. Rank, horse verification and revenue assignment bound a composite nobility to the emperor, but they did not eliminate faction or the power of regional elites.

By 1605 the empire stretched from Kabul to Bengal and into Khandesh. Its reach exceeded that of Babur's and Humayun's states, but sovereignty was graded, with claimed authority exceeding effective control in several regions.

Akbar's last years, Salim's estrangement, and the killing of Abu'l-Fazl in 1602 reveal the dynasty's recurring succession problem. Chapter 52 turns from the acquisition of power to the governing system and ideology that sustained it.

The three primary historians used here did not occupy the same position. Abu'l-Fazl supplied the imperial interpretation, Bada'uni offered a more critical courtly voice, and Nizam al-Din Ahmad assembled a chronicle with its own selection principles. Comparing them does not convert their agreements into certainty about motives or precise casualty totals; it exposes how powerful elites recorded—and sometimes justified—the making of a larger political order.

Chapter Reflection

Akbar's reign shows that empires are made as much by bargaining as by force. The institutions that bound officers to the throne helped the Mughals endure, yet the persistence of regional power reminds students that the borders of an empire on a map conceal uneven control.

The chapter also shows why sources must be read critically. The imperial chronicles preserve invaluable detail, but they were written to explain and justify the reign, and the voices of defeated rulers, villagers and soldiers survive mostly at the edges.

The problems left open in 1605, the Deccan frontier, the place of regional elites and the instability of succession, shaped the seventeenth century. Chapter 52 examines the governing and ideological system that stood behind the expansion, and Chapters 53 and 54 trace how later emperors inherited both its strengths and its limits.

Within the six-volume series, Akbar's reign is a hinge. It connects the Sultanate and Timurid inheritances traced in Volume II to the imperial economy, culture and eventual fragmentation examined in the rest of Volume III.
