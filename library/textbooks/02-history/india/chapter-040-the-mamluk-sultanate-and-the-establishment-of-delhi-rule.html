Part VIII — Islamic Frontiers and the Delhi Sultanate

The Mamluk Sultanate and the Establishment of Delhi Rule

Indian History: South Asia from the Indus Civilization to the Present

Chapter Opening

In the southern part of modern Delhi, in the district of Mehrauli, a tower of red and buff sandstone rises more than seventy meters above the ruins around it. Bands of Arabic inscription circle its fluted shafts. Beside it stands a great mosque screen whose pillars and carved stones were drawn from earlier buildings, and nearby is the tomb of a ruler who died in 1236. None of these structures was finished in a single reign, and none was built to express a finished state. They record a political project still under construction: a generation of rulers, most of them former military slaves, working to turn conquered territory into a lasting government.

Chapter 39 followed the Ghaznavid and Ghurid advance into northwestern and northern India and ended with the death of Mu'izz al-Din Muhammad of Ghor in 1206. That year is conventionally treated as the beginning of the Delhi Sultanate, but this chapter argues that it was a threshold rather than a foundation. Mu'izz al-Din left behind commanders, garrisons, revenue claims, and conquered cities. He did not leave a settled succession, a single capital, or a government able to function without him. The Sultanate emerged over roughly eight decades of experimentation, rivalry, and negotiation, and this chapter traces how.

The chapter's central problem is stated plainly: how does a conquering military network become a state? The early Delhi sultans answered it unevenly. Qutb al-Din Aibak established the first independent political base in northern India, but from Lahore, and with rivals on every side. Shams al-Din Iltutmish consolidated Delhi, subdued competing commanders, secured outside recognition of his rule, and developed methods of revenue assignment and territorial administration. Raziyya's brief reign exposed how contested the rules of succession remained. Ghiyath al-Din Balban later reconstructed the authority of the monarchy through court discipline, military reorganization, and frontier defense. Even then the Sultanate remained a negotiated, regionally uneven political system and not a modern centralized bureaucracy.

Two pressures run through the whole chapter. The first is internal: the sultans depended on powerful military commanders, many of them former slaves of the ruling house, whose loyalty was essential and never guaranteed. The second is external: from the 1220s the Mongol empire reshaped the strategic world of northwestern South Asia. Dynasties across Central Asia and Iran collapsed under Mongol attack. Delhi survived, and understanding why requires attention to frontier warfare, diplomacy, and the resources it consumed.

This chapter also asks students to think carefully about the evidence. Our narrative sources are in Persian, written by court-connected authors with their own loyalties. The most important contemporary source, Minhaj-i Siraj Juzjani's Tabaqat-i Nasiri, was completed around 1260. Ziya al-Din Barani's Tarikh-i Firuz Shahi was written about a century later and reflects the moral and political concerns of the fourteenth century. Coins, inscriptions, and monuments supplement these texts, and they sometimes complicate them. Throughout, the Sultanate must be understood as a state interacting with an already complex South Asian society that included Hindu, Buddhist, Jain, and regional political and religious institutions and a growing Muslim population of varied origins. It did not replace that society wholesale.

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to explain how Delhi became the principal center of Muslim-ruled authority in northern India, how institutions such as the iqta allowed rulers to pay armies and administer territory, why the question of legitimacy mattered so much, and why the Sultanate was both stronger in 1290 than in 1206 and still dependent on negotiation with regional elites. Chapter 41 then asks how the Khaljis transformed this state through far larger territorial expansion.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why 1206 marked a political threshold rather than the creation of a fully formed Delhi Sultanate.
  2. Distinguish Lahore and Delhi as competing political centers and explain why Delhi eventually prevailed.
  3. Trace the careers of Aibak, Iltutmish, Raziyya, and Balban and compare their approaches to state-building.
  4. Analyze the iqta system as a method of revenue assignment and military maintenance, and explain why it was not private land ownership.
  5. Assess the multiple sources of early Sultanate legitimacy, including military success, caliphal recognition, coinage, the khutba, court ritual, and relations with the ulama.
  6. Evaluate how Mongol expansion transformed the strategic environment of the Sultanate without reducing Mongol pressure to a sequence of invasions of Delhi.
  7. Interpret Raziyya's reign as a problem of succession, coalition-building, and elite politics rather than only as a story of gender.
  8. Explain the relationship between the sultan and the nobility, including the problem of the "Forty," without treating it as a formal constitutional body.
  9. Describe how cultivators, merchants, artisans, soldiers, religious communities, and dependent laborers experienced early Sultanate rule.
  10. Analyze mosques, tombs, inscriptions, and coins as evidence of how rulers made authority visible, distinguishing surviving evidence from later legend.
  11. Evaluate competing interpretations of whether the early Sultanate was centralized or negotiated.
  12. Connect the developments of this chapter to the Ghurid foundations of Chapter 39 and the Khalji expansion of Chapter 41.

Historical Context

The political landscape of northern India around 1200 was crowded. Chapter 35 examined the Rajput polities of Rajasthan and the northwest, including the Chauhans, and Chapter 34 the long contest among the Palas, Pratiharas, and Rashtrakutas. By the late twelfth century the Gahadavalas held the middle Ganges plain from Kannauj and Varanasi, the Chandellas held parts of central India, and Chauhan authority centered on Ajmer and, for a time, Delhi. Chapter 39 explained how Mu'izz al-Din Muhammad, ruling from Ghazni and Firuzkuh, defeated Prithviraj Chauhan at the second battle of Tarain in 1192, took Delhi, and defeated the Gahadavala ruler Jayachandra in 1194. Ghurid commanders also advanced eastward; one of them, Muhammad Bakhtiyar Khalji, led raids that extended Ghurid influence into Bihar and Bengal.

The Ghurid political system was not a bureaucratic empire in the later sense. Mu'izz al-Din relied on a household of trusted military slaves, ghulams, whom he trained, promoted, and entrusted with distant commands. Chief among them in India was Qutb al-Din Aibak. Others included Taj al-Din Yildiz at Ghazni and Nasir al-Din Qabacha, who held Multan and Uch. These men were connected by service, marriage, and rivalry. When the sultan was assassinated in 1206, the relationships that had held them in one structure were suddenly open to renegotiation.

The Delhi region offered real advantages. It stood near the Yamuna at a point where the Punjab plains, the Ganga-Yamuna doab, and the routes to Rajasthan and Malwa converge. It already possessed fortifications, mints, and a population of merchants and artisans. The monsoon-fed agriculture of the doab and the irrigation possibilities of the Yamuna basin offered revenue. Chapter 1 explained how routes across the northwest connect Central Asia to the Indus and Ganges systems, and this chapter shows how rulers in Delhi positioned themselves at the junction of those routes.

The strategic world beyond India was changing violently. Within a generation, the Mongol empire of Chinggis Khan destroyed the Khwarazmian state, which had displaced the Ghurids in Afghanistan, and threatened every polity of Central Asia and Iran. Students should keep in mind that the early Sultanate was built in the shadow of that catastrophe. Our sources for these events include Persian narratives and the later Ilkhanid histories, and our evidence for the Sultanate's own institutions is thinner than the confident tone of the chronicles suggests. This chapter therefore marks clearly where evidence is firm and where historians reconstruct from fragments. Chapter 44 will examine the broader society and culture of the Sultanate, and this chapter gives only the portion of that story needed to understand its early political foundations.

Section 40.1

From Ghurid Conquest to Sultanate Rule

The death of Mu'izz al-Din Muhammad of Ghor in 1206 is commonly given as the date on which the Delhi Sultanate began. Contemporary evidence supports a more cautious reading. Mu'izz al-Din was killed on the road, at Dhamiak near the Jhelum, and accounts of his killers differ; some chroniclers blame Isma'ili assassins and others Khokhar tribesmen. The political consequence is clearer than the cause. His empire had no institutional mechanism for transferring authority smoothly across the distance between Ghazni, Firuzkuh, Lahore, and Delhi, and it had no heir with a recognized claim to his Indian possessions.

What Aibak inherited in India was a network, not a state in the later sense. It consisted of garrisons in cities such as Delhi, Meerut, Koil (modern Aligarh), and Ajmer; commanders who had received assignments to maintain troops; revenue claims over agricultural districts whose existing intermediaries often remained in place; and loyalty relationships among slave commanders. Many local Hindu chiefs had been defeated, made tributaries, or left in place. Others, including the Gahadavala successors and Chauhan and Chandella lines, retained territory or recovered it. The map of "Muslim rule" in 1206 was a patchwork of garrison towns and tributary arrangements, not a continuous territory.

This is why the central question of the chapter is institutional. A conquering army can win battles and extract tribute. A state must pay its soldiers regularly, settle succession without civil war, make decisions that outlast the ruler, and persuade subjects and rivals that its authority is rightful. The early sultans of Delhi spent much of eighty years learning to do these things, and sometimes failing. Reading the period as a story of "slave kings" building an empire can hide the real work: negotiation among commanders, borrowed and adapted political ideas from the Ghaznavid, Ghurid, and Seljuk worlds, and slow experimentation with revenue and territorial administration.

Students should also resist the assumption that the sultans were building toward a settled outcome. Contemporaries could not know that Delhi would outlast Lahore, Ghazni, or Multan as the center of authority. Mongol attack, rebellion, or a ruthless rival could have ended the experiment at any time.

Section 40.2

Qutb al-Din Aibak and the First Delhi Sultan

Aibak's origins illustrate how the Ghurid system worked. According to Persian sources, he was born in Turkic Central Asia, enslaved as a child, sold in the market of Nishapur, and eventually purchased by Mu'izz al-Din. He rose by service, serving as master of the horse (amir-i akhur), and by the early 1190s commanded forces in India. After the capture of Delhi he acted as the Ghurid viceroy for the Indian territories, maintaining garrisons, sending out raids, and receiving instructions from a sultan who spent most of his time in Afghanistan and Khurasan.

Historical Vocabulary
Ghulam and Mamluk: Military Slavery in the Islamic World

Ghulam (plural ghilman) and mamluk are terms for military slaves: young men, usually of non-Muslim origin from Central Asia or the Caucasus, who were purchased, converted if necessary, educated, trained, and promoted within a ruler's household. Slave status in this context did not imply poverty or social contempt. Successful ghulams could become governors, commanders, and even rulers, and their legal status could be ended by manumission. The institution had a long history in the Abbasid, Ghaznavid, Seljuk, and Ghurid worlds. Its political logic was that a ruler could build a loyal following among men who owed everything to him and had no local kin networks of their own.

Historians often call the early Delhi rulers the "Slave Dynasty" or the "Mamluk Sultanate," terms that appear in modern textbooks and in the title of this chapter. The label can mislead. Aibak, Iltutmish, and Balban were of different origins and belonged to different lineages, so they did not form a single dynasty in the usual sense. The word "slave" should not be confused with plantation or agricultural slavery. This textbook uses "Mamluk" for convenience and "early Sultanate" for precision.

At Mu'izz al-Din's death, Aibak was the senior Ghurid commander in India, but not the only claimant to the sultan's legacy. The Ghurid throne at Firuzkuh passed to Ghiyath al-Din Mahmud, a nephew of the late ruler. Taj al-Din Yildiz, another slave commander, claimed Ghazni and wished to control the Indian possessions. Nasir al-Din Qabacha held Multan and Uch and had married into Aibak's circle. Aibak's accession in 1206 at Lahore, then the most accessible base for contact with the northwest and a city with a long record as a regional capital, was therefore a claim to be asserted, not an inheritance received.

The important distinction is that Aibak did not simply inherit "the Delhi Sultanate." He established one of several successor regimes emerging from the Ghurid political system. Chronicles report that Ghiyath al-Din Mahmud, around 1208, acknowledged Aibak's rule and sent a diploma and emblems of kingship, which also freed him from slave status. If accurate, that recognition shows how much Aibak needed it. A ruler whose authority came from a former master who was now dead required confirmation from the surviving Ghurid house.

Section 40.3

Aibak's Rule: Conquest, Patronage, and Political Legitimacy

Aibak ruled for only about four years, and his reign was shaped by his rivals' pressure. Persian sources report that he attempted to occupy Ghazni in 1208 and was quickly driven out by Yildiz, after which he withdrew to Lahore and abandoned any realistic hope of controlling the Ghurid heartland. That setback encouraged him to concentrate on the Indian territories. Conquest in India continued indirectly through subordinates. Ikhtiyar al-Din Muhammad Bakhtiyar Khalji's campaigns in Bihar and Bengal, which Chapter 39 discussed, were carried out by a commander with considerable autonomy, and after Bakhtiyar's death around 1206 conflict among Khalji nobles in Lakhnauti showed how loosely Bengal was tied to northern India from the start.

Relations with Rajput and other local powers were mixed. Ghurid forces had defeated major Chauhan and Gahadavala rulers, but other rulers survived, and some regained fortresses after the Ghurid defeats elsewhere. Aibak, like other early commanders, used a combination of military pressure, tribute agreements, and recognition of subordinate chiefs. Evidence for the details of such arrangements is thin. They were often local and temporary, and the chronicles' emphasis on dramatic campaigns can obscure them.

Aibak's patronage left more durable traces. He is credited with beginning the Quwwat al-Islam mosque at Delhi, whose construction was already underway in the early 1190s, and the first storey of the Qutb Minar, as well as the mosque known as Adhai Din ka Jhonpra at Ajmer. Section 40.17 examines the material evidence in detail. For the present narrative, the point is that building was political. A congregational mosque in the new center declared the presence of a Muslim ruler, gave the garrison a place of worship, and communicated to subjects that the regime intended to remain. The Persian chronicles also praise Aibak's generosity, and his later reputation for liberality was strong enough that tradition remembered him as a giver of lakhs.

Aibak died in 1210 in Lahore, reportedly after a fall from his horse while playing polo (chaugan). His reign established that a ruler with a base in the Punjab and a garrison in Delhi could claim sovereignty over Ghurid India, but it did not establish a durable system. He left no institutional arrangement for succession and no clear alternative to personal loyalty among commanders.

Section 40.4

The Succession Crisis after Aibak

Aibak's death exposed the structural problem of the early Sultanate. The chroniclers name Aram Shah as his successor, enthroned at Lahore by supporters among the commanders. His relationship to Aibak is unclear: later writers called him a son, and Minhaj's account leaves the matter uncertain, so the relationship remains disputed. Contemporaries seem to have regarded him as weak. He had no significant record of military leadership and no independent following among the ghulams.

The crisis involved several competing centers. Lahore was the seat of Aram Shah and the northwestern nobles. Delhi was the garrison city with its own senior officers, many of whom disliked rule from Lahore. Badaun, held by Iltutmish, was a significant province. Ghazni and Multan stood outside the system but kept their own claims. Military commanders (amirs) were bound to the household of Aibak by personal loyalty, not to a dynasty or a bureaucratic office, and when that household's head died, they had to choose among rival leaders.

It is better to understand this as a structural problem than as a story of weak individuals. Ghurid and Seljuk practice offered several models of succession: designation by the ruler, acclamation by commanders, or victory in civil war. None gave clear guidance for a system of former slaves holding an overseas conquest. The chiefs of Delhi turned to Iltutmish, a respected commander, and invited him to take the city. Iltutmish marched from Badaun, defeated Aram Shah's forces near Jud in 1211, and ended the short Lahore regime. The nobles who chose him acted from interest as well as principle: they wanted a ruler capable of protecting their holdings.

Section 40.5

Iltutmish and the Consolidation of Delhi

Shams al-Din Iltutmish was an Ilbari Turk, born in Central Asia, sold into slavery after his brothers' jealousy (according to the later narrative), and eventually purchased by Aibak. He advanced as a military commander, was given the governorship of Badaun, and married Aibak's daughter. Minhaj praises his piety and capability, and it is difficult to separate his actual qualities from the praise of a court chronicler. What is clear from the record of his policy is that he combined military effectiveness with a talent for institution-building and diplomacy.

His first task was to defeat the rival claimants. Victory over Aram Shah brought Delhi but left the north divided. Many Ghurid-era amirs refused to accept his rule, and he needed to defeat several of them in the early years, some by negotiation and some by force. A more serious challenge came from Taj al-Din Yildiz, who claimed to be Mu'izz al-Din's heir in Ghazni and asserted authority over the Indian territories. When the Khwarazmshah drove Yildiz out of Ghazni, Yildiz moved into the Punjab and seized Lahore. Iltutmish marched against him and defeated and captured him at Tarain in 1216. Yildiz was sent to Badaun, where he was later killed. This victory ended the claim that Ghazni had a right to command the Indian territories.

The second rival was Nasir al-Din Qabacha, who held Multan and Uch and sometimes extended his power to Lahore and Sindh. Qabacha was a resourceful opponent. He survived early defeats, retreated to the lower Indus, and made himself useful by resisting Khwarazmian and then Mongol pressure. Iltutmish continued campaigning against him through the 1220s, and in 1227–1228 he took Uch and besieged Bhakkar, where Qabacha drowned in the Indus (accounts say he threw himself in or died while fleeing). With Qabacha gone, Delhi controlled the Indus corridor from the Punjab to the sea, and for the first time the Delhi ruler's authority was uncontested among the heirs of Mu'izz al-Din.

Iltutmish's success reflected skill with the instruments of rule as much as battlefield victory. He kept the loyalty of his own household of slave commanders by promotion and marriage, placed trusted men in key governorships, and in time brought into the royal household a large body of Turkish slaves who later formed the nucleus of the Forty. He also cultivated the support of scholars, jurists, and religious leaders. Persian authors at his court, including the administrator Muhammad Awfi and the author of a military treatise, Fakhr-i Mudabbir, wrote works addressed to his circle, which give modern historians an unusual glimpse of the political culture of the time.

Section 40.6

Delhi Becomes the Political Center

The gradual shift from Lahore-centered and competing regional bases toward Delhi-centered rule is one of the most important developments of the period, and it was gradual. Aibak ruled from Lahore, and the first sultans needed access to Ghazni and the northwest. By Iltutmish's later reign the principal court was in Delhi, and the city had become the place that other claimants had to take or acknowledge. Students should not imagine that Delhi suddenly became a vast capital in 1211. It was a fortified city of modest size that grew into a center of royal patronage, reservoirs, mosques, markets, and residential quarters over decades. Iltutmish's construction of a large reservoir, the Hauz-i Shamsi, and his extensions to the congregational mosque and the Qutb Minar are part of this growth.

Cause and Effect

Why Delhi Became More Important Than Lahore

Long-term causes

Delhi's location between the Punjab plains, the Ganga-Yamuna doab, and the routes to Rajasthan and Malwa; its earlier role as a fortified Tomara and Chauhan city; and the agricultural and commercial wealth of the doab.

Short-term causes

The collapse of Ghurid and then Khwarazmian power in Afghanistan, which made Lahore a frontier city exposed to every upheaval in the northwest; the invitation of Delhi's nobles to Iltutmish in 1211; and the defeat of Yildiz and Qabacha.

Event or transformation

Iltutmish made Delhi the principal seat of royal authority and the place where recognition, patronage, and military assignments were decided.

Immediate effects

Commanders and scholars migrated toward Delhi; building programs there created a visible royal center; and Lahore became a frontier command, exposed to later Mongol attack.

Long-term consequences: Delhi remained the principal Sultanate capital for most of the following three centuries. Historical complexity: Delhi's rise was not inevitable. It depended on the weakness of rival centers, on Mongol pressure that pushed the political center away from the frontier, and on choices by rulers and nobles who might have chosen differently.

The new center was also a symbolic one. Delhi had been the capital of the defeated Chauhans, and the sultans' presence there signaled continuity as well as conquest. Muslim chroniclers sometimes call it dar al-mulk, "abode of sovereignty," a phrase that implies the idea of a permanent royal seat. Scholars and Sufis displaced by the Mongol conquests in Central Asia and Iran came to Delhi in increasing numbers, and the city began acquiring the cosmopolitan reputation it would hold for centuries.

Section 40.7

Iltutmish, Rival States, and the Expansion of Sultanate Authority

Iltutmish's expansion is best described as uneven incorporation. The sultan's armies moved against specific fortresses, rulers, and rival governors, and the resulting authority varied from direct garrison control to loose tribute. In Rajasthan, campaigns reportedly took Ranthambhor and Mandor in the 1220s, after Chauhan and other Rajput rulers had recovered strength in the unsettled years following Aibak's death. In central India, Iltutmish captured Gwalior after a long siege in 1231–1232. His later campaign into Malwa in 1234–1235 reached Bhilsa and Ujjain, where, according to Minhaj, a major temple was destroyed. Chapter 37 explained how temples were centers of landholding and royal legitimacy, and that context helps explain why rulers on all sides treated such institutions as political targets and political symbols.

Bengal was a separate problem. After Bakhtiyar Khalji's death, Khalji nobles in Lakhnauti fought among themselves, and a commander named Ghiyath al-Din Iwaz Khalji emerged as an independent ruler. Iltutmish marched east around 1225, compelled him to recognize Delhi's overlordship and pay tribute, and withdrew. Iwaz repudiated the arrangement, and Iltutmish's son Nasir al-Din Mahmud, sent as governor, defeated and killed him in the late 1220s. Nasir al-Din Mahmud died in Bengal in 1229, and the region's relations with Delhi remained unstable. The pattern, of conquest, submission, withdrawal, and renewed independence, would repeat for generations.

In the northwest, the defeat of Qabacha brought Multan, Uch, and Sindh under Delhi's authority, but the frontier remained fluid. Local chiefs, Khokhar communities of the Punjab, and Mongol forces all affected how far Delhi's power reached. The sultan's brief for the northwest was less conquest than management of a precarious border zone.

Section 40.8

The Iqta System and the Problem of Governing Territory

How did a sultan pay thousands of cavalry without a modern treasury, banking system, or bureaucracy able to collect revenue directly from every village? The answer in much of the Islamic world, and in the Delhi Sultanate, was the iqta. The institution had precedents in the Buwayhid, Seljuk, Ghaznavid, and Ghurid worlds, and Delhi's rulers adapted it to the conditions of northern India. Evidence for its early operation is incomplete. Detailed descriptions come largely from later writers, and the system took clearer form under later dynasties, so historians reconstruct the thirteenth-century iqta partly from chronicle references and partly by inference.

Historical Vocabulary
Iqta and Muqti

An iqta was an assignment of the right to collect revenue from a specified district or set of villages, given by the ruler to an officer (a muqti, sometimes called an iqtadar) in return for service, principally the maintenance of troops. The iqta was an assignment of income, not a grant of ownership. The land itself remained under the cultivators and the existing intermediaries, and the ruler retained sovereignty. Assignments were generally revocable and subject to transfer.

The word "feudal" is often applied to the iqta by comparison with medieval Europe, but the comparison is misleading. The muqti did not own land, did not usually hold it hereditarily, did not exercise a general private jurisdiction over a peasantry, and was in principle accountable to the central government. Land held directly by the crown was called khalisa. Chapter 37 discusses the wider debate over "feudalism" in Indian history.

In practice the system worked as follows. The ruler assigned a muqti a district whose estimated revenue was meant to cover his salary and the cost of a specified number of troops. The muqti collected revenue, usually through local intermediaries such as village headmen and hereditary revenue collectors, kept what he needed, and was expected to send any surplus to the center. He also maintained order, protected roads, and led his contingent when called on. Because military obligation and revenue collection were bound together, the iqta served as a combined fiscal, military, and administrative instrument.

Its benefits and risks were the same thing. The system let rulers keep an army in the field without a large central treasury, and it extended the reach of Delhi into regions that the court could not administer directly. But it also gave commanders a local base of income, troops, and patronage, which they could use to resist the center. Transferring muqtis from one assignment to another was a major tool of control, because a commander moved away from the region where he had built local ties lost leverage. The tension between assignment as service and assignment as local power runs through the entire history of the Sultanate. The system also did not mean the end of earlier local authority: village headmen, hereditary chiefs, and revenue-collecting intermediaries continued to operate, and the muqti depended on their cooperation.

Institutional Workshop

How an Iqta Worked: A Reconstructed Model

The following sequence is a simplified model reconstructed by historians from chronicles and later administrative writing. It is not a quotation from a thirteenth-century document, and real arrangements varied by place and period.

  1. Assignment. The sultan assigns a district to an officer, with an expectation of how many cavalry he must maintain.
  2. Assessment. The district's revenue is estimated; local intermediaries and village officials provide information about the harvest and the land.
  3. Collection. Revenue, mainly in grain and cash, is collected from cultivators, often through village headmen, and converted to cash where markets allow.
  4. Maintenance. The muqti pays his troops and himself from the revenue; any surplus is sent to the center.
  5. Accountability. The court may inspect the muqti's troops and accounts, and officials report on his conduct.
  6. Transfer. The sultan can reassign or remove the muqti, which limits the creation of hereditary local power but also breeds resentment.

Section 40.9

Sultan, Court, Ulama, and the Sources of Legitimacy

Military success explains how Aibak and Iltutmish took power, but not how they kept it. Legitimacy, a ruler's claim to rightful authority, was built from several materials. Military capacity mattered because commanders followed those who could win and pay. Dynastic claims mattered because Iltutmish married into Aibak's family and later designated his own children as heirs. Islamic political traditions provided titles, rituals, and ideals of just government. Patronage, revenue, and recognition from other rulers completed the structure. None of these sufficed alone, and religion was only one part of the whole.

The ritual markers of sovereignty in the Islamic world were the khutba, the sermon at Friday congregational prayer in which the ruler's name is mentioned, and the sikka, the right to mint coins in his name. Aibak and Iltutmish used both. A further source of authority was the caliph, the Abbasid ruler of Baghdad, who by the thirteenth century had little military power but still conferred symbolic prestige on rulers. In 1229 envoys of the caliph al-Mustansir arrived in Delhi with a diploma and robe of honor recognizing Iltutmish as sultan. Iltutmish then put the caliph's name on his coins. The recognition came after Iltutmish had already won every important military contest, which suggests it confirmed a position he had taken by force. It nonetheless mattered, because it gave him a claim few of his rivals could match.

Historical Vocabulary
Khutba and Sikka

The khutba is the sermon delivered at Friday congregational prayers. Mentioning a ruler's name in it publicly acknowledged him as sovereign, and omitting a name or substituting another was a political act. The sikka ("coin," "minting") refers to the sovereign's right to strike coins bearing his name and titles. Together, "khutba and sikka" were the two classic marks of Islamic sovereignty. Coin inscriptions and surviving monument inscriptions allow historians to see exactly what titles rulers claimed, and they sometimes differ from what chroniclers say.

The ulama, the religious scholars, jurists, and judges, played several roles. They served as qadis (judges), preachers, teachers, and advisers, and they could legitimate a ruler by praising him or damage him by withholding support. The relationship was negotiated. Some scholars were close to the court and benefited from patronage. Others, including many Sufi leaders, kept deliberate distance from rulers. The sultans needed the ulama's support, but they did not govern according to a fixed code that scholars administered. The population of the Sultanate was overwhelmingly non-Muslim, which meant that rulers governed as Muslim sovereigns over a majority population with different religious traditions. Sultanate rulers worked out practical relationships with that population: collecting tax, protecting traders, and leaving most village and caste institutions in place. Chapter 44 examines these arrangements further.

It would be wrong to reduce Sultanate legitimacy to religion alone. Contemporary writers praised sultans for justice, generosity, protection of the weak, and success in war, qualities drawn from long traditions of Persian and Islamic kingship. Kingship was explained in the language of "mirrors for princes" and in treatises like the one Fakhr-i Mudabbir wrote for Iltutmish. These texts stress that a ruler's strength lies in a strong army, a full treasury, and just rule. The sultan's authority rested on all of these together.

Section 40.10

Iltutmish and the Mongol World

The Mongol empire entered South Asian history indirectly. Chinggis Khan's armies destroyed the Khwarazmian state in Central Asia and Iran between 1219 and 1221, and the last Khwarazmian prince, Jalal al-Din Mangburni, fled southeast with a shrinking army. In late 1221 Chinggis Khan caught him on the banks of the Indus and defeated him, and Jalal al-Din escaped across the river. The Mongol ruler did not himself cross into India, and he withdrew from the region within a year or two. The event nevertheless transformed the strategic environment of the northwest: refugees, displaced soldiers, scholars, and officials streamed toward the Indus, and a new frontier power now stood immediately beyond it.

Jalal al-Din remained in the Punjab and Sindh for about two years. He sent an envoy to Iltutmish asking for refuge and an alliance. Iltutmish declined. Persian sources record that he refused politely, citing the unsuitable climate or the lack of a suitable place, and in effect denied the Khwarazmian ruler a base. The decision was prudent. A Khwarazmian army in the Punjab would have made the Sultanate's territory a battlefield and would have invited Mongol retaliation. Jalal al-Din raided Lahore's hinterland, allied with local Khokhar chiefs, defeated forces of Qabacha, and then left for Iran in 1224. The episode weakened Qabacha, which indirectly helped Iltutmish in the following years.

The point is not that the Mongols "attacked Delhi" during Iltutmish's reign. They did not. The point is that Mongol expansion shaped everything the sultan did in the northwest. It altered the balance among local powers, brought refugees who strengthened Delhi's court and army, and made the Indus frontier a permanent concern. Iltutmish avoided direct confrontation, relied on diplomacy and on the buffer provided by Qabacha and the Khokhar territory, and focused on consolidation in the Indian interior. Historians debate how far this was a deliberate strategy and how far it was good fortune, since the Mongols' attention was on Iran and Central Asia in these decades.

Section 40.11

Raziyya Sultan and the Politics of Succession

Iltutmish died in April 1236. Minhaj reports that, some years earlier, the sultan had designated his daughter Raziyya as heir, apparently judging her more capable than his surviving sons; the claim comes from a court chronicler writing under later rulers, so it must be read with caution. The nobles did not follow the designation. They placed on the throne Iltutmish's son Rukn al-Din Firuz, whose mother, Shah Turkan, was a powerful figure at court. Rukn al-Din devoted himself to pleasure, according to the chroniclers, and Shah Turkan's partisans acted cruelly against rivals. Within months, provincial governors rebelled, and the people of Delhi and a group of nobles, with Raziyya's own appeal to the populace at a Friday gathering according to later accounts, deposed him in late 1236 and enthroned Raziyya.

Raziyya's reign lasted until 1240, and her rule was unusual. She issued coins in her own name with a title that identified her as the daughter of Iltutmish. According to later writers, she abandoned female dress, held open court, and rode out in public. She faced revolts from provincial governors who refused to recognize her, including commanders in Multan, Lahore, and elsewhere, and she met some with force and others with negotiation. Her attempt to build an independent political coalition led her to promote people outside the established Turkish elite, most famously Jamal al-Din Yaqut, an African-origin (Abyssinian) officer whom Minhaj says she made master of the horse. The Turkish amirs resented the rise of an outsider, and chroniclers hint at personal intimacy; modern historians regard those insinuations as hostile rumor that cannot be verified.

In 1240 Ikhtiyar al-Din Altunia, governor of Tabarhind (Bhatinda), rebelled. Raziyya marched against him, Turkish nobles killed Yaqut, and Altunia imprisoned her. Meanwhile the nobles in Delhi enthroned her half-brother Mu'izz al-Din Bahram. Raziyya then married Altunia, and the two marched on Delhi, were defeated, and fled; Raziyya was killed in October 1240, reportedly by local people near Kaithal. The location of her tomb remains disputed, and several sites claim it.

It would be a mistake to read her fall as simply misogyny, or to celebrate it as a story of the "first woman ruler." Both readings miss the structural question: could the Sultanate's political institutions accept a ruler chosen for capability rather than established male dynastic expectation? The answer was no, but not only because of gender. Raziyya threatened the interests of a military elite whose members had built their positions under Iltutmish and who expected to control the throne through a pliable ruler. She tried to build a coalition around her that bypassed them. Her femaleness provided the language of opposition, and Minhaj's own account, which praises her abilities while lamenting that she was not a man, reflects the assumptions of the period. But provincial governors who rebelled against her had also rebelled against male rulers. Coalition-building, military patronage, and succession politics were all involved.

Section 40.12

The Sultans after Raziyya: Fragmentation and Elite Politics

The decades between Raziyya and Balban are often skipped in summaries, but they show what the Sultanate looked like when the throne was controlled by its own military elite. Mu'izz al-Din Bahram (1240–1242) was enthroned by Turkish nobles on terms that limited his authority. Chroniclers report that in his reign the office of naib, deputy or regent, was created, giving a senior commander control of the state's business. The first holder reportedly overreached and was killed, and Bahram himself was soon deposed. In December 1241 a Mongol force sacked Lahore, which helped destroy confidence in a ruler who could not defend the frontier.

Ala al-Din Masud, a grandson of Iltutmish, reigned from 1242 to 1246 amid factional struggle. He was removed by nobles who turned to another grandson, Nasir al-Din Mahmud, who held the throne from 1246 to 1266. Later tradition portrayed Mahmud as a pious, modest ruler who lived simply and copied the Quran; the story reflects hagiographic tendencies, and Minhaj dedicated his history to him. The more important fact is that the real management of the state passed to his deputy, Ulugh Khan, the future Balban.

This is a story of elite politics more than of weak individuals. Commanders held iqtas, controlled troops, and formed alliances through marriage and patronage. They put rulers on the throne and removed them when those rulers threatened their interests. The pattern was not unique to Delhi. Slave-military regimes elsewhere in the Islamic world, including the Mamluks of Egypt, faced similar conflicts. Rivalry between Turkish commanders and others who competed for power also played out in this period; around 1253 a group opposed to Balban briefly displaced him in favor of Imad al-Din Raihan, an Indian-born Muslim noble, and Balban returned to office in the next year or two. This episode shows that the political divisions were not simply "Turkish versus Indian" but involved shifting factions.

Section 40.13

Balban and the Reconstruction of Monarchical Authority

Ghiyath al-Din Balban was, like Iltutmish, an Ilbari Turk who had been enslaved, sold in Baghdad according to Barani, and eventually bought by Iltutmish. He rose through service as a cupbearer, a hunt official, and then a provincial and military commander. He married his daughter to Nasir al-Din Mahmud, and he acted as the sultan's deputy for about twenty years, using that position to build a network of followers. When Mahmud died in 1266, Balban took the throne.

Barani, our fullest source for Balban's reign, presents him as a ruler with a clear political ideology. According to Barani, Balban held that the king was God's representative on earth, the "shadow of God" (zill-i ilahi), and that the dignity of the monarchy required distance from ordinary people. He instituted ritual forms such as prostration (sijda) and kissing the ruler's feet (paibos), adopted Persian court customs including the celebration of Nauruz, and claimed descent from Afrasiyab, the legendary king of Turan. He reportedly refused to appoint men of low birth to important offices and cut his own public appearances. These details come from a fourteenth-century author with strong views about the proper hierarchy of society, so they must be handled critically. Still, the broad direction is credible: Balban wanted to raise the monarchy above the nobles who had made and unmade sultans.

His methods were practical as well as ceremonial. He built a network of intelligence officers (barids) to report on nobles and governors. He disciplined and reduced the influence of senior nobles, including, according to Barani, by having his own cousin Sher Khan, governor of the northwestern frontier, poisoned around 1270. He reorganized the army, reviewing and supporting aging troops and replenishing the cavalry. He cleared forests and suppressed brigand communities in Mewat and the doab, built forts at places such as Bhojpur, Patiali, and Kampil, and settled Afghan garrisons to secure the roads near Delhi. In 1279 a Delhi governor of Bengal, Tughril, rebelled and declared independence; Balban marched east with a large force, defeated the rebels, and punished them severely. He then installed his son Bughra Khan as governor of Bengal.

The reconstruction of monarchical authority was real but not absolute in the modern sense. Balban still depended on commanders and on revenue assignments, and his power rested on his personal energy and the army he commanded. His achievement was more political than institutional. The idea of kingship he articulated, and the practices that supported it, offered later sultans a model, and Barani's account made him a reference point for later debates about the proper conduct of kings.

Section 40.14

The Forty, the Nobility, and the Limits of Central Power

Later chroniclers, especially Barani, describe a body of leading Turkish nobles, "the Forty" (Chihilgani), who were said to be Iltutmish's most trusted slave commanders and who controlled the government in the years after his death. Barani claims that Balban eventually broke their power. The picture has been influential, but it needs qualifying. The sources do not show the Forty as a formal constitutional body with fixed membership, offices, or legal powers. The term suggests a leading circle of the military elite whose size and composition changed, and some historians doubt that it ever described a distinct, stable institution as Barani implied. The number forty may be conventional.

The underlying problem was real. A sultan needed powerful military commanders in order to rule: they led troops, held iqtas, defended frontiers, and gave the regime its coercive capacity. Yet the same commanders could threaten the sultan, and the sources show repeated cases of nobles deposing, killing, or controlling rulers. A ruler could try to limit them by promoting rivals, rotating assignments, using spies, or building a household of slaves loyal to himself personally. But each method carried risks. The tension between the sultan's need for strong subordinates and his fear of them runs through the entire early Sultanate.

Historical Debate

Was the Early Delhi Sultanate Centralized or Negotiated?

The question: Did the early sultans of Delhi build a centralized monarchy governed from the capital, or did they govern through continuous bargaining with military and local elites?

Interpretation A: Increasing centralization. Older and some more recent accounts, drawing on Barani and Minhaj, emphasize the growth of royal authority: titles and coins asserting sovereignty, recognition by the caliph, the extension of iqta administration, Iltutmish's military victories, and Balban's disciplining of the nobles. On this view, the period was one of progressive centralization culminating in a strong monarchy.
Interpretation B: A negotiated, segmentary political system. Other historians stress that sultans depended on military commanders who held their own resources, that provincial governors repeatedly rebelled, that Bengal and other regions escaped control, and that rulers worked through local intermediaries who retained their own authority. On this view, royal power was always conditional on bargaining, and what looks like centralization in the chronicles reflects court ideology more than administrative reality.

Evidence: Both interpretations use the same sources. Chronicles describe royal commands and campaigns, which support B's emphasis on repeated rebellion but also A's emphasis on royal victories. Coins and inscriptions show claims to sovereignty, but claims are not the same as administrative reach. Detailed revenue and administrative records from the thirteenth century are largely missing, which makes any strong claim about institutional centralization difficult to test.

Why it matters: The answer affects how historians understand later developments. If the early Sultanate was already centralized, later Khalji and Tughlaq measures look like extensions. If it was negotiated, they look like attempts to change the system. Most current scholarship accepts elements of both: strong royal ideology and real military successes combined with a political system that remained dependent on negotiation.

Section 40.15

Mongol Pressure and the Northwestern Frontier under Balban

By Balban's reign the Mongol empire was divided into several branches. Mongol forces on the Indus were associated with commanders based in Afghanistan and the Punjab borderlands, and the Ilkhans of Iran, the Chagatayid rulers of Central Asia, and the independent Mongol commanders of the frontier each had different interests. For the Sultanate, the result was a recurring threat from the northwest. Mongol raids reached Lahore and the lands between the Indus and the Beas in several years, and in 1241 Lahore had been sacked. Chroniclers report diplomatic contacts with the Ilkhanids as well as warfare.

Balban's response combined fortification, deployment, and diplomacy. He strengthened and garrisoned forts along the frontier, notably in the Punjab, and kept some of the best troops in the northwest. He appointed capable commanders to Multan, Lahore, Dipalpur, Sunam, and Bhatinda, and he sometimes held back from distant campaigns because the frontier required his attention. Multan and Lahore functioned as frontier commands, and Sindh and the Punjab were governed with an eye to Mongol movements. The strategy was defensive: avoid decisive pitched battle when possible, hold fortified cities, and make raids costly.

The costs were high. Around 1285, Balban's eldest son and designated heir, Prince Muhammad, governor of Multan, was killed in fighting against a Mongol force. The poet Amir Khusrau, who had been in the prince's service and was briefly a captive of the Mongols, wrote elegies for him. Balban reportedly never recovered from the loss and died in 1287. The episode shows that frontier defense consumed enormous political and military resources, including the lives of princes, even though the Sultanate survived.

Mongol Frontier Study

Why the Sultanate Survived While Other Central Asian States Collapsed

Historians have proposed several reasons, none sufficient alone:

  • Distance and geography: The Indus, the Punjab rivers, the Thar Desert, and the monsoon climate made sustained Mongol campaigns in the subcontinent costly for steppe cavalry.
  • Mongol priorities: Mongol rulers focused on Iran, Iraq, and Central Asia, and internal divisions within the empire reduced the force available for India.
  • Defensive organization: Fortified frontier towns, large cavalry forces supported by iqta revenue, and capable frontier commanders raised the cost of invasion.
  • Resources: The agricultural wealth of the Punjab and doab allowed the sultans to pay for defense.
  • Diplomacy and refugees: Iltutmish's decision in the 1220s to avoid entanglement and later contacts with Mongol powers limited conflict, and refugees strengthened Delhi's court and army.

Section 40.16

Society, Revenue, and Everyday Life under Early Sultanate Rule

Political narratives can hide the fact that the Sultanate governed a huge, varied population. Most people were cultivators living in villages whose headmen, hereditary revenue-collectors, and caste or community councils continued to operate. The Sultanate was a state placed over an existing society and drawing revenue from it, not a system that replaced it wholesale. Muslim rulers and their military elites formed a small minority; most inhabitants, including many of the new rulers' own subjects in the cities, were Hindu, Jain, Buddhist, or followers of local traditions. Chapter 44 examines conversion, Sufi communities, and cultural synthesis in more depth.

Everyday Life and Social History

Life in a Sultanate Village, Town, and Camp

Cultivators paid land revenue, usually a share of the crop converted into grain or cash, through village headmen and intermediaries. The share varied by region, and the burden was heaviest when muqtis demanded more than custom allowed or when armies marched through. Cultivators could flee, resist, or appeal, but their leverage depended on local conditions. Evidence for rural life in this period is thin, and most surviving sources describe it from the perspective of the court.

Merchants and artisans lived in towns and along trade routes. Delhi's growth attracted weavers, metalworkers, builders, and traders. Coinage became more standardized, which assisted commerce, and the military demand for horses, weapons, and supplies stimulated trade with Central Asia. Merchant guilds and caste-based occupational groups continued, and Hindu bankers and traders served as lenders and financiers to officials and soldiers.

Soldiers and military households formed a mobile population. Turkish commanders, Afghan and other troops, and local recruits moved between iqtas and campaigns, and the system of assignment meant that elites did not necessarily settle permanently in one place. Slaves and dependent laborers worked in households, courts, workshops, and construction. Captives taken in war could become slaves, and slave markets supplied courts and armies. Evidence for the numbers and conditions of dependent labor is limited.

Religious communities continued their institutions, including temples, monasteries, and shrines. Some temples were destroyed or converted during conquest, as in Ujjain, and others continued with revenue support or local arrangements. Sufi lodges (khanqahs) began to appear in the Punjab and around Delhi, and figures such as Baba Farid and Qutb al-Din Bakhtiyar Kaki were associated with this period. Their growing influence among populations of all backgrounds is a theme of Chapters 44 and 48.

Taxation was the point at which the state and rural society most directly met. Evidence suggests that the sultans generally accepted existing revenue practices, relying on local intermediaries and the hereditary chiefs often called rais or rana in the sources, and that military pressure sometimes forced these chiefs to pay more. Resistance by such chiefs and by rural communities was a recurring reason for the campaigns Balban conducted in Mewat and the doab. The sources describe these as the suppression of brigands and rebels, a perspective that reflects the viewpoint of the state; the people concerned would probably have described their actions differently, but we lack their testimony.

Section 40.17

Mosques, Tombs, Coins, and the Material Language of Sultanate Power

Rulers make authority visible. At Delhi, the earliest sultans did so through stone and metal. The Quwwat al-Islam ("Might of Islam") mosque, begun in the early 1190s under Aibak, was built partly from the columns and carved stones of earlier Hindu and Jain temples, a practice called spolia. Iltutmish extended the mosque with a larger screen of arches around 1230. The adjacent Qutb Minar, a victory tower or minaret whose lower storey Aibak began, was raised by Iltutmish, who completed several storeys, with later repairs and additions by other rulers; bands of Arabic inscription and Quranic verses decorate its shafts. Iltutmish's own tomb, built around 1235, and the tomb known as Sultan Ghari, built in 1231 for his son Nasir al-Din Mahmud and often described as the earliest surviving Islamic tomb in India, belong to the same program. At Ajmer, the Adhai Din ka Jhonpra mosque also used reused materials and received a screen attributed to Iltutmish's time.

Coinage made sovereignty portable. Ghurid and early Delhi issues combined existing South Asian coin traditions, including designs used in the region before the conquest, with new Islamic inscriptions. Iltutmish is generally credited with helping to standardize a silver tanka and a copper jital with inscriptions in Arabic naming the sultan and the Abbasid caliph. Raziyya's coins in her own name survive and are among the clearest evidence of her claim to sovereignty. Such coins show what titles rulers wanted subjects to see, and mint locations and metal content help historians trace the reach of royal authority and monetary exchange.

The date given in the inscription corresponds to about 1191–1192, which sits awkwardly with the capture of Delhi in 1192–1193 and is one reason scholars discuss when the inscription was composed. Because I have not reproduced the text here, the following is a paraphrase and not a translation.

Paraphrase: The inscription announces the building of a congregational mosque by Aibak, associates it with the Ghurid ruler and with the capture of the fortress, and records that the materials of a number of temples, traditionally read as twenty-seven, were used in its construction.

Analysis: The inscription is contemporary or near-contemporary evidence, unlike the later chronicles. It shows that the builders wanted the reuse of temple materials to be visible and recorded as a sign of conquest. It cannot tell us how Delhi's inhabitants responded, how many temples were affected, what each temple was, or whether all the reused stones came from demolished buildings. Historians disagree about how to interpret spolia: as triumphal display, as practical use of available materials, or as a blend of both and as part of a wider practice among medieval builders in many regions. The surrounding Arabic and Quranic inscriptions, carved by local craftsmen using local decorative vocabulary, also show cultural mixing.

Section 40.18

Consolidation without Uniform Control

By the late thirteenth century, what had actually been accomplished? The answer is mixed. Delhi had become the principal political center of the north. A recognizable royal court, with ceremonial, officials, and a corps of military households, existed. Revenue assignments and military administration had become institutionalized, and the monarchy had developed stronger claims to sovereignty through the khutba, coinage, caliphal recognition, and court ritual. A network of provincial authorities, including muqtis in the doab, Punjab, Multan, and elsewhere, connected the capital to substantial territories. The Sultanate had survived repeated succession crises and sustained Mongol pressure.

The limitations were also real. Provincial rebellion remained common, as Bengal's repeated revolts showed. Succession remained unstable; Balban's death in 1287 would soon produce another crisis, which Chapter 41 begins to explore. The center depended heavily on military elites. Bengal and other distant regions could escape effective control, and territorial authority varied enormously from the doab, where Delhi's power was strongest, to Rajasthan and central India, where it was intermittent. The state did not possess a modern centralized bureaucracy; it worked through personal relationships, assignments, and local intermediaries. And Sultanate rule did not erase preexisting Hindu, Buddhist, Jain, and regional political institutions; many continued under the sultans' overlordship, and some regional powers remained independent.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Political Institutions before and after 1206

What Changed

  • Delhi became the seat of a Muslim-ruled sultanate claiming sovereignty over a wide region of northern India
  • Ruling elites included Turkish slave commanders and, later, Afghan and other groups, alongside Indian-born Muslims
  • Revenue assignment through the iqta, with Persian as the language of administration and court
  • New architectural forms, including congregational mosques, minarets, and tombs, and new coin types with Arabic inscriptions
  • Growing arrival of scholars, Sufis, and soldiers from Central Asia and Iran

What Stayed the Same

  • Most of the population were cultivators living in villages under hereditary headmen and caste and community institutions
  • Local chiefs and intermediaries continued to collect revenue and exercise local authority
  • Trade routes, merchant communities, and artisan traditions persisted
  • Temples, monasteries, and Hindu and Jain religious life continued in many regions
  • Warfare among regional rulers remained a constant feature

Who experienced change most directly? Residents of garrison towns and conquered cities, rulers and officials displaced by conquest, and communities whose temples or lands were affected. Who experienced greater continuity? Most villagers, whose lives continued to be shaped by the agricultural cycle and local authority. Why the pattern matters: It prevents both the idea of total rupture and the idea that nothing changed.

Section 40.19

Review and Historical Synthesis

The central question of this chapter is how the early Delhi sultans transformed the military networks inherited from the Ghurids into a durable political system, and why that system remained both powerful and deeply dependent on negotiation with regional elites. The answer follows a chain of connected developments: conquest led to Delhi; Delhi's possession raised the problem of succession; succession crises forced commanders and rulers to build institutions; the iqta tied military service to revenue; reliance on military elites created both strength and danger; legitimacy had to be constructed from several sources; Mongol pressure imposed a constant strategic constraint; and over several generations these pressures produced institutional consolidation.

Aibak showed that a Ghurid commander could claim independent rule in India, but he held a fragile position from Lahore. The crisis after his death exposed the weakness of a regime based on personal loyalty. Iltutmish resolved that crisis by defeating rivals, moving the center of gravity to Delhi, securing the caliph's recognition, and building the iqta and the household of slave commanders that supported the regime. His achievement was real but incomplete, because he left no secure rule for succession. Raziyya's reign showed what happened when a ruler tried to build a coalition outside the dominant military elite. The decades after her reign showed how the elite could control rulers, and Balban's rise showed how one member of that elite could eventually reassert royal authority.

Balban's monarchy rested on ideology, ritual, and force. It strengthened the position of the sultan, defended the frontier against the Mongols, and suppressed rebellion, but it did not abolish the underlying dependence on commanders and local intermediaries. The Sultanate in 1287 was stronger than in 1206 in its institutions and in the claims of its monarchy, yet it remained a negotiated, regionally uneven political system. Its society continued to rest on villages and intermediaries that predated the conquest, and its culture was becoming increasingly Persianate and cosmopolitan.

Chapter 41 now turns to the Khalji revolution of 1290 and the dynasty's much larger territorial expansion into Gujarat, Malwa, Rajasthan, and the Deccan, in which Khalji rulers transformed the institutions this chapter has traced. Students should carry forward the main lesson of this chapter: states are built over generations through the slow conversion of military power into institutions, and the process leaves visible tensions that later rulers must manage.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that the Delhi Sultanate was not created fully formed in 1206. The assassination of Mu'izz al-Din Muhammad of Ghor left a network of commanders, garrisons, and revenue claims, but no settled successor and no unified administration. Qutb al-Din Aibak established an independent base at Lahore, with Delhi as a garrison city, and sought recognition from the Ghurid house. He was one of several successor rulers, not the founder of an inevitable empire.

Aibak's death in 1210 produced a succession crisis that revealed the structural weakness of a regime based on personal loyalty among slave commanders. Competing centers at Lahore, Delhi, Badaun, Ghazni, and Multan reflected competing claims. The nobles of Delhi invited Iltutmish to take the city, and his victory over Aram Shah in 1211 shifted the balance toward Delhi.

Iltutmish consolidated the state by defeating Yildiz and Qabacha, moving the political center to Delhi, securing the caliph's recognition in 1229, issuing standardized coinage, and extending iqta assignments. His campaigns into Rajasthan, central India, and Bengal produced uneven incorporation, with some regions held by garrisons and others merely tributary. The decision to refuse refuge to the Khwarazmian prince Mangburni kept Mongol warfare away from Indian soil in his reign.

The iqta, an assignment of revenue in return for military service, was the key institution of early Sultanate administration. It was not private land ownership and not feudalism in the European sense. It let the sultan support an army and extend his reach, but it also gave commanders a local base from which to resist the center. Transfers and surveillance were the main means of control.

Legitimacy rested on several foundations: military success, dynastic claims, Islamic political traditions expressed through the khutba and sikka, caliphal recognition, patronage of scholars and builders, and court ritual. Religion was one element among many, and the Sultanate governed an overwhelmingly non-Muslim population through a combination of military force, local intermediaries, and accommodation.

Raziyya's reign from 1236 to 1240 exposed the tension between dynastic succession and the interests of the military elite. Her fall involved gender but also coalition-building, military patronage, and succession politics. The decades after her reign saw rapid turnover of rulers, the creation of the office of naib, and the rise of Balban, who held effective power from 1246 and became sultan in 1266.

Balban strengthened the monarchy through elaborate court ritual, an ideology of kingship, an intelligence network, suppression of rebellion, military reorganization, and fortification. Mongol pressure on the northwestern frontier consumed enormous resources, including the life of his son Prince Muhammad. The problem of the Forty, the elite on whom the sultan depended and whom he feared, was never solved institutionally.

Evidence for the period includes Persian chronicles such as Minhaj's contemporary history and Barani's later account, along with coins, inscriptions, and monuments including the Quwwat al-Islam mosque, the Qutb Minar, and Iltutmish's tomb. Each source has limits: chronicles reflect court perspectives, and monuments cannot tell us how ordinary people responded.

By 1287, Delhi was the principal center of Muslim-ruled authority in northern India, with an established court, military administration, and royal ideology. Yet the system remained decentralized, contested, and dependent on negotiation. Chapter 41 examines how the Khaljis transformed it through much larger expansion.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a state is made. The Delhi Sultanate did not appear by proclamation. It was built across eight decades by rulers who had to solve, repeatedly, the problems of succession, revenue, loyalty, legitimacy, and defense. The solutions they developed, including the iqta, court ritual, caliphal recognition, and a fortified frontier, shaped the Sultanate for the next century.

The chapter also reveals a central tension that will recur: the need for powerful military commanders and the danger they posed. Later dynasties would respond differently, the Khaljis through expansion and market controls, the Tughlaqs through ambitious experiments, and the Lodis through a different understanding of kingship among Afghan nobles. Seeing the early Sultanate as negotiated rather than simply centralized helps make sense of those later efforts.

Finally, the chapter connects South Asia to a wider world. Turkish soldiers, Persian scholars, Sufi teachers, and Mongol armies all shaped the Sultanate's formation, while the villagers, merchants, and local chiefs of northern India gave it its economic base and much of its social character. The Sultanate was neither an alien imposition nor a simple continuation of earlier states. It was a new political formation built from many materials.

Chapter 41 now turns to the Khaljis, whose revolution of 1290 and sweeping expansion tested whether the institutions built in these decades could support a far larger empire.
