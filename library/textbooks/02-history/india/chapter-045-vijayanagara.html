Vijayanagara

Indian History: South Asia from the Indus Civilization to the Present

Chapter Opening

On the southern bank of the Tungabhadra River, among hills of tumbled granite boulders, a visitor today walks along a long bazaar street toward the gateway of a temple that was already venerated before the city around it existed. Beyond it lie ruined palace platforms, stepped tanks, canals, and the stone halls of temples whose pillars still ring when struck. This was the heart of Vijayanagara, the "City of Victory," for roughly two centuries the capital of the largest state in peninsular South Asia.

Chapters 41 through 44 followed the Delhi Sultanate as it expanded across northern India and into the Deccan, reaching its greatest extent under the Khaljis and Tughlaqs and then fragmenting. This chapter follows the other side of that story. In the aftermath of Delhi's southern campaigns, a new kingdom arose on the Tungabhadra, conventionally dated to 1336, and over the following generations became an empire controlling much of the territory south of the Krishna River. Its rulers made war on the Madurai Sultanate and the Bahmani Sultanate, built canals and monumental temples, patronized poets writing in four languages, and traded horses and textiles with merchants from the Persian Gulf to Southeast Asia and, after 1498, with Portuguese newcomers.

The chapter treats Vijayanagara as a functioning political and social system rather than merely a sequence of kings and battles. It asks how the kingdom emerged amid the collapse of older dynasties; how it organized armies and revenue through the nayaka system; how kings balanced ritual authority, military force, and local negotiation; how agriculture, crafts, and commerce supported a great urban region; and how religion and culture shaped imperial identity. It also asks why the empire's institutions survived the catastrophic defeat at Talikota in 1565, and why the empire's heirs, the Aravidu kings and the regional Nayaka states, carried its traditions into the seventeenth century.

Two cautions run throughout. First, Vijayanagara should not be reduced to a story of "Hindu empire against Muslim sultanates." Its rulers used Hindu royal ideology and patronized temples, but they also employed Muslim soldiers, adopted elements of Persianate court culture, and formed alliances across religious lines, while their rivals did likewise. Second, much of what the sources say about the empire comes from interested parties: court poets, temple donors, foreign visitors, and rival chroniclers. A central task of the historian is to compare these sources critically.

The evidence is unusually varied: thousands of inscriptions, the surviving architecture and urban remains at Hampi, literary works in Sanskrit, Telugu, Kannada, and Tamil, the travel accounts of visitors from Italy, Herat, and Portugal, and Persian chronicles from the sultanates. Each type of evidence reveals different dimensions of the empire, and each requires careful interpretation. The sections below are organized in five divisions: foundation and early expansion; warfare and dynastic change; government, economy, and society; Hampi, religion, and culture; and international connections and the crisis of the sixteenth century.

Chapter 45 connects directly to its neighbors. Chapter 46 examines the Bahmani and Deccan sultanates, whose conflict with Vijayanagara is treated here from the Vijayanagara side. Chapter 48 examines devotional movements across South Asia, and Chapter 49 the Indian Ocean and the Portuguese. This chapter limits its coverage of those subjects to what is needed for Vijayanagara's own history.

Historical Context

Volume II has so far followed the post-Gupta regional kingdoms of the Deccan, the Tamil country, the Ganges plain, and the northwest, the Muslim communities and states of South Asia, and the successive dynasties of the Delhi Sultanate. By the early fourteenth century Delhi's armies were reaching the far south in raids and campaigns, and Delhi's governors had become embedded in the Deccan.

The peninsula in this period was a land of regional kingdoms, temple-centered societies, and productive agricultural zones separated by dry uplands. The Tungabhadra marked a zone of transition between the northern Deccan and the south, and the Raichur Doab was one of the most contested districts of the peninsula. The monsoon-fed west coast, the black-soil cotton plains, the rice-growing Kaveri basin, and the Telugu delta country offered different economic bases, and long-distance trade linked them to the Indian Ocean.

Historians of Vijayanagara rely on an unusually large inscriptional corpus, supplemented by architecture, literature, travelers, and chronicles. Because many sources come from donors or courts, they emphasize royal and elite action. Students should keep in view the farmers, artisans, soldiers, and laborers whose work sustained the state but who appear mainly indirectly.

Section 45.1

Vijayanagara: Geography, Historical Sources, and the Problem of Reconstruction

Vijayanagara — "City of Victory" — was both a place and a political system. As a place, it was an urban and sacred landscape on the southern bank of the Tungabhadra River in what is now central Karnataka, among boulder-strewn granite hills that offered natural defenses, building stone, and a river that could be tapped for irrigation. As a political system, it was an imperial state that, between the fourteenth and seventeenth centuries, claimed authority over much of peninsular South Asia south of the Krishna River and, at its height, beyond it. The name was applied to the city, the kingdom, and the line of kings who ruled from it, and this chapter uses it for all three while distinguishing them where the distinction matters.

The Tungabhadra setting was not accidental. The river separates the Deccan plateau's cotton-and-millet country to the north from the more rainfall-rich lands to the south and west, and the surrounding hills lay near older centers of regional power, including the sacred site of Pampa, long associated with the god Virupaksha, and the fort of Anegondi across the river. The location commanded routes toward the Konkan and Malabar ports, toward the Telugu-speaking east, and toward the Tamil plains, and it stood close to the Raichur Doab, the fertile land between the Krishna and Tungabhadra that would be contested for two centuries. The regional geography introduced at the start of this series (Chapter 1) — rivers, plateaus, and monsoon zones — explains why a state built on the junction of several such zones could draw on very different agricultural and commercial resources.

Reconstructing this empire is difficult because its own historical record is uneven. The richest body of evidence is epigraphic: thousands of inscriptions in Kannada, Telugu, Tamil, and Sanskrit, cut on temple walls and stone slabs and on copper plates, record grants, taxes, military assignments, and royal genealogies. Epigraphy is the critical study of these inscriptions, including their language, date, patronage, and intended audience. Many are donative records, so they show what donors wished to be remembered for giving; others record settlements of disputes, boundary markings, and tax remissions, and these are often the best evidence for how government and local society actually interacted. Literary works in several languages, including royal poetry and court biographies, supply narrative and ideology. Archaeology, including large-scale field surveys at Hampi, provides evidence for urban layout, water systems, and building technology. Travelers' accounts — by a Venetian, a Timurid ambassador, and Portuguese visitors — offer eyewitness impressions filtered through foreign expectations. Persian chronicles written at the courts of neighboring sultanates describe Vijayanagara from the viewpoint of its rivals.

No single type of source can stand alone. Inscriptions favor donors and rulers; literary works praise patrons; chronicles from Bijapur or the Bahmani court are partisan; and Portuguese accounts, such as the chronicle of Fernão Nuniz, written decades after some of the events it describes, mix observation with hearsay. Many traditions about the empire's origins were written down long after the fact. A good deal of what older histories asserted with confidence — for instance, precise army sizes — rests on a single traveler's estimate and should be treated with caution. This chapter therefore distinguishes, wherever possible, among contemporary inscriptions, material remains, eyewitness testimony, and later tradition.

The historiography has itself changed. Colonial-period scholars such as Robert Sewell, whose 1900 book popularized the empire as a "forgotten empire," relied heavily on Portuguese chronicles. Twentieth-century historians in Karnataka, Andhra, and Tamil Nadu built regional narratives from inscriptions, sometimes claiming the empire for a particular linguistic heritage. Since the 1970s and 1980s, studies by historians such as Burton Stein, Noboru Karashima, Sanjay Subrahmanyam, Phillip Wagoner, and archaeologists working at Hampi have emphasized institutions, local society, and the cosmopolitan culture of the Deccan. The sections that follow draw on all these traditions.

Section 45.2

Southern India before Vijayanagara

In the early fourteenth century the peninsula south of the Krishna was divided among several regional kingdoms whose histories, examined in Volume II's earlier chapters, had been shaped by the Chola and Chalukya inheritance. In what is now southern Karnataka the Hoysalas, with their capitals at Dorasamudra (Halebid) and nearby centers, ruled a prosperous temple-building state. In the Telugu country the Kakatiyas of Warangal had built a kingdom of tank-irrigated agriculture and fortress towns. In the far south the Pandyas of Madurai had, after their thirteenth-century height, fallen into dynastic quarrels. Between these larger powers lay smaller states, including Kampili, a fortified chiefdom near the Tungabhadra that would be especially important to the story of Vijayanagara's origins.

These kingdoms were not static. Their rulers competed with one another, and their local power depended on chiefs, temple institutions, and merchant guilds that held substantial autonomy. Trade in horses, textiles, spices, and precious stones connected the interior to coastal ports. The movement of warriors and pastoral groups, the growth of tank-irrigated agriculture, and the expanding role of temples as landholders and economic institutions all shaped a political culture in which authority was negotiated among kings, local elites, and religious establishments — a pattern that Vijayanagara would inherit and adapt.

The decisive disruption was the southward expansion of the Delhi Sultanate, discussed from the northern perspective in Chapters 41 and 42. Under Alauddin Khalji, armies led by Malik Kafur raided Devagiri, Warangal, Dorasamudra, and Madurai between 1307 and 1311, extracting tribute and plunder but not yet establishing permanent rule. Under the Tughlaqs, the sultanate moved toward more direct control. Warangal was taken in 1323; Kampili fell to Delhi's forces around 1327–28; and for some years Delhi claimed provinces in the Deccan. By the mid-1330s, however, provincial governors broke away: Ma'bar (the region of Madurai) separated into a short-lived sultanate, the Kakatiya heartland rose in revolt under Telugu chiefs such as the Musunuri leaders, and the Hoysala king Ballala III attempted to restore Hoysala power. In 1347 the Bahmani Sultanate was established in the northern Deccan, a development treated in Chapter 46.

For Vijayanagara's origins this is the essential context. The kingdom did not arise in a settled political order that it simply inherited; it arose in a period of collapse, reorganization, and military opportunity, when several earlier kingdoms had been broken or weakened and new coalitions were possible. Historians therefore see Vijayanagara as one of several regional responses to the disruption caused by Delhi's expansion — the most durable among them, but not the only one.

Section 45.3

Harihara, Bukka, and the Foundation of Vijayanagara

Tradition names two brothers, Harihara (Harihara I) and Bukka (Bukka I), sons of Sangama, as the founders of the dynasty known as the Sangama line. Inscriptions show Harihara acting as a ruler on the Tungabhadra from the 1330s, and 1336 is conventionally treated as the date of the kingdom's foundation. Beyond that, the sources diverge sharply, and the first distinction to make is between inscriptions that are contemporary or near-contemporary and narratives written generations or centuries later.

The later narratives are numerous. One group, preserved in Telugu, Kannada, and Sanskrit works and in Portuguese accounts such as that of Nuniz, holds that Harihara and Bukka served the ruler of Kampili or his treasury, were carried to Delhi after Kampili's fall, were forced to convert or submit, and were later sent back to govern the region for the Sultan, whereupon they renounced Delhi's authority and founded a kingdom. Other traditions connect the brothers to the Hoysala court, perhaps as commanders in the northern part of Ballala III's realm. Still others look to the Kakatiya world. Each version has been adopted by modern scholars, and each is tied to arguments over whether the dynasty should be regarded as Kannadiga, Telugu, or something else. Historians today emphasize that these accounts are late, mutually incompatible, and shaped by the political and literary needs of the writers; the idea that a fourteenth-century warrior family belonged to a single modern linguistic nation projects later categories backward. The Delhi-and-Kampili story is the most widely repeated, but it rests largely on traditions rather than contemporary inscriptions, and it should not be treated as established.

A second tradition connects the foundation to the sage Vidyaranya, also known as Madhava, associated with the monastic center of Sringeri, who is said to have inspired and guided the brothers and to have revealed a hidden treasure to fund their kingdom. Sringeri records and later literature attest that the monastery enjoyed Vijayanagara patronage, and the scholar Sayana, whose commentaries on the Vedas are among the major intellectual works of the period, is linked to Bukka's court. But the idea that a monk founded the empire is a legitimizing story told later, and historians treat the Vidyaranya tradition as evidence for how the dynasty and the monastery remembered their relationship rather than as a reliable account of events.

What can be said securely is that Harihara and Bukka built a political base in the Tungabhadra region by combining military force, alliances with local chiefs, and the support of temples and religious institutions. Their kingdom drew on the resources of a frontier zone that had become militarily important but was no longer firmly controlled by Delhi or by the old dynasties. They also adopted an ideology of kingship that connected the new state to Hindu temple traditions, notably the worship of Virupaksha at Pampa, and their inscriptions presented them as defenders of dharma. This religious language was real, but it was also a political language; as later sections show, it coexisted with pragmatic dealings with Muslim neighbors.

Cause and Effect

The Emergence of Vijayanagara

Long-term causes

Decentralized fourteenth-century kingdoms dependent on local chiefs and temples; productive agricultural zones around the Tungabhadra; growing trade in horses and textiles; and the weakening of the Hoysalas, Kakatiyas, Pandyas, and Kampili.

Short-term causes

Delhi's invasions and the fall of Kampili around 1327–28; the breakaway of Delhi's southern governors in the 1330s; and the opportunity for new military leaders to build a regional base.

Event or transformation

Harihara I and Bukka I establish a kingdom on the Tungabhadra, conventionally dated to 1336.

Immediate effects

Rapid territorial expansion, creation of a capital and military hierarchy, conflict with the Madurai Sultanate and, after 1347, the Bahmani Sultanate.

Long-term consequences

A durable imperial system for three centuries, a pattern of nayaka-based governance, and cultural and architectural legacies in southern India.

This textbook's response

The founding is explained as the interaction of regional collapse, military opportunity, and religious and political legitimation, not as the plan of a single person or the work of a single people.

Historical complexity: The conflicting origin stories show how later writers fitted the dynasty into narratives of Hindu resistance, regional identity, or monastic inspiration; none can be treated as a complete account.

Section 45.4

The Early Sangama Dynasty and the Imperial Center

Harihara I's reign (c. 1336–1356) established the kingdom's first territorial base, and Bukka I (c. 1356–1377) greatly expanded it. By the 1370s Bukka was an imperial ruler in practice, receiving embassies and issuing grants in a large territory, and his son Harihara II (c. 1377–1404) took the titles of a sovereign, including Maharajadhiraja, a "great king of kings." Over these decades the kingdom developed the institutions that would define it: a court, a military hierarchy, royal patronage of temples, and an urban center on the Tungabhadra.

The capital was not built at once. Early rulers appear to have used the Anegondi area on the north bank of the river, with its older fort, and gradually developed the walled city across the river at what is now Hampi. The choice of site combined defense, water, and sacred geography. The area around Hemakuta Hill and the Virupaksha temple was already a pilgrimage center, and its association with the epic landscape of Kishkindha — the monkey kingdom of the Ramayana in later tradition — gave the new rulers a way to link their city to an ancient sacred past. As Section 45.19 explains, the capital grew over a century or more into a large urban region with multiple settlements, not a single walled town.

Consolidation also required political technique. The early Sangama kings had to rule a society in which chiefs, temple managers, and merchants all held substantial local power. They did so by negotiation as much as command: granting titles and revenue rights, endowing temples, making marriage alliances, and creating a hierarchy of military commanders who owed service to the crown. The ideological repertoire included the language of dharmic kingship, the performance of royal ceremonies, and the titles by which later inscriptions describe the rulers, including a Sanskrit form meaning "Sultan among Hindu kings," which expressed both opposition to and participation in a shared political world with the sultanates.

Section 45.5

Expansion across Southern India

The early kingdom grew by campaigns in several directions. In the west, rulers asserted control over the remnants of the Hoysala domain after Ballala III died around 1343 in the fighting against the Madurai Sultanate. In the east, Bukka I and his successors pushed into Andhra, contesting the lands of the Reddi chiefs of Kondavidu and the Telugu chiefs of the former Kakatiya territory, competing with the Recherla Velama chiefs of the Telangana region, and with the new Bahmani state to the north. In the south, the kingdom expanded across the Tamil plains.

The southern campaign is the best-documented early military enterprise. It is associated with Kumara Kampana, a son of Bukka I, whose operations in the 1360s and 1370s are celebrated in the Sanskrit poem Madhuravijayam ("Conquest of Madurai"), attributed to his wife Gangadevi. The poem presents Kampana as a hero whose conquest of the Madurai Sultanate restored the worship of Hindu temples, including Srirangam and Madurai's great shrine, after a period of Muslim rule. It is a valuable source and also a thoroughly ideological one: it presents the campaign as a restoration of Hindu sovereignty, in language reflecting the political needs of its patrons. Inscriptions confirm the end of the Madurai Sultanate and the extension of Vijayanagara authority over the Tamil country, but they also show that Vijayanagara rule there depended on local chiefs and institutions, including temple establishments in Kanchipuram, Srirangam, and Madurai.

By about 1400 the kingdom controlled a broad area from the Tungabhadra south to the Kaveri and beyond, with fluctuating authority on the east and west coasts. This was a different kind of control than that of a modern state. In many districts, Vijayanagara rule meant that local chiefs acknowledged the king, paid tribute, supplied soldiers, and accepted royal agents, while managing their own lands. Historians describe this as a layered or negotiated sovereignty, a theme that returns in Division III.

Section 45.6

Deva Raya I, Deva Raya II, and Fifteenth-Century Consolidation

After the death of Harihara II in 1404, a succession struggle among his sons ended with the accession of Deva Raya I (c. 1406–1422), whose reign saw both renewed warfare with the Bahmani Sultanate and the development of the capital's water infrastructure. The Venetian traveler Niccolò de' Conti, who visited around 1420, left the first extended European description of the city. Wars over the Raichur Doab and the Bahmani frontier continued, and the kingdom faced pressure from the Reddis and the Gajapatis of Odisha on the eastern side.

Deva Raya II (c. 1424–1446), sometimes called Praudha Deva Raya, is usually regarded as the strongest ruler of the fifteenth century. Later chroniclers state that he reorganized the military, increasing the use of cavalry and archers and recruiting Muslim soldiers, though the details are debated and come largely from outside sources. He campaigned in Kerala and against the Gajapati rulers, and inscriptions use grand titles that include claims of mastery over elephant-hunters and wide-ranging overlordship. The Timurid envoy Abdur Razzaq, ambassador of Shah Rukh, is usually dated to 1443 in Vijayanagara; his account, which the editors of its Persian text and its translators have discussed at length, describes a prosperous capital with seven concentric fortification lines, large bazaars, and a king of great wealth. His claim that the king's authority extended from Sri Lanka to Gulbarga should be read as an expression of how the court presented itself to visitors rather than as a map of effective control, and the precise chronology of his stay is worth treating with caution because it depends on a single narrative.

The mid-fifteenth century also revealed structural weaknesses. After Deva Raya II the central court became less stable: Mallikarjuna Raya (c. 1446–1465) faced challenges from the Gajapatis and the Bahmanis, and later Sangama rulers struggled to control powerful military commanders. Even so, institutions built in this period — the military hierarchy, the system of temple endowments, the court ritual calendar, and the urban infrastructure of the capital — persisted. They would provide the base on which later dynasties built.

Division II — Warfare, Political Competition, and Dynastic Transformation
Section 45.7

The Bahmani Rivalry and the Raichur Doab

For much of its history Vijayanagara's most persistent military competition was with the Bahmani Sultanate and, after the Bahmani state fragmented, with its successors. This section treats the rivalry from Vijayanagara's perspective; Chapter 46 examines the Bahmani and Deccan sultanates' own political and cultural history.

The contested heartland was the Raichur Doab, the triangle of land between the Krishna and Tungabhadra rivers. Its black cotton soils and river-fed agriculture made it among the most productive districts in the Deccan. It also held strategic fortresses, including Raichur and Mudgal, which had been fortified by earlier dynasties, and controlled the approaches to both capitals. For Vijayanagara, holding the Doab protected the Tungabhadra core and provided revenue and military manpower. For the Bahmanis, it was a southern frontier whose possession would open the way to the Tungabhadra and the south. Control changed hands repeatedly, and fortress sieges, raids on agricultural districts, and the capture of cattle and prisoners were routine features of campaigns. Because armies lived off the land, a campaign in the Doab could mean destroyed harvests and displaced villagers regardless of who won.

The rivalry also extended to the coasts and to the east. In the later fifteenth century the Bahmani minister Mahmud Gawan campaigned along the Konkan coast and captured Goa, a Vijayanagara-linked port, in 1472; Dabhol was already an important Bahmani port. His operations strengthened the sultanate's control of maritime routes and access to the horse trade, a reminder that the conflict was as much commercial as territorial. On the eastern side, the Gajapatis of Odisha and the Reddis of Kondavidu created a three-cornered competition in which Vijayanagara, the Bahmanis, and Odisha shifted alliances. Persian chroniclers such as Firishta narrate the conflicts with strong Bahmani bias; later traditions in Telugu and Kannada narrate them from the Vijayanagara side.

The conflict is often narrated as a religious war, but historians emphasize that it was more complicated. Both sides used religious language, and both sides attacked temples or mosques in particular campaigns, which the sources record with different emphases. But Vijayanagara rulers employed Muslim soldiers and commanders, and Bahmani and successor sultans employed Hindu officials, soldiers, and chiefs. Both competed for horses, revenue, and prestige. Alliances crossed religious lines: Vijayanagara intervened in the sultanates' disputes, and sultans sought Vijayanagara's support against each other. The frontier was therefore a zone of political competition in which religion mattered but did not alone determine alliances.

Section 45.8

Armies, Cavalry, Fortifications, and Military Organization

Vijayanagara's power rested on its military capacity, and its military organization illustrates both the empire's strengths and its structural limits. The army combined infantry, cavalry, elephants, and, increasingly, firearms and artillery. Infantry were numerous and drawn from many regional and social groups, including professional soldiers, chiefly retinues, and levies from territories under nayaka commanders. Elephants served in battle and in ceremony. Cavalry were the most prestigious and expensive arm, and the empire's dependence on imported horses was a persistent strategic concern.

Horses did not breed well in the humid south, so the kingdom imported them by sea from Arabia and Persia through ports such as Ormuz, Goa, Bhatkal, and Honavar. The Portuguese, after taking Goa in 1510, tried to control this trade and used it as leverage in dealings with Vijayanagara and with its northern rivals, a subject returned to in Section 45.25. The horse trade shows how the empire's land power relied on maritime commerce.

Fortifications were central to warfare. The capital had multiple lines of walls, and districts across the empire were dotted with hill forts and fortified towns held by commanders. Fortress warfare, sieges, and raids on harvests were often decisive. The introduction of gunpowder weapons changed the economics of both attack and defense, and Portuguese gunners and matchlock-armed troops appear in some accounts of early sixteenth-century campaigns. Contemporary and later writers, including Nuniz, give army figures in the hundreds of thousands, but such numbers are rhetorical or hearsay and cannot be treated as census-like evidence.

Recruitment depended on the nayaka system, discussed in Section 45.15. Rather than paying a standing army entirely from a royal treasury, the crown assigned military commanders rights to revenue from territories in return for maintaining specified numbers of soldiers. This made the army large and flexible but also meant that a substantial part of its strength depended on the loyalty of powerful subordinates.

Section 45.9

The Saluva Dynasty and the Rise of the Tuluvas

The later Sangama rulers faced growing difficulty in controlling their own military elite. Defeats in the east, losses to the Gajapatis of Odisha, and quarrels over succession weakened the court. Around 1485 a powerful general, Saluva Narasimha, took the throne, ending the Sangama dynasty and founding the short-lived Saluva line. Inscriptions and literary sources present him as a restorer of imperial order, who recovered lost territories and strengthened the army.

The change reveals the character of Vijayanagara politics: kingship was not simply inherited but depended on the support of military commanders who controlled revenue and troops. Narasimha's strength came from his command of this network. After his death around 1491, however, his sons were young, and real power passed to a general, Tuluva Narasa Nayaka, who acted as regent and consolidated control over the army and the provinces. Narasa Nayaka was succeeded as the effective ruler by his son Vira Narasimha, who became the effective ruler after his father’s death around 1503 and took the throne in 1505, founding the Tuluva dynasty.

The Tuluva rise was therefore a transfer of power within the military elite rather than a conquest from outside. It also reflected regional changes. The Tuluvas, whose name indicates links to the Tulu-speaking coast, brought new groups into the imperial center, and the shift occurred when sea trade and Portuguese activity on the west coast were increasing the importance of the western ports. This transition prepared the ground for the reign of the dynasty's greatest ruler.

Section 45.10

Krishnadevaraya and the Height of Imperial Power

Krishnadevaraya, who came to the throne in 1509 and ruled until 1529, is the best known of Vijayanagara's kings. Succeeding his half-brother Vira Narasimha, he governed during a period when the empire was at its widest extent and the capital at its most elaborate. The sources about him are unusually rich: inscriptions, Telugu and Sanskrit literature, including the Telugu poem Amuktamalyada, attributed to him, and detailed Portuguese accounts by Domingo Paes, who visited around 1520–22, and Fernão Nuniz, who recorded traditions about the reign.

Krishnadevaraya's reign shows how kingship worked in practice. He maintained the army, supervised the nayakas, toured the provinces, and used ceremonies to display royal power. The court calendar included major festivals, notably the autumn Mahanavami festival, whose ritual of the king's reviewing of troops, tribute, and performers is described by foreign visitors. He also invested heavily in temple construction and endowments, including at Hampi, Tirupati, Kanchipuram, and Srisailam. Inscriptions at Tirupati record his gifts, and images of the king and his queens survive there.

He was also a patron of learning in multiple languages. Telugu poets, including Allasani Peddana, flourished at his court, and he is credited with the Amuktamalyada, a poem on the Tamil poet-saint Andal, which also contains reflections on statecraft. Scholars debate how much of the political advice in the poem reflects his own views or conventions of the genre. Later tradition remembers him as a model king, and Telugu and Kannada memory both claim him; the empire he governed, however, was multilingual, and he used titles in several languages.

Historians caution against reading the reign as a golden age without qualification. The court's wealth rested on revenue extracted from agricultural and commercial economies; the empire's strength depended on warfare; and many of the achievements celebrated by courtiers and visitors depended on the labor of cultivators, artisans, and dependent workers who left far fewer records.

Section 45.11

Krishnadevaraya's Conquests and Diplomacy

Krishnadevaraya's military campaigns were extensive, and it is important to distinguish their sequence. Early in his reign, in the region of the northern frontier, he confronted the Bahmani successor states. Conventional reconstructions place a campaign against the Bidar and Gulbarga region around 1512 (some place it in 1511), after which he took titles that celebrated his role in restoring a Bahmani prince, evidence of the complex relations between Vijayanagara and the sultanates in which Vijayanagara could act as a kingmaker rather than simply as an enemy. This northern operation is separate from the eastern campaigns that followed.

The most important eastern campaigns were against the Gajapati kingdom of Odisha, whose rulers had expanded southward along the east coast and into the Telugu country. Between about 1513 and 1518, Krishnadevaraya captured the fortress of Udayagiri (c. 1513–14) and then Kondavidu (1515), and extended his control into the coastal Andhra country; subsequent peace and marriage ties with the Gajapati ruler Prataparudra stabilized the northeastern frontier. Inscriptions and temple records preserve the commemoration of these victories, including images brought from conquered places to Vijayanagara, such as the Krishna image from Udayagiri, which the Krishna Temple inscription dates to its consecration at Hampi on 16 February 1515.

The 1520 siege and battle of Raichur against Ismail Adil Shah of Bijapur is the best-known military engagement of the reign. Paes and Nuniz describe a large, carefully organized campaign culminating in the capture of the fortress and the rout of Adil Shah's forces; Persian accounts present events differently. Portuguese soldiers served in Krishnadevaraya's army, and the campaign shows the increasing importance of firearms. The aftermath demonstrates the reach of Vijayanagara's power: Krishnadevaraya dominated the Raichur Doab and was the strongest ruler in the peninsula.

Diplomacy matched war. Krishnadevaraya negotiated with the Portuguese at Goa, who sought commercial privileges and, in return, the supply of horses to Vijayanagara rather than to Bijapur. Through marriages and treaties with neighboring rulers, and through a network of subordinate chiefs, he ensured that the imperial system was held together by relationships as much as by force.

Section 45.12

Imperial Succession after Krishnadevaraya

Krishnadevaraya's death in 1529 left a succession problem. He was followed by his half-brother Achyuta Raya (c. 1529–1542), under whom the empire kept most of its territory but faced political rivalries, pressure from Bijapur on the Raichur Doab, and intrigue among court factions. The strongest figures were relatives of the royal house and military leaders, notably Aliya Rama Raya (often called simply Rama Raya), son-in-law of Krishnadevaraya, and Salakaraju Tirumala, a relative of Achyuta Raya by marriage, whose influence at court generated rivalry. The reign also saw activity in the far south: Achyuta Raya's commanders campaigned against Travancore and in the Tamil country, and appointments of nayakas at Madurai, Thanjavur, and Gingee are traditionally associated with this period, a connection Section 45.15 explores.

The contest for influence at court is reported in Telugu and Sanskrit works and in Nuniz and Persian sources, which differ in their sympathies. According to the traditions, factions struggled over who would control the king and the treasury; the sultanates intervened, sometimes at the invitation of a faction. After Achyuta Raya's death, a struggle over succession ended with the installation of Sadasiva Raya (c. 1542–1570) as the nominal king, whom Rama Raya kept under close control; Persian and Portuguese sources describe the king as effectively a figurehead.

Real power was exercised by Rama Raya, who acted as regent and chief minister with his brothers Tirumala and Venkatadri. Contemporary sources and later Persian chronicles describe him as a skilled politician who intervened in the sultanates' disputes, allying with different Deccan rulers to balance their strength. The brothers controlled military appointments and territory, and they built or endowed temples in the capital; their ascendancy shows both the strength of the nayaka-based elite and the risk of the monarchy becoming a symbol while real authority passed to powerful ministers.

This was not unusual in the political history of the period, but it did mean that when crisis came the empire's central government relied on a single dominant family. Section 45.27 returns to Rama Raya's policies and their consequences. Historians also debate how far Rama Raya's dominance represented usurpation: some sources describe him as a regent who preserved the dynasty's name, while others, especially hostile chronicles, treat him as a usurper in everything but title.

Division III — Government, Economy, and Social Organization
Section 45.13

Kingship, Court Institutions, and Imperial Legitimacy

Vijayanagara kings claimed authority through several overlapping idioms. Sanskrit inscriptions invoked epic and Puranic models of kingship; Telugu and Kannada texts celebrated royal virtue; temple rituals identified the king with divine patronage, in particular with the god Virupaksha, in whose name the early Sangama kings signed their grants; and the sovereign's court employed imperial titles. The king was both a warrior and a donor, and royal gifts to temples and Brahmans were central to legitimacy. Royal gifts such as the tulapurusha, in which a king is weighed against gold that is then distributed, are recorded in the inscriptions of several rulers and served as public demonstrations of wealth and piety.

At the same time, the court did not exist in a Hindu cultural world sealed off from its neighbors. Visitors and inscriptions indicate that Vijayanagara kings adopted aspects of Persianate court culture, including certain forms of dress, the use of titles equivalent to those of sultans, and the presence of Muslim soldiers and officials. Phillip Wagoner has argued that this reflects participation in a shared Deccan political culture in which courts borrowed and competed with one another; Hindu rulers and sultans used overlapping symbols — canopies, thrones, robes of honor, and titles — to assert status, and the use of these forms did not imply submission.

The central administration included ministers and officials responsible for treasury, records, military affairs, and provincial supervision, and a hierarchy of courtiers and household officials. Inscriptions mention royal secretaries, accountants, and officers who supervised the royal household; the Telugu word rayasam designated a secretary who drafted royal orders. Court ceremonial, especially the Mahanavami festival, displayed the king's relationship with his subordinates: nayakas appeared with their troops, presented tribute, and received honors, so that the ritual reaffirmed the hierarchy on which the state depended. Foreign visitors described the pageantry in detail but with the limits of outsiders' understanding.

Legitimacy was also built through genealogy and memory. Dynastic inscriptions traced the kings' ancestry to mythic lineages, and later poets presented the rulers as successors to ancient kings. The practice of consecrating kings, receiving homage from subordinates, and issuing grants in public assemblies created the impression of a stable order. Yet the evidence from succession struggles shows that legitimacy was repeatedly contested, and that military support and the endorsement of powerful nayakas were as important as ritual.

Section 45.14

Provincial Government and the Limits of Central Authority

Beneath the royal court, the empire was divided into provinces, often called rajyas or mandalas, each placed under a governor, who could be a member of the royal family, a trusted general, or a local lord. In the early decades, members of the royal family commonly governed territories: Bukka I's sons and kinsmen are recorded as governors in the Tamil country, the west coast, and the Telugu region, and the title mahamandaleshvara (great provincial lord) appears in inscriptions for senior officers. Provinces were subdivided into smaller units, using terms such as nadu, sima, and sthala depending on region, down to the localities where villages and temples managed their own affairs. Governors collected revenue, supervised justice, and raised troops, and they were expected to forward tribute and attend the court on required occasions.

Local administration relied on village officials and institutions that existed before Vijayanagara. In the Kannada and Telugu regions, village headmen (known by terms such as gauda and reddi), accountants (karanam or karnam), and hereditary village servants managed revenue assessment, record-keeping, and local disputes. In the Tamil country, village and temple assemblies (urs, sabhas, and nadus) continued to appear in inscriptions, though their autonomy was increasingly shaped by the demands of nayakas and governors. Temples were often the main local institutions for storing records, managing irrigation, and settling disputes, so local government overlapped with temple administration.

The effectiveness of this system varied across space and time. Around the capital, royal agents had direct authority. In outlying areas, especially in the Tamil country and the eastern coast, local chiefs, temple trustees, and merchant organizations remained powerful, and governors needed to cooperate with them. Inscriptions reveal bargaining: grants of land or titles in exchange for loyalty, confirmation of local privileges, and negotiated settlements of disputes. When a governor exceeded his authority or a community felt aggrieved, petitions and local protests occurred; some inscriptions record cultivators and artisans suspending work or leaving villages until taxes were adjusted.

Historians have debated how centralized the empire was. Some earlier scholars saw a bureaucratic empire with firm royal control. Others argued that Vijayanagara resembled a "segmentary" state, in which a ritual and military center exercised authority over semi-autonomous local units. The evidence supports neither extreme: royal authority was real, but it was exercised through negotiation with local power-holders. The distance from the capital, the strength of local institutions, and the personality of the king all influenced how far orders could be enforced.

Historical Debate

Was Vijayanagara a Centralized Empire or a Negotiated, Segmentary State?

The question: Did Vijayanagara kings rule through a bureaucratic hierarchy that enforced royal will, or did royal authority rest on negotiation with semi-autonomous chiefs, temples, and local institutions?

Interpretation A — A centralized military-bureaucratic empire: Earlier scholarship, drawing on foreign accounts and the language of royal inscriptions, emphasized an imperial hierarchy of governors and nayakas under the king's control, with substantial revenue and military power flowing upward to the court.
Interpretation B — A segmentary or negotiated state: Burton Stein and others argued that the king exercised ritual and military sovereignty while local units, especially temples and chiefs, retained substantial autonomy. Other historians, working on inscriptions and revenue, criticized the segmentary model for understating the crown's fiscal and military capacity and emphasized a "military-fiscal" state in which nayakas were directly tied to the crown through service obligations.

Evidence: Both views depend on inscriptions that record land grants, tax assessments, and military obligations; on Nuniz's description of the nayaka system; and on the political history of rebellion and regional independence after 1565.

Why it matters: The answer affects how historians understand the empire's expansion, its use of religion and ritual, and the reasons for its fragmentation after Talikota. Many current scholars treat the empire as strongest where royal agents and nayakas were closely supervised and as more negotiated at the periphery.

Section 45.15

The Nayaka and Amara-Nayaka Systems

The nayaka system was the empire's main method of connecting central power with local resources. A nayaka was a military chief or commander. An amara-nayaka was a commander who held an amaram, a grant of the right to collect revenue from a territory, in return for maintaining a specified contingent of soldiers (infantry, cavalry, and elephants) and paying a portion of income, or tribute, to the king. Some nayakas were members of the royal family or long-serving generals; others were local chiefs whose position the crown recognized. The Telugu and Tamil terms amaramu and nayankara appear in inscriptions, and the arrangement is discussed at length by Nuniz.

Nuniz presents the system as a bargain: nayakas paid fixed sums to the king and kept troops for the royal army, retaining the rest of the revenue for themselves. His figures for the contingents are unreliable, but the structure is confirmed by inscriptions that record nayakas' obligations and by land records that show assignments within the territories of specific villages. The system allowed the crown to mobilize large forces without maintaining all troops directly. It also integrated local elites into the imperial system: a chief who might otherwise have been a rival became a client with an interest in the empire's success.

In practice, the arrangement varied. In the Karnataka core, nayakas were more closely supervised by royal officials; in the Telugu country they often came from established warrior families (such as the Velamas, Reddis, and Kammas); in the Tamil south, nayakas coexisted with powerful temple institutions and with chiefly lineages such as the polygars (palaiyakkarar). Traditional accounts credit Viswanatha Nayaka with establishing the Madurai Nayaka kingdom and organizing the palaiyam, or military estate, system there under Achyuta Raya, with a similar tradition for the Nayakas of Thanjavur and Gingee; these accounts blend history with later legend, and the inscriptions suggest more gradual developments. The Keladi (Ikkeri) Nayakas in the west rose from similar beginnings.

The system had inherent tensions. A nayaka who controlled revenue, forts, and troops could become independent if the center weakened. Rulers tried to prevent this by supervising nayakas through royal agents, requiring attendance at court, taking members of nayaka families into royal service, and rotating assignments. The system worked best under strong kings. After 1565, as Section 45.29 shows, powerful nayakas in the Tamil country and Karnataka converted their positions into regional kingdoms. Historians continue to debate whether the nayaka system was a form of feudalism, a type of military-fiscal contracting, or a distinct Indian arrangement; most now avoid the feudal label because nayakas' rights were conditional and tied to service and tribute rather than to personal vassalage.

Historical Vocabulary
Nayaka and Amara-Nayaka

A nayaka was a military chief or captain. An amara-nayaka held an amaram, a territorial assignment from which he collected revenue in return for maintaining soldiers and rendering tribute to the king. The term should not be translated simply as "feudal lord": the grants were conditional, the holders had obligations to the crown, and their relationships with local communities varied.

The nayaka system explains how Vijayanagara raised armies, and its evolution explains how regional kingdoms such as those at Madurai and Thanjavur later emerged.

Section 45.16

Agriculture, Irrigation, Revenue, and Rural Society

The empire's wealth rested on agriculture. Core regions around Hampi and the Tungabhadra produced millet, rice where irrigation was available, cotton, and sugarcane; the black soils of the Raichur Doab were famous for cotton; the Kaveri delta and the Tamil plains supported intensive rice cultivation; coastal districts produced pepper, coconut, and other cash crops. Pastoral groups raised cattle and sheep, and forest peoples supplied timber and other resources. The pattern of land use depended on rainfall: dry-land districts relied on tanks, wells, and millets, while the river valleys and deltas supported rice and sugarcane.

Irrigation was a major area of royal and local investment. Rulers, nayakas, temples, and merchants built tanks, canals, and diversion dams, and inscriptions commemorate such works. Near the capital, canals drew water from anicuts (diversion dams) on the Tungabhadra to irrigate agricultural land and supply the city, a system that archaeological survey has traced in detail; the Basavanna and Raya canals are among those associated with the capital's hinterland, and a large tank at Kamalapuram served the royal center. Foreign visitors, including Paes, describe the engineering of the water system, and Krishnadevaraya is credited with founding the town of Nagalapura (modern Hosapete) with a large tank. Tank construction in the Telugu and Tamil country expanded the area of paddy cultivation, and temples often managed irrigation resources.

Revenue came from land taxes assessed in kind or cash and from a range of other levies on occupations, markets, and transport. Inscriptions record rates, exemptions, and disputes. Taxes were often assessed by the productivity of the land category (wet, dry, or garden), and the king or the amara-nayaka assigned shares to temples through grants. Dues on occupations such as weaving, oil-pressing, and shop-keeping were collected and sometimes remitted by royal order or by local agreement, which suggests that the tax system was negotiated. The tax demand was heavy, and cultivators sometimes protested by abandoning villages, petitioning, or refusing to pay, as some inscriptions record.

Land rights were layered. Peasants cultivated; village assemblies and temples held rights; local lords collected; and amara-nayakas held higher claims. Brahmadeya villages, assigned to Brahmans, and devadana lands, assigned to temples, were administered by specific institutions, and many were cultivated by tenants and laborers. The resulting hierarchy of claims makes it hard to describe a simple relationship between state and peasant. It also meant that when the center weakened, the same lands continued to produce revenue for local holders, which helps explain the continuity of rural society after 1565.

Section 45.17

Crafts, Manufacturing, Markets, and Internal Commerce

Artisans and merchants were essential to the empire. Weavers produced cotton textiles for local use and for export; metalworkers made tools, weapons, and temple bronzes; stoneworkers and masons built the monuments; and jewelers, goldsmiths, and diamond cutters supplied the court. The region's mineral resources, including iron, and the diamond-bearing areas of the Krishna basin, supported both craft and trade.

Textile production was the largest manufacturing sector. Weavers' communities, including those known in inscriptions as Kaikolars in the Tamil country and Devangas and Padmasalis in the Telugu and Kannada regions, wove cotton and silk for temples, courts, and markets, and migrated or were settled by rulers in new towns. Dyeing and printing, especially on the Coromandel Coast, supplied textiles that were exported to Southeast Asia and the Middle East. Weavers often organized in caste and guild-like associations that negotiated taxes and customs with local officials and temples, and some inscriptions record agreements regulating the weavers' payments to temples. Textile workers were not simply passive: disputes over taxes sometimes led them to withdraw labor or relocate.

Metalworking and stone craft were closely linked to temples and the state. Archaeological survey in the Hampi region has documented evidence of iron smelting, and blacksmiths, goldsmiths, and bronze casters worked for military and religious needs. Bronze images of kings and queens preserved at Tirupati and elsewhere show the quality of casting. Stonemasons and sculptors, working in granite, were organized in hereditary groups that moved between building projects, as stylistic links between distant monuments suggest.

Merchant organizations operated across the region. Older trading corporations such as the Nanadesis and Ayyavole associations appear in earlier inscriptions, and in the Vijayanagara period a range of merchant communities, including the Balijas and Komatis of the Telugu country and the Chettis of the Tamil country, appear as donors, taxpayers, and participants in local governance. They transported goods between the interior and the coast, financed temples, and negotiated tax arrangements with local rulers. Money circulated in gold coins called varahas (known to Europeans as pagodas) and in smaller silver and copper denominations, along with the fanam, and contemporary descriptions mention money changers and credit.

Markets in the capital impressed foreign visitors: Abdur Razzaq and Paes describe bazaars full of jewels, textiles, and foodstuffs, with specialized streets for different goods. Archaeological work at Hampi has identified bazaar streets lined with colonnaded structures. Internal commerce linked agricultural regions to urban centers, and routes ran between the Tungabhadra, the western ports, and the eastern coast. Temple festivals and periodic fairs were major commercial events, and pilgrims moved goods as well as devotion.

Section 45.18

Social Hierarchy, Caste, Gender, and Everyday Life

Society under Vijayanagara was hierarchical and varied by region. Brahmans held prestige and landed endowments and served as ministers, scholars, and temple managers. Warrior and chiefly groups, such as Kshatriya-claiming lineages and powerful local lords, held military and administrative roles. Peasant communities, merchant groups, artisan groups, and large communities of laborers and servants made up the bulk of the population. The relationship between varna categories and the many local jatis is complex: inscriptions use both, and many ruling families claimed varna status they had acquired through political success.

In the Tamil country, and to a lesser extent in other southern regions, inscriptions distinguish "right-hand" (valangai) and "left-hand" (idangai) groupings. These were broad alliances of occupational and agrarian communities, with the right-hand typically including groups connected to agriculture and local landholding and the left-hand groups connected to crafts and trade, though the membership varied by locality. They competed over precedence, honors, and ritual privileges, and disputes are recorded in temple inscriptions and in local assemblies; rulers and governors sometimes arbitrated. They should not be read as identical to religious sects, nor as a fixed division throughout South Asia; the evidence is concentrated in the Tamil country and is uneven in time.

Women appear in the sources in several roles. Royal and elite women were patrons: Krishnadevaraya's queens are recorded as donors, and Gangadevi's poem shows a woman as a literary author. Temple women, including devadasis, performed in rituals and held property in some contexts, and inscriptions record gifts made by and to temple women. Foreign visitors described the large royal household and the performance of sati by elite women, and memorial stones in Karnataka, including those known as sati stones, attest such practices, though their frequency and social range are uncertain. Most women worked in agriculture, crafts, and domestic service and are largely invisible in elite sources.

Slavery and dependency existed in several forms. Visitors such as Nuniz and Paes mention slaves, war captives, and servants; inscriptions record the sale or gift of individuals to temples and the pledging of persons for debts. The terms used vary and may describe different degrees of unfreedom, from household servitude to bonded agricultural labor, and the evidence does not allow precise numbers. Everyday life for most people revolved around agricultural cycles, village temples and festivals, and local markets. Harvest failures, wars, and heavy taxation could force families into debt or migration. These realities are visible only indirectly in the sources, but they must be kept in view when assessing the splendor reported by travelers.

Everyday Life and Social History

Village, Temple, and Bazaar

For most people, life under Vijayanagara was local. A cultivating household worked fields watered by rain or a village tank, paid revenue to the village headman or the holder of the land, and took part in temple festivals that structured the calendar. Weavers worked at home looms and sold to local merchants; stonemasons moved between building projects; laborers and servants worked on temple lands and in the households of elites.

In the capital, an observer would have seen pilgrims at the Virupaksha temple, merchants in the bazaars, soldiers on the roads, and performers in festival processions. Large public ceremonies drew crowds from across the countryside. Much of this life is known only indirectly, through inscriptions recording taxes, gifts, and disputes, and the testimony of foreigners; the voices of cultivators and artisans are rarely heard directly.

Division IV — Hampi, Religion, Intellectual Life, and Imperial Culture
Section 45.19

Hampi: Geography, Archaeology, and the Imperial Capital

The ruined city at Hampi (a modern name derived from Pampa) is one of the largest archaeologically documented premodern urban landscapes in South Asia. UNESCO inscribed it as a World Heritage Site in 1986 and describes more than 1,600 surviving remains, including temples, fortifications, residential areas, and hydraulic structures. Its significance lies not only in its monuments but in the possibility of studying a whole urban region.

The landscape is dominated by granite boulders and ridges, the river, and an alluvial plain to the south. Archaeologists distinguish a "sacred center" on the river bank, around Hemakuta Hill and the Virupaksha temple with its bazaar street; a "royal center" to the south, with palatial enclosures; and an extensive "urban core" and suburbs with temples, bazaars, and residential quarters. Beyond the walls, surveys have documented settlements, fields, tanks, canals, and roads in the surrounding countryside, showing that the city depended on an integrated rural hinterland. Outlying towns such as Kamalapuram and Nagalapura (modern Hosapete), founded by Krishnadevaraya, extended the urban region. The sacred landscape also matters: local tradition identified the area with the Ramayana's Kishkindha, and shrines on Matanga and Anjanadri hills and across the river draw on this identification, so that the city was embedded in a landscape of pilgrimage rather than being a secular capital.

Modern knowledge of Hampi results from successive efforts. Colin Mackenzie surveyed the ruins in the early nineteenth century; the Archaeological Survey of India and the Karnataka state department carried out conservation and excavation; and from the 1980s the Vijayanagara Research Project, directed by John Fritz and George Michell, documented architecture and urban plans. Complementary projects, including the Vijayanagara Metropolitan Survey led by Carla Sinopoli and Kathleen Morrison, studied the hinterland through systematic field survey, mapping thousands of structures and sites and analyzing patterns of settlement, agriculture, and pottery distribution. These projects show that the capital's population drew on villages and craft areas across the surrounding region and that the rural landscape was reorganized around the city.

Archaeology also reveals what monuments alone do not. Surface survey and excavation have identified craft production areas, residential compounds, shrines, wells, and water channels beyond the royal and temple zones, and they have shown that large parts of the urban core were occupied by modest houses, not palaces. Evidence for Muslim and Jain religious sites, for cemeteries, and for different pottery assemblages gives glimpses of the city's social diversity. Interpretation is still challenging: many building names, such as "Lotus Mahal" or "Elephant Stables," are modern or colonial labels and do not necessarily reflect original functions, and dating relies on a combination of inscriptions, architectural style, and stratigraphy.

Section 45.20

The Royal Center, Fortifications, and Monumental Architecture

The royal center was an enclosed zone of palaces, platforms, water structures, and ceremonial spaces. Its best-known features include the Great Enclosure, which contains the Mahanavami Dibba, a tiered stone platform associated with the autumn festival; a stepped tank of finely cut stone; and administrative and residential structures. Surrounding it, the Zenana Enclosure (a modern name) holds structures including the Lotus Mahal, a pavilion with arched openings showing the blend of regional and Indo-Islamic architectural elements, and the Elephant Stables, a long row of domed chambers. Nearby are the Queen's Bath and other water structures, again named by later observers.

Hampi's fortifications used massive dry-stone masonry, taking advantage of natural boulders, and enclosed the urban core and royal center in several lines. Foreign accounts, including Abdur Razzaq's description of seven concentric fortifications, give a sense of how visitors experienced the city, though they cannot be matched exactly with surviving remains; survey has identified multiple wall circuits, gateways with bent entrances, and watchtowers, as well as roads that carried processions and troops. Gateways were designed to control access and to stage ceremonial entry.

Interpretation of the royal center rests on archaeology and comparison with textual descriptions, notably those of Paes and Nuniz. Because the royal buildings were built largely of perishable materials above stone platforms, much of their original appearance has been lost, and reconstruction is partly hypothetical. Excavations in the royal center have exposed the foundations of timber-framed halls, which suggests that the "palaces" described by visitors were largely wooden structures on granite plinths. The architecture shows the cosmopolitan nature of the court: some structures use arches, domes, and vaults familiar from Deccan sultanate architecture, alongside traditional temple forms, and the terms used by the court for some buildings overlap with Persianate vocabulary. Whether these buildings were mainly residential, ceremonial, or military is debated, which is a reminder that the royal center was a place of display as much as government.

Historical Vocabulary
Mahanavami

Mahanavami refers to the ninth day of the autumn festival associated with the goddess, a major royal ceremony at Vijayanagara. Foreign visitors described processions, tribute presentations, military reviews, and performances. The ceremony expressed relationships among the king, the nayakas, and the divine; it was later continued in the Dasara celebrations of successor states such as Mysore.

Historical Vocabulary
Hindu Suratrana

A Sanskrit royal title found in Vijayanagara inscriptions, understood by historians as "Sultan among Hindu kings." The word suratrana is derived from the Arabic-Persian title sultan. It illustrates that the dynasty claimed a Hindu political identity while using the vocabulary of a wider Deccan political culture, and it cautions against reading Vijayanagara as isolated from its Muslim neighbors.

Section 45.21

Urban Markets, Waterworks, Residential Areas, and Public Life

Water management was central to the city's functioning. A network of canals, aqueducts, tanks, and wells drew water from the Tungabhadra and its tributaries to irrigate fields and supply the city, linking the urban region to its agricultural base. Archaeological surveys have traced channels that carried water over long distances, sometimes through masonry aqueducts, and stepped tanks (pushkarinis), including ritual ones, combined practical and ceremonial functions. Kings and nayakas sponsored such works, and inscriptions note them. The royal center had its own supply and drainage, which implies planning and maintenance by a specialized workforce.

Bazaars lay along broad avenues leading to temples, notably the Hampi Bazaar before the Virupaksha temple and the bazaar streets near the Vitthala and Achyutaraya temples. These were lined with pillared shops and rest halls. Visitors observed merchants selling gems, textiles, and food. Paes described specific markets for horses and for everyday goods, and the area's economic life was shaped by the periodic festivals that drew pilgrims and traders. Bazaar streets were also public spaces where processions, performances, and religious gatherings took place, which meant that commerce, worship, and political display overlapped.

Residential districts included elite compounds and more modest housing, with distinct neighborhoods for different occupations and communities. Elite households, such as those of nayakas and officials who spent time at the capital, occupied walled compounds with courtyards and wells, while artisans and laborers lived in clustered dwellings often built of mud and thatch that survive only as foundations. Evidence suggests a Muslim quarter in the urban area, consistent with the presence of Muslim soldiers and merchants recorded in other sources, and Jain temples indicate a Jain community. Nearby settlements housed artisans and laborers.

The urban population was supported by a large labor force: builders, water carriers, servants, grooms, soldiers, and performers. Visitors reported crowded streets, public festivals, performances by dancers and wrestlers, and the movement of troops. The urban population is not known with precision; estimates vary widely, and it should be seen as one of the largest cities of its time in South Asia but not given a firm number. The city's food supply depended on the surrounding countryside and on grain brought by merchants, which made the capital vulnerable to disruption of the rural economy, as the events of 1565 demonstrated.

Section 45.22

Temples and Vijayanagara Architecture

Temples were the dominant monumental structures. The Virupaksha temple, devoted to a form of Shiva and his consort Pampa, predates the empire and was expanded and enriched by Vijayanagara patrons, including a large gateway tower (gopura) and a pillared hall attributed to Krishnadevaraya. The Vitthala temple, dedicated to a Vaishnava deity associated with Pandharpur, is the most elaborate complex, with intricately carved halls, including the so-called musical pillars, and a stone shrine in the form of a chariot; its principal construction took place across the reigns of Deva Raya II, Krishnadevaraya, and Achyuta Raya. The Hazara Rama temple, within the royal center, is decorated with narrative reliefs from the Ramayana, and its function was probably as a royal temple. The Krishna temple and the Tiruvengalanatha (Achyutaraya) temple are other major examples.

Vijayanagara temple architecture drew on earlier Chola, Hoysala, and Chalukya traditions and developed distinctive features: monumental gateway towers, large pillared halls (mandapas) with elaborately sculpted piers, and "kalyana mandapas" for ceremonial marriage rituals of the deity. Temples also had enclosing walls and subsidiary shrines, and temple streets became commercial and ritual axes of urban life. Sculpted pillars with rearing animals (yalis) and horsemen became a hallmark, and their iconography has been interpreted as a statement about royal military power.

These monuments were built by skilled sculptors and masons working in granite, often organized in hereditary or guild-like groups, with royal, nayaka, and merchant patrons. Their inscriptions list the donors and gifts, giving insight into patronage and administration. Stylistic variation shows regional exchange: Tamil gopuras, for example, spread through the empire and influenced the later Nayaka temple building at Madurai, Srirangam, and elsewhere. Outside Hampi, major Vijayanagara-period work survives at Lepakshi in Andhra, whose Virabhadra temple preserves murals and sculpture associated with Virupanna Nayaka in the reign of Achyuta Raya, and at Vellore, Srirangam, Kanchipuram, and Tirupati, where royal gifts and royal portraits are recorded.

Section 45.23

Religion, Temple Institutions, and Imperial Patronage

Temples were not merely religious buildings; they were institutions that held land, employed priests, musicians, dancers, and servants, managed endowments, lent money, and organized festivals. Royal gifts to major temples strengthened the court's relationship with local communities and reinforced legitimacy. Inscriptions record elaborate grants of villages, tax revenues, and gold, and temple managers often kept the records that are now the main source for local history.

The empire's religious world was plural. Shaiva and Vaishnava traditions both flourished: the early kings emphasized Virupaksha, while later rulers, especially Krishnadevaraya and the Tuluva and Aravidu kings, patronized Vaishnava temples, including Tirupati and Srirangam, and Sri Vaishnava teachers. Inscriptions associated with the Sangama general Gopana record the restoration of the Ranganatha image to Srirangam after the defeat of the Madurai Sultanate, a story that was remembered as a landmark of royal piety. The Dvaita (Madhva) tradition also received royal favor, notably through the teacher Vyasatirtha, associated with Krishnadevaraya's court, and the Haridasa devotional singers drew on his circle. Virashaiva (Lingayat) and Smarta institutions, such as the monastery at Sringeri, were patronized.

Jain communities remained influential, with temples at Hampi and in the Karnataka region, including the Ganigitti temple of 1385. An inscription of 1368 from Sravana Belgola records that Bukka I settled a dispute between Jains and Sri Vaishnavas, declaring that their traditions would be protected and that the two communities should regard each other with respect; it is often cited as evidence of royal arbitration among religious communities, though it also shows that disputes occurred. Muslim communities lived in the city and the empire as soldiers, merchants, and officials. Persian chroniclers claim that Deva Raya II built a mosque for Muslim soldiers in the capital, and archaeology has identified Islamic buildings at Hampi, though the dating and function of particular structures are debated.

Patronage was also a political tool. Rulers cultivated relationships with temple managers, rewarded loyal chiefs with temple honors, and used temple festivals to bring communities into the orbit of the court. Temple women — devadasis — performed ritual song and dance and are recorded as donors and as recipients of temple land. At the same time, conflicts could arise over precedence, land, and taxation. Interpretations of Vijayanagara as a "Hindu empire" capture the dynasty's self-presentation but miss the multi-religious composition of the military and urban population, a point that connects with Chapter 48's treatment of devotional and Sufi movements across the region.

Section 45.24

Literature, Languages, Learning, and the Arts

Vijayanagara supported a multilingual literary culture. Sanskrit remained the language of royal inscriptions, scholarship, and some poetry. Sayana's commentaries on the Vedas, produced in association with the early Sangama court, and Gangadevi's Madhuravijayam are important Sanskrit works, and the Dvaita philosopher Vyasatirtha wrote influential treatises that defended his tradition against rival schools. In Kannada, the poet Kumaravyasa retold the Mahabharata, the Virashaiva poet Chamarasa composed a long work in the reign of Deva Raya II, and the Haridasa tradition of devotional singers, including Purandaradasa and Kanakadasa, produced songs that influenced later Karnatak music.

Telugu literature flourished particularly in the sixteenth century. Krishnadevaraya's Amuktamalyada, a poem on the saint Andal that also reflects on kingship, and the works of poets associated with his court, including Allasani Peddana (author of the Manucharitra), Nandi Timmana, and others, are central to the Telugu literary canon. Dhurjati and Tenali Ramakrishna are linked to the same literary world, and Ramaraja Bhushana (Bhattumurti) composed at Rama Raya's court. The later tradition of the "eight elephants" (ashtadiggajas) of Krishnadevaraya's court is a literary memory whose historicity in detail is uncertain. Tamil literature continued under local patrons and temple institutions, and Tamil poets of the period, including those associated with Sri Vaishnava and Shaiva centers, composed in a mixture of devotional and courtly genres.

Learning included theology and philosophy, grammar, astronomy, medicine, and music. The musicologist Ramamatya wrote the Svaramelakalanidhi, completed in 1550 at the request of Aliya Rama Raya, after the initiative associated with Rama Raya's brother Venkatadri; it is an important work on the theory of ragas that shaped later South Indian music. Sculpture, painting, and dance flourished in temples and courts. Temple murals, such as those on the ceiling of the Virupaksha temple's hall and the larger paintings at Lepakshi, documented epic and Puranic stories, and Paes' descriptions of courtly performances by women dancers and musicians show the integration of performance into royal ceremony.

The literature shows the empire's cultural complexity: rulers who patronized Sanskrit also supported vernacular languages, and poets wrote of regional pride while serving an empire of many peoples. It also shows constraints: much of the surviving literature is courtly, composed by educated elites, and reflects patron interests, while the oral traditions of cultivators, pastoralists, and artisans survive only through later collection.

Section 45.25

Overseas Commerce, Horse Imports, and Portuguese Connections

Vijayanagara's coasts gave it access to Indian Ocean commerce. Ports on the west coast, including Bhatkal, Honavar, Mangalore, and Barkur, and ports on the east, including Pulicat and Nagapattinam, handled exports of textiles, rice, pepper, iron, and other goods, and imports of horses, luxury goods, and metals. Duarte Barbosa, who described the western coast in the early sixteenth century, noted that ports such as Bhatkal exported rice and sugar and imported horses, and that Muslim, Hindu, and Jain merchants dominated the trade. On the Coromandel coast, textiles made for Southeast Asian markets passed through ports connected with Chettis and Muslim shipowners, a commerce that Chapter 49 places in its wider Indian Ocean context.

Horses were the most strategic import. They came by sea from Hormuz, Arabia, and Persia, and the supply determined the balance between Vijayanagara and its northern rivals. After seizing Goa from Bijapur in 1510, officials of the Estado da Índia, the Portuguese Crown’s maritime establishment in Asia, tried to direct horse imports to their preferred clients and used the trade as leverage. They issued passes (cartazes) to shipping and tried to channel coastal commerce through Goa. Vijayanagara rulers negotiated: Krishnadevaraya reportedly sought Portuguese horses and good relations, and Portuguese envoys visited the court; the Honavar-based maritime captain and corsair Timoja (Timayya) assisted the Portuguese in the capture of Goa, which shows how coastal elites could act between the empire and foreign powers.

Documentary evidence includes Portuguese chronicles and letters, Paes' and Nuniz's accounts, and inscriptions recording customs duties and port administration, but Vijayanagara documents about the relationship are sparse, and Portuguese sources tend to magnify their own role. The relationship should not be understood as dependence in a single direction. Vijayanagara could shift trade among ports, play Portuguese and sultanate interests against each other, and use Portuguese gunners and military supplies for specific campaigns. Portuguese settlers and missionaries also established communities in the south, including at San Thome (Mylapore) on the Coromandel coast, where they interacted with local rulers.

Later, after Talikota, Portuguese ties with successor regimes, including the Nayakas and Aravidu kings, developed further, and in the early seventeenth century the Dutch and English entered the coastal trade with the permission of local governors, as Section 45.29 notes. These later connections show that the commercial openings created under Vijayanagara's coastal governors outlasted the empire's central authority.

Section 45.26

Foreign Travelers and the Reconstruction of Vijayanagara

Travelers' accounts are among the best-known sources for Vijayanagara, but they require critical reading. Niccolò de' Conti, a Venetian merchant, visited around 1420, and his account, recorded by the humanist Poggio Bracciolini in Italy, reports on the city's size, customs, and festivals; because it passed through an interviewer and a translator, it is a mediated source. Abdur Razzaq, an envoy from the Timurid ruler Shah Rukh, visited in 1443 and gave a detailed account of the court and city from a Persianate perspective, comparing what he saw with the courts of Herat and Hormuz. In the early sixteenth century, Portuguese visitors included Duarte Barbosa, who described the west coast and its trade, and Domingo Paes, whose description of Krishnadevaraya's capital, around 1520–22, is among the most vivid. Fernão Nuniz, a horse-trader, probably around 1535–37, compiled a chronicle of the kings and a description of the empire, partly based on what he heard. After the capital's destruction, the Venetian Cesare Federici saw the ruins in 1567.

The accounts differ in what they emphasize. Conti notes customs and festivals; Razzaq describes ceremonial, the king's wealth, and the city's fortifications, and his mention of fortifications is the main source for the "seven lines" tradition; Paes describes streets, markets, temples, and the royal household and its festivals, typically comparing buildings to those he knew; Nuniz records genealogies, the military system, and revenue claims, with numbers that cannot be verified. Federici, writing after the sack, provides evidence of devastation and of the survival of some settlement in the area.

Each account has limits. Travelers saw only parts of the city, often spoke through interpreters, and interpreted what they saw through their own experience. Numerical claims about armies, revenue, or city circumference are frequently exaggerated or based on hearsay. Nuniz's chronology of early kings is unreliable because it records oral tradition, though his description of the nayaka system and of Krishnadevaraya's reign is more useful. Historians therefore use the accounts primarily where they can be checked against inscriptions and archaeology, and they pay particular attention to what travelers describe from direct observation, such as festivals, markets, and architecture. They also ask what is missing: none of the travelers wrote about daily life in villages, and none was a neutral observer.

Section 45.27

Aliya Rama Raya and the Changing Politics of the Deccan

During the mid-sixteenth century Aliya Rama Raya was the effective ruler of Vijayanagara. His strategy was to exploit rivalries among the Deccan sultanates. After the Bahmani Sultanate fragmented in the late fifteenth and early sixteenth centuries into Bijapur, Ahmadnagar, Golconda, Bidar, and Berar, the Deccan became a field of shifting alliances, which Chapter 46 treats from the sultanates' side. Rama Raya intervened repeatedly, allying with one sultanate against another, supporting claimants, and extracting concessions, territory, and prestige. Persian chroniclers describe his interventions as arrogant; they also show that sultans themselves sought his alliance when it was useful.

This policy gave Vijayanagara great influence but also created resentment. Rama Raya's armies, which included Muslim commanders and soldiers, campaigned in the sultanates' territories, and some Persian sources accuse his forces of disrespect toward mosques and local communities, while noting that Muslim sultans also committed similar acts in warfare. Such accounts must be read cautiously, as they were written to explain defeat or justify later coalition. Meanwhile the sultanates themselves resolved some disputes through marriage alliances and diplomacy, notably between Bijapur and Ahmadnagar in the early 1560s, which reduced the incentives for rivalry and increased the possibility of joint action. The immediate political fact is that, by the early 1560s, the sultanates of Bijapur, Ahmadnagar, Golconda, and Bidar had reason to unite against him, and they did so.

Within Vijayanagara, Rama Raya's dominance also created risk. He relied on his brothers and clients, and his own power depended on the continuing loyalty of the nayakas. The decision to meet the confederate sultanate armies in the field in 1565 reflected confidence built on decades of success, and, according to Persian accounts, it also reflected underestimation of the coalition's artillery and cohesion. Whether the alliance was a coordinated plan or a response to an opportunity is debated, and historians treat the claims by some chroniclers that Rama Raya's insults caused the war as retrospective explanations.

Section 45.28

The Battle of Talikota in 1565 and the Destruction of the Capital

The battle that ended Vijayanagara's imperial dominance was fought in January 1565 (traditionally dated 23 January) near the Krishna River, at Rakkasa-Tangadi, an area close to the town of Talikota, from which the battle takes its conventional name. Sources conflict on the exact day, and some reconstructions propose dates a day or more later, so the traditional date should be treated as conventional rather than certain. The allied armies of Bijapur, Ahmadnagar, Golconda, and Bidar faced Vijayanagara's forces under Rama Raya and his brothers. Accounts, mostly from the sultanate side, such as Persian chronicles of Firishta and Rafi'uddin Shirazi, describe heavy fighting, the effective use of artillery, and a decisive moment when Rama Raya was captured and killed. His army broke and fled.

Contemporary and later reports differ on specific details: the sizes of the armies, the role of defections, and the exact sequence of events. Historians treat claims that Muslim commanders in Vijayanagara's army betrayed Rama Raya as uncertain, since they come from later narratives that seek explanations for the defeat. What is clear is that the Vijayanagara command was destroyed, and that the army's collapse exposed the capital.

After the victory the sultans' troops entered Vijayanagara and plundered it. Tirumala, Rama Raya's brother, escaped with the nominal king Sadasiva Raya and with treasure to Penukonda. Narratives describe months of looting and burning, and archaeological evidence confirms destruction in parts of the city, including royal buildings and some temples; Federici, visiting in 1567, described the deserted city. But the destruction was uneven. Some monuments survived with little damage, parts of the sacred center, including the Virupaksha temple, continued to receive worship, and settlement persisted in the surrounding region. The city never regained its role as an imperial capital, though some communities remained. The destruction was severe, but historians emphasize that the empire's institutions and its population did not disappear with the city.

Section 45.29

The Aravidu Dynasty, Regional Nayaka States, and Vijayanagara's Legacy

Tirumala Raya re-established royal authority from Penukonda, and around 1570 he took the throne himself, founding the Aravidu dynasty. The Aravidu kings, including Sriranga I and Venkata II (c. 1586–1614), maintained the imperial title and recovered some territory; Venkata II moved the center of government to Chandragiri and Vellore and kept control over parts of the Tamil country and Andhra. The kingdom remained among the major powers of the south, and its court continued patronage of temples, literature, and the arts. In the early seventeenth century the Dutch established a settlement at Pulicat with the permission of local authorities connected to Chandragiri.

The dynasty, however, faced growing independence among the nayakas. The Nayakas of Madurai, Thanjavur, and Gingee in the Tamil country, and the Keladi (Ikkeri) Nayakas in the west, ruled increasingly as regional kings, building temples and fortifications and sometimes acknowledging Vijayanagara's overlordship only nominally. In the north, the Deccan sultanates, particularly Bijapur and Golconda, expanded south. After Venkata II's death in 1614, a succession conflict weakened the dynasty, and later Aravidu rulers, including Venkata III and Sriranga III, lost territory to Bijapur and Golconda by the 1640s, though Sriranga III continued to claim the title for years afterward from refuges in the south.

The English East India Company's acquisition of the site of Madras illustrates the situation. In August 1639 Francis Day obtained a grant of land at Madraspatam from Damarla Venkatappa (also rendered Venkatapathy) Nayaka, a regional governor under the Chandragiri court; the grant, endorsed in the Company records by Andrew Cogan, permitted the establishment of a fortified settlement, later called Fort St George. The Company's records and later accounts report that the grant was subsequently confirmed by the Vijayanagara king, though the sources differ on the date and details of this confirmation. The episode shows both the persistence of the imperial title and the reality that local nayakas, not the king, made decisions about land and trade.

Vijayanagara's legacies were durable. The nayaka states carried forward its administrative practices, temple building, and cultural patronage, while the Wodeyars of Mysore, who gained independence from Vijayanagara's nominal rule in the early seventeenth century, drew on its ceremonial traditions, including the Dasara festival. Telugu and Kannada literary cultures, Karnatak music, temple architecture, and the memory of the empire as a political ideal shaped later regional identities. Historians also caution that modern nationalist narratives have sometimes claimed Vijayanagara for particular political purposes; the evidence shows a more complex state whose rulers adopted many cultural influences.

Section 45.30

Review and Historical Synthesis

Vijayanagara began as a political response to the crisis created by the Delhi Sultanate's southern expansion and the collapse of older regional kingdoms. Harihara and Bukka combined military force, local alliances, and religious patronage to create a kingdom on the Tungabhadra, and their successors extended it across the peninsula. The empire's expansion rested on several mutually reinforcing elements: the agricultural resources of the Tungabhadra and Kaveri regions; the control of the Raichur Doab and the horse trade; a military system based on nayakas who held revenue rights in exchange for service; and a court culture that used temple patronage, ritual, and titles to present royal authority.

That same system explains the empire's characteristic structure. Authority was exercised through negotiation with powerful subordinates, local temples, and merchant groups rather than through uniform bureaucratic control. In strong reigns, such as those of Deva Raya II and Krishnadevaraya, the arrangement produced large armies, monumental architecture, and cultural patronage. In weaker moments, it created opportunities for military leaders to dominate the court, as in the Saluva and Tuluva transitions and the regency of Rama Raya. The interdependence of the center and the nayakas, and the strategic importance of Deccan frontier politics, shaped both expansion and eventual vulnerability.

The empire's religious identity was real but not exclusive. Vijayanagara rulers patronized Shaiva, Vaishnava, and Jain institutions, claimed to defend dharma, and presented themselves in Sanskrit as protectors of Hindu temples, yet they used Muslim soldiers and officials, shared elements of Persianate court culture, and negotiated with the sultanates. Conflicts with the Bahmani successors had religious dimensions but were also contests over territory, revenue, and prestige.

Talikota in 1565 destroyed the capital but not the empire's institutions. The Aravidu kings continued at Penukonda and Chandragiri, while nayakas built regional states that preserved administrative and cultural traditions. For the student of South Asian history, Vijayanagara therefore illustrates how a large premodern state could be both powerful and decentralized, and why events such as a single battle rarely explain the transformation of a political order.

This chapter has treated the empire primarily on its own terms and from its own southern center. Chapter 46 turns to the Bahmani Sultanate and its Deccan successors, whose rivalry with Vijayanagara has been narrated here from one side. Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced Vijayanagara from its foundation in the aftermath of Delhi's southern expansion to the regional Nayaka kingdoms of the seventeenth century. It has argued that the empire is best understood as a negotiated military-fiscal system rather than a uniform bureaucracy or a purely religious state.

The foundation of the kingdom around 1336 by Harihara and Bukka occurred in a landscape of collapsing dynasties: the Hoysalas, Kakatiyas, Pandyas, and Kampili had been weakened or destroyed. Conflicting traditions about the brothers' origins, including the Vidyaranya narrative, show how later writers shaped the dynasty's memory.

Under the early Sangama kings the kingdom expanded across the Tamil country, Karnataka, and parts of Andhra. The capital on the Tungabhadra developed into a major urban region. Deva Raya II and his predecessors consolidated institutions, as the accounts of Niccolò de' Conti and Abdur Razzaq indicate.

The Bahmani rivalry centered on the Raichur Doab, a prized agricultural and strategic region. The conflict combined religious language with territorial, fiscal, and political competition, and both sides employed soldiers and officials of other faiths.

Military power rested on infantry, cavalry, elephants, fortresses, and increasingly firearms, supported by imported horses and by the nayaka system, in which commanders held revenue assignments in exchange for providing troops.

Dynastic change from the Sangamas to the Saluvas and Tuluvas demonstrated that kingship depended on the support of military commanders. Krishnadevaraya's reign (1509–1529) marked the height of imperial power, with conquests against the Gajapatis and Bijapur, extensive temple patronage, and notable literary culture.

Government, economy, and society showed layered authority. Irrigation, agriculture, crafts, and merchant organizations supported the state, while caste, gender, and labor relations varied by region. The debate over centralization and segmentation shows why historians stress negotiation between center and locality.

Hampi's archaeology and architecture offer evidence for urban organization, water management, royal ceremony, and religious patronage. Temples such as Virupaksha, Vitthala, and Hazara Rama were also economic and political institutions. Literature in Sanskrit, Telugu, Kannada, and Tamil reflected the empire's multilingual world.

The Portuguese presence after 1510 and the accounts of foreign travelers illustrate Vijayanagara's commercial and diplomatic connections, as well as the problems of using traveler sources.

Rama Raya's policy of intervening in sultanate politics, and the coalition of Bijapur, Ahmadnagar, Golconda, and Bidar, led to the defeat at Talikota in 1565 and the sack of the capital. Yet the Aravidu dynasty and the Nayaka states preserved much of the empire's institutions and culture.

The central lesson is that large premodern states often relied on flexible arrangements of power, and that a single battle rarely explains a political order's transformation.

Chapter Reflection

Vijayanagara matters because it shows how a large state could be built on negotiation among military commanders, temples, merchants, and local communities. Its history complicates simple stories of religious conflict and of imperial centralization, and it shows how political order in the Deccan emerged from the interaction of Hindu and Muslim courts that shared many practices.

The empire also shows the importance of material and environmental foundations: rivers, tanks, black-soil cotton, ports, and horses. Its monuments were products of agricultural surplus, craft skill, and labor, and its fall shows how vulnerable a political system can be to military defeat and to the autonomy of its own subordinates.

Its cultural legacy was durable. Telugu and Kannada literary cultures, Karnatak music, temple architecture, and ceremonial traditions persisted through the Nayaka states, Mysore, and beyond. At the same time, the empire's memory has been claimed for modern political purposes, which makes critical study essential.

Chapter 46 now turns to the Bahmani and Deccan sultanates, the rivals and partners with whom Vijayanagara shared a political world. Chapters 50 onward, on the Mughals, will show how the Deccan's successor states and the south's Nayaka kingdoms were drawn into a still larger imperial system.
