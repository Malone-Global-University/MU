Part XI — The Eighteenth-Century Transformation

The Mughal Empire after Aurangzeb

Indian History: South Asia from the Indus Civilization to the Present

Chapter Opening

Aurangzeb died in his camp at Ahmednagar in the Deccan on 3 March 1707, after almost half a century on the throne, and was buried at Khuldabad in a grave that tradition remembers as strikingly modest. Within weeks his sons were marching against one another. Thirty-two years later, in March 1739, a Persian army occupied Delhi itself, and the city's markets and mosques became the scene of a massacre and a plunder whose memory survived for generations. Between those two moments lies the problem of this chapter: how did the largest state in the early modern subcontinent come to lose effective control over most of its territory, and what, exactly, was lost?

The conventional answer, long dominant in textbooks, was a story of decline and fall. Aurangzeb's religious policies and his protracted Deccan wars, it said, exhausted the empire; weak and dissolute successors then surrendered power to grasping nobles; foreign invaders delivered the final blows; and the resulting vacuum invited the British. That narrative drew on real evidence and was articulated by serious historians, most notably Jadunath Sarkar. But it has been revised substantially by several generations of scholarship, and this chapter takes the revision as its organizing task. The weakening of Delhi was real, consequential, and in part violent. It was not the same thing as the disappearance of political order, the collapse of commerce, or the end of Mughal political culture.

The chapter's central argument is that the eighteenth century was a period of political reconfiguration. Authority that had been concentrated, however imperfectly, in the emperor and a salaried imperial nobility was redistributed among provincial governors, military commanders, zamindars, bankers, regional courts, and new regimes that were frequently built from Mughal materials. Offices, titles, revenue instruments, and ceremonial forms continued to circulate, and many of the powers that outgrew Delhi still sought its recognition. The Mughal emperor became less a ruler who commanded than a sovereign who legitimated, and that change in function, rather than a simple disappearance of the state, is what historians now try to explain.

The chapter proceeds in five divisions. The first (§§57.1–57.4) follows the succession struggles from 1707 to 1720 and the rise and fall of the Sayyid brothers, who made and unmade emperors. The second (§§57.5–57.8) examines court factions, the reign of Muhammad Shah, and the changing balance between Delhi, the provinces, and local landholders. The third (§§57.9–57.12) turns to fiscal, agrarian, and military-institutional pressures, and to the emergence of provincial autonomy in Bengal, Awadh, Hyderabad, and elsewhere. The fourth (§§57.13–57.16) treats the invasions of Nadir Shah and Ahmad Shah Durrani and the road to the Third Battle of Panipat. The fifth (§§57.17–57.20) assesses the redistribution of power across South Asia, the persistence of Mughal legitimacy, and the historiographical debate over decline and transformation.

This chapter carries a limited responsibility within Part XI. It establishes the political framework for the chapters that follow, but it does not write them. The Maratha polity, its Peshwas and its campaigns culminating at Panipat belong to Chapter 58. Sikh military organization, Mysore under Haidar Ali and Tipu Sultan, Travancore, the Jats and the Rohillas belong to Chapter 59. The detailed histories of Bengal, Awadh, and Hyderabad belong to Chapter 60, and the European trading companies to Chapter 61. The Company's acquisition of fiscal and territorial power in Bengal belongs to Chapter 62. Where this chapter touches those subjects it does so only as far as the argument about Delhi requires.

The evidence for the period is abundant and unevenly distributed. Persian chronicles written by Mughal officials, among them Khafi Khan, Warid, and Anand Ram Mukhlis, narrate court politics from within and carry the biases of their authors' positions. Later histories such as Ghulam Husain Khan Tabatabai's Siyar al-Mutakhkhirin look back with the benefit and the distortion of hindsight. Imperial orders, revenue manuals, court newsletters (akhbarat), and the archives of regional families preserve administrative detail. Coins and seals show who was claiming sovereignty in whose name. European factory diaries and correspondence, Maratha and Rajput records, and Afghan and Iranian chronicles supply views from outside the Mughal court. No single source family gives a neutral picture, and the chapter repeatedly asks whose perspective each represents.

A word of caution about hindsight governs the whole chapter. Because the East India Company later became the dominant power in much of South Asia, it is tempting to read every eighteenth-century development as a step toward 1757, 1764, or 1857. That reading erases the choices of regional rulers, bankers, commanders, and emperors who could not know the outcome. The chapter therefore treats the period on its own terms, as one in which several futures remained open, and in which Mughal institutions were reused, contested, and reinterpreted rather than simply abandoned.

By the end of the chapter, students should be able to explain why Mughal central authority weakened after 1707, to distinguish between the breakdown of an imperial center and the reorganization of political life across a region, to weigh competing scholarly explanations, and to understand why the emperor's name continued to matter long after his armies and treasury had ceased to command obedience.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how the Mughal succession system operated and why the succession crises of 1707–1720 differed in their consequences from earlier contests.
  2. Trace the reigns of Bahadur Shah I, Jahandar Shah, Farrukhsiyar, and Muhammad Shah, identifying the political constraints each faced.
  3. Analyze how the Sayyid brothers acquired and lost power and how kingmaking changed relations between the emperor and the nobility.
  4. Distinguish court factions from fixed ethnic blocs and explain how patronage, kinship, and military followings structured noble competition.
  5. Describe the jagirdari crisis and evaluate the argument that pressure on revenue assignments weakened the imperial political system.
  6. Assess the relationship among revenue demands, zamindar power, and rural resistance without assuming a uniform agrarian crisis.
  7. Explain how governors and commanders who controlled troops and revenue became increasingly independent of Delhi.
  8. Narrate and contextualize Nadir Shah's invasion and the sack of Delhi, distinguishing contemporary testimony from disputed later estimates.
  9. Situate Ahmad Shah Durrani's invasions within wider competition for northern India and explain their consequences for Delhi.
  10. Explain why Mughal titles, farmans, coins, and ceremonies retained political value after Delhi's practical power declined.
  11. Compare the interpretations of Sarkar, Satish Chandra, Irfan Habib, M. Athar Ali, Muzaffar Alam, C. A. Bayly, and later scholars.
  12. Evaluate whether the decline of the imperial center amounted to a general collapse of eighteenth-century South Asian political and economic life.

Historical Context

Chapters 50 through 56 traced the Mughal Empire from Babur's victory at Panipat in 1526 to the cultural world of the seventeenth century. Under Akbar, as Chapters 51 and 52 explained, the empire acquired its characteristic institutions: the mansabdari system, which ranked imperial officers by numerical grades (zat and sawar); the jagirdari system, by which most high officers were paid through temporary assignments of the right to collect land revenue from designated territories; and a revenue administration, most fully developed in the northern plains, that measured and assessed agricultural production. These arrangements bound a diverse nobility of Iranian, Central Asian, Afghan, Rajput, and other origins to the emperor through service, salary, and rank. Chapter 54 showed how Aurangzeb extended the empire to its greatest territorial extent through the conquest of Bijapur and Golconda in 1686–87 while becoming mired in a long contest with Maratha forces that was never decisively resolved.

The empire Aurangzeb left in 1707 was therefore both at its largest and under visible strain. Its nominal territory extended from Kabul and Kashmir to the Coromandel coast and from Bengal to Gujarat. Its nobility had grown in size, partly through the incorporation of Deccani and Maratha elites. Its court, as Chapter 56 described, remained a major center of Persianate culture, patronage, and ceremony. At the same time, the Deccan campaigns had stretched finances and personnel, regional resistance in several theaters had not been extinguished, and officers in the field often found their assignments difficult to collect from. Whether these pressures were symptoms of a structural crisis or the ordinary friction of a very large state is one of the central questions of this chapter.

Two features of Mughal politics deserve emphasis at the outset because they shaped everything that followed. First, the succession had no fixed rule of primogeniture. Princes were expected to compete, and a prince's chance of success depended on his own forces, his alliances with nobles, and control of the capital and treasury. This had always made succession dangerous, but it had also tended to select a ruler who had demonstrated military and political ability. Second, imperial authority rested on a continuous, mutually dependent relationship among the emperor, the nobility, and local landholders. The emperor could not govern without nobles who raised and led troops and collected revenue, and nobles could not maintain their positions without the emperor's patronage and recognition. When that relationship became unbalanced, the whole system could be affected.

The chapter also draws on a rich and contested historiography. The first major scholarly narrative, Sarkar's multi-volume Fall of the Mughal Empire, emphasized political decay and the failings of rulers. From the 1950s and 1960s, Satish Chandra, M. Athar Ali, and Irfan Habib moved the discussion toward institutional and agrarian explanations. From the 1980s, Muzaffar Alam, C. A. Bayly, and others redirected attention to the regions, arguing that the eighteenth century saw the creation of new political formations rather than only the destruction of an old one. Section 57.19 examines this debate directly. Throughout the chapter, readers should note that most of the evidence comes from sources that were generated at or near the Mughal center and may therefore overstate its importance.

Division I — Succession, Dynastic Instability, and the Imperial Court

Section 57.1

The Succession Crisis of 1707

Aurangzeb died on 3 March 1707 at Ahmednagar, in his late eighties, with three surviving sons who each had a claim and a following: Muhammad Mu'azzam, who held the northwestern command at Kabul and was soon to take the title Shah Alam; Muhammad Azam, who was with his father in the Deccan; and Muhammad Kam Bakhsh, the youngest, who held a southern command at Bijapur. The Mughal dynasty had no rule of primogeniture and no tradition of settling the throne by designation alone. Princes were expected to mobilize armies, win over nobles, and secure the capital and its treasury, and the loser could expect imprisonment, blinding, or death. Aurangzeb himself had come to the throne by defeating his brothers, and Shah Jahan's sons had fought the same struggle in 1657–1658, as Chapter 53 described.

Azam moved first. He was near the emperor's camp, he commanded significant forces, and he declared himself emperor and advanced northward. Mu'azzam, farther away, proclaimed himself ruler at Kabul and marched toward Hindustan. The two armies met in June 1707 at Jajau, between Agra and Dholpur, where Mu'azzam's forces, supported by experienced nobles and artillery, defeated and killed Azam and two of his sons. Mu'azzam took the title Bahadur Shah and entered Agra, whose treasury he secured. Kam Bakhsh, who had also proclaimed himself emperor in the south and issued coins and orders in his own name, was defeated near Hyderabad in January 1709 and died of his wounds. The sequence resembled earlier succession wars in its violence and in its decisive military resolution.

What differed in 1707 was the context. The war of succession was fought by an empire whose resources had been diverted for decades into the Deccan, whose senior officers had been in the field for years, and whose treasury, according to contemporary writers, was less able to sustain a long campaign than in earlier generations. The princes' armies were drawn from the same pool of nobles, and the decision of a senior noble to support one claimant could determine the outcome. Some older nobles who had served Aurangzeb for decades were now old men who calculated carefully which candidate would confirm their rank and assignments. A successful claimant owed his throne in part to the nobles who had backed him and needed to reward them with offices, ranks, and revenue assignments. That dependence, rather than the existence of a succession struggle as such, was new in degree.

Historians therefore distinguish the immediate crisis from the longer pressures that were already present. The succession war did not itself produce the empire's institutional strains; they were visible in the later years of Aurangzeb, when contemporary writers complained of the difficulty of finding productive assignments for officers, of troops left unpaid, and of persistent resistance in several regions. The succession struggle exposed those strains and added a new one, namely that a ruler's authority now rested more explicitly on the continuing support of powerful nobles. The evidence for these events comes chiefly from Persian histories written by Mughal officials, notably Khafi Khan's Muntakhab al-Lubab, together with later compilations and the records of Rajput and European observers. William Irvine's early twentieth-century Later Mughals remains a detailed narrative of the sequence, though it reflects the assumptions of its time.

It would be misleading to read the events of 1707 as the beginning of an inevitable collapse. Bahadur Shah, a mature and experienced prince in his mid-sixties, took the throne as a recognized claimant with real political skills, and many contemporaries evidently expected him to stabilize the empire. The next section examines how far he succeeded, and why his short reign nonetheless left the underlying questions unresolved.

Section 57.2

Bahadur Shah I and the Limits of Imperial Restoration, 1707–1712

Bahadur Shah entered the throne with an agenda of conciliation. After the exhausting confrontations of Aurangzeb's final decades, many writers describe the new emperor as willing to negotiate with rulers and communities that had been in conflict with Delhi. His reign lasted only five years, and it is difficult to separate what he intended from what circumstances allowed. The assessment has varied considerably. Older historians, following Khafi Khan's critical tone toward later Mughals, saw him as weak and indecisive; more recent writers have emphasized the genuine constraints on a sovereign who inherited an overextended military establishment and a court full of ambitious nobles.

His relations with the Rajput rulers illustrate both the aims and the limits of his approach. The rulers of Marwar (Jodhpur) and Amber (Jaipur), who had been alienated during Aurangzeb's reign and in particular by the disputes over the Marwar succession, took advantage of the confusion in 1707 to reassert control over their territories. Bahadur Shah marched into Rajasthan in 1708 and, after negotiation, restored their status and recognized them within the imperial system, though the arrangement left their relations with Delhi more conditional than in the age of Akbar. A subsequent combination among Rajput rulers, who pressed their claims during the contest for influence at court, showed that these accommodations rested on practical advantage rather than on trust. Chapter 35 and Chapter 54 provide the longer background to Rajput political culture and Mughal-Rajput relations.

The confrontation with the Sikh forces led by Banda Singh Bahadur was more violent. Banda, who emerged in Punjab after the death of Guru Gobind Singh in 1708, led a movement that drew support from cultivators and others and that attacked Mughal officials in the region. In May 1710 his forces defeated the Mughal governor of Sirhind, Wazir Khan, and occupied the town. Bahadur Shah, who was then in Rajasthan, marched north in person and forced Banda from his strongholds, most notably at Lohgarh in December 1710. Banda escaped into the hills and was not captured until 1715, during the reign of Farrukhsiyar. The Sikh uprising and its social base are discussed in Chapter 59; for present purposes its significance lies in showing that provincial resistance could take territory, administer it, and force the emperor to campaign in person.

The reign also revealed the growing importance of senior nobles. Several powerful figures, among them Zulfiqar Khan and the wazir Munim Khan, retained influence, and appointments, promotions, and the allocation of assignments were constant sources of friction. Bahadur Shah's attempt to manage these relationships required constant negotiation and did not yield a settled balance. Late in 1711 he went to Lahore, where he died in February 1712 without having arranged the succession. His death immediately produced a new contest among his four sons, and the brief stability of his reign dissolved. Historians generally treat the reign as a period in which restoration was attempted and found to be more difficult than expected, rather than as an opportunity wasted through simple weakness.

Section 57.3

The Struggle for the Throne, 1712–1719

When Bahadur Shah died in February 1712, his four sons, Jahandar Shah, Azim-ush-Shan, Rafi-ush-Shan, and Jahan Shah, contested the throne. This struggle differed from the war of 1707 in an important respect: it was decided less by the princes' own military gifts than by the combinations of nobles who backed them. Zulfiqar Khan, the mir bakhshi and the son of a long-serving wazir, organized an alliance of the princes against Azim-ush-Shan, who was the most powerful of the brothers and who held major provincial positions. The coalition defeated and killed Azim-ush-Shan near Lahore, and the remaining princes then fought among themselves. Jahandar Shah emerged as emperor in March 1712, with Zulfiqar Khan as his wazir, and the rival claimants were eliminated.

Jahandar Shah's reign lasted less than a year. Contemporary writers, including Khafi Khan, portray him as dominated by his favorite, Lal Kunwar, and as profligate in the distribution of titles and revenue, and these characterizations have shaped later judgments. They deserve caution: Khafi Khan wrote from the perspective of an official who disapproved of the reign's perceived lack of discipline, and criticism of royal favorites formed a conventional moral vocabulary in Persian historical writing. Zulfiqar Khan himself pursued a program that included arrangements with Rajput and Maratha leaders and attempts to reduce expenditure, but his policies alienated others at court and weakened his base.

Farrukhsiyar, the son of Azim-ush-Shan, challenged Jahandar Shah from Bengal and Bihar, where he had been stationed. He was supported by two brothers, Sayyid Abdullah Khan and Sayyid Husain Ali Khan, who were officials of high rank in the eastern provinces. Their forces defeated Jahandar Shah near Agra in January 1713. Jahandar Shah and Zulfiqar Khan were taken to Delhi and executed. Farrukhsiyar, who thus gained the throne largely through the military power of the Sayyids, appointed Abdullah Khan as wazir and Husain Ali Khan as mir bakhshi, giving them control of the civil and military branches of government.

The relationship between Farrukhsiyar and the Sayyids was uneasy from the beginning. The emperor sought to assert independence by building alliances with other nobles who resented the brothers' power, and the brothers in turn sought to control appointments and to remove officials who opposed them. The reign saw significant developments, including the capture and execution of Banda Singh Bahadur in 1715–1716, the abolition of the jizya in 1713 (a decision that Chapters 52 and 54 place in the longer history of Mughal religious policy), and the grant in 1717 of a farman to the English East India Company confirming trading privileges in Bengal, discussed in Chapter 61. But the central dynamic was the continuing struggle between emperor and ministers. In February 1719 the Sayyids, supported by Maratha contingents sent to Delhi under an arrangement discussed in Chapter 58, deposed Farrukhsiyar. He was imprisoned, blinded, and killed in the following weeks. The Sayyids then installed two young princes in succession, Rafi ud-Darajat and Rafi ud-Daula, both of whom died within months, and in September 1719 they enthroned Roshan Akhtar as Muhammad Shah.

These sequences matter because they changed the terms of political competition. In 1707 the winner of a succession war was a prince with an army. After 1712 the throne was increasingly shaped by nobles who could create and remove emperors, and the evidence for this comes mainly from narrative histories that, as the following Source Window shows, view these events through the lens of officials who both participated in and were affected by them.

Primary Source Window

Khafi Khan and the Later Mughal Court

Text: Muntakhab al-Lubab (Selected Essence) · Author: Muhammad Hashim, known as Khafi Khan · Date: completed in the early 1730s · Language: Persian · Source type: narrative court history by a Mughal official

Context: Khafi Khan served in the Mughal administration and wrote a history of the dynasty from Babur through his own time. His account of Aurangzeb's reign and of the successors to 1707 and after is among the most detailed Persian narratives of the period. Aurangzeb is reported to have discouraged official court history in his later years, and Khafi Khan's decision to compile a history on his own initiative shapes both his perspective and his evidence: he drew on personal knowledge, conversations with officials, and earlier writings. His narrative of the later Mughals was written with the benefit of hindsight, a few decades after the events and in the context of the disorders of the 1720s and 1730s.

Editorial paraphrase (not a verbatim translation): Khafi Khan repeatedly attributes the instability of the post-1707 reigns to the conduct of rulers and favorites, to the conflicts among powerful nobles, and to the failure of officials to carry out their duties. He is sharply critical of Jahandar Shah's court and of the Sayyid brothers' control over Farrukhsiyar, and he treats the loss of discipline as a moral and political failure. (Any verbatim passage must be verified against an identified edition or translation before publication.)

Analysis: The work is indispensable for the sequence of events and for the attitudes of an administrator who worked within the system. Its limitations are equally important. Khafi Khan wrote as a member of the imperial service, with loyalties, patrons, and grievances that shaped his judgments, and he used the conventional moral vocabulary of Persian historiography, in which dissolute rulers and grasping favorites explain political misfortune. His narrative gives little attention to rural society, to non-Persian sources of authority, or to the experience of those outside the court, and it tends to frame institutional strains as personal failings.

Questions for students:

  1. What kind of explanation for political change does Khafi Khan prefer, and why might an official favor it?

  3. What can historians learn from the work about court politics and administrative expectations?

Section 57.4

The Sayyid Brothers and the Politics of Kingmaking

Sayyid Abdullah Khan and Sayyid Husain Ali Khan, known together as the Sayyid brothers or the Barha Sayyids from their origin in the Barha region of the upper Doab, were the most influential figures in Delhi from 1713 to 1720. They belonged to a family with a long history of imperial military service, and their power rested on a combination of military following, regional connections in the Gangetic plain and Bengal, and control over key offices. Abdullah Khan as wazir managed the revenue administration and the emperor's household, while Husain Ali Khan as mir bakhshi controlled military appointments and stood at the head of a large following. Together they constituted what contemporaries and later historians have described as a ministry holding the emperor in tutelage.

Their position was not simply a matter of personal ambition. The Sayyids needed support from other nobles to maintain their position, and they cultivated alliances with Rajput rulers, with Hindu administrators, and with some Maratha and other leaders. Their policies included conciliatory measures toward non-Muslim subjects, among them the abolition of the jizya, and arrangements with the Marathas under which Shahu received recognition of his claim to collect chauth and sardeshmukhi in the Deccan provinces, a settlement whose significance Chapter 58 examines. The brothers also faced determined opposition, especially from nobles who regarded them as upstarts and from those who resented their control of patronage. Farrukhsiyar himself spent years trying to dislodge them, and the resulting maneuvers at court occupy much of the narrative of his reign.

The deposition and killing of Farrukhsiyar in 1719 was a decisive moment. It was not unprecedented for a Mughal emperor to be removed, but the removal of a reigning emperor by his own ministers, and the subsequent installation of successive young princes who were treated largely as figureheads, was a significant departure. The two short reigns of Rafi ud-Darajat and Rafi ud-Daula, who both died in 1719, were followed by the installation of Roshan Akhtar as Muhammad Shah, a teenager whose accession the Sayyids hoped to control. The pattern of ministerial kingmaking showed that political legitimacy still required a Timurid prince on the throne, but that the choice of who occupied it could be determined by powerful nobles.

Opposition to the Sayyids grew among nobles who feared their influence, notably Nizam-ul-Mulk and Muhammad Amin Khan, together with other senior officers, and among the Turani nobility. In 1720 Husain Ali Khan, who was accompanying the young emperor on a campaign toward the Deccan, was assassinated in October by a conspirator. Abdullah Khan raised another claimant and marched against the imperial party but was defeated after fighting at Hasanpur on 13–14 November 1720 and captured. The Sayyid ascendancy ended, and with it the most direct instance of ministerial control over the emperor.

The episode shaped political expectations afterward. Emperors who survived learned to distrust over-mighty ministers, and nobles learned that control over the capital, the army, and the treasury could, in the right circumstances, determine who ruled. At the same time, the fall of the Sayyids did not restore the earlier relationship between emperor and nobility; instead it left the court to be shaped by competing groups, the subject of the next section.

The brothers' use of an emperor from the established dynasty is especially revealing. They did not generally seek to replace Mughal kingship with a new state ideology. Instead they claimed to operate the existing state more effectively than the sovereign whose signature and ceremonial presence they still required. Control of the palace, the capital's garrison, and access to princes therefore mattered as much as nominal command of an office. Their enemies likewise argued that they were defending legitimate monarchy rather than proposing a new constitutional order. In this struggle, the person of the emperor could be constrained while the institution of emperor remained indispensable. This helps explain why the brothers installed new sovereigns rather than openly abolishing the throne.

Division II — The Weakening of Imperial Authority

Section 57.5

Court Factions, Noble Households, and Imperial Competition

Writers of the period and later historians describe the Mughal court as divided into factions, and the most common labels are Turani, Irani, and Hindustani. The Turanis were nobles of Central Asian origin, the Iranis of Iranian origin, and the Hindustanis, a category covering a range of others, were nobles of Indian-born families, including Indian Muslims such as the Sayyids, Afghans, and Rajputs and other Hindu officers. The labels were real in the sense that contemporaries used them, but they were never fixed or unified blocs. Satish Chandra's study of the court from 1707 to 1740 showed that factional alignments were fluid, that families and individuals changed sides when interests shifted, and that the groups were far from internally cohesive.

What held factions together were interests and relationships rather than shared origin. A noble's position depended on patronage: appointments to provincial and military offices, the allocation of revenue assignments, and the favor of the emperor and ministers. Nobles maintained households that included relatives, clients, soldiers, and dependents, and these followings were an important source of military and political strength. Marriages, shared service, and obligations of loyalty created networks that could be mobilized in disputes. Faction in this sense was an ordinary structure of political competition in a state that distributed power largely through personal ties.

Competition was sharpened by scarcity. As later sections explain, the number of officers seeking productive assignments grew faster than the revenue available to satisfy them. A faction that gained control of the wazirate or the mir bakhshi's office could shape the distribution of assignments and positions, and those who lost out faced decline. The struggles of the 1710s and 1720s thus had an institutional dimension: they were contests over who controlled the machinery of patronage and over how a limited stock of revenue was divided. Describing them simply as the failings of selfish courtiers, as many chroniclers did, obscures that structural pressure.

Factions also had regional dimensions. Nobles with strong regional connections, such as Nizam-ul-Mulk in the Deccan and Saadat Khan in Awadh, had alternatives to continued dependence on the court, and the possibility of building a regional base shaped their calculations. A noble who could draw on resources in a province was less vulnerable to loss of influence at Delhi, and some chose to move away from the center altogether. The development of regional power, discussed in §57.12, was in this way connected to the competition at the court.

Historical Vocabulary
Faction, Khanazad, and the Turani–Irani–Hindustani Labels

Khanazad (literally "house-born") designated nobles whose families had served the dynasty for generations and who enjoyed claims to patronage on that basis. Turani, Irani, and Hindustani were labels for groups of nobles by region of origin. In practice these were shorthand for networks of patronage and kinship rather than permanent political parties.

The terms matter because they are easily misread as ethnic or religious blocs with fixed interests. Historians use them cautiously: nobles of the same label often competed, and alliances regularly crossed them. Modern ideas of national or ethnic identity should not be projected onto these categories.

A closer view of noble households also complicates the conventional language of faction. The following of a senior noble was maintained through gifts, stipends, military employment, recommendations, and expectations of protection. Secretaries and revenue officials could move information and money; cavalry commanders could bring armed men; provincial contacts could provide refuge when court appointments changed. These relationships were expensive and had to be renewed. A nominally prestigious appointment that yielded insufficient resources might therefore destroy a noble's credit with dependents. Factional politics were linked to the capacity to pay and protect followers, not merely to proximity to the emperor. That connection between political influence and fiscal resources becomes particularly important in the jagirdari debate.

Section 57.6

Muhammad Shah and the Problem of Restoring Central Power, 1719–1748

Muhammad Shah reigned for nearly three decades, longer than any other eighteenth-century Mughal emperor, and his reign is central to the question of whether restoration was possible. He came to the throne as a teenager under the Sayyids' control and, after their fall in 1720, had to rebuild the court's working relationships. Nobles who had opposed the Sayyids, led by Muhammad Amin Khan and Nizam-ul-Mulk, now held the most influential positions. Muhammad Amin Khan became wazir and, after his death in 1721, the office was administered through interim arrangements while Qamar-ud-din Khan pursued his own claims; Nizam-ul-Mulk assumed the wazirate in 1722 and attempted a program of reform.

The record of that reform is revealing. Nizam-ul-Mulk, an experienced noble who had served in the Deccan, sought to restore discipline, regularize assignments, and strengthen administration. He found, according to the chroniclers, that he faced resistance from a court he regarded as lax and from rivals who resented his influence. Within two years he left Delhi for the Deccan, where in 1724 he defeated a rival appointed by the emperor, Mubariz Khan, at the battle of Shakkar Kheda. He thereafter ruled the southern provinces in practice autonomously while formally acknowledging the emperor's authority, and his career is explored further in §57.12 and Chapter 60. His departure showed that a leading noble who could not reshape the center might choose to build his own base elsewhere.

The long middle of Muhammad Shah's reign was a period of relative internal calm at Delhi and of political fragmentation in the provinces. The emperor made appointments, presided over a court of considerable cultural vitality, and patronized poets, musicians, painters, and scholars. The nickname "Rangila" ("the colorful"), by which he is often remembered, comes from later tradition and reflects the view that he was devoted to pleasure; it should be treated with the caution due to retrospective characterizations, since the same patronage sustained a vibrant culture of Urdu and Persian poetry, music, and painting, and the emperor's court remained a significant political center. Political fragmentation and cultural patronage were not contradictory: the court's ceremonial and artistic life continued while its military and fiscal capacity eroded.

The emperor's capacity to act was also limited by the financial and military circumstances addressed in the following sections. Provincial governors increasingly controlled the troops and revenues in their regions, remitted less to the center, and made their own arrangements with local powers. The Marathas, under Peshwa Baji Rao I, extended their influence into Malwa and Gujarat and in 1737 raided Delhi's environs; the emperor's reliance on Nizam-ul-Mulk to meet them produced the Maratha blockade and defeat of his position near Bhopal in December 1737, followed by the agreement of 7 January 1738 over Malwa. These developments, treated more fully in Chapter 58, preceded the more dramatic crisis of 1739.

Muhammad Shah's reign thus represents neither a simple restoration nor a simple failure. He survived politically, restored the stability of the court, and sustained the dynasty's symbolic and cultural prestige, but he could not reconstitute the fiscal and military foundations of earlier imperial power. He died in April 1748, after the invasions of Nadir Shah and during the first of Ahmad Shah Durrani's interventions.

The setback at Bhopal also exposed a problem of political coordination. The imperial court could summon a celebrated former minister who commanded substantial Deccan resources, but it could not easily dictate his financial interests, alliances, or strategy. Negotiations after campaigns were often conducted by men whose regional commitments survived any temporary return to imperial service. The court consequently continued to confer appointments and issue orders while increasingly depending on bargains it could not enforce unilaterally. This was not a total loss of administrative capacity: royal confirmations could still resolve disputes or provide valuable titles. Yet the distance between courtly authorization and territorial implementation grew. The episode should be understood as one expression of that broader separation, not as a self-contained defeat caused by the personal weakness of Muhammad Shah.

Section 57.7

The Fragmentation of Imperial Military and Administrative Control

An important distinction in the study of this period is between formal sovereignty and effective capacity. The emperor remained the formal source of appointments and titles, and provincial offices continued to be conferred in his name. But in practice the ability to command troops, collect revenue, and enforce decisions increasingly depended on those who held local resources. The imperial military consisted largely of contingents raised and led by nobles, who were paid or supported through their assignments. A noble who controlled his own revenue and troops could act with considerable independence, and the emperor lacked a large standing force of his own with which to compel obedience.

Provincial offices illustrate the change. The subadar or nazim was the governor, the diwan was in charge of revenue and was in principle appointed from Delhi independently of the governor, and the faujdar held local military and police authority. The system was designed to give the center several channels of control and to prevent any one official from accumulating all powers. In the eighteenth century these offices were increasingly combined or held by the same person or by members of the same family, and governors acquired the right to nominate subordinates. Bengal under Murshid Quli Khan, who held the office of diwan and later of nazim, and Awadh under Saadat Khan, who combined governorship with other positions, are notable examples, studied in detail in Chapter 60.

This did not mean that Delhi had ceased to matter. Governors continued to seek confirmation of appointments, to send remittances in varying amounts, and to use the emperor's name in coins, correspondence, and public ceremonies. The relationship was one of negotiated autonomy: a provincial ruler needed the legitimacy that imperial recognition offered, and Delhi needed the revenue and order that provincial rulers could provide. The balance between these needs shifted over time and varied by region. In Bengal substantial remittances continued for some decades, while in the Deccan they were more irregular and in the northwest more affected by invasion.

The result was an empire of uneven texture. In some areas imperial offices operated more or less as before, in others they were transformed into hereditary regional authorities, and in still others Delhi's claims were ignored or contested. The pattern should not be generalized from a single example. Historians such as Muzaffar Alam have emphasized that the process involved not simply the weakening of authority but its reconstitution at a regional level, a point taken up in §57.12 and §57.19.

An imperial appointment retained legal and ceremonial meaning, but its effectiveness depended on a chain of practical intermediaries. A subadar had to secure cooperation from revenue officers, forts, influential families, transport suppliers, and local armed groups. Orders that passed from Delhi to a provincial capital still had to be translated into action by district officials and by zamindars who possessed local coercive power. Communications across the empire could be slow, especially during campaigns and seasonal disruption. A governor who held the purse and the garrison was well placed to interpret an instruction selectively. The resulting fragmentation did not everywhere follow provincial borders: individual districts and routes might answer to different authorities, while imperial officers could retain influence in a province whose governor acted autonomously.

Section 57.8

Zamindars, Local Authorities, and the Changing Foundations of Power

Below the level of governors and nobles lay the zamindars, a broad category (discussed in Chapter 55) that included hereditary landholders with rights to collect revenue and exercise local authority, and ranged from small village-level intermediaries to rulers of substantial territories. Zamindars were essential to the Mughal revenue system, which depended on them to collect from cultivators and to maintain order, and many kept armed followings. Their relationship with the imperial state was one of cooperation and contest, since they both served as its agents and resisted its demands when they conflicted with their own interests.

In the eighteenth century the bargaining position of zamindars and other local power-holders often strengthened. As imperial officials, whose assignments were temporary, had less ability to enforce collection, and as governors sought local allies, those who controlled land, forts, and men had more room to negotiate. In some areas zamindars gained greater control over revenue and acquired formal recognition from regional governments; in others they expanded their territories by absorbing neighbors. Rajput rulers, Jat leaders, Rohilla commanders, and many lesser landholders fit this pattern in different ways, and several of the movements discussed in Chapters 58 and 59 grew from such local foundations.

The consequences of this change were uneven. Where a strong regional government emerged and managed to incorporate zamindars into a stable arrangement, as in parts of Awadh and Bengal, local authority and the regional state could reinforce one another. Where no such arrangement succeeded, zamindars could become sources of instability, resisting revenue demands and fighting for territory. The same development, the growth of local power, therefore produced consolidation in some regions and disorder in others. Scholars differ on how to characterize it: Irfan Habib stressed the oppressive revenue burden and the resulting conflicts, while Alam and Bayly gave more weight to the way zamindars, merchants, and officials participated in building new regional orders, a debate examined in §57.10 and §57.19.

The key point for this chapter is that the foundations of effective authority were shifting downward and outward. Political power increasingly depended on the capacity to mobilize local resources and to bargain with local elites, and less on appointment from the imperial center. That shift underlies the fiscal and institutional pressures examined in the next division.

Power at village and district level cannot be understood solely as opposition to the state. Local intermediaries frequently sought an imperial grant or the recognition of a powerful provincial government to strengthen their claims against competitors. They supplied troops, horses, provisions, and intelligence as well as taxes; in some cases they also controlled fortified settlements and trading routes. The bargaining position of a landholding lineage was shaped by its ability to attract cultivators and retain revenue-producing land. Where authority was contested, a peasant might face competing demands from an appointed collector and a local chief, but in other places a negotiated settlement could reduce uncertainty. These divergent outcomes caution against treating every region as subject to a single process of rural decay.

Division III — Fiscal Pressures, Institutional Crisis, and Provincial Autonomy

Section 57.9

The Jagirdari Crisis and the Competition for Revenue Assignments

Chapters 51 and 52 described how the Mughal state paid most of its high officers. A mansabdar held a numerical rank that fixed his salary and the number of troops he was expected to maintain, and his salary was normally met by a jagir, an assignment of the right to collect the land revenue of a specified territory. A jagir was supposed to yield a revenue, assessed in advance as its jama, matching the officer's salary. Officers were regularly transferred from one jagir to another so that they would not build local roots. The crown retained some territory as khalisa land, whose revenue went directly to the treasury. The system worked as long as the number of officers and the productive land available to pay them stayed in rough balance.

The scholarly argument about the jagirdari crisis, associated above all with Satish Chandra and M. Athar Ali, holds that this balance broke down in the later seventeenth century. Athar Ali's analysis of the Mughal nobility under Aurangzeb showed that the number of mansabdars, and especially of high-ranking ones, grew substantially, particularly after the conquest of Bijapur and Golconda, which brought large Deccani and Maratha contingents into the imperial service. The conquered territories, however, were slow to be brought under effective administration and were often difficult to collect from, and the crown also retained significant areas as khalisa. The result was a gap between the revenue assessed on paper and the revenue that could actually be realized, the distinction between jama and hasil. Officers received assignments whose realized yield fell short of their entitlements.

Contemporary writers noticed the problem. Observers in Aurangzeb's last years commented on the scarcity of productive jagirs, on officers who held rank but had no assignment (bejagiri), and on territory kept in reserve (paibaqi) rather than distributed. The consequence, as Chandra argued, was intensified competition for the best assignments, frequent disputes over transfers, and pressure on the cultivators and local intermediaries within a given jagir, since an officer expecting a short tenure and an uncertain yield had incentives to extract as much as possible quickly. Where assignments were difficult to realize, officers turned to arrangements such as ijara, the farming out of revenue collection to contractors who paid a fixed sum, and to the acquisition of more permanent local interests. These responses tended to loosen the link between the imperial assignment and the center's control.

The argument has been influential and also criticized. Some historians, notably Alam, have questioned whether a structural crisis of the jagirdari system explains the specific political outcomes of the eighteenth century, pointing out that the nobility adapted flexibly in some regions and that provincial governors who consolidated local power did not simply collapse under the pressure of competition. Others have observed that the evidence for the late seventeenth century is stronger than for the eighteenth and that the interpretation depends partly on how one reads imperial revenue statistics, which were compiled for administrative purposes and may not give a precise picture of actual collections. What the debate establishes is that competition for revenue assignments was a real feature of Mughal politics, that it intensified after the Deccan conquests, and that it formed part of the context in which court factions and provincial autonomy developed. Its relative weight as a cause remains disputed and is the subject of the first Historical Debate below.

Historical Vocabulary
Jagir, Paibaqi, Jama and Hasil, and Ijara

A jagir was a temporary assignment of land-revenue rights to an officer in lieu of salary. Paibaqi referred to land held in reserve for later assignment. Jama was the assessed revenue and hasil the amount actually collected; the gap between them is central to arguments about the crisis. Ijara denoted revenue farming, the contracting out of collection for a fixed payment.

These terms help explain why an officer's nominal rank might not translate into real income. They should not be equated with European feudal fiefs: jagirs were assignments of revenue, not hereditary landholdings, though in practice some became more durable than the system intended.

Section 57.10

Agrarian Tensions, Revenue Extraction, and Rural Resistance

The fiscal pressures of the imperial system were felt most directly in the countryside, and Irfan Habib's Agrarian System of Mughal India (first published in 1963) built an influential interpretation around the relationship between revenue demand and rural resistance. Habib argued that the Mughal state took a large share of the agricultural surplus, often a half or more of the produce in the fertile northern plains according to his reading of the evidence, and that the pressure on peasants, together with the instability caused by frequent transfers of jagirs, led to flight, impoverishment, and revolt. In this view the uprisings of the late seventeenth and early eighteenth centuries, among them those of the Jats near Agra and Mathura, the Satnamis, the Sikhs, and others, reflected an agrarian crisis in which peasants and zamindars joined in resisting imperial demands.

Satish Chandra's earlier work connected the agrarian situation to the jagirdari crisis: officers under pressure raised demands on cultivators, and the resulting resistance undermined the revenue base on which the entire system rested. Together these arguments offered a materialist explanation for imperial weakening that stressed structural pressures over the personalities of rulers. They also placed rural society at the center of political history, a significant shift from narratives focused on the court.

Subsequent scholarship has qualified the picture in several ways. First, the movements Habib and Chandra grouped together had varied social bases and aims. The Sikh movement involved religious identity and institutional development as well as agrarian grievance; Maratha forces combined local landholders, military entrepreneurs, and a developing state; Jat leaders were often zamindars seeking to consolidate local power. Treating them uniformly as peasant risings flattens these differences. Second, regional studies by Alam, Bayly, and others have shown that agrarian conditions varied: some areas experienced expanding cultivation, active commercial markets, and new rural elites, while others saw conflict. Third, the evidence for high revenue demands and their effect is stronger for some northern regions than for others, and it is difficult to establish a consistent empire-wide trend.

A cautious reading holds that revenue extraction and rural resistance were real and significant in certain regions and periods, that they interacted with the jagirdari pressures of the officer class, and that local landholders were important actors rather than passive victims. It does not support the assumption that every rural conflict reflected the same underlying crisis or that agricultural production declined everywhere. For some areas the evidence indicates growth in cultivation, trade in cash crops, and the rise of new market towns during the same decades in which Delhi's authority was weakening, a point that returns in §57.11 and §57.19.

Everyday Life and Social History

Living through Political Change: Village, Town, and Capital

For most inhabitants of the empire, the changes of the early eighteenth century were experienced locally rather than as the fall of a dynasty. A cultivator might find that the revenue collector who arrived each season was a different official, a contractor, or the agent of a local zamindar, with different expectations about what was due and different willingness to bargain. In years of poor rain, a village's capacity to meet a fixed demand could decide whether families stayed or moved to another village or to a town. Where several authorities competed, cultivators sometimes found room to negotiate; in other places they faced multiple demands.

Towns and markets show a more varied picture. Some administrative towns lost patrons when governors left or armies moved on, while market towns and regional capitals attracted merchants, bankers, and artisans. Delhi itself had a large population, a rich culture of poetry, music, and painting, and a dense network of households tied to the court, and the sack of 1739 and later occupations brought real losses of life and property, as §57.14 describes. Soldiers, who could be paid irregularly or not at all, formed another group affected by fiscal strain; unpaid troops could become a source of disorder or switch to a better-paying patron. These accounts remain partial, because most surviving evidence was produced by officials, courtiers, and merchants and only rarely records the views of cultivators, laborers, or women.

The chronology of agrarian resistance further qualifies a simple cause-and-effect story. Conflict between imperial officials and zamindars existed long before 1707, and peasant protest might originate in a disputed assessment, a claim to inheritance, damage from soldiers, or the exclusion of a local community from political office. A successful regional leader could incorporate cultivators into a new coalition while continuing to demand substantial taxes. For that reason a rebellion against imperial authority should not automatically be interpreted as a rebellion against exploitation, and its outcome should not automatically be classified as an improvement in rural welfare. To evaluate the social consequences, historians ask which groups gained control of land and revenue, how payments changed, and whether farmers could continue to cultivate in security.

Section 57.11

Military-Fiscal Decentralization and the Changing Imperial State

The Mughal state was a military-fiscal state in the sense that its capacity to field troops depended directly on its capacity to collect revenue, and the two were tied together through the assignment system. A commander who controlled revenue controlled the means to pay soldiers, and a soldier's loyalty ran primarily to the officer who paid him. This arrangement worked to the center's advantage as long as officers rotated among assignments and depended on imperial appointment. It worked less well when officers acquired stable local resources, since they could then maintain troops and bargain with the center from a position of relative strength.

Several developments encouraged that decentralization. The prolonged Deccan war had put large forces in the field under commanders who had to find ways to support them locally. Persistent regional challenges required governors to maintain troops on the spot. And as remittances to Delhi became harder to enforce, governors had reason to spend their revenues on their own forces rather than send them north. A governor who commanded a strong army and controlled revenue could ignore orders he disliked and make local arrangements with landholders and other powers. The center, in turn, had few means of enforcement short of mobilizing another noble against him, a strategy that tended to increase rather than reduce the number of independent armies.

The changing military environment reinforced this process. Throughout South Asia, military entrepreneurs, from Maratha commanders to Rohilla and Afghan captains to Rajput and Jat chiefs, raised forces that could be hired or allied with, and the increased availability of cannon, muskets, and professional infantry in the course of the century altered the balance between local powers and the center. These are topics in later chapters, but their significance here is that military manpower had become a market in which many regional rulers could participate, rather than a monopoly held by Delhi.

It is important not to equate the decline of the imperial treasury with a decline in all economic activity. Historians who have studied regional economies, notably Bayly in his work on north Indian towns and trade and others who have examined textile production, banking, and commerce, found that many regions experienced continued or even expanding commercial activity in these decades. Revenue that no longer flowed to Delhi was often retained and spent regionally, supporting local courts, armies, and markets. What had declined was the imperial center's access to resources, not necessarily the productivity of the regions it had once taxed. This distinction is a crucial part of the case for reading the century as a reconfiguration, and it is the point on which the second Historical Debate turns.

Historical Debate

What Caused the Mughal Imperial Crisis?

The question: Why did Mughal central authority weaken after 1707: because of failures of rulers and nobles, or because of deeper structural pressures on the empire's institutions?

Interpretation A — Dynastic, factional, and moral explanations: Jadunath Sarkar, whose Fall of the Mughal Empire remained the standard narrative for decades, stressed the character of rulers and nobles, the corruption and factionalism of the court, and the consequences of Aurangzeb's policies. This view drew on the Persian chroniclers, who themselves explained events largely through personal conduct and moral decline.
Interpretation B — Fiscal, jagirdari, agrarian, and military-institutional explanations: Satish Chandra, M. Athar Ali, and Irfan Habib argued that structural strains explain the crisis: the growth of the nobility relative to available revenue, the gap between assessed and collected revenue, and the burden of revenue demands on cultivators. On this view, factionalism was a symptom, not a cause, of competition over scarce resources.

Evidence: The first view relies on narrative histories and court accounts; the second on revenue statistics, mansab lists, and agrarian records. Critics of the structural view, including Alam and Bayly, note that the evidence for system-wide crisis is uneven and that regional outcomes varied, while defenders point to the pattern of complaints across multiple sources.

Why it matters: The answer affects whether the weakening of Delhi appears as the avoidable result of poor leadership, the predictable outcome of institutional strain, or the product of several interacting causes. Most current accounts favor the last, giving significant weight to structural pressures while allowing a role for political choices.

Military decentralization involved more than the movement of soldiers away from the capital. To field a campaign, an officer needed reliable credit, grain, draft animals, fodder, mounts, and routes of supply; money assigned in a distant district was not equivalent to cash available to pay men now. Commercial lenders and merchant suppliers could help bridge that gap, but dependence on them brought another set of negotiations. Forts and artillery required specialized labor, while cavalry followings depended on relationships of patronage and land revenue. A provincial ruler who could promise regular payment often attracted experienced imperial officers. The rebuilding of armies under regional authorities was therefore both a consequence of the center's difficulties and a cause of the growing autonomy of those authorities.

Section 57.12

Provincial Autonomy and the Emergence of Successor Regimes

The combination of weakened central control, local resource control, and military entrepreneurship produced a series of regimes that historians call successor states. The term is useful but potentially misleading, since it can suggest that these states simply replaced the empire. Many of their rulers continued to hold Mughal offices, to use Mughal titles and administrative terms, and to acknowledge the emperor's nominal sovereignty. Alam emphasized that their autonomy developed within the framework of Mughal political culture rather than in opposition to it. This section offers only an institutional overview; Chapter 60 provides the detailed histories.

Bengal offers one pattern. Murshid Quli Khan, who had been appointed diwan of Bengal under Aurangzeb, became in effect the dominant provincial authority, holding the governorship from 1717. He maintained the regular remittance of revenue to Delhi for some years while controlling appointments and cultivating alliances with local landholders and with banking families. His successors continued to rule with substantial autonomy and the office became hereditary in practice, though formal imperial confirmation continued to be sought. Awadh offers a second: Saadat Khan, appointed governor in 1722, established a regime in which he held the offices of governor and revenue administrator and passed his authority to a relative, with the emperor's recognition. Hyderabad offers a third: Nizam-ul-Mulk established his authority in the Deccan after 1724, and his house retained the Mughal title Asaf Jah while acting as an independent ruler.

What these cases share is the conversion of an imperial office into a regional base. The governors acquired the right to appoint subordinates, to control the troops stationed in their province, and to collect revenue, and they made it difficult for Delhi to replace them. They did not, as a rule, declare independence outright. Their continued acknowledgment of the emperor was practical: it provided legitimacy in dealing with local elites and with other powers, and it allowed them to claim that they governed on his behalf. A governor could thus claim an imperial title while exercising practical independence, and the arrangement could suit both sides.

The regimes differed in origin, resources, and relationship with Delhi. Some, like Awadh and Hyderabad, were founded by officials from the Mughal nobility; others, such as those of Maratha, Sikh, Jat, and Rohilla leaders, emerged from different social and political foundations and are treated in Chapters 58 and 59. The distinction between breakdown and restructuring is essential here. Muzaffar Alam's study of Awadh and the Punjab argued that the crisis of the empire in these provinces involved the attempt to preserve Mughal political arrangements under changed circumstances, with local elites and governors reconstituting authority rather than abandoning it. Not every region fits this model, but it has become an influential way of understanding the period.

An imperial governor could negotiate with merchants, lenders, and locally entrenched landed families on his own account while still seeking confirmation from Delhi. Division IV — Foreign Invasions and the Reshaping of Northern India

Section 57.13

Nadir Shah, Iran, and the Northwestern Frontier

The invasion of 1739 grew from events in Iran and Afghanistan as much as from conditions in Delhi. Nadir Quli, of the Afshar Turkmen tribe of Khurasan, rose to prominence as a military commander in the collapse of the Safavid dynasty. After Afghan Ghilzai forces had overthrown the Safavids at Isfahan in 1722, Nadir first served the Safavid claimant Tahmasp II, drove out the Afghan occupiers around 1729–1730, and fought Ottoman and Russian forces on Iran's western and northern frontiers. He deposed Tahmasp in 1732, ruled for a time as regent for an infant Safavid prince, and in 1736 was proclaimed shah, founding the Afsharid dynasty. His power rested on a large, mobile army and on revenues extracted with considerable force from Iranian and conquered territories.

The northwestern frontier of the Mughal Empire was the link between these developments and Delhi. The region of Kabul, Ghazni, and Peshawar had been a Mughal province since Babur's time, while Kandahar, a strategic fortress and trading center, had been disputed between the Mughals and the Safavids for more than a century and had passed back and forth between them. Chapters 50 through 53 described the earlier importance of this frontier. By the early eighteenth century, Mughal frontier garrisons, which had once been supported by substantial revenue assignments and by payments to Afghan tribal chiefs, were reported to have fallen into neglect, and the Mughal court's attention was focused elsewhere.

Nadir's campaign against the Ghilzai Afghans of Kandahar, which ended with the capture of the city in 1738, brought him to the border of Mughal territory. Fugitives from Kandahar sought refuge in Mughal-held Kabul and Ghazni, and Nadir demanded that the Mughal court not shelter them. Contemporary accounts report that his envoys received no satisfactory reply and that one of them was killed or detained on the frontier, though accounts differ in the details. Nadir then advanced into Mughal territory, taking Ghazni and Kabul in 1738 and Peshawar by the end of the year, with little effective resistance from the imperial garrisons.

It is misleading to reduce the invasion to a quest for treasure, though the wealth of Delhi was certainly known and plunder was a major consequence. Nadir needed resources to maintain his army and to fund continuing wars, and the Mughal capital was an obvious source. But he also pursued strategic aims: to secure his eastern frontier, to prevent Afghan groups from using Mughal territory as a refuge, and to demonstrate his power to rivals. The invasion also reflected a Mughal frontier policy that had weakened, and a court that, according to the sources, did not mobilize in time. Writers in Delhi circulated stories that certain nobles, especially Nizam-ul-Mulk and Saadat Khan, had invited Nadir to intervene; such reports appear in several sources but are difficult to verify and reflect the suspicions of a divided court. The next section turns to the events themselves.

The frontier itself must be treated as a shifting political zone, not merely a line that invaders crossed. The Mughal court's ability to influence the Afghan highlands had long depended on payments, alliances, military posts, and negotiated passage. As Iranian and Afghan rulers competed to control Kandahar, Kabul, and trade routes, officials in Punjab were forced to respond to threats that Delhi might recognize too late. The dispute over refugees gave Nadir Shah a diplomatic justification, but access to resources, security on the eastern approaches to his empire, and the prestige of defeating a wealthy neighbor also mattered. The invasion thus grew from regional geopolitics interacting with Mughal military weakness; neither the invader's ambition nor the court's failure alone is a sufficient explanation.

Section 57.14

The Battle of Karnal and the Sack of Delhi, 1739

After Peshawar, Nadir's forces advanced to Lahore, whose governor Zakariya Khan submitted in January 1739, and then crossed the Punjab toward Delhi. Muhammad Shah, belatedly roused, set out from the capital with a large army that included contingents under Nizam-ul-Mulk, Qamar-ud-din Khan, and Khan Dauran, and was joined by Saadat Khan of Awadh. The two armies met at Karnal, about a hundred kilometers north of Delhi, in February 1739. The Mughal force was larger in numbers according to most accounts, but it was encumbered by its camp and baggage and was divided in command. The battle, fought on 24 February, was decided in a few hours. Saadat Khan's contingent arrived late and attacked piecemeal, Khan Dauran was killed, and Nadir's disciplined cavalry and musketeers overcame the Mughal forces. Numerical estimates of the armies and of casualties vary widely among sources and should not be treated as precise.

Negotiations followed. Nizam-ul-Mulk mediated, and Muhammad Shah met Nadir and was taken, in effect, into custody, though treated with ceremony. The two moved toward Delhi, which Nadir entered on 20 March 1739. Saadat Khan, who had been captured and who was blamed by some for encouraging Nadir to demand a large indemnity, died in Delhi soon afterward. Persian troops were quartered in the city, officials began to collect an indemnity, and Nadir's name was reportedly read in the khutba and used on coinage, a gesture of sovereignty that is discussed in §57.18.

On 21 March a rumor spread in the city that Nadir had been killed, and violence broke out in which Persian soldiers were attacked. On 22 March Nadir responded by ordering a general massacre, which according to accounts continued for several hours until Muhammad Shah and Nizam-ul-Mulk pleaded for its end. Persian troops killed many of the city's inhabitants, plundered houses and markets, and set fires in several quarters. Estimates of the death toll range from the low tens of thousands upward, and later accounts tend to inflate them; historians treat the numbers with caution while accepting that the loss of life and property was severe. After the massacre, officials and nobles were pressed to deliver money and valuables, and the search for wealth continued for weeks. Nadir left Delhi in May 1739, taking with him, according to the sources, an immense quantity of cash, jewels, and treasures, including the Peacock Throne and the Koh-i-Noor diamond, and large numbers of craftsmen and elephants. Estimates of the value of his plunder differ greatly and are not reliable to any precision.

Before leaving, Nadir restored Muhammad Shah to the throne and formally confirmed his rule, but he secured the cession of the territories west of the Indus. The consequences were significant even though Mughal rule in Delhi continued. The treasury lost a large proportion of its accumulated reserves, imperial prestige was severely damaged, the military's weaknesses were exposed, and the northwest, from which Kabul had already been lost, was now open to further pressure. Nadir's remission of revenue demands in his own domains for a period, reported by some sources, reflects the scale of the wealth he took. The invasion did not by itself cause the Mughal empire's decentralization, which had begun earlier, but it accelerated the weakening of the center and reduced its capacity to respond to later challenges.

Sources for the events fall into groups with distinct perspectives. The official Persian chronicle of Nadir's reign, by Mirza Mahdi Astarabadi, presents the campaign as a legitimate and successful enterprise. Accounts by Mughal writers, among them eyewitnesses in the service of the court, describe the shock and humiliation of the defeat and the suffering of the city, while Indian merchants and European observers added further details. The next Source Window examines the problem of reading such testimony.

Section 57.15

Ahmad Shah Durrani and the Afghan Invasions

Nadir Shah was assassinated by his own officers in June 1747, and his empire fragmented. One of his commanders, Ahmad Khan of the Abdali Afghans, who had led a contingent of Afghan cavalry in his service, withdrew to Kandahar with his forces and was chosen as ruler by a gathering of Afghan leaders in the autumn of that year. He took the title Durr-i Durran, and his tribe came to be known as the Durranis, giving the name to the polity he founded. Ahmad Shah consolidated power in Kandahar, Kabul, and neighboring regions, and he needed revenues to sustain an army and to bind Afghan leaders to his rule. The prosperous plains of Punjab and the wealth of northern India were obvious objectives.

His first invasion of Hindustan, in the winter of 1747–1748, ended in a Mughal success: forces under Prince Ahmad Shah Bahadur, Qamar-ud-din Khan, and the Punjab governor Mu'in ul-Mulk (known as Mir Mannu) repulsed him at Manupur near Sirhind on 11 March 1748, though Qamar-ud-din Khan was killed. Muhammad Shah died the following month. Ahmad Shah returned in 1749 and compelled the Punjab authorities to transfer the revenue of several districts, and in 1752, after continued pressure and the weakness of the Delhi court, which was convulsed by struggles among its ministers, the Mughal government agreed to cede or assign the revenues of Punjab and Multan to him. Mir Mannu continued to govern at Lahore, now in effect as a Durrani appointee, until his death in 1753.

Events in Delhi added to the instability. The court was divided between rival ministers, among them the wazir Safdar Jang of Awadh and the Mughal commander Imad ul-Mulk, and in 1754 Imad ul-Mulk deposed and blinded the emperor Ahmad Shah Bahadur and installed Alamgir II. The Durrani invasion of 1756–1757 exploited this weakness. Ahmad Shah occupied Delhi in January 1757, plundered the city, and sent forces into the surrounding region, including an attack on Mathura and Vrindavan that contemporary sources describe as violent. He confirmed Alamgir II as emperor, left Najib ud-Daula, a Rohilla commander, as a key power in the capital, and installed his son Timur Shah as his representative in Lahore.

These invasions should be placed within the wider competition for northern India rather than as isolated episodes of external destruction. Ahmad Shah dealt with local and regional actors, including Mughal officials, Rohilla and other Afghan leaders in the Doab, the ruler of Awadh, Sikh groups in Punjab, and the Marathas, who were advancing from the south. He was invited into Indian politics by some of these actors, among them Najib ud-Daula and, in 1760, others who feared Maratha dominance, and his interventions cannot be understood apart from those alliances. His polity also had its own needs: its stability depended on revenues and plunder that the plains could provide, and its attention was divided between Afghanistan, Iran, and the Indian frontier.

The consequences for Delhi were substantial. The Punjab, which had been a rich Mughal province and a source of revenue and soldiers, was now largely outside effective imperial control. The capital had been repeatedly occupied and plundered. Provincial rulers and regional powers looked less to the emperor and more to one another and to the new strategic reality of Durrani power. The imperial court survived but with a sharply diminished range of action.

Section 57.16

The Road to Panipat and the Limits of Mughal Imperial Power

By the late 1750s, northern India had become a field of competition among several powers, none of which was the Mughal emperor. The Marathas, under the Peshwa's leadership and with commanders such as Raghunath Rao and Malhar Rao Holkar, had moved north, intervened in Delhi politics, and in 1758 drove Durrani garrisons out of Lahore and the Punjab, advancing as far as Attock. Chapter 58 treats this expansion in detail. Ahmad Shah, for his part, regarded the loss of Punjab as a direct challenge. Najib ud-Daula and other Rohilla and Afghan leaders in the Doab, and Shuja ud-Daula of Awadh, whose interests were threatened by Maratha ambitions, were drawn toward the Durranis, while Rajput and Jat rulers pursued their own calculations.

Within Delhi the situation deteriorated further. In November 1759 Imad ul-Mulk, the wazir, had Alamgir II killed, and a prince who was then outside the capital, Ali Gauhar, was proclaimed emperor as Shah Alam II while living away from Delhi, in the east. The court thus lost both a stable ruler and the capacity to act. Ahmad Shah returned to northern India in 1759, occupied Delhi in early 1760, and positioned himself between the Maratha army, commanded by Sadashiv Rao Bhau and accompanied by Peshwa's son Vishwas Rao, and its sources of supply.

The two armies met at Panipat on 14 January 1761, the site of earlier battles in 1526 and 1556. The Marathas suffered a major defeat; Vishwas Rao and many commanders were killed, and the army was largely destroyed. The detailed campaign, the logistics, the composition of the coalitions, and the battle itself belong to Chapter 58. For this chapter, the significance of Panipat lies in its consequences for the balance of power. The Marathas' northern expansion was checked for a decade, though not ended; Ahmad Shah, rather than annexing Delhi, returned to Afghanistan, leaving Najib ud-Daula as the main authority in the capital and acknowledging Shah Alam II as emperor; and no single power replaced Mughal authority as paramount in northern India.

That outcome underlines the limits of Mughal imperial power by the 1760s. The emperor had no army capable of deciding the contest and was a subject of the arrangements others made. Shah Alam II was absent from Delhi for years, living later under the protection of Shuja ud-Daula and, after the battle of Buxar in 1764 and the arrangements of 1765 discussed in Chapter 62, at Allahabad. He returned to Delhi in January 1772 with Maratha support. The imperial title nonetheless remained a political resource, a point taken up in §57.18.

Cause and Effect

The Weakening of Delhi's Authority, 1707–1761

Long-term causes

Growth of the nobility relative to assignable revenue; strain from the Deccan wars; dependence of emperors on military nobles; limited imperial standing forces; strong local landholders; weak frontier garrisons.

Short-term causes

Succession wars; ministerial kingmaking; factional competition; the departure of leading nobles to provincial bases; delayed Mughal response to Nadir and Ahmad Shah.

Immediate effects

Loss of treasure and prestige in 1739; loss of control over Punjab and Multan; repeated occupation of Delhi; emperors made and unmade by ministers.

Long-term consequences

Redistribution of authority among provincial and regional powers; continued use of Mughal legitimacy; a political landscape in which later European companies could participate as one actor among several.

Historical complexity: No single cause explains the outcome. Structural pressures, political choices, and external shocks interacted, and the results differed by region.

Panipat also shows the difference between winning a major battle and constructing a durable empire. The Durrani coalition's victory over the Maratha army did not yield continuous Afghan government throughout the northern plains; Ahmad Shah withdrew to territories that demanded his attention elsewhere. The Marathas, though badly shaken, retained important resources in western and central India and eventually recovered political influence in the north. In Delhi, meanwhile, competing ministers and military patrons continued to determine the conditions under which the emperor could be recognized. The battle transformed bargaining positions but resolved neither the fiscal weakness of the Mughal center nor the problem of who could claim authority across the subcontinent. This conclusion prepares students for the regional histories that follow without treating 1761 as the predetermined arrival of British rule.

Division V — Mughal Legitimacy, Regional Transformation, and Historical Interpretation

Section 57.17

The Redistribution of Political Power across South Asia

The weakening of Delhi did not produce a single regional outcome. By the middle of the eighteenth century, political authority across the subcontinent was divided among a number of regimes of very different kinds, and the relationships among them were increasingly complex. This section offers a map of that landscape, with the detailed treatment reserved for the chapters that follow.

The Maratha polity, built from the kingdom of Shivaji and led by the Peshwas from Pune, expanded across western and central India and into the north, collecting revenue claims such as chauth and sardeshmukhi and building a confederacy of commanders (Chapter 58). Sikh military groups organized in Punjab and, after the Durrani retreats, gradually established control over its territory (Chapter 59). Jat leaders around Agra and Bharatpur and Rohilla commanders in the Doab built local polities, while Rajput rulers maintained their states and negotiated with Delhi and the Marathas. In the south, Mysore under Haidar Ali was emerging as a major military state (Chapter 59). Autonomous provincial governments in Bengal, Awadh, and Hyderabad, as discussed in §57.12, were forming their own courts and administrations (Chapter 60).

These powers varied in their origins. Some were led by former Mughal nobles who converted office into hereditary authority; some by military leaders of regional communities; some by chiefs who consolidated lands and forts. They also differed in how they treated Mughal institutions: some reproduced the mansabdari-jagirdari framework, some adapted it, and others built different arrangements. Relationships among them were far from uniformly hostile. Alliances, marriages, tribute, and shared use of imperial titles linked even rivals, and a regional ruler might be at once an opponent of one neighbor and an ally of another.

Taken together, the pattern was one of multipolarity. No single power, whether Maratha, Afghan, Mughal, or any other, could dominate the whole of the subcontinent, and the competition itself drove military and administrative innovation, including the growth of trained infantry, artillery, and more systematic revenue arrangements, topics for the following chapters. The weakening of Delhi opened political space in which many actors operated. The European trading companies, which Chapter 61 examines, entered that space as one set of participants among many, a fact that guards against reading the period only as a prelude to British conquest.

Multipolarity also changed how political actors understood geography. A provincial center might control its surrounding countryside but depend on tolls or negotiated passage to move an army through a neighbor's territory. Important roads, river crossings, grain markets, and fortified towns could become points of competition rather than routine components of an imperial communications system. The commercial effects varied: warfare interrupted some movements, while new courts and armies generated demand for horses, cloth, weapons, and credit. No single indicator describes all these transactions. Political change redistributed opportunities as well as burdens, making it necessary to study regional markets and local government separately from the fortunes of Delhi.

Section 57.18

The Persistence of Mughal Sovereignty, Titles, and Political Legitimacy

Even as the emperor lost the ability to command, imperial recognition continued to matter. Provincial governors sought formal appointments and confirmations; regional rulers received titles and robes of honor; coins continued to bear the emperor's name and regnal year in mints far from Delhi; and the khutba, the Friday sermon invoking the sovereign, continued to name the Mughal ruler in many places. These forms were not empty. In a political culture that associated rightful authority with imperial grant, the emperor's sanction helped rulers deal with subordinates, rivals, and subjects, and it linked their authority to a long tradition of Timurid sovereignty.

Historians therefore distinguish symbolic sovereignty from effective territorial government. The first persisted long after the second had receded. A governor might send little revenue to Delhi yet carefully secure a farman confirming his office, because the confirmation strengthened his position against rivals. Other powers used Mughal legitimacy too. The Marathas, as noted in §57.4, obtained formal recognition of their revenue claims through imperial grants, and later in the century Mahadji Shinde would hold imperial offices as the protector of Shah Alam II. Even a power like the Durranis, which had its own claims to sovereignty, acknowledged a Mughal emperor in Delhi rather than replacing him.

The dynasty survived the century in this reduced condition. Shah Alam II, who returned to Delhi in January 1772, was blinded in 1788 by a Rohilla commander, Ghulam Qadir, and was taken under British protection after 1803. The Mughal line continued in Delhi until Bahadur Shah II was removed after the Rebellion of 1857 (Chapters 69 and 70). The later Company conquest is not narrated here; the point for this chapter is that Mughal sovereignty, titles, and ceremonial retained political value even when the emperor's army and treasury were gone, and that this value was recognized by Indian and European powers alike.

Historical Vocabulary
Farman, Sanad, Khutba, and Sikka

A farman was an imperial order or grant issued in the emperor's name; a sanad was a grant of office, rank, or rights; the khutba was the Friday sermon that named the ruler; and sikka was the right of coinage, normally in the sovereign's name. Together they were the principal forms through which sovereignty was publicly claimed and recognized.

They matter here because they show how legitimacy could survive the decline of command. A reader should avoid assuming that a ruler who used the emperor's name was necessarily obedient to him.

Mughal Authority from Aurangzeb to Shah Alam II

What Changed

  • Provincial offices became hereditary or near-hereditary regional bases.

Who experienced change most directly? The forms of legitimacy deserve careful distinction. A farman was an imperial command; a sanad might record an entitlement or appointment; a khilat or robe of honor publicly marked recognition; coinage and the khutba proclaimed claims to sovereignty. Their precise legal and ceremonial meanings varied by place and period. None by itself established that the emperor possessed troops capable of enforcing his will. Yet these documents and acts could help settle rival claims, recruit allies, or make a new government appear continuous with the established order. In 1717 even the English East India Company sought concessions through imperial authority, while the later conflict over implementation revealed how powerful provincial interpretation had become.

Section 57.19

Mughal Decline or Eighteenth-Century Transformation?

The historiography of the eighteenth century has been one of the most vigorous in South Asian studies. The first great modern account was Jadunath Sarkar's four-volume Fall of the Mughal Empire, completed in the mid-twentieth century and based on extensive Persian and Marathi sources. Sarkar traced a narrative of decay from Aurangzeb's reign onward, with emphasis on the failings of rulers and nobles, the intrigues of the court, and the damage done by invasions, and he regarded the period as one of political and social deterioration. His work established the chronology and drew attention to a wide range of sources, and its influence long outlasted the particular judgments it contained.

From the 1950s a different set of questions emerged. Satish Chandra's Parties and Politics at the Mughal Court (1959) analyzed factions as expressions of structural competition and linked the court to the jagirdari crisis. Irfan Habib's study of the agrarian system (1963) argued that heavy revenue demands and rural resistance were central to the crisis, and M. Athar Ali's work on the nobility (1966) documented the growth of the officer class and the pressure on assignments. These historians, whose approach is often described as structural or materialist, agreed that the roots of imperial weakness lay in the institutions and economy of the empire itself rather than in the characters of its rulers, and they shared a conviction that the crisis was real and consequential. Athar Ali revisited this dispute in “Recent Theories of Eighteenth Century India” (1986–87), and, more explicitly, in “The Mughal Polity—A Critique of Revisionist Approaches” (1993). He questioned explanations that in his view undervalued the coercive and institutional foundations of imperial authority while acknowledging the need to examine regional evidence.

The revisionist movement, associated with Muzaffar Alam, C. A. Bayly, and others, shifted attention from the center to the regions. Alam's Crisis of Empire in Mughal North India (1986) argued that in Awadh and Punjab the instability of the early eighteenth century involved a restructuring in which governors, zamindars, and local elites negotiated new arrangements, and that the empire's institutions continued to shape regional politics. Bayly's Rulers, Townsmen and Bazaars (1983) and Indian Society and the Making of the British Empire (1988) highlighted the vitality of merchants, bankers, and service communities, and argued that commerce and urban life continued to develop in many regions. Later work by scholars such as Stewart Gordon, Chetan Singh, Jos Gommans, and others, and the essays collected by P. J. Marshall in The Eighteenth Century in Indian History: Evolution or Revolution?, extended and qualified these arguments, examining Maratha and Rajput states, military organization, and the economic evidence.

The debate turns on several questions. One is the interpretation of evidence: the structural school relies heavily on revenue and administrative records, while revisionists use regional chronicles, family papers, and commercial records, and each set of sources illuminates different parts of the picture. Another is scale. Decline at the imperial center is well documented, but whether it implies general economic or social decline depends on which regions and which indicators are considered; evidence for expanding commerce and state-building in some regions does not erase evidence of disruption and violence in others, including Delhi and the northwest. A third is the meaning of the terms: "decline" can describe the fortunes of a dynasty, the capacity of a state, or the condition of a society, and these are not identical. Most historians now see the eighteenth century as a period both of imperial breakdown and of regional reconstruction, and the argument is over the weight given to each, and over the continuity between the Mughal and successor orders.

The revisionist position has itself been criticized. Some scholars argue that emphasis on regional vitality can understate the real suffering caused by warfare and fiscal pressure and the genuine loss of imperial integration, and that evidence for commercial continuity is stronger for some regions than for others. These criticisms are part of an ongoing discussion, and the best-supported accounts avoid both a narrative of general collapse and an uncritical celebration of regional dynamism.

Historical Debate

Did the Decline of Delhi Constitute a General Collapse of Eighteenth-Century South Asia?

The question: Does the weakening of the Mughal center indicate a general political and economic collapse, or a reorganization of authority at regional levels?

Interpretation A — Fragmentation and disruption: Sarkar and, in different ways, the structural historians emphasized the breakdown of imperial order, the violence of invasion and civil conflict, and the strains on the countryside, arguing that the loss of a unifying state damaged security and prosperity and left the subcontinent vulnerable.
Interpretation B — Regional state-building and commercial continuity: Alam, Bayly, and others emphasized the creation of effective regional governments, the continued vigor of trade, banking, and urban life, and the reuse of Mughal institutions, arguing that the decline of Delhi did not entail general collapse.

Evidence: Interpretation A draws on Persian narratives, revenue records, and accounts of invasion; interpretation B draws on regional chronicles, family papers, and commercial records. The evidence differs in coverage and does not point uniformly in one direction.

Why it matters: The answer shapes how historians explain later developments, including the ability of external powers to expand, and whether those developments are read as the consequence of collapse or as the outcome of competition among several viable states.

The argument is not settled by choosing one historian and declaring an entire century either prosperous or impoverished. A sound comparison begins by defining the unit of analysis. Section 57.20

Review and Historical Synthesis

This chapter has traced the Mughal Empire from the death of Aurangzeb in 1707 to the aftermath of Panipat in 1761, and the argument that has run through it can be stated in several linked propositions. Succession struggles after 1707, and especially the ministerial kingmaking of the Sayyid brothers, changed the relationship between the emperor and the nobility, making the throne dependent on powerful military nobles. Court factions, understood as networks of patronage rather than ethnic blocs, competed for a limited stock of offices and assignments, and the competition had a structural dimension rooted in the pressures on the jagirdari system.

Fiscal and agrarian strains, military decentralization, and the growing bargaining power of zamindars and regional commanders shifted effective authority to the provinces. Governors in Bengal, Awadh, and Hyderabad converted imperial offices into regional bases while continuing to acknowledge the emperor. The invasions of Nadir Shah in 1739 and of Ahmad Shah Durrani from 1748 exposed and deepened the center's weakness, caused severe suffering in Delhi and the northwest, and removed Punjab from effective Mughal administration. By 1761, no power could claim paramountcy in northern India.

Yet the chapter has also argued that these developments should be read as a reconfiguration of authority rather than a disappearance of political order. Provincial regimes used Mughal institutions and language, many regions maintained or expanded commerce, and the emperor's name, titles, and farmans continued to confer legitimacy on rulers who did not obey him. The collapse of effective central control was a major historical transformation, but the redistribution of authority did not mean that all political institutions, commercial networks, and cultural systems disappeared. The historiography reflects this: older narratives of decline, structural accounts of fiscal and agrarian crisis, and revisionist emphases on regional state-building each capture part of the picture.

The chapter also leaves open questions that later chapters take up. The Maratha expansion, which reached its peak and met its greatest reverse at Panipat, is the subject of Chapter 58. Chapters 59 and 60 examine the Sikh, Mysore, Jat, Rohilla, and other regional powers and the provincial regimes of Bengal, Awadh, and Hyderabad in greater detail, and Chapter 61 turns to the European companies that were becoming participants in this competitive political world. The transition to the Company's acquisition of fiscal and territorial power in Bengal belongs to Chapter 62, and it should be understood as one development within the multipolar landscape this chapter has described, not as its inevitable conclusion.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the political reconfiguration of South Asia between the death of Aurangzeb in 1707 and the Third Battle of Panipat in 1761. It began from a historiographical problem: whether the weakening of the Mughal center should be described as civilizational collapse, or as the redistribution of authority among new and old political actors. The evidence supports the second description more than the first, without denying the real damage done to the imperial center and to many communities.

The succession war of 1707, in which Bahadur Shah I defeated his brothers, resolved the throne quickly but left the new emperor dependent on powerful nobles. His reign sought conciliation with Rajput rulers and confrontation with Banda Singh Bahadur's Sikh uprising, and it ended in 1712 with another contest among the princes. From 1712 to 1719 succession was increasingly determined by noble coalitions, culminating in the rise of the Sayyid brothers, who made and removed emperors until their defeat in 1720.

Court politics in these decades was structured by factions that were networks of patronage, not fixed ethnic blocs. The reign of Muhammad Shah (1719–1748) restored some stability at Delhi and sustained a vigorous cultural life, but it could not rebuild the fiscal and military capacity of the earlier empire. Nizam-ul-Mulk's departure to the Deccan illustrated how senior nobles could build regional bases.

Effective authority shifted toward governors, commanders, and local powerholders. Zamindars and other landholders gained bargaining power, and provincial offices became hereditary or near-hereditary. The distinction between formal imperial sovereignty and actual administrative and coercive capacity became central to political life.

Institutional and fiscal explanations, associated with Satish Chandra, M. Athar Ali, and Irfan Habib, stress the growth of the nobility relative to assignable revenue, the gap between assessed and realized revenue, and agrarian pressures. Critics and revisionists such as Muzaffar Alam and C. A. Bayly argue that the evidence for empire-wide crisis is uneven and that regional restructuring and commercial continuity were as significant as breakdown. The chapter has presented both without declaring a single cause.

Nadir Shah's invasion, culminating in the defeat at Karnal in February 1739 and the massacre and plunder of Delhi in March, exposed the weakness of Mughal frontier defense and coordination and damaged imperial treasury and prestige. Ahmad Shah Durrani's repeated interventions from 1748, and the cession of Punjab and Multan in 1752, removed the northwest from effective Mughal control and placed Delhi under repeated pressure.

By 1761, when Durrani and allied forces defeated the Marathas at Panipat, northern India was a field of competition among several powers, none of them the Mughal emperor. The wider landscape included Maratha, Sikh, Jat, Rohilla, Mysorean, and provincial regimes, whose histories are told in Chapters 58 through 60, and European trading companies, treated in Chapter 61.

Mughal legitimacy nevertheless persisted. Farmans, titles, coinage, and the khutba continued to confer authority on rulers who no longer obeyed the emperor, and the dynasty survived in reduced form into the nineteenth century. Distinguishing symbolic sovereignty from effective government is essential to understanding the period.

The chapter's source base consists of Persian chronicles written from within or near the court, administrative and revenue records, regional archives, and European and Iranian accounts. Each reflects its author's position, and later retrospective works such as the Siyar al-Mutakhkhirin add hindsight. Careful use of this evidence supports a picture in which the collapse of central control was a major historical transformation, but one in which institutions, commerce, and culture continued in new forms.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it asks students to resist one of the most familiar stories in South Asian history, that of an empire falling and a conqueror waiting. The evidence shows a more complicated process in which a powerful center lost command, authority moved outward, and many political actors made choices whose outcomes were not foreordained.

The chapter reveals processes that recur in other periods: the dependence of states on the cooperation of armed elites, the way fiscal arrangements shape political loyalty, and the capacity of legitimacy to outlast power. It also shows how different regions of South Asia experienced the same weakening differently, from the violence of Delhi and the northwest to the state-building of several provinces.

Its place in the six-volume series is that of a hinge. It closes the story of the Mughal imperial order as a functioning center and opens the multipolar eighteenth century that the next chapters explore, beginning with the Marathas. Understanding that the Mughal framework remained a shared language of politics helps explain why later powers, including European ones, engaged with it.

Chapter 58 now turns to the Maratha polity, whose rise and reverses form one of the principal strands of the century.
