Part VII — Regional Kingdoms and the Early Medieval Order

Temples, Land, Caste, and Commerce in Early Medieval South Asia

Indian History: South Asia from the Indus Civilization to the Present

Chapter Opening

Sometime in the early eleventh century, in the Kaveri delta of the Tamil country, officials of the Chola kings had a village’s revenue recorded on sheets of copper, strung them on a ring sealed with the royal emblem, and handed them to the community of a Buddhist monastery. The monastery stood at the port of Nagapattinam; it had been built with the support of a ruler of Srivijaya, a maritime kingdom across the Bay of Bengal; and the document recording its endowment was written in Sanskrit and Tamil. A single object thus joined a Tamil agrarian village, a royal court, a Buddhist institution, and a Southeast Asian king. Such documents are the raw material of this chapter.

Chapters 31 through 36 followed the regional kingdoms of early medieval South Asia: Chalukyas and Rashtrakutas in the Deccan, Pallavas and Cholas in the Tamil south, Palas and Pratiharas in the north and east, Rajput polities in the northwest, and the Himalayan, Kashmiri, and Sri Lankan states. Those chapters gave dynastic and political narratives. This chapter asks a different question: what sustained those states? What was happening in fields, villages, temples, workshops, markets, and harbors beneath the courts and battlefields? Rulers needed revenue, soldiers, literate officials, ritual legitimacy, and local allies. The answers lay in how land was cultivated and how rights over it were distributed, in the settlement of Brahmins and the growth of temples, in the social groups that performed labor and were ranked against one another, and in the merchants who carried goods between the interior and the sea.

The chapter’s central argument is that early medieval South Asia, broadly between about 600 and 1300 CE, underwent a broad but regionally uneven transformation. Cultivation expanded into new tracts. Rulers and local elites reassigned rights over land and revenue. Brahmins and religious institutions became important participants in regional state formation. Occupational and social identities became increasingly localized. Merchants linked inland production to expanding commercial networks. These developments cannot be reduced to simple economic decline, nor to a single model of “Indian feudalism.” Political decentralization, agrarian expansion, temple-centered institutions, social differentiation, monetized exchange, and long-distance commerce could coexist, and often did.

The evidence for these processes is unusual in its form. Historians rely heavily on inscriptions: copper-plate charters recording grants, and stone inscriptions engraved on temple walls recording gifts, disputes, assemblies, and tax arrangements. These are supplemented by legal digests, chronicles such as Kalhana’s history of Kashmir, accounts by Arab, Persian, and Chinese travelers and geographers, documents preserved in the Cairo Geniza, and archaeological evidence from temples, tanks, and ports. Most of this evidence was produced by or for elites and institutions. Cultivators, laborers, and women appear mainly as recorded in other people’s documents, a limitation that this chapter keeps in view throughout.

The chapter also asks students to think carefully about the vocabulary of historical explanation itself. For more than half a century, historians have argued over whether early medieval India should be described as “feudal,” whether its towns and trade declined, and how far Brahmins and temples reorganized rural society. These debates are not merely academic. They shape how the thousand years between the Guptas and the Delhi Sultanate are understood: as a period of fragmentation and stagnation, or of regional creativity and structural change. Section 37.17 treats this historiography in full, and the chapter closes in Section 37.18 by showing how the pieces fit together before Chapter 38 turns to the arrival of Islam in Sindh and the early Muslim presence in South Asia.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why early medieval South Asia did not have a single uniform economy, and describe the major regional variations in agrarian and commercial life.
  2. Trace the processes of agrarian expansion and explain how rulers, Brahmins, temples, and local communities participated in them.
  3. Distinguish among ownership of land, claims on revenue, and bundles of administrative, fiscal, and judicial rights in the interpretation of land grants.
  4. Analyze the roles of Brahmins and brahmadeya settlements in regional state formation without treating Brahmins as a uniform or merely passive group.
  5. Assess the temple as a landholding, ritual, and economic institution, and explain why it should not be reduced to a “bank” or an autonomous corporation.
  6. Reconstruct the lives and obligations of peasants and laborers from inscriptional evidence, while recognizing the limits of elite records.
  7. Distinguish varna from jati and explain how occupational and regional communities became increasingly visible in the record.
  8. Evaluate the evidence for the roles of women as workers, property holders, donors, and patrons.
  9. Describe merchant corporations, markets, towns, and ports, and connect inland production to Indian Ocean exchange.
  10. Interpret the evidence of coinage, taxation, and revenue without assuming that fewer coins meant less commerce.
  11. Compare major interpretations of early medieval society, including the “Indian feudalism” model and its critics, and identify the evidence on which each depends.
  12. Connect agrarian expansion, land grants, religious institutions, social hierarchy, and commerce into an integrated explanation of regional political order.

Historical Context

The social and economic order of the early medieval centuries was built on foundations laid earlier. By the Gupta age (Chapters 26–30), land grants to Brahmins and religious institutions were already recorded, as Chapter 27 showed, and the Satavahana and Vakataka periods offer earlier examples (Chapter 22). Temple worship, which Chapter 28 traced through Puranic religion, was becoming a major focus of royal and community patronage. The merchant organizations and maritime routes examined in Chapters 24 and 25 had linked ports on the Arabian Sea and Bay of Bengal with the Roman, Southeast Asian, and Chinese worlds. What changed after the sixth century was less the invention of these institutions than their scale, spread, and combination.

Politically, the period was marked by the emergence of many regional kingdoms rather than a single imperial center. Chapters 31 through 36 followed those kingdoms in turn. Their rulers governed through a mixture of direct administration and delegated authority, relying on subordinate chiefs, often called samantas, on local assemblies, on kin and military followers, and on religious specialists who legitimated royal power. Geography mattered. Rainfall-fed rice and millet zones, river deltas, black-soil cotton lands, desert-edge pastoral regions, mountain valleys, and long coastlines supported very different economies, and a statement true of the Kaveri delta might not hold for Rajasthan or the Kathmandu Valley.

Students should also understand the character of the evidence. Inscriptions survive unevenly across regions and periods. The Tamil country has thousands of temple-wall inscriptions, while some regions have far fewer, and the survival of records does not map neatly onto economic importance. Most inscriptions record gifts and privileges, not ordinary transactions. Historians therefore infer broader patterns from partial and elite-centered evidence, and different historians have drawn different conclusions from the same body of material. This chapter treats the period as roughly 600 to 1300 CE, with the understanding that processes began and ended at different times in different regions. It does not retell dynastic history, which belongs to Chapters 31–36, and it does not narrate the arrival of Muslim communities and conquerors in Sindh and beyond, which is the subject of Chapter 38.

Section 37.1

The Social and Economic Foundations of the Early Medieval Order

A reader who has worked through Chapters 31 to 36 may come away with an impression of kings, dynasties, and wars. Cholas fought Chalukyas, Pratiharas contested Kannauj, Kashmir’s rulers campaigned across the mountains, and Sri Lankan kings built reservoirs and monasteries. Yet none of these states could have operated without what their inscriptions take for granted: villages that produced grain and paid dues, specialists who performed rituals and kept accounts, artisans and laborers who built and maintained temples and tanks, and merchants who moved cloth, spices, horses, and metals. This chapter examines those foundations.

The first point to establish is that there was no single “early medieval economy.” A Chola village in the Kaveri delta, dependent on irrigated rice and recorded in detail on temple walls, differed profoundly from a Rajput fortress zone on the edge of the Thar, from a Bengal delta settlement in the Pala realm, or from a terraced valley in Nepal. Rainfall, soil, rivers, distance from the coast, and the strength of local elites all produced different arrangements. Historians therefore speak of regional patterns and of processes that recur in different forms, not of one uniform system.

Three broad developments recur across regions, though not everywhere at once. The first is agrarian expansion: new lands were brought under cultivation and new settlements founded. The second is the reassignment of rights: kings, queens, chiefs, and officials transferred claims over land and revenue to Brahmins, temples, monasteries, and followers, creating layered structures of rights. The third is commercial and social localization: regional markets, merchant corporations, and occupational communities took recognizable local forms, even as ports and long-distance trade grew. Each development shaped the others, and the remaining sections trace them in turn before returning to their combined effect.

This chapter proceeds from land to institutions to people to commerce. Sections 37.2 through 37.5 treat agrarian expansion, land grants, and Brahmin settlement. Sections 37.6 and 37.7 examine temples. Sections 37.8 through 37.12 turn to peasants, water, caste, labor, and women. Sections 37.13 through 37.16 examine merchants, towns, ports, and money. Section 37.17 addresses the historiography, and Section 37.18 draws the argument together.

Section 37.2

Agrarian Expansion and the Making of New Countrysides

Between about the sixth and thirteenth centuries, cultivation spread into tracts that had previously been forested, pastoral, or sparsely settled. The evidence is indirect but widespread. Inscriptions record the founding or renaming of villages, the establishment of Brahmin and temple settlements on fresh land, the construction of tanks and canals, and the definition of boundaries between estates. Place names ending in elements such as -mangalam in the Tamil country or -agrahara in Karnataka often mark settlements created or reorganized through grants. Archaeological surveys, where available, show new village sites and, in some regions, the growth of settlement density.

Expansion took different forms. In the Kaveri delta and other river plains, it often meant the extension and intensification of wet-rice cultivation supported by canals and tanks. In the drier Deccan plateau and parts of Karnataka and Andhra, it combined dry-crop cultivation of millets and pulses, cotton on black soils, and tank irrigation where terrain allowed. In the Ganga plain and Bengal, rice, sugarcane, and other crops expanded alongside river-based settlement. In Rajasthan and Gujarat, wells and water-lifting devices supported cultivation and settlement in semi-arid zones, while pastoralism remained significant. In Kerala, a coastal strip of rice fields, coconut and areca gardens, and pepper-bearing hills supported both agriculture and the spice trade. In the Kathmandu Valley and Kashmir, valley agriculture depended on carefully managed irrigation. These contrasts matter: agrarian expansion was not one process but many.

The new countrysides were created through negotiation and sometimes coercion among several groups. Rulers claimed authority over land and its revenue. Local chiefs and landholders controlled labor and local knowledge. Brahmin and temple recipients brought ritual prestige and, often, literate management. Cultivators, many already living in an area, were incorporated into new revenue arrangements or were settled on new lands. Pastoral and forest communities were drawn into agrarian economies in different ways, sometimes as cultivators, sometimes as herders and suppliers of forest products, and sometimes as laborers or dependents. Historians caution against the older assumption that “empty” forest was simply cleared by settlers; many apparently new lands were already occupied or used by communities whose presence the charters do not record.

Agricultural knowledge also circulated. Texts such as the Krishiparashara, a treatise on farming usually assigned to the early medieval period and associated with eastern India, offer guidance about seasons, plowing, and rainfall, although their dates and exact origins are uncertain. Such texts show that agrarian expertise was an object of written attention, but they cannot be taken as a description of what most cultivators actually did. As with so much in this period, the voices of those who worked the soil must be inferred from the records of those who taxed, granted, and endowed it.

Section 37.3

Land Grants: What Was Actually Granted?

Land grants are the most distinctive documents of the early medieval period. Most survive as copper-plate charters: sets of plates, often joined by a ring bearing a royal seal, engraved in Sanskrit or a regional language, and sometimes in both. Others are stone inscriptions. A typical charter begins with an invocation and a genealogy of the donor, names the recipient, identifies the village or fields concerned by their boundaries, lists the rights and revenues transferred, states exemptions, and ends with imprecations against anyone who violates the gift and sometimes the names of the officials responsible. The format varied by region and period, but its general structure was widespread.

The central interpretive problem is the question in this section’s title. Grants are often described, loosely, as “gifts of land.” But the recipient did not necessarily receive full ownership in a modern sense. In many cases what was transferred was the donor’s claim to revenue: the share of produce or payments that cultivators in the village otherwise owed to the king or his officials. The cultivators typically continued to work the fields. In other cases the grant included additional rights, such as the right to collect certain dues, to receive labor services, or to exclude royal officials and soldiers from entering the village to levy supplies. Charters list such categories in elaborate formulae. Sometimes land itself, such as uncultivated fields or tanks, was transferred. Sometimes the grant simply confirmed existing holdings and exempted them from taxes.

Because rights were layered, a single village might be subject to a king, an intermediary chief, a Brahmin or temple grantee, and a local assembly or community, each with different claims. Cultivators, too, often held hereditary rights to occupy and till particular land. The language of “ownership” can mislead precisely because it implies a single owner with exclusive control. Many historians instead describe land rights in this period as bundles of claims over land, produce, and people, differently distributed among several parties. How far the recipients exercised administrative and judicial powers, and how far grants weakened royal authority or instead extended it, are exactly the questions debated in Section 37.17.

Charters also reveal how states worked. A grant required the cooperation of local officials, who had to be informed of the donation, measure its boundaries, and respect its terms. Many grants name those officials or describe the measurement and delivery of the gift. They thus document an administrative apparatus, not merely a ruler’s generosity. Grants could be made to Brahmins, to Buddhist and Jain institutions, to temples, to military and administrative followers as remuneration, and, in some cases, to repair or expand existing endowments. They could also be made by queens, chiefs, and officials, not only kings, a reminder that authority was shared among many elites.

Section 37.4

Brahmadeyas, Agraharas, and Brahmin Settlement

Among the commonest beneficiaries of grants were Brahmins. A settlement created or endowed through a grant to Brahmins is called by different names in different regions: brahmadeya (literally “Brahmin gift”) in much of the south, agrahara in Karnataka and Andhra and in many Sanskrit texts, and other terms elsewhere. In the Tamil country, Brahmin villages sometimes took the name chaturvedimangalam, indicating a settlement of scholars of the four Vedas. Inscriptions describe the settlement of Brahmin families, their shares of land, and the rules by which they managed shared resources.

The Uttaramerur inscriptions of the early tenth century, engraved under the Chola king Parantaka I, show how one such settlement functioned. They describe the organization of a village assembly (sabha), the qualifications for those eligible to serve on its committees, the selection of members by a method involving drawing lots, and rules disqualifying those guilty of certain offenses. The inscriptions are remarkable and have been widely discussed, but they should not be romanticized. The assembly governed a Brahmin settlement whose eligibility rules were restricted by property, learning, and age, and it operated within a royal system that levied revenue and could intervene. It is misleading to call it “democratic” in a modern sense, and Uttaramerur should not be treated as typical of all Tamil villages, still less of villages elsewhere. Chapter 33 discusses Chola local government in its political context.

The brahmadeya was not simply a pious reward. Settling Brahmins on land could serve several purposes at once. It supported ritual and learning; it established a literate and often Sanskrit-trained population in a locality; it integrated new or frontier areas into a royal order; and, because recipients often managed or improved the land, it could promote cultivation. In some cases, Brahmin settlements were founded on land already under cultivation, with existing cultivators becoming dependents or tenants of the new grantees. In others, they were established on land newly developed, sometimes with irrigation works. The historian cannot assume that every brahmadeya represented clearing of untouched forest.

Brahmins were not a uniform group. Some were ritual specialists and teachers, others administrators, ministers, poets, or lawyers; some lived off agricultural income, and others worked their own land or relied on temple service. Regional Brahmin communities differed in custom, status, and relationships with local rulers. Nor were Brahmins the only recipients: Buddhist and Jain monasteries, temples, and non-Brahmin individuals also received grants, as Sections 37.6 and 37.12 show. Describing Brahmin settlement as a single process of “Sanskritization” obscures these differences and implies a one-way spread of a unified culture. The relationship between Sanskritic and local religious and social practice varied by region and was reciprocal, not simply imposed.

Historical Vocabulary
Brahmadeya and Agrahara

Brahmadeya (Sanskrit, “Brahmin gift”) designates a village or land grant assigned to Brahmins, and by extension the settlement formed by the recipients. Agrahara is a related term used widely in Sanskrit and in Kannada and Telugu regions for a Brahmin settlement or the grant that created it. Both could refer to the gift, the settlement, or the community, and usage varied by region and period.

These terms matter because they mark one of the main institutions through which rulers, Brahmins, and local societies were connected. They should not be read as proof that all villages were Brahmin villages. Most villages were not, and many Brahmin settlements coexisted with non-Brahmin villages governed by other assemblies. Related terms such as devadana (gift to a deity) and pallichchandam (gift to a Jain institution) show that other religious recipients received similar endowments.

Multiple Perspectives

A Village Is Granted: Differing Positions in the Same Transaction

The ruler and his officials: A grant allowed a ruler to reward and bind a recipient, display piety, and extend authority into a locality without the cost of direct administration. It also reduced, in the short term, the revenue the ruler could collect from that village. Charters preserve the ruler’s own language of merit and legitimacy.
The Brahmin recipients: For the recipients, a grant provided support for ritual and learning, security of livelihood, and a base of local influence. It also created obligations, for example to maintain the ritual and to manage the lands or the dependent households. Their own accounts survive mainly in the formal language of the charter.
The resident cultivators: For people already on the land, a grant could mean little change beyond a new recipient of their dues, or it could mean new obligations, new intermediaries, restrictions, or displacement. They rarely appear as parties to the transaction; their names may appear only as witnesses to boundaries or as an undifferentiated population. Their experience can be recovered only indirectly.
Local chiefs and officials: Existing local elites might welcome the grant, resent the loss of income and influence, or negotiate its terms. Immunities that kept royal agents out of a village could also limit the chief’s own access, and local disputes over boundaries and rights are a recurring theme of surviving inscriptions.
Section 37.5

Brahmins and Regional State Formation

Brahmins mattered to regional kingdoms for reasons beyond landholding. Many early medieval dynasties began as military or local ruling families whose claim to rule was not self-evident. They needed ways to present their power as legitimate and enduring, and Brahmin specialists supplied them. Royal genealogies composed in Sanskrit and recorded in charters connected the ruling house to mythical or heroic ancestors. Ritual ceremonies such as royal consecrations and great gifts expressed royal status. Court poets and Brahmin ministers composed inscriptions, drafted charters, and maintained records. Legal scholars produced and interpreted texts on law and kingship; Vijnaneshvara’s Mitakshara, written about 1100 CE at the Western Chalukya court, is a notable example.

Brahmin settlements also functioned as nodes in networks of literacy, ritual, and local administration. A brahmadeya might support teachers and students, host recitation of texts, serve as a center for dispute settlement, or provide scribes and accountants. In some regions, Brahmin families became hereditary officials in the royal administration, and in others they served as advisers to local chiefs. The spread of Sanskritic court culture and its interaction with regional languages, including Tamil, Kannada, Telugu, and Bengali, owed much to these networks, though local rulers and non-Brahmin groups also patronized and shaped regional literature.

Brahmins were therefore active participants in state formation, not merely beneficiaries. Historians such as Hermann Kulke and B. D. Chattopadhyaya have emphasized the role of Brahmanical ritual, temple cults, and land grants in integrating local societies into regional polities and in legitimating emerging dynasties. Others stress that the same Brahmins could assert independence from rulers, and that conflicts between rulers and Brahmin or temple institutions are recorded as well. Kalhana’s Rajatarangini, written in twelfth-century Kashmir, criticizes oppressive officials and kings and includes episodes of tension with Brahmin communities, a reminder that sources written by Brahmins could speak critically of power.

Subordinate chiefs, often called samantas, were a second key element of regional polities. Rulers governed in part through them, receiving tribute and military service in return for recognition of their local authority. Land grants and revenue assignments were among the instruments through which rulers bound samantas, Brahmins, temples, and officials to the dynasty. Whether this arrangement indicated weakness or a flexible form of state building is among the central points of historical disagreement discussed in Section 37.17. The Chola, Pala, Pratihara, and Rajput cases examined in earlier chapters differ considerably on this point, and the generalizations in this section should be read with those differences in mind.

Cause and Effect

The Expansion of Land Grants

Long-term causes

Precedents of grants from the Satavahana and Gupta periods; growing royal need for literate administrators and ritual legitimacy; the spread of temple worship; and regional agrarian expansion that created new lands and settlements to be organized.

Short-term causes

Local competition among regional dynasties and chiefs; the need to reward followers and religious specialists without a salaried bureaucracy; and the desire of individual rulers to display piety and establish merit.

Immediate effects

New Brahmin, temple, and monastic settlements; reorganization of revenue and obligation in many villages; growth of local literacy and administrative expertise; and layered rights over land in localities.

Long-term consequences

Persisting regional elites of Brahmin and other landholding groups; temples as major local institutions; and an ongoing historical debate over whether this arrangement weakened or strengthened central authority.

Historical complexity: Grants cannot be explained by royal piety alone, nor by weakness alone. They served religious, fiscal, military, and political purposes at the same time, and their effects differed by region, by the strength of the ruler, and by the character of local society.

Section 37.6

Temples as Landholders and Institutional Centers

From the seventh century onward, and especially from the ninth, the construction of permanent temples in stone and brick became a major activity across much of South Asia. Chapters 31 through 35 described the monumental temples of the Chalukyas, Pallavas, Cholas, and others. Here the focus shifts from architecture to institution. A temple of any size required resources: land, produce, cash, oil, cattle, and skilled and unskilled labor. These came through endowments by kings, queens, officials, merchants, assemblies, and ordinary devotees, recorded in inscriptions on temple walls and, sometimes, in copper plates.

Endowments took many forms. Land was given outright or as the assignment of its revenue, with produce earmarked for particular purposes such as daily offerings, festivals, or the maintenance of lamps. Donors gave livestock whose products supported ritual lamps, with herdsmen undertaking to supply a fixed quantity of ghee, a pattern widely attested in Chola inscriptions. Cash deposits were made with village assemblies or other bodies, which paid interest to the temple. Temples thus became owners or beneficiaries of land and recipients of regular income, and, in some regions, lenders to local communities.

It would be a mistake to treat temples as simple economic machines, or to describe them as “banks.” They were first of all religious institutions, devoted to worship and to the maintenance of the sacred image, and their activities were embedded in particular local economies, politics, and ideas of merit. Their management involved priests, trustees, assemblies, and royal officials in varying proportions in different places. Temples were also not uniformly wealthy; many were modest local shrines. The vast majority of surviving inscriptions concern a minority of large institutions, and the enormous records of the Chola country do not represent every region.

Temples were not the only religious landholders. Buddhist monasteries, such as the great establishments of Bengal and Bihar discussed in Chapter 34, and Jain institutions in Karnataka and the Tamil country were also endowed with land and revenue. Chinese pilgrims’ accounts record that Nalanda was supported by the revenues of numerous villages. In Sri Lanka, Chapter 36 discussed how monasteries held estates and enjoyed immunities. In Nepal, inscriptions from the Licchavi period onward record endowments for temples and their upkeep. The Hindu temple was therefore a major, but not the only, form of religious institution anchored in land.

Primary Source Window

The Leiden Copper Plates: Revenue for a Buddhist Monastery at Nagapattinam

Long preserved at Leiden University and therefore known as the Leiden Plates, the charters were restituted to the Government of India in 2026.

Context: The Leiden plates record the grant of revenue from a village, usually identified as Anaimangalam, to a Buddhist monastery at Nagapattinam. The monastery was associated with a ruler of the maritime kingdom of Srivijaya in Southeast Asia, and its name reflects that connection. The original grant was made under Rajaraja I; the larger charter, in Sanskrit and Tamil, was issued under his son Rajendra I to record and confirm it, and the smaller Tamil charter shows the endowment being reconfirmed under Kulottunga I. Like other grants, they identify the donor’s lineage, define the village, and state the privileges attached to the gift. They therefore document not one act but the continuing recognition of an endowment across several reigns.

Paraphrase of the document’s main features: The charters assign the revenues of a named village to the monastery for its maintenance, identify the donor as the Chola king, set out the privileges attached to the gift, and place the arrangement under royal protection. They are written to be authoritative and permanent, and they exist in more than one language so that both Sanskritic court usage and local Tamil practice are represented.

Analysis: The plates show several points at once. A Buddhist institution could receive the same kind of revenue assignment as a Hindu temple or Brahmin settlement, so the royal patronage of religion was not confined to Brahmanical institutions. The plates document an overseas link: a monastery at a South Indian port was connected to the ruling house of a Southeast Asian kingdom, at a time when Chola and Srivijaya politics were closely connected, a subject discussed in Chapter 33. And the record shows how revenue, not ownership as such, was the object of the grant. What the plates do not show is the experience of the village’s cultivators, the actual size of the monastery’s income, or how the revenue was collected in practice.

Section 37.7

Temple-Centered Production, Labor, and Exchange

A large temple employed and supported many people. Inscriptions at major temples list priests, cooks, garland makers, gardeners, lamp tenders, accountants, musicians, drummers, dancers, guards, carpenters, potters, washermen, and other service personnel, and record the land, shares of produce, or payments assigned to each. The workers were not all temple “employees” in a modern sense. Some held land in return for service, some received fixed shares of produce, some were assigned from particular communities, and some were dependents or bondservants. The temple thus organized both skilled and unskilled labor, and the conditions of that labor varied widely.

Temples also stimulated exchange. They required large quantities of rice, oil, ghee, camphor, incense, cloth, flowers, metals, and building materials, which had to be produced, procured, and transported. Festivals drew pilgrims, and fairs, markets, and visiting merchants gathered around them. In some places, temple-centered settlements grew into important towns, as Section 37.14 discusses. Merchants and craft groups also made gifts to temples, so that commerce and temple support reinforced one another: donation by a merchant corporation could serve religious purposes, express status, and cement relationships with rulers and local elites.

Redistribution was one significant function. Produce collected as endowment income was used for rituals, but surplus might support the temple’s personnel, scholars, or the feeding of pilgrims, and in times of stress, temple resources could be used to support local communities. Inscriptions also record temples lending grain or cash at interest to villages and individuals. Historians disagree about the extent and importance of such lending, and the frequency of recorded cases may overstate or understate its real scale. What is clear is that temples were deeply embedded in local economies as holders of resources and as organizers of labor.

Students should be cautious about generalizing from the richest evidence. The Chola temples of the Kaveri basin, above all the Brihadisvara temple at Thanjavur, provide the most detailed records of temple personnel and endowments, and many studies of temple economy draw heavily on them. Other regions had different temple systems, different relationships between temples and rulers, and fewer surviving inscriptions. A temple in the Kathmandu Valley, supported by community trusts known as guthis, a temple in Kerala governed by local Brahmin assemblies, or a Rajput-period temple in Rajasthan operated under different arrangements from a Chola royal temple. Chapter 33 discusses the Brihadisvara temple as an expression of Chola imperial power. Here its inscriptions are used as an example of temple economy, not as a model of all temples.

Case Study: A Temple Economy

The Brihadisvara Temple at Thanjavur, Early Eleventh Century

The inscriptions engraved on the walls of the Brihadisvara temple, completed under Rajaraja I in the early eleventh century, are among the fullest records of a temple establishment in early medieval South Asia. They list gifts of villages’ revenues, gold and silver vessels, livestock assigned to supply ghee for lamps, and numerous staff, including, according to the inscriptions, several hundred women attached to the temple as dancers and singers, along with musicians, accountants, and other personnel assigned to the temple’s service. Houses in a quarter near the temple were allotted to many of them.

This evidence shows a temple functioning simultaneously as a royal monument, a ritual center, a landholder, and an employer. It also shows how the ruler’s patronage connected the temple to wider networks: the revenue assigned came from villages across the Chola realm, and the objects and materials involved moved through merchants and craft groups. At the same time the case has limits. The record documents the ideal arrangement as written down by the court and the temple, not necessarily how it operated across generations. It shows an exceptional royal foundation, not an average temple. And the status and daily lives of the women and the lower-ranking workers must be inferred, since they are present in the record mainly as names and allotments.

Section 37.8

Villages, Peasants, and Rural Production

The great majority of South Asians in this period lived in villages and made their living from cultivation, herding, and related crafts. Yet they are the hardest actors to see. Inscriptions record what was owed by the cultivators, not what the cultivators said. Historians reconstruct their situation from details of revenue, boundaries, assemblies, hero-stones, disputes, and legal prescriptions, and from comparison between regions. The picture that results is of considerable variety, and one should resist the older image of the Indian village as an isolated, self-sufficient, unchanging “little republic.” Villages were connected to markets, temples, and rulers, and their internal arrangements changed.

Obligations took several forms. Cultivators owed a share of the crop, often a fraction of the harvest, as well as other dues in cash or kind, and in some cases labor services. Inscriptions use many terms for these payments, and their meaning varied with time and place. Tamil inscriptions, for example, speak of tax (irai) and of land made tax-free (iraiyili) when gifted to temples. Sanskrit charters in northern and eastern India enumerate categories of dues and exactions. Forced labor, called vishti in some Sanskrit sources, is mentioned in charters and in Kalhana’s account of Kashmir as a burden that could be remitted by immunity or imposed by officials.

Rural society was differentiated. In many regions there were landholding cultivator groups with hereditary rights, tenant cultivators who worked land they did not themselves hold, and landless laborers. In the Tamil country, inscriptions distinguish between landholders and cultivating tenants, and between the main village settlement and separate quarters where other communities lived. Differences in wealth and status were therefore built into the village. Village assemblies, whether brahmadeya sabhas, non-Brahmin ur assemblies, or merchant nagarams, managed local affairs such as tanks, temple lands, and tax payment, but their membership was limited, and their relationship to the ordinary cultivator was often unequal.

Peasants were not passive. Hero-stones, memorial stones commemorating men who died defending cattle or villages, are found in large numbers in Karnataka and the Tamil country, and attest to local warfare and cattle raiding as well as to local military leaders. Inscriptions also record disputes, petitions, and in some cases collective action by village communities in response to excessive demands. Peasants could flee, resist, or negotiate. The Kaivarta uprising against the Pala king in Bengal in the eleventh century, described in the court poem Ramacharita from a hostile perspective, is one famous example of rural and local unrest, though its character is difficult to reconstruct from a source written by the court’s supporters.

Everyday Life and Social History

Reconstructing a Village from Inscriptional Evidence

Imagine the evidence a historian might assemble for a village in the Tamil country in the eleventh century. A temple inscription records a donor’s gift of land and the dues it would yield. Another notes the boundaries of the village and mentions neighboring settlements. A third records the assembly’s decision to dig or repair a tank and to assess the cost on the landholders. A hero-stone commemorates a cattle raid. Elsewhere, the inscriptions mention the village’s weavers, potters, and other artisans, and record a quarter inhabited by separate communities. Evidence from another region would look quite different.

From such evidence a historian can reconstruct how the village was organized, which groups held land, which provided services, and how revenue and religious endowment flowed out of the village. A household’s year was structured by the agricultural calendar: preparing fields, sowing with the arrival of the rains, tending irrigation channels, harvesting, and paying dues. Festivals and temple rituals gave the year a ritual shape. But the evidence stops where ordinary life begins. It tells almost nothing about household composition, diet, health, childhood, folk belief, or how most families felt about their obligations. Historians should therefore use careful phrases such as “the inscriptions suggest” and acknowledge that the village they reconstruct is the village as the record-makers saw it.

Section 37.9

Water, Environment, and Agrarian Power

Control and management of water shaped agrarian life and power throughout South Asia. Because the monsoon brings most rainfall in a few months and varies from year to year, cultivation depended on storing, diverting, or lifting water. The solutions differed by landscape. In the Tamil country, tanks (eri) collected rain and river water and fed fields through sluices, and canals drew water from rivers such as the Kaveri. Inscriptions mention committees responsible for tanks and record gifts and taxes assigned to maintain them. Large tank systems, such as those in the Chola country, are associated with rulers of the ninth through eleventh centuries, though many smaller tanks were built and maintained locally.

In Sri Lanka, as Chapter 36 described, a tradition of reservoirs and channels supported the dry-zone kingdoms of Anuradhapura and Polonnaruwa, and irrigation works there were closely associated with royal prestige. In Kashmir, Kalhana attributes major flood-control and land-reclamation works in the ninth century to the engineer Suyya, whose projects, he says, changed the course and management of the Vitasta (Jhelum). The Kathmandu Valley’s terraced rice fields depended on carefully managed channels, supported by endowments recorded in inscriptions. In Rajasthan and Gujarat, wells and other water-lifting systems made cultivation possible in semi-arid zones, while in Bengal the challenge was often too much water: floods, shifting rivers, and delta conditions shaped where and how people settled.

Water was thus a source of agrarian power. Whoever controlled irrigation could influence cultivation, settlement, and revenue. Rulers and local elites built and endowed irrigation works for merit as well as for revenue. Temples and Brahmin settlements sometimes held tanks and water rights, and local assemblies supervised maintenance. Water management required labor, and the burden of digging, repair, and clearing fell on villagers, sometimes through forced labor. Environmental limits also mattered. Drought, flood, and crop failure could drive people from villages and produce famine, and some inscriptions and chronicles record such crises. Over the long term, irrigation could also cause problems such as silting and waterlogging.

Historians should avoid reading environmental conditions as destiny. The same monsoon produced different arrangements in different societies, and human choices about settlement, crops, and water use shaped outcomes. Nor should the contribution of royal patronage to irrigation be exaggerated. Much irrigation was local and cumulative, created and maintained by communities over generations and rarely recorded in inscriptions. The major works described in royal and monastic records represent only the visible portion of a much larger agrarian infrastructure.

Section 37.10

Jati, Occupation, and the Localization of Social Order

Social hierarchy in early medieval South Asia is often summarized by the fourfold scheme of varna: Brahmins, Kshatriyas, Vaishyas, and Shudras. This scheme, set out in religious and legal texts, describes an ideal ordering of society, and by this period it was long established in Brahmanical thought. But it was not how most people identified themselves or were identified in daily life. They belonged to jatis, birth-based communities that were typically local or regional, often linked with particular occupations, customs, and rules of marriage, and ranked, sometimes disputably, within local society. Varna offered a normative framework, and jati was the lived reality.

Inscriptions and texts of the early medieval period increasingly name such communities. Charters and temple records mention cultivators, weavers, oil-pressers, potters, goldsmiths, scribes, herdsmen, fishermen, and many others. In Tamil inscriptions, groups such as the Vellalas, landowning and cultivating communities that provided many Chola officials, and artisan and merchant groups appear prominently, and from about the eleventh century the paired divisions known as the right-hand (valangai) and left-hand (idangai) groups are mentioned. In northern and eastern India, kayasthas, scribal and administrative communities, become prominent in charters and in Kashmir’s chronicle. Legal commentaries and Puranic texts explain the proliferation of mixed jatis through theories of intermarriage between varnas, an attempt by Brahmanical authors to fit the multiplicity of local communities into the fourfold scheme.

Historians have long debated why jati became more prominent in the record. Some argue that agrarian expansion, the incorporation of new groups into settled society, the growth of specialized crafts and trades, and the integration of tribal and local communities into Brahmanical and royal frameworks created many new occupational and regional groups, whose ranking was negotiated through ritual, marriage, and patronage. Others emphasize that jatis existed earlier but became more visible as inscriptions and legal texts proliferated. The two explanations are not mutually exclusive. What seems clear is that social order was becoming localized: what mattered in a given region was how particular communities were ranked and related to one another, not a single, pan-Indian scheme.

The chapter must therefore guard against several errors. Caste did not suddenly appear in this period; hierarchy and exclusion have much earlier roots, as Chapters 6 and 7 showed. Nor was caste a rigid, unchanging four-fold system imposed identically everywhere: ranking, occupation, and mobility varied by region and could shift through political and economic change. Groups could claim higher status by adopting practices, obtaining patronage, or gaining political power. Colonial classification would later reshape and harden caste categories, as Chapter 73 discusses. In the early medieval period, caste was a changing set of local institutions, not a timeless survival.

Historical Vocabulary
Varna and Jati

Varna (literally “color” or “class”) refers to the fourfold classification of society into Brahmins, Kshatriyas, Vaishyas, and Shudras found in Sanskrit religious and legal texts. Jati (literally “birth”) refers to the many local, usually endogamous communities into which people were born, often associated with a particular occupation or regional identity. The two terms describe different things: varna is a theoretical scheme, and jati a social reality.

The distinction matters because early medieval texts and inscriptions use varna language to describe a society that was actually organized into numerous jatis, and because later writers often collapsed the two. Modern English “caste” is used for both, which can mislead. This textbook uses the terms precisely and avoids assuming that either maps neatly onto the other or remained constant across time and place.

Section 37.11

Social Hierarchy, Labor, and Marginal Communities

Hierarchy meant different things to different people. At the top, Brahmins, rulers, and large landholders enjoyed ritual and material advantages. At the base, many communities performed labor that was essential but regarded as low or polluting by the dominant norms. Legal and religious texts describe groups treated as ritually excluded, often called chandalas or by other terms, and earlier Chinese pilgrims such as Faxian and Xuanzang had noted outcaste communities living apart. In the Tamil country, Chola-period inscriptions distinguish the main village settlement from separate quarters, and some inscriptions refer to communities such as the paraiyar who lived in such quarters. These textual and inscriptional references show exclusion as a feature of everyday space, though practices varied by region and the evidence cannot be read as a uniform pan-Indian system.

Dependent and unfree labor was part of the social landscape. Inscriptions record individuals and families assigned to temples or granted along with land, and cases of people placing themselves or being placed in service to an institution, sometimes in conditions of hardship or famine. Legal texts discuss categories of slaves and bonded labor. Forced labor obligations, as noted in Section 37.8, were imposed on cultivators and artisans. Because the sources come mostly from donors and recipients, they record these arrangements in the language of gifts and service, and rarely preserve the perspective of those who were given.

Expanding states and agrarian systems incorporated different peoples in different ways. Forest and hill communities, pastoral groups, and others who lived outside the settled agrarian order were drawn into it through trade, tribute, military service, or conversion into cultivators and laborers, and some retained considerable autonomy. Some groups gained status through service to rulers, and others were marginalized. The fortunes of these communities depended on local circumstances. Kalhana’s account of Kashmir criticizes officials who extorted peasants, and other sources record resistance, flight, and revolt. The social order was thus contested, not simply imposed.

Historians must acknowledge sharp limits in the evidence here. Marginal and dependent communities seldom produced written records, and what survives is shaped by the interests of elites and institutions. Terms for such groups may have changed in meaning, and they can be mistranslated or over-interpreted when read through later categories of caste and untouchability. Careful historical work compares regions, identifies patterns, and refrains from generalizing beyond the evidence. It also rejects the opposite error of minimizing exclusion because it is difficult to document.

Section 37.12

Women, Property, Work, and Religious Patronage

An account of land, temples, and commerce that ignored half the population would be badly incomplete. Women appear in the evidence of this period in many roles. They worked in agriculture, spinning and weaving, food processing, and other crafts, and in household production. Women were attached to temples as dancers, singers, and servants. Queens, royal women, and women of wealthy families made grants, built temples, and endowed lamps and other services. Women of other social ranks also appear as donors in inscriptions, giving modest gifts such as lamps, land, or livestock, and their gifts show how widely religious patronage extended.

Royal and elite women were important patrons. In the Tamil country, queens of the Chola house, among them Sembiyan Mahadevi, widow of the Chola king Gandaraditya and mother of Uttama Chola, are recorded as endowing and rebuilding temples, and Kundavai, a sister of Rajaraja I, is recorded as making gifts to temples. In the Deccan, Chalukya queens are associated with temple building at Pattadakal, as Chapter 31 noted. These women acted through their own resources and, in some cases, authority, and their gifts shaped regional religious and architectural landscapes. They also illustrate that patronage was a form of political and social action, not only piety.

Property rights are discussed in legal texts. The Mitakshara and other commentaries treat women’s property (stridhana), inheritance, and the position of widows, and different schools and regions took different positions. Such texts are prescriptive and reflect Brahmanical legal reasoning; they do not establish what most women actually held. Inscriptions record women as holders and donors of land and other property, which shows that in practice some women controlled significant resources. Customs of marriage, widowhood, and inheritance varied by region, caste, and class.

Not all evidence points toward agency. Memorial stones in several regions commemorate women who died on their husbands’ funeral pyres, evidence for the practice of sati that Chapter 67 will discuss in its colonial context, though the practice was far from universal and its prevalence in this period is hard to measure. Temple women, often called devaradiyar in the Tamil country, were attached to temples in a status that was both respected and dependent, and historians continue to debate their social position, since later colonial and reformist discourse transformed how the institution was understood. Women of lower-ranked communities, laborers, and enslaved women appear least often. Treating women as a group would again be misleading, since class, caste, region, and religion shaped their positions.

Section 37.13

Merchants, Markets, and Merchant Guilds

Inscriptions from the eighth century onward document organized merchant groups in many regions. Their names, structure, and activities varied. The best known is the Ayyavole Five Hundred (in Tamil, Ainnurruvar, “the Five Hundred”), a corporation whose name derives from Aihole in Karnataka and whose members are recorded in inscriptions across the Deccan and the Tamil country from about the ninth century. Its Tamil-language record at Barus, on the northwest coast of Sumatra, dated 1088 CE, attests a South Indian merchant presence in Southeast Asia. Other corporations included the Manigramam, associated with the Tamil country and Kerala and attested also in Southeast Asian inscriptions, the Anjuvannam of Kerala, and groups known as Nanadesis (“of various countries”). Local merchant assemblies, nagarams, governed trading towns in the Tamil country.

These organizations performed several functions. They set rules for members, supervised trade, provided security for caravans and ships, collected and paid dues, and made gifts to temples and other institutions. Their inscriptions record donations, agreements with rulers, and privileges. They were often armed or accompanied by armed retinues, and some acquired political significance by lending to, or collaborating with, rulers and by serving as links between production areas and coastal ports. Rulers granted them privileges, including exemptions from certain tolls, in order to attract trade and revenue.

The Kerala copper plates of the ninth and tenth centuries provide a striking example of merchant privilege and religious diversity. The Tharisapalli plates, issued in the mid-ninth century by a ruler of the Kerala region, record the grant of rights and privileges to a church at Kollam and to Christian merchants and others there, and bear witnesses’ signatures in several scripts. The Jewish copper plates of Cochin record rights granted to a Jewish merchant leader. These documents show merchants of different religions as recipients of royal privilege, within a framework in which Anjuvannam and Manigramam were recognized institutions. Chapter 38 discusses the Muslim merchant communities of the western coast.

Historians should be cautious about generalizing. Guilds changed in name, membership, and influence from place to place and from century to century, and there was no permanent pan-Indian merchant organization. Evidence is regionally concentrated, with the richest inscriptions in the Deccan and Tamil country. The relation of merchants to rulers and temples also varied: some corporations were closely tied to royal patrons, others were more independent, and the extent of their autonomy is debated. What the evidence does establish is that organized merchant groups were active in internal and overseas trade, and that they were significant participants in the economic and religious life of early medieval society.

Section 37.14

Towns, Commercial Centers, and the Question of Urban Change

Did towns decline between the Gupta age and the thirteenth century? In the 1980s, R. S. Sharma argued that many cities of the early historic period suffered “urban decay” between about 300 and 1000 CE, pointing to the abandonment or shrinking of some earlier sites, the reduced circulation of coins, and the weakening of long-distance trade. This view formed part of his wider argument about feudalism, discussed in Section 37.17. It prompted extensive research and considerable criticism.

Critics, notably B. D. Chattopadhyaya, accepted that some earlier urban centers shrank or disappeared, but argued that the evidence also reveals new kinds of urban places and an “urban process” that was more varied than decline implies. Market towns grew at the junction of trade routes and in agrarian regions; inscriptions refer to local markets and fairs, and to trading posts where tolls were collected. Temple towns and pilgrimage centers such as Kanchipuram, Madurai, Srirangam, Chidambaram, Bhubaneswar, and Kashi attracted population, craft production, and trade. Royal capitals, including Kannauj, Thanjavur, and later Gangaikondacholapuram, were administrative and commercial centers. Ports flourished on the coasts, as Section 37.15 shows. Archaeological evidence is uneven and often limited, which makes firm quantitative conclusions difficult.

Early medieval towns often differed from the earlier cities of the Ganga plain in form. Many were less clearly separated from the surrounding countryside, grew around temples and markets rather than around royal citadels alone, or were dispersed clusters of settlement. Merchant assemblies, craft quarters, and temple precincts structured urban space. Specialized markets, whose names appear in inscriptions, concentrated particular goods, and itinerant traders and fairs connected rural producers to wider networks. The question is therefore less whether towns existed than what kinds of towns existed, where, and how they were organized.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From Early Historic Cities to Early Medieval Urban Forms

What Changed

  • Some earlier urban centers, particularly in parts of northern India, shrank or were abandoned, and some patterns of coinage changed
  • New urban forms emerged around temples, markets, pilgrimage centers, regional capitals, and ports
  • Merchant corporations and local assemblies took on increasingly regional characters
  • Urban growth was more regionally variable, with strong development in the Tamil country, Deccan, and on coasts

What Stayed the Same

  • Exchange between town and countryside, and between inland producers and coastal ports, persisted
  • Artisans and merchants remained important to royal and religious institutions
  • Towns remained centers of ritual and political life

Who experienced change most directly, and why the pattern matters: Residents of declining older centers experienced contraction, while those in new temple towns and ports experienced growth. Understanding both prevents the false picture of uniform decline or uniform prosperity, and shows why regional comparison is essential.

Section 37.15

Ports and the Indian Ocean Economy

South Asia’s coasts faced two great maritime systems: the Arabian Sea, linking the subcontinent with the Persian Gulf, Red Sea, and East Africa, and the Bay of Bengal, linking it with Southeast Asia and China. Monsoon winds structured the sailing seasons, as Chapter 25 explained for the early historic period. In the early medieval centuries, ports on the western coast, including Bharuch and Khambhat in Gujarat, Thana and Chaul on the Konkan coast, Mangalore and Kollam on the Malabar coast, and on the eastern side Nagapattinam, Mamallapuram, and Tamralipti, sent out and received goods and travelers. Mantai in northwestern Sri Lanka linked the island with both systems. Many ports were towns with resident merchant communities of different origins.

The evidence is varied. Arab and Persian authors, among them the merchant Sulayman (c. 851 CE), al-Masudi (c. 943), and al-Idrisi (1154), describe Indian ports and rulers. Chinese works such as Zhao Rugua’s thirteenth-century description of foreign peoples list Indian ports and commodities. Documents from the Cairo Geniza, a repository of Jewish records from Egypt, preserve letters and accounts of merchants trading with India. Indian inscriptions record merchant gifts and privileges, and archaeology yields imported ceramics, coins, and harbor remains. Each source has biases: foreign authors often relied on hearsay, and inscriptions emphasize gifts rather than trade volumes. Together they show lively and diverse exchange.

Goods moved in both directions. Indian exports included cotton textiles from Gujarat, the Coromandel Coast, and Bengal; pepper and other spices from Malabar; aromatic woods, gems, ivory, and, in some periods, iron and steel. Imports included horses brought from Arabia and Persia, which South Indian rulers sought for their armies, as well as luxury goods, metals, and, from the east, Chinese ceramics and silk. Arab writers refer to a king they call “Balhara,” usually identified with the Rashtrakuta rulers, as friendly to merchants, which suggests that western Indian rulers deliberately courted trade. In the Bay of Bengal, Tamil inscriptions in Southeast Asia and Chinese records of Chola embassies show active exchange, and Chapter 33 discussed Chola naval ambitions in that context.

The trade was not merely a matter of foreign merchants arriving at Indian ports. Indian merchants and shipowners also traveled, and Indian and foreign communities lived side by side in port towns. The ports also linked the interior: cotton, pepper, and other goods had to be produced, collected, and brought from inland areas by merchants, caravans, and local markets, and imported goods flowed inland. The prosperity of agrarian regions and of coastal ports were thus connected. Chapter 38 examines the Muslim merchants of the western coast in detail, and Chapter 49 returns to the Indian Ocean in the age before the Portuguese. The present section’s purpose is to show how maritime trade fit into the larger socioeconomic structure.

Section 37.16

Money, Taxation, Revenue, and the Problem of Economic Measurement

Money is a difficult subject for this period. After the abundant gold coinage of the Gupta age, some regions of northern and central India show fewer coins, lower-denomination or debased issues, or a shift toward other forms of payment. In the northwest and Rajasthan, small silver-copper coins, such as the gadhaiya type, and silver drammas circulated in the Pratihara and later periods. In the Deccan, the Western Chalukyas and others issued gold pieces known as gadyana. The Cholas issued gold and copper coins, and Tamil inscriptions regularly record payments in gold, cash, and paddy. In Bengal and Odisha, cowrie shells served as small change, and some charters of the Sena rulers calculate revenue in units of cowries.

Revenue was collected in kind and in cash, in proportions that varied by region and period. Charters list the dues owed by cultivators and the rights transferred: shares of produce, special levies, tolls on trade, taxes on crafts and markets, and labor services. Customs and market tolls, collected at points such as market towns and ports, were a source of income and a form of regulation of trade. Land surveys and assessments are attested, notably in the Chola country in the early eleventh century, and indicate attempts to systematize revenue. Payments in kind, such as measured quantities of paddy, remained important, and the relative importance of cash and kind was not the same everywhere.

The problem of measurement is serious. Historians cannot reconstruct trade volumes, population, production, or national income for this period. Coin finds depend on accidents of preservation and excavation, and the absence of coins in a region does not prove the absence of exchange: credit, barter, bullion, cowries, and perishable media can circulate without leaving archaeological traces. Inscriptions record what was important to donors and institutions, not the full range of transactions. Sharp conclusions from the number of coins or inscriptions are thus risky. Studies of Chola inscriptions by scholars such as Noboru Karashima and Y. Subbarayalu have shown extensive use of money and measured payments in that region, while evidence from other regions, such as parts of the north, suggests different patterns. A balanced approach acknowledges regional differences and avoids equating “fewer coins” with “no commerce.”

Section 37.17

Was Early Medieval India “Feudal”? The Historiographical Debate

The preceding sections examined the same body of evidence, land grants, villages, temples, taxes, coins, towns, and merchants, that has fueled one of the most influential debates in modern South Asian historiography. The question is whether the term “feudalism,” developed to describe medieval Europe, usefully describes early medieval India. The debate has never been only about a word. It has concerned how historians understand the character of the state, the status of the peasantry, the relation between politics and economy, and the meaning of the centuries between the Guptas and the Delhi Sultanate.

The model of “Indian feudalism” was most fully developed by R. S. Sharma, beginning with his book Indian Feudalism (1965) and extended in later work such as Early Medieval Indian Society: A Study in Feudalisation (2001). Sharma argued that from about the Gupta period, rulers made increasing grants of land and revenue to Brahmins, religious institutions, and officials, often together with administrative and judicial privileges and immunities from royal intervention. This, he argued, created a hierarchy of intermediaries between king and peasant, a form of political decentralization, and the extension of control over peasants, who were increasingly subject to forced labor and restrictions. He linked this to a decline in long-distance trade, towns, and coinage, and to a more self-sufficient, localized rural economy. The historian D. D. Kosambi had earlier distinguished “feudalism from above,” created by rulers’ grants, from “feudalism from below,” arising from the rise of local landlords, an idea that influenced later work. Sharma’s later writing emphasized “feudalization” as a process rather than a fixed system.

Critics challenged the model on several grounds. Harbans Mukhia argued that Indian conditions differed fundamentally from European feudalism: peasant production by family labor on small holdings, without a large lord’s demesne cultivated by serfs, remained central, and the peasants were not legally tied to the land as serfs. Others, including B. D. Chattopadhyaya, argued that the model overstated decline, neglected regional variation, and obscured the dynamics of state formation, agrarian expansion, and urban and commercial change. Scholars of South India, including Burton Stein, proposed the “segmentary state,” in which kings exercised ritual sovereignty over a series of relatively autonomous local societies. Hermann Kulke and others proposed integrative models, in which temples, cults, and grants incorporated localities into expanding polities. Studies of inscriptions, especially from the Chola country, showed substantial monetization and commerce. Some of these approaches are themselves criticized, for instance for underestimating the coercive power of rulers.

Historical Vocabulary
Feudalism

Feudalism is a term originating in the study of medieval Europe, where it is associated with relationships of lordship and vassalage, military service in exchange for land, decentralized political authority, and a peasantry subject to lords. Historians vary in how they define it, and some regard the term as too elastic to be useful.

In the Indian context, “feudalism” is an analytical model proposed by some historians to describe the effects of land grants, intermediaries, and decentralization. It is not a fact on which all agree, and it is not a label that settles questions. This textbook uses it as a term in a debate and does not employ it as a period name.

Historical Debate

Was Early Medieval India Feudal?

The question: Do land grants, intermediary chiefs, and decentralized authority in early medieval India constitute a form of feudalism, marked by economic and urban decline, or do they reflect other processes of state formation and economic change?

Interpretation A — Indian feudalism (R. S. Sharma and others): Grants transferring revenue and administrative and judicial rights created a hierarchy of intermediaries and fragmented political authority. The grants and associated immunities, together with forced labor, bound peasants more tightly. This process coincided with the decline of long-distance trade, towns, and coinage, producing a more localized agrarian economy. Sharma’s later work spoke of feudalization as a process.
Interpretation B — Rejection or substantial revision (Harbans Mukhia, B. D. Chattopadhyaya, and others): The central features of European feudalism, including serfdom and the lord’s demesne, were largely absent; family-based peasant production persisted. Evidence shows regionally varied agrarian expansion, state formation, and continued, in some regions growing, commerce and urban forms. The model, critics argue, mistakes one region’s or period’s evidence for the whole and treats political decentralization as economic decline.
Interpretation C — Alternative models of the state (Burton Stein, Hermann Kulke, and others): Rather than debating feudalism, these scholars propose models such as the segmentary state, in which sovereignty was ritual and layered, or the integrative state, in which temples, cults, and grants incorporated local societies into regional polities. Others emphasize the coercive and fiscal powers of kings, which these models may understate.

Evidence: All sides draw on the same kinds of evidence: copper-plate and stone inscriptions, legal texts, chronicles, numismatic data, archaeological surveys, and foreign accounts. They differ in how they interpret grant clauses, how they weigh regional evidence, and how they read the absence or presence of coins and towns. Evidence from the Chola country and from northern India has often supported different conclusions.

Why it matters: The debate affects whether the centuries between the Guptas and the Delhi Sultanate are understood as a period of decline and fragmentation, or as one of creative regional transformation, agrarian growth, and institution building. It also shows how historical models shape the questions historians ask. This textbook treats the debate as unresolved, and finds that its most durable contributions are the focus on land rights, the importance of regional comparison, and the recognition that decentralization and economic vitality could coexist.

Section 37.18

Review and Historical Synthesis

The core question of this chapter has been: how did agrarian expansion, land grants, religious institutions, social hierarchy, and commerce interact to produce the regional political order of early medieval South Asia? The preceding sections suggest an answer built from several connected processes, none of which can stand alone.

Agrarian expansion, examined in Sections 37.2 and 37.9, supplied the basic resource: new lands, new settlements, and irrigation works that increased and stabilized production. But expansion required organization, and the reassignment of rights through land grants (Section 37.3) was one of the main ways rulers, queens, chiefs, and officials organized it. Grants bound recipients to donors, created layered claims on land and revenue, and sometimes extended royal influence into localities. Brahmin settlements (Sections 37.4 and 37.5) brought literacy, ritual legitimacy, and administrative skill, while temples (Sections 37.6 and 37.7) concentrated resources, labor, and exchange in institutions that were simultaneously religious, political, and economic. These institutions helped integrate localities into regional polities and gave dynasties lasting sacred centers.

Peasants, laborers, and artisans (Sections 37.8 and 37.11) produced the surplus on which all of this depended, under obligations that varied from region to region and that elite records describe only indirectly. Social hierarchy became increasingly expressed through localized jatis (Section 37.10), whose ranking, occupation, and relationships were negotiated within particular regions, and women’s roles as workers, property holders, and patrons (Section 37.12) show that the system included, and depended upon, the work and resources of women at all levels. Merchants and their corporations (Section 37.13), towns (Section 37.14), and ports (Section 37.15) connected inland production to Indian Ocean trade, supported by monetary and revenue arrangements that varied by region (Section 37.16). Commerce, in turn, fed temples and rulers through gifts, tolls, and taxes, and many merchants were themselves donors who shaped temple and royal patronage.

The regional political order that resulted was characterized by multiple centers of authority, layered rights, and dense links among rulers, religious institutions, local elites, and commercial groups. The historiographical debate (Section 37.17) shows that historians interpret this order differently: as feudal decentralization, as a variety of state formation, or as a set of regionally distinct arrangements. What the evidence supports across these views is that decentralization and economic vitality were compatible, that regional variation was fundamental, and that religious institutions and land rights were central to how power worked.

Chapter 38 now turns to a development that unfolded alongside these processes: the arrival and establishment of Muslim communities in South Asia, beginning with the Arab presence on the western coast and in Sindh. Many of the social and economic structures examined in this chapter, including agrarian institutions, merchant networks, and Indian Ocean commerce, formed the setting into which Muslim merchants, soldiers, and rulers entered, and Chapter 44 will examine how those structures changed under the Delhi Sultanate.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the social and economic structures beneath the regional kingdoms of early medieval South Asia, roughly 600 to 1300 CE. It argued that this period saw a broad but regionally uneven transformation in which cultivation expanded, rights over land and revenue were reassigned, Brahmins and religious institutions became important participants in regional state formation, social identities became increasingly localized, and merchants linked inland production to wider commercial networks.

Agrarian expansion, the chapter showed, took many forms, from wet-rice cultivation in river plains to dry-crop farming on the plateau and valley agriculture in the Himalayan region. It was supported by irrigation works created by rulers, institutions, and local communities, and it did not simply involve clearing empty forest, since many lands were already used by communities whose presence charters rarely record.

Land grants, preserved mainly in copper-plate charters, were examined as bundles of rights. They could transfer claims to revenue, exemptions, and in some cases administrative or judicial privileges, rather than absolute ownership of land. Brahmins were significant recipients, and brahmadeya settlements such as Uttaramerur served as centers of learning, ritual, and local administration. But Brahmins were not a uniform group, and temples, Buddhist and Jain institutions, and others also received grants, as the Leiden plates illustrate.

Temples emerged as major landholding and institutional centers that employed large numbers of workers, organized production and exchange, and attracted pilgrims and merchants. They were religious institutions embedded in particular local economies, not simply banks, and the rich evidence from Chola Thanjavur cannot be taken as typical of all regions.

Peasants, who produced the surplus, are hard to see in sources written by and for elites. The chapter reconstructed what can be known of their obligations, differentiation, and resistance, rejecting the image of the timeless, self-sufficient village. It also examined water management as a basis of agrarian power and as a field of environmental limits.

On social hierarchy, the chapter distinguished varna, a theoretical scheme, from jati, a set of local birth-based communities. It stressed that caste was neither newly invented in this period nor a rigid, uniform system, and it noted the evidence for exclusion, dependent labor, and the incorporation of forest and pastoral communities. Women appeared as workers, property holders, donors, and temple personnel, with experiences that varied by class and region.

Merchant corporations such as the Ayyavole Five Hundred, Manigramam, and Anjuvannam, along with towns and ports, connected South Asia to Arabian Sea and Bay of Bengal networks. The debate over urban decline was shown to be more complicated than a simple narrative of collapse, with new forms of urban life developing alongside the contraction of some older centers. Coinage and revenue arrangements varied by region and cannot be used simply to measure commerce.

The historiographical debate over “Indian feudalism” was presented as an ongoing conversation among scholars who read the same evidence differently. The chapter concluded that decentralization and economic vitality could coexist and that regional variation is essential. This account prepares the way for Chapter 38, which turns to the early Muslim presence in South Asia.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has asked students to look beneath the political narratives of Chapters 31 through 36 and to see the villages, temples, workshops, and harbors on which regional kingdoms rested. Doing so changes the picture of the early medieval centuries. Instead of an interlude of fragmentation between empires, they appear as a period of creative and varied institution building, in which new settlements, religious foundations, social communities, and commercial networks took shape in many regions.

The chapter also illustrates how history is written from partial evidence. The copper plates and temple inscriptions that make this subject accessible were produced by and for donors, recipients, and institutions. Reading them well requires attention to what they say, what they assume, and whose voices they leave out. The feudalism debate shows that even careful historians can read the same evidence differently, and that the models they use shape the questions they ask.

The structures examined here left lasting legacies. Temples remained central institutions of regional society, Brahmin and other landholding elites persisted in many regions, jati identities continued to develop, and merchant networks carried goods and ideas across the Indian Ocean. Later chapters will trace how these structures were transformed, adapted, and sometimes challenged under the Delhi Sultanate, the Vijayanagara and Deccan states, the Mughal Empire, and colonial rule, and Chapter 73 will return to caste as classified under the colonial census.

Within the six-volume sequence of this work, Chapter 37 closes Part VII by explaining how the regional order of early medieval South Asia worked. It connects the political histories of Volume II’s first chapters to the arrival of Islam and the creation of new states in the chapters that follow, and it reminds students that the history of power is inseparable from the history of land, labor, belief, and exchange.
