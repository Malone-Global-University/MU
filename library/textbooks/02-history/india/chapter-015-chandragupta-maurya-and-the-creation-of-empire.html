Part IV — The Mauryan Age
15

Chandragupta Maurya and the Creation of Empire

Indian History: South Asia from the Indus Civilization to the Present

Chapter Opening

Alexander's departure from South Asia did not produce a durable Macedonian order. During the same decades in which Alexander's successors fought over his empire, another political transformation was underway in the Gangetic basin. The Nanda dynasty fell, Chandragupta Maurya seized Magadha, and a new ruling house began expanding across northern South Asia. The result was the largest political formation the subcontinent had yet seen.

Chapter 14 ended with Macedonian authority eroding in the northwest: the satraps and garrisons Alexander left behind lost their footing amid assassinations, rebellions, and the wars of his successors far to the west. This chapter turns to the far larger consolidation that intersected with that collapse. Its center was not the Indus plain but Magadha, the fertile, populous, and richly taxed state of the middle and lower Ganges whose rise Chapter 10 traced from Bimbisara through the Nandas. Its capital was Pataliputra. Its new ruler, whom the sources call Chandragupta, belonged to a lineage remembered as the Mauryas.

The chapter's central question is simple to state and difficult to answer: how did Chandragupta transform the political resources of Magadha into an empire extending across much of northern South Asia, and how securely can historians reconstruct the government that sustained it? The first half of that question concerns a process of empire formation. The second concerns evidence, and here the difficulty is severe. No inscription of Chandragupta survives. No administrative archive survives. The narrative traditions about his rise, preserved in Buddhist, Jain, Brahmanical, and Greco-Roman writings, were composed or compiled centuries after him and often reshape his life into dynastic legend. The famous Sanskrit treatise on statecraft associated with his minister Chanakya is, in the form we possess, a much later composite.

This chapter therefore argues on two levels. Historically, the Mauryan Empire did not arise from an empty political landscape or from the achievement of a single exceptional founder. Chandragupta inherited the territorial, fiscal, military, and urban foundations developed under earlier Magadhan rulers and the Nandas, exploited political instability in both the Gangetic basin and the northwest, and built a new dynasty whose authority eventually extended across much of northern South Asia. Methodologically, the sources for his reign are late, fragmentary, and often retrospective, so the precise roles of Chandragupta, Chanakya, the Arthashastra tradition, and Mauryan institutions are harder to reconstruct than later accounts imply.

Two temptations must be resisted. The first is personal explanation: Mauryan success as the genius of Chandragupta, or of his clever brahmin adviser. The second is teleology: Magadha as destined to unify "India." Neither the sources nor sound historical method support either claim. This chapter also avoids the phrase "unified India." Mauryan authority came to encompass an unprecedented proportion of northern South Asia and important northwestern territories, but the deep south lay outside direct Mauryan rule, and there was no state called India in any modern sense.

The chapter follows a route from east to west and back. It begins with the fall of the Nandas (15.1), the historical figure of Chandragupta (15.2), and the problem of Chanakya and the Arthashastra (15.3). It then examines expansion across northern South Asia (15.4) and the conflict and settlement with Seleucus I (15.5). It returns to Pataliputra (15.6), and asks how the empire was governed (15.7) and paid for (15.8). It closes with Chandragupta's son Bindusara, whose reign shows that the dynasty outlived its founder (15.9), and a synthesis (15.10). Ashoka, the Kalinga war, and the inscriptions belong to Chapter 16; the sustained analysis of Mauryan society and economy belongs to Chapter 17; and the empire's fragmentation belongs to Chapter 18.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain what Chandragupta inherited from earlier Magadhan rulers and the Nandas, and why the transition from Nanda to Mauryan rule cannot be narrated as a clean, well-documented story.
  2. Distinguish among Buddhist, Jain, Brahmanical, and Greco-Roman traditions about Chandragupta's origins and rise, and identify the limits of each as evidence.
  3. Explain why the identification of Sandrokottos with Chandragupta matters for ancient South Asian chronology.
  4. Evaluate the Arthashastra as evidence, distinguishing a developing South Asian statecraft tradition from a contemporary Mauryan administrative record.
  5. Trace the expansion of Mauryan authority across the Gangetic basin and northwest while avoiding false precision about a conquest itinerary.
  6. Assess the conflict and settlement between Chandragupta and Seleucus I, including the reasons for caution about the "territory for elephants" account and the marriage alliance.
  7. Describe Pataliputra as an imperial center and analyze Megasthenes as a mediated rather than surviving eyewitness source.
  8. Analyze provincial government in terms of varying degrees and forms of authority rather than uniform bureaucratic control.
  9. Explain how army, transport, and revenue made it possible to sustain conquest, and why ancient numerical claims must be handled cautiously.
  10. Assess the significance of Bindusara's reign for imperial continuity, distinguishing evidence from later tradition.
  11. Construct a multicausal explanation for the creation of the Mauryan Empire that avoids single-factor and teleological arguments.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. What political, military, fiscal, and urban resources did Chandragupta inherit from Magadha and the Nandas?
  2. Why do the traditions about Chandragupta's rise disagree, and what does their disagreement reveal about how dynasties were remembered?
  3. How can historians use a text such as the Arthashastra without treating it as a literal description of Chandragupta's government?
  4. What does "unprecedented consolidation" mean when the sources do not record a detailed conquest itinerary, and when the deep south remained outside Mauryan control?
  5. How did the collapse of Macedonian authority in the northwest create opportunities without simply causing the Mauryan Empire?
  6. What can be reconstructed about the Seleucid-Mauryan settlement, and what remains uncertain?
  7. How should historians read a source like Megasthenes, whose work survives only in later quotation and paraphrase?
  8. What is the difference between conquering a large territory and governing it, and what evidence bears on that difference?
  9. Why does Bindusara's reign matter even though evidence for it is thin?

Historical Context

Chapter 9 traced the Second Urbanization and the political competition among the mahajanapadas, and Chapter 10 explained why Magadha emerged from that competition as the dominant state of the Ganges basin. Its advantages included a productive alluvial heartland, river communication, access to iron and forest resources to the south, fortified capitals at Rajagriha and later Pataliputra, and rulers willing to expand through warfare and annexation. By the mid-fourth century BCE the Nandas ruled a Magadhan state that later Greek writers, writing about Alexander's campaigns, described as formidable in infantry, cavalry, chariots, and elephants. Whatever the exact numbers, the tradition of Nanda military and fiscal strength is consistent across our sources.

Northwestern South Asia developed along a different trajectory. As Chapter 14 showed, Gandhara and the Indus region had been drawn into the Achaemenid Empire, then were briefly contested by Alexander. When Alexander turned back from the Hyphasis and later died in 323 BCE, the arrangements he left in the Punjab and Indus valleys were unstable. Local rulers, Macedonian officers, and satrapal appointees competed, and the successors' wars pulled Macedonian attention westward. The northwest was thus a zone of political fluidity at exactly the moment a new dynasty was consolidating power in the east.

Historians face a sharp change in the evidentiary landscape at this point. For Magadha's earlier history, Chapter 10 relied on Buddhist and Jain narratives and Puranic dynastic lists. For Chandragupta, the sources multiply but do not thereby become secure. Greek and Roman authors, including Justin (abridging the lost Trogus), Plutarch, Appian, Strabo, and Arrian, preserve material about a ruler they call Sandrokottos or Androkottos and about Megasthenes, who had direct access to Chandragupta. Sanskrit dramatic and narrative works, Puranas, Buddhist chronicles from Sri Lanka, and Jain narratives preserve alternative traditions. A later inscription from Gujarat gives valuable but indirect evidence of Mauryan reach. Not one of these sources is a contemporary Mauryan document. Chapter 1's warning about distinguishing contemporary evidence from later tradition therefore applies with special force.

A final piece of context concerns scale. Earlier states in northern South Asia, including Magadha itself, governed substantial territories, so the Mauryas did not invent territorial rule. What changed was the size of the political system, its incorporation of both the Gangetic plain and northwestern frontier zones, and its diplomatic standing among the Hellenistic kingdoms. Explaining that change is this chapter's task.

Section 15.1

The Fall of the Nandas

To understand Chandragupta's rise, students should first recall what the Nandas left behind. Chapter 10 traced how Magadha grew from a regional state into the strongest power of the Ganges basin. The Nandas, who ruled from Pataliputra in the fourth century BCE, inherited that position and extended it, according to later Puranic tradition, by destroying rival ruling lines. Whatever the reliability of such claims, the Nanda state clearly controlled a very large part of the Gangetic plain, and the traditions that survive credit it with immense treasure, a large army, and an effective system of taxation. Later classical accounts of Alexander's campaign describe the ruler of the Gangaridai and Prasii as commanding an enormous army. These reports contributed to Greek perceptions of the formidable power lying farther east, although, as Chapter 14 emphasized, campaign exhaustion, logistics, monsoon conditions, and the soldiers' unwillingness to continue also shaped the refusal at the Hyphasis. The accounts are an indication that eastern power was widely regarded as substantial, but they are not contemporary Nanda documentation.

Chandragupta therefore did not build an empire from nothing. He took over a capital, a productive and populous core, an established practice of collecting revenue over a wide territory, and a military system capable of sustained campaigning. Much of what later ages credited to the Mauryas, including a large army, a wealthy treasury, and a capital administered on an imperial scale, may already have existed in some form under the Nandas. This is the single most important corrective to any account that presents the Mauryan state as a sudden invention.

How the Nandas fell is much less clear. Different traditions offer different stories. Brahmanical and Sanskrit literary traditions, including the later drama Mudrarakshasa and several Puranic and commentarial accounts, portray a brahmin, Chanakya, who has been insulted by the last Nanda king and who then engineers his downfall by installing Chandragupta. Buddhist chronicles from Sri Lanka, particularly the Mahavamsa tradition and its commentary, also connect Chanakya to the overthrow, though in different detail. Jain traditions associate Chandragupta with a related but distinct set of narratives. Greco-Roman authors such as Justin say only that Sandrokottos, of humble origin, rose against the ruler of the Prasii, whom he is said to have made himself unpopular, and that he afterward controlled a large territory.

These accounts share a general outline: Chandragupta overthrew the Nanda king and took Magadha. They disagree about the details, and they were composed long after the events. The Mudrarakshasa, for instance, was written several centuries later, and its plot is a dramatic invention built on inherited legends. The Buddhist chronicles were compiled in Sri Lanka in the first millennium CE. Justin's epitome of Trogus dates to the Roman period. Historians therefore should not narrate a clean story in which the people hated the Nandas, and Chandragupta and Chanakya overthrew them. That story owes far more to dramatic convention and dynastic apologetic than to demonstrable evidence.

A safer conclusion is that Chandragupta displaced the Nanda dynasty during the political upheavals of the late fourth century BCE, probably around 321 BCE by the conventional chronology, and that the exact sequence, coalition, and circumstances remain poorly documented. Whether he acted through military conquest, palace coup, alliance with disaffected elites, or some combination cannot be determined. Section 15.10 returns to the point that the transition, whatever its form, was possible because Nanda Magadha had already accumulated the resources on which a successor could draw.

Section 15.2

Chandragupta Maurya

Chandragupta is the historical founder of the Mauryan dynasty, and his reign is conventionally dated from about 321 BCE to about 297 BCE. Those dates are approximations built from a combination of Greek synchronisms and the regnal lengths recorded in later Puranic and Buddhist lists, which do not agree precisely. Puranic tradition assigns him around twenty-four years, and the Sri Lankan chronicles agree closely. Students should treat "c. 321" as a scholarly convention, not a documented year.

His origins are narrated variously. Buddhist tradition presents him as a member of the Moriya clan, connected to a region of the Himalayan foothills, and thereby of respectable warrior lineage. Brahmanical traditions offer other accounts, some associating him with the Nanda household or with low status through his mother. Jain traditions treat him as a devoted follower of the Jain faith. The Greek tradition preserved in Justin describes a young man of humble birth who gathered followers and rose to power. Even the name "Maurya" is explained in different ways. Because each community tends to make Chandragupta's ancestry suit its own view of legitimate kingship, these accounts reveal more about later religious and political memory than about his actual birth.

The same caution applies to episodes that appear in popular retellings: a childhood royal game, an exile, a meeting with Alexander, or an escape from Nanda pursuit. Some Greek sources do mention a meeting between Chandragupta and Alexander, but they are late and their reliability is doubtful. Students should treat such stories as part of the legend of the dynasty rather than as reconstructed biography.

Historical Vocabulary
Sandrokottos and Synchronism

Greek and Roman authors refer to an Indian ruler named Sandrokottos (also Androkottos or Sandracottus), who met Seleucus and to whose court Megasthenes was sent. In 1793 the Orientalist William Jones proposed that this name rendered the Sanskrit Chandragupta. The identification is now widely accepted because the Greek accounts fit the Indian traditions about a founder of a dynasty in Pataliputra who ruled shortly after Alexander.

The identification matters because it provides a synchronism, a link between two chronological systems. Greek events such as the death of Alexander (323 BCE) and Seleucus's campaign (c. 305 BCE) can be dated with reasonable confidence. By tying Chandragupta to those events, historians gained a fixed point for early South Asian chronology, from which later dates such as those of Ashoka can be calculated. Much of ancient South Asia's political chronology depends on this single equation, and that dependence is itself a reason for careful handling.

Chandragupta's real historical importance lies not in reconstructed biography but in what can be inferred from the political order that followed him: a dynasty that held Magadha and controlled a territory of unprecedented extent, that negotiated with a Hellenistic king from a position of strength, and that transmitted power to a son. The following sections attempt to reconstruct that order while marking where the evidence supports firm claims and where it only permits inference.

Section 15.3

Chanakya and the Tradition of the Arthashastra

No figure is more closely associated with Chandragupta in later memory than the brahmin adviser known variously as Chanakya, Kautilya, and Vishnugupta. Later Sanskrit tradition treated these names as belonging to a single person, the astute minister who engineered the overthrow of the Nandas and guided the new ruler in governing an empire. The same tradition credits him with the Arthashastra, a large Sanskrit treatise on artha, which in this context means material welfare, political economy, and the practice of government.

The Arthashastra is a remarkable text. Its fifteen books address the training of a king, the appointment and supervision of officials, the management of towns and forts, the administration of law, the suppression of threats to the state, the conduct of diplomacy, the theory of the state's seven constituent elements, the conduct of war, the organization of a network of spies, revenue, and the regulation of trade, mining, agriculture, and crafts. It outlines a theory of interstate relations, often called the mandala theory, that arranges neighboring states into circles of allies and enemies. It reflects a strong interest in the systematic use of state power for the ruler's security and for the material prosperity of the realm. As a source for the history of South Asian political thought, it has no rival from the ancient world.

Students must therefore absorb a strong warning. The surviving Arthashastra is not a Mauryan government handbook written down by Chandragupta's prime minister around 320 BCE. The text was rediscovered in manuscript form and published in the early twentieth century, and from the beginning some scholars treated it as a genuine Mauryan document. Later textual scholarship has increasingly rejected that view. Analysis of the text's language, structure, internal inconsistencies, and references shows a work that is composite and substantially later than the Mauryan period. In a detailed textual study, Mark McClish has argued that the text was not composed at one moment by one author, and he places an earlier core around the first century BCE, with later redaction associated with a figure named Kautilya around the third century CE. Other scholars propose different chronologies, and specific arguments continue, but the older view of a single Mauryan-era composition is widely regarded as untenable in its simple form.

This does not make the Arthashastra useless for Mauryan history. It means that Chapter 15 uses it in a specific way: as evidence for a developing South Asian statecraft tradition, some elements of which may preserve older political ideas, rather than as a photographic description of Chandragupta's administration. The text may draw on earlier practice, and its emphasis on revenue, espionage, provincial supervision, and diplomacy is plausibly consistent with the needs of a large early state. But when the text describes a detailed hierarchy of superintendents, exact tax rates, or a specific arrangement of provinces, historians cannot assume that these were the arrangements of Chandragupta's realm.

The historical figure Chanakya is a separate problem from the text. Some form of the tradition that Chandragupta had a brahmin adviser is old and widespread, appearing in Buddhist, Jain, and Brahmanical sources, and it may preserve a kernel of fact. But that adviser's precise role, career, and even identity as a single individual cannot be established from contemporary evidence. Notably, Megasthenes' surviving fragments, though they describe Mauryan governance, do not mention him. Historians therefore usually speak of Chanakya as a figure of tradition whose historical core, if any, is unrecoverable.

Historical Vocabulary
Arthashastra and Statecraft Tradition

The word Arthashastra combines artha, material well-being and the resources that sustain a state, with shastra, a systematic treatise or body of learned teaching. It names both a specific text and a genre. Several works and teachers of this kind existed in ancient South Asia, and the extant text refers to earlier authorities whose writings are lost.

A statecraft tradition is a body of ideas about rule that develops over time within learned communities, often reflecting both practice and aspiration. It differs from an administrative record, which documents what a government actually did. Students must keep the distinction in mind: a treatise may describe how authors thought a ruler ought to govern, not how any particular ruler did.

Section 15.4

Conquest of Northern India

The heart of Chandragupta's achievement was the expansion of Magadhan power into a territorial system much larger than any earlier one in South Asia. The general path can be described as follows: from a Magadhan core inherited from the Nandas, through consolidation of the Gangetic basin, to movement toward the northwest, and the creation of a large northern imperial territory. The sources, however, do not provide a detailed conquest itinerary, and any reconstruction that names specific campaigns, dates, and battles would claim more precision than the evidence allows.

What is known comes from indirect evidence. Later classical authors report that Sandrokottos held all of India, or a very large portion of it. Plutarch offers a rhetorical claim about an enormous army sent to overrun the country, which cannot be taken as a numerical statement. These claims are consistent with the Greek accounts of the settlement with Seleucus, which imply control of territory extending to and beyond the Indus. In western India, an inscription of the Shaka ruler Rudradaman, dating to around 150 CE, records that a Mauryan governor named Pushyagupta, appointed under Chandragupta, constructed the dam that created the Sudarshana lake in Saurashtra. That is a later source recalling earlier events, but it is strong evidence that Mauryan authority reached the Kathiawar peninsula early in the dynasty's history.

Students should also avoid the phrase Chandragupta unified India. It misdescribes both the territory and the nature of Mauryan rule. Mauryan authority came to encompass an unprecedented proportion of northern South Asia and important northwestern territories. But the far south, including the Tamil-speaking regions examined in Chapter 23, remained outside direct Mauryan rule even under Ashoka, when the empire reached its greatest securely documented extent (Chapter 16). Nor was authority within the core uniform, as Section 15.7 shows.

Conquest required more than battlefield victory. Expanding a state involved installing garrisons in strategic fortified places, negotiating with local rulers and chiefs who might be left in place under conditions of loyalty, establishing revenue arrangements, building or improving communications, and placing trusted officials or relatives in provincial centers. It also involved the management of regional elites, whose cooperation could be more effective than force. The evidence for such arrangements under Chandragupta is thin, but the later Mauryan record and the general logic of holding conquered territory make it reasonable to infer that similar methods were used.

The northwest deserves special attention. After Alexander, the territories of the Punjab and Indus valleys contained a patchwork of local rulers, former Macedonian appointees, and communities that had resisted Alexander. Some Greek sources report that Alexander's successors in the region were killed or driven out, and Justin says Sandrokottos freed India from Macedonian rule. That statement is a rhetorical simplification, and students should not conclude that Chandragupta expelled every Macedonian official in a single campaign. Chapter 14 traced the erosion of Macedonian authority, much of which resulted from the wars of the successors and local rebellion. Chandragupta's role was to extend Mauryan authority into a region that had lost a stable overlord. Alexander's invasion thus created an opportunity, but it did not cause the Mauryan Empire.

Section 15.5

Conflict with Seleucus I

After Alexander's death, his generals fought over his empire for decades. Seleucus I Nikator eventually gained control of Babylonia and then of much of the Iranian plateau and Central Asian territories formerly held by Alexander. He is reported to have campaigned toward the Indus, and the conflict with Chandragupta is usually placed around 305 to 303 BCE. This is the direct continuation of Chapter 14, since it shows how the collapse of Alexander's empire ultimately confronted a new power in South Asia.

The surviving literary evidence for the conflict is extremely brief. Appian, writing in the second century CE, says that Seleucus crossed the Indus and fought Sandrokottos, king of the Indians, before the two made an agreement that included a marriage relationship. Justin reports that Seleucus made a treaty with Sandrokottos. Strabo (15.2.9) states that Seleucus ceded lands formerly held by Alexander and received 500 elephants. Historians have traditionally reconstructed these ceded territories as including parts or all of Paropamisadae, Arachosia, and Gedrosia, but the exact geography is not stated in a surviving treaty and remains debated. Plutarch mentions that Sandrokottos gave elephants to Seleucus. No detailed narrative of the fighting survives, and there is no report of a decisive battle.

From these fragments, historians have reconstructed a settlement. Seleucus, facing serious rivals in the west, appears to have recognized, in the conventional reconstruction, Mauryan control over substantial territories around and south and east of the Hindu Kush, in return for elephants that would prove valuable in his western wars. Elephants played a decisive part at the Battle of Ipsus in 301 BCE, where Seleucus and his allies defeated Antigonus, and later evidence about Seleucus's elephant force is consistent with the tradition of a large transfer from the east. The settlement also opened diplomatic relations; Megasthenes, a Greek diplomat probably associated with Seleucus (a plausible but not demonstrable connection), had direct access to Chandragupta.

Students should nonetheless build in caution. The exact territorial terms cannot be perfectly recovered. Ancient authors disagree on which regions were transferred, and they wrote long after the event. The conventional story of a straightforward exchange of land for 500 elephants is being reassessed in current scholarship, including recent work that questions the ways the so-called Treaty of the Indus has been reconstructed from Appian and Justin and suggests that later imperial geographic rhetoric shaped what these authors wrote. The number 500 is a classical-source claim, though one supported by later evidence concerning Seleucus's elephant force.

Likewise, the marriage alliance should be presented as an interpretation of ambiguous classical evidence. The Greek term used by Appian can imply a marriage connection, and some scholars consider a dynastic marriage probable, but the details are unknown: who married whom, and whether a Seleucid or Macedonian bride was involved cannot be determined. It should not be stated as unquestioned fact.

The historical result matters more than the reconstruction of every clause. Mauryan authority emerged dominant in the northwestern borderlands while Seleucus redirected his energies westward. In the conventional reconstruction, the settlement gave Chandragupta control of routes linking the Indus system to Iran and Central Asia, made the Mauryan state a recognized participant in Hellenistic diplomacy, and established a pattern of exchange with the Seleucids and other Hellenistic kingdoms that continued under his successors.

Section 15.6

Pataliputra and the Imperial Center

The chapter now returns to the east. Pataliputra was the political center of an enlarged Magadhan empire, building directly on the developments of Chapters 9 and 10. Its location near the confluence of major river systems, principally the Ganges and the Son, made it a hub of river communication across the eastern plain. The city gave the ruler command of a highly productive agricultural hinterland, access to river transport for troops, goods, and revenue, and a position from which routes ran west toward the Yamuna and Punjab and south toward the resource zones of central and eastern India.

As an imperial center, Pataliputra concentrated the court, the ruler's household, the highest officials, treasuries and stores, and a substantial military presence. It functioned as the point to which provincial officials reported and from which orders, in principle, radiated outward. Provisioning a city of such size and importance required a steady flow of grain and other goods drawn from surrounding districts, an example of the way the capital and the countryside were bound together by revenue and supply. Its role as a center connecting the Gangetic core with provincial territories made it more than a large city; it was the hinge of the imperial system.

Archaeological work at Patna, including excavations at sites often linked to the Mauryan city such as Bulandibagh and Kumrahar, has revealed timber palisade remains and evidence of a pillared hall that some scholars connect to Mauryan-period construction. These finds are consistent with descriptions of a wooden-walled city, but the archaeological record of ancient Pataliputra is partial, complicated by later urban occupation and the river's movements, and interpretations of individual finds are not uniformly agreed.

Literary evidence for the city comes chiefly from Megasthenes, a Greek diplomat probably associated with Seleucus, who had direct access to Chandragupta; his exact chronology and movements are uncertain. He is credited with a work called the Indica, which described Mauryan India, its geography, peoples, customs, and government. Megasthenes was an unusually well-placed observer, but there is a crucial source warning. His Indica does not survive independently. What historians possess are fragments and paraphrases preserved by later authors, including Strabo, Arrian, Diodorus Siculus, and Pliny, who selected material for their own purposes and sometimes disagree with one another. Students should therefore not write that Megasthenes tells us exactly what Mauryan Pataliputra looked like. The more accurate formulation is that later classical authors preserve material attributed to Megasthenes, who visited Chandragupta's court and is an unusually important but mediated witness to Mauryan India.

The Primary Source Window later in this chapter examines his description of the city and uses it to show how source transmission itself becomes part of historical method.

Section 15.7

Provincial Government

A large territory cannot be governed simply by an army in the field. The fifth-century BCE Achaemenid Empire, discussed in Chapter 14, offers a comparison: it combined a strong center with satrapies, local dynasts, and communications infrastructure. The Mauryas confronted a similar problem in a different setting, and the difference between conquering a large territory and governing it is the central question of this section.

Sources allow historians to identify plausible mechanisms without reconstructing a full system. Royal relatives or trusted individuals could be appointed as governors in key regions. The Junagadh inscription's reference to Pushyagupta, described as a provincial governor in the west, is direct, if later, evidence for this practice under Chandragupta. Regional centers, some of which are known from later Mauryan sources, could serve as bases from which governors supervised districts. Officials at different levels collected revenue, maintained order, and communicated with Pataliputra. Local elites, including chiefs and heads of communities, remained essential intermediaries. Garrisons secured strategic places. Roads and rivers carried messages and troops. These mechanisms, working with differing intensities, held together an unusually extensive polity.

The major interpretive safeguard is this: do not reconstruct a perfectly centralized bureaucratic state from the Arthashastra. That text describes numerous superintendents and detailed regulation, but Section 15.3 has shown why it cannot be treated as a transcript of Mauryan practice. Nor should the familiar map of Mauryan territory be mistaken for equal administrative penetration everywhere. A better model is a powerful imperial center exercising varying degrees and forms of authority across regions. In the Gangetic heartland, control was likely dense and direct. In outlying or frontier regions, authority could be exercised through governors, garrisons, negotiated obligations, and the recognition of local leaders. In forest and upland zones, control may have been episodic.

Evidence for Mauryan provincial organization becomes much better under Ashoka, principally because his inscriptions name viceroys, regional officials, and administrative categories, and because they were set up across a very wide area. That evidence belongs to Chapter 16 and, in social and economic detail, Chapter 17. Chapter 15 may point out that such evidence exists, and that later arrangements probably had roots in Chandragupta's period, but it should not attribute Ashoka's provincial arrangements automatically to his grandfather.

Section 15.8

Army and Revenue

This section explains how such an empire functioned materially. A useful guiding question is: what resources are necessary to maintain conquest after the army has moved on? Holding territory requires garrisons, officials, roads, fortifications, and the ability to move forces to trouble spots. All of these cost money and labor, so that military capacity and revenue capacity are two sides of a single problem.

Later classical writers describe an enormous Mauryan military establishment. Pliny, drawing on earlier sources, reports very large numbers of infantry, cavalry, and elephants attributed to the Prasii, and Plutarch offers a still larger claim. These figures cannot be presented as census-quality statistics. They may reflect real scale, exaggeration, or rhetorical convention, and the sources do not agree. What can be said confidently is that the Mauryan state fielded a large army with the traditional four arms of infantry, cavalry, chariots, and elephants, and that elephants were valued enough to be a prominent element of diplomacy with Seleucus. Megasthenes' fragments also describe administrative arrangements for the army, but they survive only in later summaries.

Supplying such an army meant reliable grain, animals, fodder, transport, and weapons. River communication in the Gangetic plain facilitated supply, and roads across drier and hillier regions supported movement of forces and officials. Elephants in particular required forest sources for capture and large quantities of fodder, so the state's control of forest resources mattered for military purposes.

The fiscal base was above all agriculture. Revenue in the form of a share of the crop, together with other levies, supported the court, officials, and army. Trade and customs added to income where routes and markets were under Mauryan control, including in ports and along river routes, though the specifics are hard to recover for Chandragupta's reign. The state also made demands on productive populations for labor and services. Chapter 17 examines the economy and society that supported these demands in depth, so this section confines itself to their role in sustaining conquest.

Students should use the Arthashastra comparatively and analytically. It contains extended discussion of revenue heads, superintendents of agriculture, mines, forests, and trade, and detailed statements about rates and obligations. These passages are valuable for understanding what South Asian political writers thought a state should control and how they organized fiscal ideas. It would be an error, however, to state that Chandragupta's government collected exactly these taxes. The safest statement is that the Mauryan empire depended on agricultural revenue and other levies, administered through a hierarchy of officials, and that the theoretical literature of the statecraft tradition offers a way of imagining how such a system could have been organized.

Section 15.9

Bindusara and Imperial Continuity

Bindusara should not be treated merely as Chandragupta's son and Ashoka's father. His reign demonstrates that the Mauryan state survived the founder, and the ability of an early empire to transfer power without collapse was itself a significant achievement. The traditional chronology places his reign from about 297 to about 273 BCE, with Puranic and Buddhist sources giving figures in the mid-twenties, though these numbers differ and are approximate.

Succession after Chandragupta is recorded only in later traditions. Bindusara is named in Puranic and Buddhist lists as the second Mauryan king, and Greek sources refer to a king called Amitrochates, usually taken to render a Sanskrit epithet such as Amitraghata, "slayer of enemies." The preservation of the dynasty is the main fact, and it suggests that the administrative and military apparatus the founder built could function under new leadership.

Diplomatic connections with the Hellenistic kingdoms continued. Greek tradition preserves a report that Bindusara asked the Seleucid king Antiochus I, Seleucus's son, to send him sweet wine, dried figs, and a sophist, and that Antiochus agreed to send the first two but replied that Greek law did not allow the sale of a sophist. The anecdote is preserved by a later author, Athenaeus, and its details cannot be verified, but it is consistent with the existence of continuing contact. Another Greek writer, Deimachus, is said to have served as an envoy to Bindusara's court. Contacts of this kind demonstrate that Mauryan diplomacy did not end with Chandragupta.

Evidence for Bindusara's territorial policy is thin. Later Tibetan and other traditions claim that he conquered a large area of the south, and some modern accounts assert that he completed the conquest of the Deccan. These claims should be treated with caution. They are late and are not securely supported by contemporary evidence. It is possible that Mauryan authority extended further into peninsular India during his reign, and Ashoka's inscriptions later attest Mauryan presence in parts of the Deccan, but attributing the expansion specifically to Bindusara is inference. The Buddhist tradition also preserves a story of a rebellion at Taxila that Bindusara sent his son Ashoka to suppress, a narrative which if it contains a historical kernel would suggest continuing difficulties in the northwest, but again the source is late and legendary in character.

Traditions concerning Chandragupta's final years require the same care. Jain sources, including later narratives and inscriptions at Shravanabelagola in Karnataka, describe how Chandragupta became a Jain ascetic, abdicated, traveled south with the teacher Bhadrabahu, and died by sallekhana, the ritual fast unto death. These traditions are important within Jain memory and may preserve some historical memory, but they are considerably later than the events, appear in different forms, and are not straightforward contemporary biography. The chronology of Bhadrabahu himself is debated. This chapter therefore notes the tradition without using it to fix the details of Chandragupta's death.

Section 15.10

Review and Historical Synthesis

This chapter has asked why a Mauryan Empire became possible at the end of the fourth century BCE. The answer must be multicausal. It involves Magadhan foundations, Nanda resources, the productive and strategically located capital at Pataliputra, military organization, revenue capacity, regional political instability, Chandragupta's successful dynastic seizure, the opportunity presented by the northwest after Alexander, the accommodation of local elites, and continuing imperial consolidation. No single-factor explanation is adequate.

Section 15.1 showed that the Nandas had already assembled a wealthy, populous, and militarily formidable state, and that the manner of their overthrow is poorly documented. Section 15.2 separated the historical founder from legend and explained why the identification of Sandrokottos with Chandragupta anchors ancient South Asian chronology. Section 15.3 introduced the Arthashastra tradition and argued that it should be used as evidence for statecraft thought, not as a Mauryan handbook. Section 15.4 traced expansion without false precision and stressed that Mauryan authority was unprecedented in extent but not universal.

Section 15.5 examined the settlement with Seleucus, showing that its outcome, Mauryan dominance in the northwestern borderlands and entry into Hellenistic diplomacy, is clearer than its terms. Section 15.6 returned to Pataliputra and Megasthenes, demonstrating how mediated evidence must be read. Sections 15.7 and 15.8 showed that governing and financing an empire required provincial officials, local intermediaries, garrisons, communication, and agricultural revenue, with varying degrees of central control. Section 15.9 argued that Bindusara's reign confirms dynastic continuity even where evidence is thin.

Three claims must be rejected. Alexander did not create the Mauryan Empire: his invasion disturbed the northwest, but the Mauryan state grew primarily from Magadhan foundations. Chanakya's genius did not create it either: the figure is largely a construct of later tradition, and the state did not depend on a single adviser. Magadha was not destined to unite India: its rise resulted from contingent political, economic, and military choices, and even at its height the Mauryan state did not include the deep south.

By the death of Bindusara, a genuinely durable imperial dynasty existed. Yet the most famous transformation of Mauryan rule had not yet occurred. Chandragupta had created the imperial framework and Bindusara preserved it. Under the next Mauryan ruler, however, the meaning of kingship itself would become the subject of an extraordinary experiment in public political communication. Chapter 16 turns to Ashoka, the Kalinga war, the inscriptions, and the transformation of Mauryan kingship.

Primary Source Window

Megasthenes on Palimbothra (Pataliputra), preserved in Strabo, Geography 15.1.35–36, and Arrian, Indica 10

Creator: Megasthenes, Greek diplomat probably associated with Seleucus, with direct access to Chandragupta · Date: composed c. 300 BCE; the later authors who preserve it wrote in the first century BCE–second century CE · Type: Lost ethnographic and geographic work surviving in later quotation and paraphrase

Context: Megasthenes had direct access to Chandragupta and wrote a description of India known as the Indica. The work is lost. Strabo and Arrian, writing centuries later, used it and other sources, selecting, summarizing, and sometimes criticizing it. The chain of transmission is: Megasthenes, then the lost Indica, then later Greek and Roman quotation or paraphrase, then the modern historian. Each link can introduce error or change of emphasis.

Paraphrase: The later authors report that Palimbothra, at the meeting of two rivers, was an exceptionally long and narrow city, surrounded by a timber palisade pierced for archers and by a broad moat, with many gates and towers.

Analysis: This description is consistent with the archaeological discovery of timber palisade remains at Patna and with the general picture of a large riverside capital. It offers an outsider's view of the city, and it comes from someone with access to the court but not necessarily to ordinary life. The numerical details, such as dimensions and numbers of gates, are given differently by different authors and should be treated as reported claims. Because the original is lost, historians cannot determine what Megasthenes said and what later authors added or changed.

Questions for students:

  1. What does this source say about the appearance and defenses of Pataliputra?
  2. Who created it, and how might Megasthenes' position as a foreign envoy shape what he observed?
  3. What can historians learn from it, particularly when combined with archaeology?
  4. What can historians not establish from a source that survives only in later quotation?
Historical Debate

Can the Arthashastra Be Used as Evidence for Chandragupta Maurya's Government?

The question: Does the surviving Arthashastra describe Mauryan government, or is it evidence for a later and different stage of South Asian political thought?

Interpretation A — The text substantially reflects Mauryan statecraft: Early modern scholars, following the tradition that identifies Kautilya with Chandragupta's minister, argued that the treatise preserves the administrative system of the Mauryan period. They pointed to correspondences with Megasthenes' descriptions and with aspects of Ashoka's later administration, and to the text's concern with a large territorial state, which suits an empire like the Mauryan.
Interpretation B — The text is a composite, substantially later work: Modern textual criticism has emphasized the text's internal inconsistencies, its layered composition, its references to authorities and practices that fit later periods, and its language. McClish argues that the extant text is not the product of a single author at a single moment, with a core perhaps in the first century BCE and later redaction around the third century CE. On this view, the Arthashastra is evidence for a developing statecraft tradition, though individual passages may preserve older ideas.

Evidence: Both positions rely on the same text and on comparisons with Megasthenes, Ashokan inscriptions, and later sources. They differ in how they weigh linguistic and structural analysis of the text against apparent parallels in content, and in how they interpret the parallels, which may reflect shared ideas rather than direct description.

Why it matters: The answer determines whether historians can use the treatise's detailed descriptions of superintendents, taxes, and espionage to reconstruct Chandragupta's government. If the text is later, such descriptions must be treated as evidence for ideals or for later practice. The conclusion should not be that the text tells us nothing about Mauryan India. Instead students should learn to distinguish a statecraft tradition from a contemporaneous administrative record, and to use the former cautiously to frame questions about the latter.

Student task: Identify one feature of the Arthashastra that might reflect the needs of any large early state, and one that would need independent Mauryan-period confirmation. Explain what kind of evidence would provide it.

Multiple Perspectives

Living through a Dynastic Transition: Reconstructed Perspectives

Note: these perspectives are reconstructed analytical illustrations based on the political situations described in this chapter, not quotations or accounts from named historical individuals.

Chandragupta consolidating a new dynasty: A ruler who had seized a powerful throne needed to secure the capital, retain the loyalty of officials and military commanders, and prevent rivals from exploiting the change. Expansion offered plunder, revenue, and prestige, but also risk.
A former Nanda official or local elite deciding whether to serve the new regime: Officials skilled in revenue and administration could be valuable to a new ruler, and cooperation could preserve status and property. Refusal could invite punishment. Continuity of personnel is one plausible reason the Nanda administrative base could pass to the Mauryas.
A northwestern ruler encountering expanding Mauryan authority: After Alexander, local rulers in the Punjab and Indus region faced shifting overlords. Submission to a strong Mauryan power might protect a ruler's position, while resistance risked conquest. Local leaders had to weigh distance, resources, and the prospect of outside help.
A Greek diplomat, probably associated with Seleucus, at Pataliputra: A Greek diplomat such as Megasthenes viewed the Mauryan court from outside, interpreting unfamiliar institutions through Greek categories. His account reflects both privileged access and cultural distance.
Continuity and Change Over Time

Nanda Magadha to Chandragupta's Mauryan Empire

What Changed

  • The ruling dynasty changed from the Nandas to the Mauryas
  • The territorial scale expanded dramatically, incorporating the northwest and reaching western India
  • The state acquired a new diplomatic position through its settlement with Seleucus and exchange of envoys with the Hellenistic world
  • The frontier with the northwest and Iran became a zone of active imperial management

What Stayed the Same

  • Pataliputra remained the capital and the Gangetic plain the productive and political core
  • Revenue from agriculture remained the fiscal foundation
  • The military system, including infantry, cavalry, chariots, and elephants, remained central
  • The experience of governing a large territory continued from earlier Magadhan practice

Who experienced change most directly: Officials and elites at the capital and in the northwest saw the greatest change in their political circumstances. Cultivators in the Gangetic core may have experienced substantial continuity in agrarian obligations, but the evidence does not permit historians to measure changes in taxation or labor demands securely. Why the pattern matters: It shows that the Mauryan Empire was less a break with the past than an expansion of an existing state.

Cause and Effect

Why Was Chandragupta Able to Create a Much Larger Empire?

Long-term causes

Magadha's agricultural wealth, river position, and capital; centuries of state formation; the Nandas' accumulated treasury and army.

Short-term causes

Instability during the Nanda succession; Alexander's disruption of the northwest and the wars of his successors; Chandragupta's success in seizing the throne and securing elite cooperation.

Event or transformation

The rapid creation of a territorial system spanning much of northern South Asia and the northwestern borderlands, confirmed by the settlement with Seleucus.

Immediate effects and long-term consequences

A recognized imperial dynasty, new diplomatic ties with the Hellenistic world, and the foundation on which Ashoka's reign and later Mauryan governance would rest.

Historical complexity: Students should weigh inherited Magadhan capacity, Nanda resources, military organization, political instability, northwestern opportunity, elite cooperation, and Chandragupta's leadership without choosing a single cause. Different historians may assign different weights to these factors, but none can plausibly claim that one alone explains the outcome.

Everyday Life and Social History

What Did Dynastic Change and Imperial Expansion Mean for Ordinary People?

The evidence for ordinary experience during Chandragupta's reign is far weaker than the evidence for ideals of government and elite structures. Honest history must say so. Nonetheless, the political changes described in this chapter almost certainly affected different groups differently.

For cultivators in the Gangetic core, a change of dynasty may have altered little in the daily rhythm of farming, and revenue and labor obligations may have continued, though Chandragupta-era evidence does not allow historians to measure any change. Soldiers and their families were directly affected by recruitment and campaigns. Officials and scribes may have found new opportunities or lost position. Merchants may have benefited from more integrated routes, especially in the northwest, though they would also have faced state demands. Communities in newly incorporated regions such as the northwest and Saurashtra may have encountered new authorities and revenue claims, and forest and hill communities, whose resources such as elephants and timber mattered to the state, may have faced new demands. These are plausible models, not documented findings. Chapter 17 examines these social relationships in depth.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced the emergence of the Mauryan dynasty and the unprecedented consolidation of territory under Chandragupta. Its governing argument is that the Mauryan Empire did not arise from an empty political landscape or from the achievement of a single founder. It grew from Magadhan and Nanda foundations, exploited instability in the Gangetic basin and northwest, and produced a dynasty whose authority extended across much of northern South Asia.

The chapter began with the fall of the Nandas, showing that Chandragupta inherited a wealthy, populous, and militarily formidable state. The traditions about his overthrow of the Nandas differ and are late, and the chapter declined to narrate a clean story of popular hatred and a clever adviser. It concluded only that Chandragupta displaced the Nandas around 321 BCE under circumstances poorly documented.

The chapter then separated the historical Chandragupta from legend. The identification of Sandrokottos with Chandragupta provides a synchronism that anchors early South Asian chronology, but the childhood stories, meetings with Alexander, and origin narratives cannot be treated as biography.

The Chanakya and Arthashastra problem was one of the chapter's central source-method lessons. The surviving Arthashastra is a composite and substantially later work, and it is best used as evidence for a South Asian statecraft tradition, not as a Mauryan government handbook. The historical Chanakya remains a figure of tradition.

The chapter examined expansion across northern South Asia without false precision. Mauryan authority came to encompass an unprecedented proportion of northern South Asia and important northwestern territories, with evidence from classical sources and the later Junagadh inscription, but the deep south remained outside direct rule and control varied across regions.

The conflict with Seleucus I, around 305 to 303 BCE, resulted in Mauryan dominance in the northwestern borderlands and in diplomatic relations with the Hellenistic world. The traditional account of territory exchanged for 500 elephants and a marriage alliance rests on sparse and ambiguous classical evidence and is under reassessment.

Pataliputra served as the imperial center, and Megasthenes offers an important but mediated view of it, surviving only in later quotation. The chapter showed that provincial government involved governors, local elites, garrisons, and communications, with authority exercised in varying degrees, and that army and revenue, centered on agricultural taxation, sustained the empire, with ancient numerical claims requiring caution.

Bindusara's reign demonstrated that the dynasty survived its founder, with continuing diplomacy and possible expansion, though evidence is thin and later traditions, including Jain accounts of Chandragupta's death, cannot be treated as contemporary. The synthesis argued for a multicausal explanation, rejecting claims that Alexander, Chanakya, or destiny created the empire. Chapter 16 turns to Ashoka.

Chapter Reflection

Chapter 15 matters because it marks the creation of a new scale of political organization in South Asia. The Mauryan Empire brought much of the Gangetic plain, western India, and the northwestern borderlands into a single dynastic system, and it made that system a participant in the diplomacy of the Hellenistic world.

It also matters methodologically. The chapter shows how quickly the evidence for a major transformation can turn out to be late, fragmentary, and mediated. The founder is known through legend, the great treatise is a later composite, and the most detailed eyewitness survives in fragments. Learning to draw careful conclusions from such material is a skill that later chapters will continue to demand.

The chapter also connects regions. Magadha, the Punjab, the Hindu Kush, Iran, and the Hellenistic kingdoms appear in a single story, and the Mauryan expansion shows how developments in the Gangetic east and northwestern frontier could reinforce one another. Continuities from the Nandas, and the persistence of Pataliputra and agricultural revenue, remind students that empires grow from earlier foundations.

Chapter 16 now turns to Ashoka. With Chandragupta's framework and Bindusara's continuity in place, students will meet a ruler who left inscriptions in his own name, and who used them to communicate a distinctive conception of kingship to his subjects.
