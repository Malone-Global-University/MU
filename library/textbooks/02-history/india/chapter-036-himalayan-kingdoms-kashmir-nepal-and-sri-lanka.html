Part VII — Regional Kingdoms and the Early Medieval Order

Himalayan Kingdoms, Kashmir, Nepal, and Sri Lanka

Indian History: South Asia from the Indus Civilization to the Present

Chapter Opening

In the middle of the twelfth century, in the high-walled valley of Kashmir, a Sanskrit poet named Kalhana finished a long verse history of the kings of his homeland. Almost at the same moment, on an island across the Palk Strait, the court of a Sinhalese king was commissioning reservoirs so vast that later generations would call one of them a sea. A third world lay between them: the Kathmandu Valley, where Buddhist monasteries, Shaiva shrines, and royal pillars crowded a small and fertile basin on the road to Tibet. None of these places sat on the Ganges plain, and none belonged to the better-known imperial cores of Rajasthan, the Deccan, or the Tamil country. Yet each sustained durable states, rich literate cultures, and dense links to the wider world.

Chapter 35 examined Rajput polities and the shifting northwest; Chapters 31 through 34 traced the Chalukyas, Rashtrakutas, Pallavas, Cholas, Palas, and Pratiharas. Chapter 36 turns to regions that those chapters touch only at the edges. Its subject is early medieval state formation in mountain valleys, along trans-Himalayan corridors, and on an island. The geographic span is large and the chronology runs from roughly the seventh century to the later twelfth, with some earlier background. The chapter treats three distinct political zones: Kashmir, the Kathmandu Valley (the setting of what early sources call "Nepal"), and Sri Lanka.

The chapter's central argument is that early medieval South Asia was not organized only around the Ganges plain, Rajasthan, the Deccan, and the Tamil country. Mountain valleys and the island of Sri Lanka supported durable states whose political institutions reflected distinctive environments while remaining deeply connected to wider South Asian networks. Kashmir and Nepal linked the subcontinent to Tibet and Central Asia. Sri Lankan rulers drew on irrigation systems, Buddhist institutions, maritime connections, and competition with South Indian powers. Geography constrained these states, but it also provided resources, routes, and strategic advantages that allowed them to exercise substantial regional power.

The three regions are not treated as a miscellany. They are compared. Each had a heartland defined by water and terrain: the Jhelum valley with its lake and flood plain, the Bagmati and Vishnumati basin of the Kathmandu Valley, and the dry-zone plains of northern and north-central Sri Lanka made productive by storage reservoirs. Each produced a distinctive literature, and each produced a historical tradition, since Kalhana's Rajatarangini and the Sinhalese chronicle tradition of the Mahavamsa and Culavamsa are among the most sustained historical narratives of premodern South Asia. Each also left behind a rich body of material evidence in temples, monasteries, inscriptions, coins, and engineered landscapes.

The evidence presents recurring problems of source criticism. Kalhana wrote centuries after the Karkota rulers whose conquests he celebrated. The Lankan chronicles were composed by monastic authors with specific religious and political concerns. Nepal's earliest political history survives chiefly in stone inscriptions, with later chronicles supplying a framework of uncertain reliability. Students should expect the chapter to ask repeatedly what a source can establish, who produced it, and what it leaves out.

The chapter also resists two familiar temptations. The first is to project modern borders backward: early medieval "Nepal" was largely a Kathmandu Valley political world, Kashmir was not an Indian or Pakistani "territory" in any sense those states would later claim, and Sri Lanka's politics did not run along modern ethnic lines. The second is to treat mountains and seas as barriers. Passes, rivers, and straits were also corridors, and the states studied here were in constant contact with their neighbors.

Chapter 36 stands at a hinge in Volume II. It completes the survey of early medieval regional kingdoms begun in Chapter 31, and Chapter 37 will then ask what agrarian expansion, land grants, temples, and merchant networks meant across all these regions. The sources of Chapter 36's evidence also anticipate the arrival, discussed in Chapters 38 and 39, of Muslim-ruled states on the northwestern frontier.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why Kashmir, Nepal, the Himalayan corridors, and Sri Lanka should be studied as regional states in their own right rather than as peripheries of the plains.
  2. Analyze how the geography of the Kashmir Valley, the Kathmandu Valley, and Sri Lanka's dry zone shaped agriculture, defense, routes, and political organization.
  3. Trace the rise of the Karkota dynasty and the political history of Kashmir from the seventh century to the early eleventh century.
  4. Evaluate Kalhana's Rajatarangini as historical evidence, distinguishing what can be corroborated from what is literary tradition, especially concerning Lalitaditya Muktapida.
  5. Describe the religious and intellectual life of Kashmir, including Buddhist, Brahmanical, and Shaiva traditions and the development of Sanskrit literary and aesthetic theory.
  6. Explain the political and religious world of the Kathmandu Valley from the later Licchavi period to the early Malla era, without projecting the modern state of Nepal backward.
  7. Assess the importance of Himalayan trade and religious networks linking Kashmir, Nepal, Tibet, Central Asia, and the Gangetic plain.
  8. Describe later Anuradhapura's kingship, Buddhist institutions, and irrigation economy.
  9. Explain the Chola conquest of Sri Lanka from the standpoint of its effects inside the island, and evaluate the period of Chola rule and the restoration of Lankan kingship under Vijayabahu I.
  10. Compare state formation in Kashmir, Nepal, and Sri Lanka, identifying how each used difficult geography and how none was isolated.
  11. Distinguish contemporary evidence from later chronicle tradition in the study of all three regions.

Historical Context

By the seventh century, the political landscape of South Asia was shifting from the large imperial formations of the Gupta age toward regional states, a transformation traced in Chapters 30 through 35. Kashmir and the Kathmandu Valley had earlier been connected to the Kushan and Gupta worlds (Chapters 21 and 26 through 30), and Kashmir had a long Buddhist history, including the Kushan-period tradition of scholarly assemblies discussed in Chapter 21. Nepal's Licchavi rulers had adopted Sanskrit royal inscriptions on the Gupta model. In Sri Lanka, the kingdom of Anuradhapura, examined in Chapter 24, had maintained a Buddhist monarchy supported by a sophisticated irrigation system for roughly a thousand years before the period covered here.

The wider Asian setting mattered enormously. In the seventh and eighth centuries, the Tibetan Empire expanded across the plateau and into the western mountains, while the Tang dynasty projected power across Central Asia. Kashmir and Nepal sat between these powers and the Indian plains, and their rulers could draw on, resist, or ally with each of them. In the south, the Palk Strait separated Sri Lanka from Tamilakam by a narrow stretch of water, and the rise of the Pallavas, Pandyas, and Cholas (Chapters 32 and 33) made the island's relationship with the Tamil south ever more consequential. The Indian Ocean commerce discussed in Chapter 25 and examined again in Chapters 37 and 49 brought merchants, monks, and diplomats to the island's ports.

The evidence is uneven. Kashmir has a major historical narrative, supported by inscriptions, coins, and monumental remains. The Kathmandu Valley has an unusually dense corpus of Licchavi stone inscriptions, but few narrative histories before the later chronicles. Sri Lanka has the Pali and Sinhalese chronicles, many Sinhala and Tamil inscriptions, and extensive archaeological remains at Anuradhapura and Polonnaruwa. Students should keep the differences in mind: Kalhana is a historian with a method, the Licchavi inscriptions are royal and religious records, and the Lankan chronicles are monastic histories with their own purposes.

A final point concerns scope. Chapter 33 owns the Chola imperial system, and Chapter 36 examines the Chola conquest only for what it meant inside Sri Lanka. Chapter 35 owns Rajput politics and the changing northwest, and Chapter 37 owns the general analysis of land grants, temple economies, Brahmins, jati, and the feudalism debate. Chapter 36 refers to irrigation, monasteries, and endowments where they are necessary to explain particular states, but does not perform that general synthesis.

Section 36.1

Beyond the Plains: Mountains, Valleys, and Islands in Early Medieval South Asia

Histories of early medieval South Asia often follow the large plains and plateaus: the Ganges basin, Rajasthan, the Deccan, and the Tamil country. Those are the regions of the largest armies and best-known dynasties. But political life also unfolded in places where terrain imposed sharp limits. Three kinds of environment organize this chapter: enclosed mountain valleys (Kashmir and the Kathmandu Valley), the passes and plateaus connecting them to Tibet and Central Asia, and an island whose northern half is a dry plain dependent on stored water.

Geography in this chapter should be treated as a set of political variables rather than as static background. Altitude shortens the growing season and limits the number of people who can live in an area, but a mountain basin with reliable snowmelt and fertile alluvial soil can support dense settlement, as both Kashmir and the Kathmandu Valley did. Enclosure protects against invasion and makes a valley easier to unify, yet it also means that rulers depend on a small number of routes for contact, trade, and military supply. Passes can be defended by a few troops, but they are also the channels through which armies, merchants, monks, and diplomats arrive. In Sri Lanka, the Palk Strait offers less a barrier than a short crossing, and monsoon winds shaped when ships could move.

Sri Lanka's dry zone illustrates a different kind of geographic variable. Rainfall is seasonal and unreliable, rivers are modest, and agriculture depends on storing monsoon water in reservoirs. Whoever controlled the construction and maintenance of tanks controlled the surplus on which armies, monasteries, and courts depended. Irrigation was therefore not a technical detail but a foundation of political power, a theme developed in Sections 36.12 and 36.17.

The chapter treats these regions with three cautions. First, they were not peripheries: contemporaries in India, Tibet, China, and Southeast Asia knew them as significant powers. Second, they were not isolated: each of the states studied here maintained diplomatic, commercial, religious, or military contact with several neighbors. Third, modern borders must not be read backward. The chapter's regions are historical units (a valley, an island, a trade corridor), not proto-nations, and the term "Nepal" in most early sources refers to the Kathmandu Valley and its neighborhood rather than the territory of the later state.

Section 36.2

Kashmir: Geography, Sources, and the Making of a Regional Power

The Kashmir Valley lies between the Pir Panjal range to the south and the main Himalayan chain to the north and east. The Jhelum (Vitasta) flows through it, and the valley floor, built of lake deposits and river alluvium, is fertile and densely populated. Rice, along with other crops, can be grown on the floor, and the valley's famous saffron grows on higher terraces. The valley is not easily reached. Traditional approaches include routes over the Pir Panjal toward the Punjab, a route along the Jhelum gorge, and tracks to the north toward Gilgit, Ladakh, and the Karakoram. Winter closes some passes entirely. This combination of agricultural wealth and defensible enclosure is the foundation of Kashmiri statehood.

The valley was also a place where several long traditions met. Buddhism flourished in Kashmir from the Mauryan and Kushan periods (Chapters 16 and 21), and the region produced influential Buddhist scholars and monasteries. Brahmanical and Shaiva traditions grew alongside them. Sanskrit was the language of learning and administration. Kashmiri rulers also maintained diplomatic contact with the Punjab plains, with the region of Gilgit and Baltistan, and with the Tibetan and Chinese worlds.

The evidence for the region is unusually rich for early medieval South Asia, and its variety is itself a lesson. Inscriptions and copper-plate records are relatively few in Kashmir compared with the Deccan, and the dynastic narrative depends heavily on literary sources. Coins issued by the Karkota and later kings survive and provide corroboration for some rulers. The temple remains, including the ruins at Martand, Avantipura, and Parihasapura, supply material evidence. Chinese sources provide an external voice: the pilgrim Xuanzang visited Kashmir around 631–633 CE and described its rulers, monasteries, and learned community, and Tang dynastic records mention embassies from Kashmir.

The central narrative source, though, is Kalhana's Rajatarangini ("River of Kings"), composed in Sanskrit verse in 1148–49 CE in eight books. Kalhana states that he consulted earlier chronicles, inscriptions, land grants, coins, and local traditions, and he offers a distinctive early discussion of how a historian should write. This makes the work extraordinary, but it also means that it must be read critically. Kalhana wrote some four centuries after the Karkota kings and eight centuries or more after the legendary kings of his earliest books. His treatment of the later period, closer to his own time, is generally more reliable than his treatment of the distant past, and his account of any ruler is shaped by moral judgment, literary convention, and the political position of his own time. Section 36.8 returns to the work as a whole.

Historical Vocabulary
Chronicle Traditions: Tarangini and Vamsa

The title Rajatarangini uses tarangini, "river" or "stream," a metaphor for a flowing narrative of kings (raja). In Sri Lanka, the Pali Mahavamsa ("Great Chronicle") and its continuation the Culavamsa use vamsa, "lineage" or "chronicle." Both traditions are narrative histories that organize the past around kings, but they are not modern histories. They have a moral and religious purpose, they may preserve older material, and they were compiled long after many of the events they describe.

Historians therefore use such chronicles as evidence to be tested against inscriptions, coins, archaeology, and foreign accounts. A chronicle is useful when it can be cross-checked, and its silences and emphases are themselves informative about the concerns of its author and patrons.

Section 36.3

The Rise of the Karkota Dynasty

According to Kalhana, the founder of the Karkota line was Durlabhavardhana, who came to power in the early seventh century and whom the chronicle presents as having married into the family of the preceding Gonanda-line king Baladitya. Modern scholars usually place the beginning of his reign around 625 CE, a date that depends on Kalhana's chronology and on cross-checking with the Chinese pilgrim Xuanzang, who visited Kashmir during the reign of a powerful king whose name is generally associated with this period. The dynasty's name is linked to a serpent (naga) lineage, and Kalhana connects Kashmir's early rulers to the world of serpent deities who were central to local sacred geography.

The Karkota rise was part of a wider shift. The decades after the collapse of the Gupta empire and the death of Harsha (Chapter 30) opened space for regional kingdoms across the northern plains and the mountains. Xuanzang reports that the Kashmiri king of his time had extended influence over neighboring territories. The details remain uncertain, but it is plausible that the dynasty used the valley's resources to establish authority over adjoining mountain districts and toward the Punjab foothills. The formation of a durable state in the valley meant bringing local rural elites within a system of royal authority. Kalhana later describes powerful rural landholders as Damaras; such families became major actors in Kashmiri politics, although how far the category can be projected back into the earliest Karkota period is uncertain.

Under Durlabhavardhana's son Durlabhaka (also called Pratapaditya II), and then under Chandrapida and Tarapida, the dynasty consolidated. Chinese records show that in the early eighth century the Tang court recognized a Kashmiri king whose name is generally equated with Chandrapida, suggesting that Kashmir was already part of Tang diplomatic calculations about the western frontier and the Tibetan advance. The pattern is important: the Karkotas were not an isolated mountain dynasty but a participant in an Asia-wide system of embassies, titles, and alliances.

The institutions of the state included a court of ministers, military commanders, and officials drawn from literate communities. Kalhana's later narrative speaks of the influence of scribal families and of palace factions, and he describes tax collection and the royal treasury in some detail. As with all of Kalhana's account, the picture is most reliable for the period closest to his own time, so historians use the early Karkota narrative cautiously and prefer inscriptions, coins, and foreign records where they exist.

Section 36.4

Lalitaditya Muktapida and Kashmiri Imperial Ambition

Lalitaditya Muktapida (traditionally c. 724–760 CE) is the Karkota king most remembered by posterity. Kalhana devotes a long and vivid passage to his reign, describing a series of campaigns that carry the king from the Punjab and Kannauj to the east, south, and west, and to the north across the mountains into Central Asia. He is presented as a conqueror, a builder, and a patron of Kashmiri cults. Modern readers must separate what this narrative can establish from what it magnifies.

Several elements are well supported by other evidence. Tang records mention a Kashmiri king, generally identified with Lalitaditya, who in the 730s sought Chinese support against the Tibetans. This matches the strategic situation: Tibetan power had pushed toward Gilgit and Baltistan in the eighth century, and in 747 a Chinese army under Gao Xianzhi crossed the high mountains to attack Tibetan positions in the region. For a Kashmiri ruler, alliance with China was a reasonable counter to a dangerous neighbor. Kalhana also reports conflict with Yashovarman of Kannauj, a ruler known from other sources, including a Prakrit poem composed in his honor and Chinese records of an embassy. The Kannauj of that age is discussed in Chapter 34.

Other parts of the narrative are harder to accept. The list of regions that Lalitaditya is said to have conquered includes distant lands, legendary territories, and implausibly rapid marches. The account has the shape of a conventional digvijaya, a literary "conquest of the quarters" that symbolically asserts the universal reach of a king, and its details may have been shaped by tradition, poetry, and later legend. A reasonable reading is that Lalitaditya was a powerful ruler who extended Kashmiri influence into the Punjab foothills and the northwest, contested the Gangetic plain with Kannauj, and engaged in diplomacy with Tang China, but that the full conquest narrative should not be converted into a literal map of an empire across Asia.

Kalhana's treatment also contains moral and political commentary. The chronicle praises Lalitaditya's valor and generosity while recording episodes of cruelty and the extravagance of his foundations, and it describes the fiscal pressure that large campaigns and building projects placed on the valley. Kalhana in this way draws attention to a real structural problem: a state with a small agricultural base that undertook large external ventures could strain the countryside. That is evidence for how a twelfth-century historian understood royal power, and it is a hint, though not proof, about eighth-century conditions.

Historical Debate

How Far Did Lalitaditya's Power Extend, and How Reliable Is Kalhana?

The question: Does Kalhana's account of Lalitaditya's conquests describe a Kashmiri empire of subcontinental or Asian scale, or does it magnify a regional power's more limited achievements?

Interpretation A — A genuine imperial moment: Some historians stress that independent evidence confirms important elements of the story: Tang records of Kashmiri embassies, the conflict with Yashovarman, and the geopolitical context of Tibetan pressure. They argue that Kalhana had access to older traditions and that Lalitaditya's reign really did mark Kashmir's greatest political reach, extending influence across the northwest and into the Punjab and perhaps the upper Ganges plain.
Interpretation B — A literary conquest narrative: Others emphasize that Kalhana wrote some four centuries later, drew on traditions shaped by praise poetry, and used a conventional pattern of universal conquest. They point out that there is little inscriptional or numismatic evidence for sustained Kashmiri rule beyond the northwest and that many of the places named in the narrative cannot be verified. On this view, Lalitaditya was a significant regional ruler whose reputation was enlarged by memory.

Evidence: Both positions use the same sources: the Rajatarangini, Chinese dynastic records, the Prakrit poem on Yashovarman, coins, and monuments. The debate turns on how much weight to give a late narrative when independent corroboration is sparse.

Why it matters: The answer affects how historians understand the political geography of eighth-century northern India (discussed again in Chapter 34), the relative power of Kashmir, Kannauj, and Tibet, and the way chronicle traditions shape historical memory, including modern political uses of the past.

Student task: List the claims about Lalitaditya that are supported by a source independent of Kalhana, and those supported only by him. What additional kinds of evidence would help decide between the interpretations?

Section 36.5

Martand, Kingship, and the Material Culture of Karkota Power

The most celebrated surviving monument of Karkota Kashmir is the Sun Temple at Martand (Mattan), near Anantnag. Tradition, beginning with Kalhana, credits Lalitaditya with building it, and architectural historians generally date it to the eighth century. The temple stands on a high plateau (a karewa) with a wide view over the valley. Its main shrine rises in the center of a large rectangular courtyard enclosed by a colonnade of cells, and its stonework shows pedimented doorways, trefoil arches, and fluted columns. Scholars have long debated how much the style owes to older Gandharan and Gupta forms, to local woodworking traditions, and to the architecture of neighboring regions. What is clear is that Kashmiri builders developed a distinctive regional style, not a copy of any one source.

Kingship and religion were closely linked in such foundations. A royal temple to the sun placed the king's name on the landscape and tied his rule to a cult with cosmic associations. Kalhana attributes numerous foundations to Lalitaditya, including the city of Parihasapura, with Buddhist and Brahmanical shrines. The Karkota court patronized both Brahmanical temples and Buddhist monasteries rather than favoring one tradition exclusively, a pattern that fits Kashmir's religious plurality (Section 36.7). Building on this scale required stone, labor, and revenue, and the chronicle's description of fiscal strain should be read alongside the physical evidence of ambition.

Other material remains complete the picture. Coins issued by Kashmiri rulers carry royal names and iconography, though the coinage of the period is less abundant than in many other regions. Bronze and stone sculptures from Kashmir, including Buddhist and Hindu images, show a distinctive style that influenced art in western Tibet and Gilgit (Section 36.11). Manuscripts from the Gilgit region, found in the twentieth century, preserve Buddhist texts in the same cultural world. Together these remains show that Karkota power was expressed through patronage and landscape as well as through armies. Chapter 37 treats the general economics of temples and endowments, and this section keeps to the Karkota case.

Section 36.6

Kashmir after the Karkotas: Utpala Power and Political Change

Kashmiri history did not end with the Karkotas. Kalhana reports that after Lalitaditya the dynasty declined through succession disputes, weak rulers, and the growing influence of court factions. Jayapida, who ruled in the later eighth and early ninth centuries, is remembered as a patron of learning and an adventurer, though Kalhana's account of his travels is highly colored. By the middle of the ninth century the Karkota line had ended, and a new dynasty, the Utpalas, took power under Avantivarman (traditionally 855/856–883).

Avantivarman's reign is associated with political recovery, temple building at Avantipura, and a patronage of scholars. One of the most striking episodes in the chronicle concerns his engineer, Suyya, who is credited with major flood control and drainage works on the Jhelum, which reclaimed land and reduced the devastation of floods and famine. Kalhana's account is partly legendary, but it reflects a real problem: the valley floor is vulnerable to flooding, and managing the river was a prerequisite of prosperity. This makes Suyya's story an environmental history of the valley as well as a tale of royal patronage.

The tenth century was more turbulent. Kalhana describes rulers who were weak or dominated by palace guards, called tantrins, and by powerful landholders, the Damaras, who built fortified positions and challenged royal authority. Queens could be powerful actors: Didda, who held power as regent and ruler from the mid-tenth century until her death in about 1003, receives long and ambivalent treatment from Kalhana, who describes her ruthlessness but also her political skill. The Lohara dynasty, descended from Didda's family, followed. This chapter stops here, though Kashmir's later political history, including the sultanate period, belongs to Chapter 47.

The overall pattern matters more than any single reign. Kashmir was a durable state, but the same enclosure that protected it also fostered competing local power centers. Rulers needed Damara cooperation and also feared Damara autonomy. Kashmir's history shows how a regional state could be strong against external threats while remaining unstable within, a pattern that recurs in other regions studied in this volume.

Section 36.7

Religion and Intellectual Life in Kashmir

Kashmir in this period was religiously plural. Buddhist monasteries remained active and respected: Xuanzang described numerous monasteries and learned monks in the seventh century, and Chinese and Tibetan sources show Kashmir as a center from which Buddhist scholars and texts traveled. Brahmanical cults of Vishnu, Surya, and local deities flourished with royal support, and tradition holds that serpent shrines marked the valley's sacred geography. Rulers patronized more than one tradition. It would be wrong to portray Kashmir as a place where one religion replaced another in a simple sequence.

Within this setting, one tradition acquired remarkable intellectual prominence: the non-dualist Shaiva philosophy often called Kashmir Shaivism. Its early scriptural and teaching traditions are associated with works such as the Shiva Sutras, attributed by tradition to Vasugupta in the ninth century, and with thinkers like Somananda and, most importantly, Utpaladeva (tenth century), who developed the Pratyabhijna ("recognition") school. The Trika system, a tantric Shaiva tradition, was systematized by Abhinavagupta (c. 975–1025), whose writings synthesized ritual, philosophy, and aesthetics. These thinkers argued that ultimate reality is a free, self-aware consciousness identified with Shiva, and that liberation lies in recognizing one's identity with it.

This intellectual flowering should be understood in its social context. The thinkers belonged largely to Brahmanical scholarly communities, wrote in Sanskrit, and depended on patrons and teaching lineages. Their writings reached beyond the valley, and their ideas influenced later Shaiva thought across South Asia (a theme treated in Chapter 48). Yet Shaiva philosophy was not the entire religious life of Kashmir. Village cults, serpent worship, Buddhist devotional practice, and Vaishnava traditions persisted, and the intellectual writings of a few scholars do not tell us what most people believed or did.

Buddhism's position also changed over time. Kashmiri monasteries were centers of learning and of manuscript copying, and scholars from Kashmir played a major role in transmitting Buddhist texts and teachings to Tibet and to Central Asia, a subject returned to in Section 36.11. At the same time, the evidence suggests that in the centuries covered here the dominant sources of royal patronage and intellectual prestige increasingly lay with Brahmanical and Shaiva institutions. Historians avoid describing this as simple "decline," since Buddhist communities continued, but the balance of patronage shifted.

Section 36.8

Sanskrit Learning, Literature, and Historical Memory in Kashmir

Kashmir was one of the great centers of Sanskrit learning, building on the classical tradition surveyed in Chapter 29. Court poets and scholars produced epics, lyric poetry, story collections, manuals of statecraft, and works of literary theory. The ninth-century critic Anandavardhana, associated with the court of Avantivarman, developed the theory of dhvani ("resonance" or "suggestion") in his Dhvanyaloka, arguing that the highest poetry works through suggested meaning. Abhinavagupta wrote commentaries that explored the psychology of aesthetic experience (rasa), linking literary theory to his philosophy. These works shaped Sanskrit criticism across the subcontinent.

Satire, narrative, and practical writing also flourished. The eleventh-century writer Kshemendra composed works of social satire and moral instruction that depict courtiers, ascetics, and merchants with a sharp eye. The Kathasaritsagara of Somadeva, a large collection of tales in Sanskrit verse, was composed in Kashmir in the eleventh century. A ninth-century work attributed to the minister Damodaragupta, the Kuttanimata, depicts urban life and courtesans with striking social detail. These texts are valuable evidence for ordinary and elite life in towns and courts, though they are literary and satirical and must be read as such.

Kalhana's Rajatarangini belongs to this world. It is a work of poetry and history, and its author draws on Kashmiri earlier chronicles, many now lost. Kalhana is aware of the difficulty of his task: he criticizes earlier writers for carelessness, says that a good historian must be free of love and hatred, and acknowledges that he reconstructed the past from inscriptions, grants, and coins as well as from older accounts. He is more critical than many premodern historians, but his notion of history includes moral judgment, miracles, and legend, and he has strong opinions about the destructive effects of tyranny and of palace corruption.

The result is a source of unusual depth. A student who learns to read Kalhana learns the general method for reading premodern chronicles: identify the author's date, purposes, and sources; test claims against independent evidence; treat the closer past with more confidence than the remote; and distinguish between what the text states and what historians infer. The Sri Lankan chronicles in Sections 36.12 through 36.17 require the same skills.

Section 36.9

The Kathmandu Valley and Early Medieval Nepal

The Kathmandu Valley is an oval basin roughly 25 kilometers across, drained by the Bagmati and its tributaries and surrounded by hills. Its fertile lake-bed soils and its position on routes between the Gangetic plain and Tibet made it one of the most important settlement zones of the central Himalaya. In early medieval sources, "Nepal" refers primarily to this valley and its neighborhood, sometimes called the Nepal Mandala, not to the whole territory of the modern state. The valley's inhabitants, ancestors of the Newar community, spoke a Tibeto-Burman language, though Sanskrit was the language of inscriptions and learning.

The political world of the period is known chiefly through the Licchavi dynasty, which ruled the valley from about the fifth century into the eighth and left roughly two hundred stone inscriptions. These records, written in Sanskrit, document royal grants, temple endowments, and the titles of officials. They show a monarchy with a developed administration and with powerful local institutions. Around the early seventh century, Amshuvarman, who rose first as a high official and then as ruler, dominated the valley; Xuanzang, who did not visit Nepal but reported what he heard, describes him as a learned king who composed a Sanskrit grammar. Amshuvarman's reign is associated with an important phase of contact with Tibet.

That contact was real but is hard to reconstruct. Tibetan tradition, recorded in later sources, says that a Nepalese princess (Bhrikuti) married the Tibetan emperor Songtsen Gampo and brought Buddhist images, but this is based on much later accounts, and Chinese and Tibetan sources disagree about details. Chinese records preserve a better-documented episode: in the 640s, after the death of Harsha, the Chinese envoy Wang Xuance was attacked in northern India and, according to Chinese sources, raised troops from Tibet and from Nepal to defeat the attackers. This episode shows that the Kathmandu Valley could supply soldiers and was a recognized player in a wider Himalayan and Tang diplomatic world. It was also in the range of Tibetan influence, and Licchavi rulers at various times may have been subordinate, allied, or independent of Tibetan power.

The end of the Licchavi period is poorly documented. The inscriptions thin out in the eighth century, and a period of political fragmentation follows, during which local rulers and warlord families competed. The Nepal Samvat era, which began in 879 CE, reflects a new cultural and political consciousness, and chronicles refer to dynasties such as the Thakuris. Over the following centuries, the valley emerged into the early Malla period, and in the late eleventh century a ruler named Nanyadeva established a kingdom in the southern plain that touched the valley's politics. Historians have relatively little contemporary evidence for these centuries, and the later chronicles that fill the gap must be treated as traditions rather than records.

Section 36.10

Kingship, Religion, and Urban Life in the Nepal Valley

Licchavi kingship drew on North Indian models. Kings adopted Sanskrit titles and genealogies, and inscriptions describe the ruler as devoted to the Shaiva deity Pashupati, whose shrine on the Bagmati became (and remains) one of the valley's great religious sites, or to Vishnu, whose temple at Changu Narayan is among the oldest. At the same time, Buddhist monasteries (viharas) and stupas, including the Svayambhu stupa on its hilltop, received patronage. The valley's religion was not an either/or choice: Buddhist and Brahmanical institutions coexisted, and Newar Buddhism developed distinctive forms of monastic life that persisted into modern times.

Administrative evidence in the inscriptions suggests a combination of royal authority and local institutions. Land and other resources were assigned to temples, monasteries, and religious foundations, and officials supervised revenue and justice. The inscriptions also mention local councils and organizations of residents that managed property and public works. This does not make the valley a "feudal" society in the textbook sense, a debate belonging to Chapter 37; it shows instead a locally rooted political order in which royal power depended on negotiation with religious and local institutions.

The valley's settlements were compact and dense. Archaeological and inscriptional evidence shows towns arranged around temples, water spouts, rest houses, and markets. Artisans in metal, stone, wood, and manuscript production worked for religious and secular patrons. The metalworking and sculptural traditions of the valley grew so well known that Newar artists were later sought in Tibet and beyond. The valley was therefore an urban and craft center as well as an agricultural one, a point to hold in mind when comparing it with Kashmir.

Everyday Life and Social History

Fields, Water, and Festivals in Three Landscapes

Most people in all three regions were cultivators. In the Kashmir Valley, farmers grew rice on irrigated land and managed flooding along the Jhelum, which could destroy crops and cause famine, as Kalhana's story of Suyya suggests. In the Kathmandu Valley, rice terraces on the valley floor supported a densely settled population, and inscriptions record rights over irrigation channels and public wells. In Sri Lanka's dry zone, villagers depended on tanks for water, and the labor to repair embankments was an obligation to local authorities, monasteries, or the king.

Religious life shaped the calendar. Festivals, processions, and pilgrimage brought together people of many groups. Women appear in the record as donors and as members of royal families; in the Kathmandu Valley, inscriptions record women's gifts, and in Sri Lanka, the graffiti at Sigiriya preserve verses composed by visitors, including women, in the eighth through tenth centuries. Yet our sources are heavily weighted toward courts, monasteries, and elites, and the experience of laborers, herders, and dependent peasants must be inferred cautiously.

Primary Source Window

Paraphrase: The record praises the king's genealogy, his piety toward his parents, and his military success against rivals. It is evidence of elite religious and political culture. It does not reveal how most people lived, how the army was recruited, or how the valley's economy was organized. It also reflects the perspective of a king commissioning his own memorial.

Section 36.11

Himalayan Trade Networks and Transregional Exchange

The Himalaya is often described as a barrier, but in the early medieval centuries it was also a system of corridors. Routes ran from Kashmir north through Gilgit and the Karakoram toward Central Asia and the Tarim basin; from Kashmir east through Ladakh toward western Tibet; and from the Kathmandu Valley north over passes to Tibet and south to the Gangetic plain. Chinese pilgrims, Tibetan translators, Central Asian merchants, and Indian monks used these routes. Rulers of mountain states benefited from controlling points along them and from the revenue and prestige that came with hosting travelers.

Goods that moved along these routes included salt, wool, medicinal herbs, musk, gold, horses, metals, textiles, and luxury items. Specific volumes are hard to establish because the surviving evidence is fragmentary: inscriptions, pilgrim accounts, and the objects themselves. What is clearer is the exchange of religious goods and knowledge. Buddhist monks carried manuscripts and images from Kashmir and Nepal to Tibet. Kashmiri scholars and artists were influential in western Tibet, and in the later tenth and eleventh centuries the Tibetan translator Rinchen Zangpo studied in Kashmir and brought back texts and craftsmen. Newar artisans and the Nepalese Buddhist community similarly shaped Tibetan art and religion. The eleventh-century Bengali teacher Atisha traveled through Nepal on the way to Tibet, a reminder of how the corridors connected Chapter 34's Pala Buddhist world with the plateau.

Political events moved along the same corridors. Tibetan expansion in the eighth century into the western mountains forced Kashmir and the Gilgit region to respond, and Chinese expeditions crossed the Pamirs. In the Kathmandu Valley, rulers allied with or resisted Tibetan power. These cases show that mountain states were not "peripheral" or isolated but operated in a geopolitical system that stretched from the Gangetic plain to Chang'an and Lhasa.

Section 36.12

Later Anuradhapura: Kingship, Buddhism, and the Irrigation State

The Sri Lankan kingdom of Anuradhapura, discussed in its early centuries in Chapter 24, remained the island's principal political center through the later first millennium CE. In this later phase, roughly from the seventh to the tenth centuries, the monarchy ruled the northern and north-central region called Rajarata from Anuradhapura, while the southeast (Ruhuna) and the central highlands (Malaya) had their own local power. Kings of the period legitimated themselves as protectors of Buddhism, builders of monasteries and reservoirs, and descendants of the legendary royal line recorded in the Mahavamsa. Succession disputes were frequent, and several rulers were backed by factions of nobles, monks, and South Indian allies.

Buddhist institutions were central. The great monastic communities, notably the Mahavihara and the Abhayagiri, held land and received royal gifts, and they had distinct intellectual and ritual traditions. The Abhayagiri was open to a wider range of texts and Mahayana and tantric ideas and had links with Buddhist networks across Asia, while the Mahavihara represented a more conservative Theravada tradition and was the milieu in which the Mahavamsa was produced. Rulers sometimes favored one over the other. The chronicles, written from a Mahavihara perspective, are therefore not neutral about the Abhayagiri.

The material base of the state was irrigation. The kingdom's heartland lies in the dry zone, where reservoirs (vavi, later wewa) store monsoon rain for rice cultivation. Kings built and repaired tanks and channels, and monasteries managed their own water. The scale of the engineering is evident in surviving embankments and sluice structures. Control of water gave rulers a basis for taxation and patronage, but it also created vulnerabilities: breached tanks and neglected canals reduced yields, and political instability could wreck the system.

Environment & Society

Irrigation and Political Power in Sri Lanka's Dry Zone

Sri Lanka's dry zone receives most of its rain in concentrated monsoon periods and suffers long dry spells. Farmers and kings responded by building tanks fed by diverted streams and by linking tanks in chains across the landscape. Each tank required embankments, spillways, and sluices, and each needed regular repair. A state that could mobilize labor and materials for such works could support a large population and a monastic and royal establishment.

The relationship ran both ways. Kings gained legitimacy and revenue from irrigation, and the chronicles praise rulers who build tanks. Yet the system was also vulnerable to warfare, neglect, and environmental change. Historians still debate how far the later shift of political centers, from Anuradhapura to Polonnaruwa and eventually to the wetter southwest, reflects irrigation problems, invasion, disease, or political choice, and this chapter treats it as a multicausal question.

Historical Vocabulary
Tank (vava) and Nikaya

A tank, in South Asian English, is a reservoir formed by an earthen embankment across a stream or hollow, known in Sinhala as vava or wewa. It is not a military vehicle or a small container. Its meaning in this chapter is a major piece of public infrastructure.

A nikaya is a monastic fraternity or lineage. In Sri Lanka, the Mahavihara, Abhayagiri, and Jetavana were rival nikayas with their own estates and traditions. The reunification of the sangha under Parakramabahu I (Section 36.17) was therefore a political as well as a religious act.

Section 36.13

Sri Lanka and the Politics of the Tamil South

The Palk Strait is narrow, and contacts between Sri Lanka and the Tamil country were continual. Merchants, soldiers, monks, and royal families crossed it, and the rise of strong dynasties on the mainland (Chapters 32 and 33) made Lankan politics increasingly entangled with theirs. Manavamma, who regained the Lankan throne in the late seventh century, is said in the chronicles to have done so with Pallava assistance. Lankan kings sought marriage alliances and military help from South Indian courts, and South Indian rulers sometimes intervened in Lankan succession.

The ninth century shows how rapidly relations could change. The Pandya king Srimara Srivallabha invaded Sri Lanka in the reign of Sena I, occupied Anuradhapura, and carried off treasure; Sena I withdrew to the hills. His successor Sena II later retaliated by sending an expedition that, according to the chronicles, sacked Madurai. The tenth century saw a new pattern as the Cholas became the dominant mainland power. When the Chola king Parantaka I defeated the Pandya ruler, the Pandya king fled to Sri Lanka and sought help; the Lankan king's support proved insufficient, and the Pandya left royal regalia behind when he went on to Kerala. Those regalia became a point of conflict between the Lankan court and the Cholas.

Historians should avoid reading this history as a contest between a single Sinhalese nation and a single Tamil enemy. The actors were dynasties, courts, and armies, and alliances crossed what later nationalists would call ethnic lines. Lankan kings married South Indian princesses, hired South Indian troops, and supported Tamil mercantile guilds, whose inscriptions survive on the island. Tamil merchants and Buddhist monks of the south were part of Lankan society. At the same time, the sustained rivalry between Lankan kings and the Pandya and Chola dynasties was real, and the eventual Chola conquest was a profound shock. The next section addresses it. Chapter 33 gives the Chola imperial perspective, and this chapter keeps its focus on the island.

Section 36.14

The Chola Conquest of Sri Lanka

Around 993 CE, Rajaraja I launched an invasion of Sri Lanka, in the reign of Mahinda V, and Chola forces took the northern Rajarata, including Anuradhapura. The Chola court's own inscriptions celebrate the conquest, and the Lankan chronicles lament it. Both sets of sources are partisan. The Chola record is a victory proclamation. The Culavamsa, composed by monastic authors in a later century, emphasizes the destruction of monasteries and the loss of royal authority. Historians reconcile them by looking at what physically changed on the ground, including the archaeology of the royal cities, the evidence of Chola-period inscriptions on the island, and the shift in administrative geography.

The Chola administration moved the center of government from Anuradhapura to Polonnaruwa, which the Cholas renamed Jananathamangalam. The strategic logic is easy to see: Polonnaruwa lay closer to the eastern routes, was less exposed to the northwest coast, and was better placed to supervise the Mahaweli river system and communications with the Chola homeland. Anuradhapura's decline as a royal capital was therefore partly a result of conquest and partly a decision about where to govern. Rajendra I, the son of Rajaraja, extended Chola control over the rest of the island's central lands about 1017 and took the Lankan king, Mahinda V, to the Chola country, where he died in captivity in 1029. The south-eastern region of Ruhuna remained outside direct control for some time.

It would be wrong to claim that the Cholas "destroyed" the civilization of Anuradhapura. Their conquest profoundly altered the island's political geography. Royal patronage of the great Buddhist monasteries was disrupted, and some monasteries were damaged. But Buddhist communities continued, irrigation works were not abandoned wholesale, and the population remained. The Cholas themselves built Shaiva temples at Polonnaruwa, some of which survive, and Chola inscriptions record administration of land and local government. Chapter 33 treats the Chola system as a whole, and the purpose here is to identify what conquest meant within Sri Lanka.

Cause and Effect

The Chola Conquest of Sri Lanka, c. 993–1017

Long-term causes

Centuries of Palk Strait entanglement, rivalry among Lankan, Pandya, Pallava, and Chola courts, and the rise of a Chola state with resources and maritime ambitions.

Short-term causes

The Pandya regalia and refuge in Lanka, Lankan succession weaknesses under Mahinda V, and the aggressive policy of Rajaraja I and Rajendra I.

Immediate effects

Fall of Anuradhapura as capital, relocation of administration to Polonnaruwa, capture of the Lankan king, and disruption of royal Buddhist patronage.

Long-term consequences

A new political geography centered on Polonnaruwa, a period of resistance, and the eventual reconstitution of Lankan kingship under Vijayabahu I.

Historical complexity: Chola motives included strategic, commercial, and dynastic concerns. No single cause suffices, and Lankan weakness was as important as Chola strength.

Section 36.15

Chola Rule, Resistance, and the Reconstitution of Lankan Kingship

Chola control of the island's north and center lasted roughly seven decades. Inscriptions and archaeology document a Chola military, fiscal, and administrative presence, with Polonnaruwa as the principal base and written records of land, taxation, and endowments. Tamil merchant guilds, including the group known as the Five Hundred, appear in inscriptions on the island and show that commerce linked Sri Lanka's ports and inland centers to the mainland. The Chola rulers favored Shaiva temples and also, in some cases, supported Buddhist foundations, as the contemporary patronage of a Buddhist monastery at Nagapattinam on the mainland suggests.

Resistance centered in Ruhuna in the south-east, where Lankan rulers and local leaders maintained a separate political base. Over time, a prince who took the name Vijayabahu gathered support, and fought several campaigns against Chola forces. He established himself as a claimant in Ruhuna around 1055, a date from which later chronicle reckoning counts his reign (hence the traditional 1055–1110). Chola power was already under pressure from conflicts on the mainland, and after prolonged warfare he captured Polonnaruwa and Anuradhapura around 1070, ending Chola rule in the island, and was consecrated king at Anuradhapura. The Culavamsa presents his campaign as the restoration of Buddhist kingship.

The restoration should not be described simply as national liberation. Vijayabahu's armies included South Indian troops, called velaikkara, and his court maintained marriage ties with mainland Indian ruling families, including Kalinga on the eastern coast, while other connections linked the dynasty to the Pandya south. Later in his reign, some of those troops rebelled, a reminder that mercenary forces had their own interests. The political identities of the period were dynastic, regional, and religious rather than ethnic in the modern sense. Vijayabahu's kingship also required rebuilding institutions. The chronicle says that he restored monasteries and that, because the ordained community had declined, he sent to Burma (Ramanna) for monks to reestablish proper ordination. This episode shows both the fragility of monastic institutions after conquest and the island's connection to Theravada communities in mainland Southeast Asia.

Multiple Perspectives

Experiencing Chola Occupation in Sri Lanka

These perspectives are reconstructed from the types of evidence described, not from quotations by named individuals.

The Chola court and administrators: Inscriptions present the conquest as a royal triumph, a strategic extension of Chola maritime reach, and an opportunity to extract revenue and tribute. Polonnaruwa under Chola control was a regional administrative base.
Lankan Buddhist monastic communities: The chronicles, produced in monastic circles, stress the loss of royal patronage and the disruption of the sangha. This is a source with a clear viewpoint, and it may exaggerate the destruction.
Tamil merchants and local elites: Guild inscriptions show merchants who benefited from commercial integration and sometimes acted as patrons of temples and Buddhist institutions. For them, the era may have brought opportunity as well as disruption.
Peasant cultivators in the Rajarata: The sources say little directly. Revenue demands, military requisitioning, and the maintenance of tanks would have been constant concerns, regardless of who sat in the capital.
Resistance leaders in Ruhuna: For them, the occupation was a challenge to their kingship and legitimacy, and the struggle drew on regional loyalties and Buddhist ideology as well as dynastic ambition.
Section 36.16

Polonnaruwa and the Rebuilding of the Lankan Kingdom

Polonnaruwa did not appear from nowhere after the Cholas. It had been a modest settlement and a provincial center before the conquest, was developed as the Chola administrative base, and was then adopted by Vijayabahu I as his capital. This continuity is one of the chapter's key points: Chola rule changed the geography of Lankan power by making Polonnaruwa the political center, and restored kingship built on that change rather than reversing it. Vijayabahu did consecrate himself at Anuradhapura, honoring its ritual importance, but his government sat at Polonnaruwa.

Vijayabahu's reign (to about 1110) was devoted to reconstruction: restoring irrigation works, rebuilding Buddhist institutions, rewarding supporters, and managing a quarrelsome court. After his death, the kingdom entered a period of dynastic dispute. Several claimants competed, and the eventual victor was Parakramabahu I, who emerged from a branch of the family based in the central and southern regions. His rise shows that Lankan politics remained regionally rooted: Ruhuna and Malaya still provided power bases from which a ruler could challenge the center.

The restored kingship rested on three pillars. First, the king as defender of Buddhism, with royal control over the sangha and its endowments. Second, control of irrigation and agricultural revenue. Third, military power, now including both Sinhala-speaking levies and professional troops. The royal palace complex, council chamber, and the great stupas and image houses of Polonnaruwa, built in later reigns, express this ideology in stone.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Anuradhapura → Chola Rule → Polonnaruwa

What changed? The political capital moved from Anuradhapura to Polonnaruwa. The Cholas introduced Shaiva temples and a mainland-style administration, and the monastic landscape was reshaped. The restored kingship was based in the new capital.

What remained continuous? The dry-zone irrigation economy, the ideology of Buddhist kingship, the great monastic institutions, Anuradhapura's ritual prestige, and the pattern of regional centers in Ruhuna and Malaya.

Who experienced change most directly? Royal courts, monastic communities that depended on royal patronage, and the people near the capitals. Who experienced more continuity? Most cultivators, whose lives were governed by the seasons and the tank.

Why does the pattern matter? It shows that conquest transformed political geography without obliterating the society underneath, and that restoration was never a simple return.

Section 36.17

Parakramabahu I, Polonnaruwa's High Point, and Comparing Mountain and Island States

Parakramabahu I (r. c. 1153–1186) is the most celebrated king of Polonnaruwa. The Culavamsa describes him as a unifier who defeated rival claimants, reorganized the army, and built on a grand scale. He reconstructed and expanded the irrigation system, notably through the huge reservoir complex known as Parakrama Samudra ("Sea of Parakrama"), and tradition attributes to him the principle that no water should flow to the sea unused. He also reformed the sangha, bringing the rival nikayas under a single authority, and his reign produced the great monuments of Polonnaruwa, including the royal palace precinct and the image house known as the Lankatilaka. The chronicle also records overseas ventures, including involvement in Pandya succession disputes and a conflict with Burma, which suggest an ambitious foreign policy.

The chronicle, however, is a panegyric composed for a patron, and later events show the limits of the achievement. After Parakramabahu's death, the kingdom was destabilized by dynastic competition and by interventions from South India, setting the stage for a new period of crisis that later chapters take up. The point for this chapter is not to rank Parakramabahu but to see how a ruler combined irrigation, Buddhist reform, military force, and display to maintain a powerful island state.

Comparative State Formation

How Three Regions Used Difficult Geography

Kashmir used an enclosed, well-watered valley as the base of a state that was defensible and wealthy but dependent on a few passes and troubled by powerful landholding families. Its rulers projected influence outward through diplomacy and campaigns, and its enduring contribution was intellectual and literary.

The Kathmandu Valley used a compact, fertile basin on the route between the plains and Tibet. Its kingship rested on negotiation with local institutions and religious foundations, and its urban and artisan culture made it a node in Himalayan exchange.

Sri Lanka used an island separated from the mainland by a narrow strait and an irrigated dry zone. Its kings built power through control of reservoirs and the Buddhist sangha, and they were repeatedly drawn into the politics of the Tamil south.

Common features: Each region combined agricultural surplus with defensible position. Each developed a regional religious and literary tradition and a historical tradition. None was isolated. Differences include the role of urbanism (strongest in the Kathmandu Valley), the importance of irrigation (decisive in Sri Lanka), and the role of intellectual export (greatest in Kashmir).

Section 36.18

Review and Historical Synthesis

The synthesis question for this chapter is: How did geography shape state formation without isolating Kashmir, Nepal, and Sri Lanka from the wider political and cultural history of South Asia? The preceding sections suggest an answer in four parts.

First, geography created the conditions for durable regional states. The Kashmir Valley's fertile floor and mountain walls, the Kathmandu Valley's compact basin on a trans-Himalayan route, and Sri Lanka's irrigated dry zone each supported dense populations and a surplus that could sustain courts, temples, monasteries, and armies. In each case, the physical setting also posed specific problems: floods and factional landholders in Kashmir, dependence on a few routes and shifting overlords in the Kathmandu Valley, and the maintenance of tanks and the pressure of South Indian rivalry in Sri Lanka.

Second, none of the three regions was isolated. Kashmir's kings dealt with the Tibetan and Tang worlds and with Kannauj and the Punjab. Kashmiri and Nepalese monks, craftsmen, and scholars worked across the Himalaya and shaped Tibetan Buddhism and art. Lankan kings married into South Indian families, hired Tamil troops, and faced Pandya and Chola invasions, while Lankan monasteries linked to Burma and other Buddhist regions. Mountains and straits were corridors as well as barriers.

Third, all three regions generated a distinctive combination of religion, learning, and historical memory. Kashmir's Shaiva philosophy, aesthetic theory, and Kalhana's chronicle; the Newar Buddhist and Shaiva culture of the Kathmandu Valley; and the Mahavihara tradition and chronicles of Sri Lanka show how regional societies adapted the shared Sanskrit and Buddhist heritage to local conditions. Fourth, the chapter's evidence is always partial. Kalhana, the Lankan chronicles, and the Licchavi inscriptions illuminate elites and courts, and historians must ask what they omit.

The chapter also prepares what follows. Chapter 37 asks what agrarian expansion, land grants, temple economies, and merchant networks meant across these and other early medieval societies. Chapters 38 and 39 turn to Muslim communities in Sindh and the Ghaznavid and Ghurid states on the northwestern frontier, whose arrival would reshape Kashmir's neighbors and, eventually, Kashmir itself (Chapter 47).

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined three regional zones of early medieval South Asia that lay outside the better-known plains and plateau cores: Kashmir, the Kathmandu Valley, and Sri Lanka. It argued that these regions supported durable states, developed distinctive cultures, and were integrated into wider networks, and that their geography both constrained and enabled their political development.

Kashmir's enclosed valley supported the Karkota dynasty from the seventh century. Under rulers such as Lalitaditya Muktapida, the dynasty took part in the diplomacy of Tibet, Tang China, and the northern plains. Kalhana's twelfth-century chronicle is the principal narrative source, but its account of Lalitaditya's conquests requires critical reading against Chinese records and other evidence. Karkota power was also expressed in monuments such as the Martand temple.

After the Karkotas, the Utpala dynasty under Avantivarman presided over recovery and the flood-control works associated with Suyya. The later tenth century saw instability, powerful Damara landholders, palace guards, and the rule of Queen Didda, followed by the Lohara dynasty. Kashmir's religious life was plural, and the valley became a center of Shaiva philosophy and Sanskrit literary theory under thinkers such as Utpaladeva, Anandavardhana, and Abhinavagupta.

The Kathmandu Valley, known as "Nepal" in early sources, was governed by the Licchavis, whose inscriptions document royal ideology, religious patronage, and local institutions. Its Buddhist and Shaiva traditions, craft production, and urban settlement made it a hub of Himalayan exchange. The end of the Licchavi period is poorly documented, and later chronicles must be used cautiously.

Himalayan trade and religious networks connected Kashmir, Nepal, Tibet, and Central Asia. Goods, monks, manuscripts, artisans, and armies moved along mountain corridors, and Kashmiri and Newar culture shaped Tibetan Buddhism and art. Mountains were corridors as well as barriers.

In Sri Lanka, later Anuradhapura combined Buddhist kingship with a dry-zone irrigation economy. Rivalry among Lankan, Pandya, Pallava, and Chola courts led to repeated intervention across the Palk Strait. The Chola conquest of about 993–1017 ended Anuradhapura's primacy and made Polonnaruwa the principal center, but did not destroy the island's society, and the Cholas ruled for roughly seven decades.

Resistance based in Ruhuna led to the restoration of Lankan kingship under Vijayabahu I, who established himself as a claimant in Ruhuna around 1055, and who recovered Polonnaruwa and Anuradhapura and was consecrated around 1070. His campaigns drew on South Indian troops and alliances, which cautions against a simple Sinhala-versus-Tamil narrative. He rebuilt monastic institutions with help from Burma.

Parakramabahu I consolidated the kingdom, expanded irrigation works, reformed the sangha, and built the monuments of Polonnaruwa, though the chronicle that praises him is a panegyric and the kingdom soon experienced instability. A comparison of the three regions shows common patterns of agricultural surplus, defensible position, and literate religious traditions, alongside differences in urbanism, irrigation, and intellectual export.

The chapter's method has been as important as its narrative. Students have practiced reading chronicles, inscriptions, foreign accounts, and archaeological remains critically, and have seen how modern borders and national narratives can distort the study of regions that were never simply peripheral or isolated.

Chapter Reflection

Chapter 36 matters because it widens the lens of early medieval South Asian history. A survey that follows only the great plains and plateau dynasties would miss states in which learning, religion, trade, and engineering reached remarkable heights. Kashmir produced political power and also some of the subcontinent's most sophisticated philosophy and literary theory. The Kathmandu Valley developed an urban, artisanal, and religiously layered culture at the junction of Indian and Tibetan worlds. Sri Lanka built an irrigation state that sustained a Buddhist monarchy for centuries.

The chapter also shows how geography works in history. It does not dictate outcomes, but it shapes the resources, routes, and vulnerabilities that rulers and communities must handle. The same mountains that protected Kashmir fostered powerful local landholders. The same strait that made Sri Lanka a distinct kingdom drew it into mainland politics. Corridors carried monks, armies, goods, and ideas, linking regions that maps might seem to separate.

Later chapters will return to these regions: Kashmir's sultanate period in Chapter 47, Sri Lanka and the Indian Ocean in Chapter 49, and the modern history of all three in Volume VI. The habits practiced here, such as testing chronicles against independent evidence, resisting modern borders, and attending to environment and everyday life, will matter wherever the textbook turns next.

Chapter 37 now steps back to ask what agrarian expansion, land grants, temples, caste, and commerce meant across these and the other regional kingdoms of early medieval South Asia.
