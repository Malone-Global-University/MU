Part XI — The Eighteenth-Century Transformation
59

Sikhs, Mysore, and Other Regional Powers

Indian History: South Asia from the Indus Civilization to the Present

Chapter Opening

By the early 1760s, three very different experiments in political power had taken shape in widely separated parts of the subcontinent. In Punjab, bands of mounted Sikh fighters, having survived campaigns by Mughal governors and Afghan invaders, assembled at Amritsar and increasingly asserted control over territory. Farther south, Haidar Ali, who had risen through the army of the Wodeyar kings of Mysore, acquired effective control of their kingdom while retaining its royal family. At the southern tip of the peninsula, Karthika Thirunal Rama Varma governed Travancore, whose predecessor Marthanda Varma had defeated Dutch forces at Colachel in 1741 and dedicated the kingdom to Sri Padmanabha in 1750. These were neither miniature copies of the Mughal Empire nor three stages of a single inevitable process: each political formation emerged from different institutions, resources, and rivalries.

Chapter 57 traced the weakening of Mughal imperial authority after 1707, and Chapter 58 followed the most extensive of the new political networks, the Maratha polity. This chapter widens the lens. It asks what else emerged in the space that Mughal weakness opened, and it argues that the answer is not simply disorder. Across the eighteenth century, communities, soldiers, ministers, and kings in different regions built states and military formations that differed sharply in their institutions, their sources of legitimacy, their relationships with local society, and their strategies for raising money and soldiers. The chapter's governing claim is that eighteenth-century South Asia was a competitive political environment in which regional powers experimented, rather than a dwindling imitation of an imperial past.

The chapter is deliberately comparative. It examines five groups of polities. The Sikh political formations of Punjab were collective and confederated, bound together by a shared religious identity and a tradition of deliberation. Mysore under Haidar Ali and his son Tipu Sultan was, by contrast, a centralizing military-fiscal state whose rulers sought to reduce intermediary power and to draw directly on the agricultural and commercial wealth of the Kaveri basin and the western coast. Travancore, under Marthanda Varma, combined military reorganization with a royal ideology centered on a temple. The Jat rulers of Bharatpur built a territorial state out of fortified villages and agrarian resources in the Braj country between Delhi and Agra. The Rohilla Afghans established chieftaincies in Rohilkhand on the basis of military recruitment networks and a distinctive construction of Afghan lineage.

Placed side by side, these histories show that there was no single way to be a power in the eighteenth century. Some states relied on cavalry and mobility, others on fortresses; some on revenue farming and negotiated local authority, others on salaried officials and direct assessment; some drew their legitimacy from Islam, some from a temple deity, some from a shared religious community, and some from genealogy and military reputation. Religion mattered to political identity in each case, but so did inheritance, taxation, alliances, access to markets and ports, and the willingness of local cultivators and landholders to cooperate. Readers should resist any explanation that reduces these states to religious solidarity alone.

The chapter also takes care to avoid two kinds of retrospective distortion. The first is the tendency to read these states chiefly through their later encounters with British power. Colonial authors, and later nationalist ones, often judged Mysore by its wars with the East India Company and judged the Sikh polity by its eventual subordination or by the subsequent kingdom of Ranjit Singh. Those encounters matter, and they are treated in Chapters 64 and 65, but they were not the principal concern of the people who lived in these states during most of the eighteenth century. The second distortion is the temptation to treat any one ruler, above all Tipu Sultan, as a vehicle for present-day political argument. This chapter treats Tipu's government as a historical subject, attending to both the documented record of patronage and the documented record of coercion.

The evidence for these histories is correspondingly varied, and every body of sources carries a distinct bias. Mughal court newsletters (akhbarat) record how the imperial government saw Sikh activity. Sikh historical memory survives in genealogies and in narratives composed decades or generations after the events they describe. The administrative record of Mysore comes largely through Persian letters and chronicles preserved, translated, and selected by British officials who were at war with Tipu. For Travancore, Dutch East India Company records provide a rare contemporary foreign account alongside local chronicles and temple records. For the Rohillas, Persian genealogical and historical writing by Rohilla elites themselves is available, and it must be read as an exercise in constructing legitimacy as much as a record of fact. Each Primary Source Window in this chapter models how to read one of these traditions critically.

The chapter proceeds in five divisions. Division I (§§59.1–59.4) reconstructs Sikh mobilization from the aftermath of Guru Gobind Singh's death through the Sikh seizure of territory in the 1760s. Division II (§§59.5–59.8) examines the Wodeyar kingdom of Mysore and Haidar Ali's rise and government. Division III (§§59.9–59.12) is the chapter's fullest study of institutional state-building, following Tipu Sultan's reign. Division IV (§§59.13–59.16) turns to Travancore, the Jats, and the Rohillas, which receive independent analysis and are not treated as footnotes to the better-known states. Division V (§§59.17–59.20) compares their military systems, fiscal practices, and models of sovereignty and closes with a historical synthesis.

By the end of the chapter, students should be able to explain why the regional states of the eighteenth century took divergent forms, why military development in each case depended on fiscal resources and political coalitions, and why the decline of Mughal authority did not produce an institutional vacuum. They should also be ready for Chapter 60, which examines a different but equally important form of political transformation: the provincial regimes of Bengal, Awadh, and Hyderabad that retained Mughal forms while becoming effectively independent.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Trace the course of Sikh political mobilization from Banda Singh Bahadur's campaigns of 1709–1716 through the Sikh seizure of territory in the 1760s.
  2. Explain the institutional features that allowed Sikh communities to sustain collective military action, including the jathas, the Dal Khalsa, the gurmata, and the misls.
  3. Evaluate competing descriptions of the misls as a confederacy, a set of territorial principalities, or an evolving military-religious network.
  4. Describe the institutional foundations of Mysore under the Wodeyars and explain how Haidar Ali transformed existing state structures rather than inventing a state from nothing.
  5. Analyze the revenue, recruitment, and fiscal arrangements of Haidar Ali's government and assess their effects on local chiefs and agrarian communities.
  6. Assess Tipu Sultan's administrative centralization, commercial initiatives, and diplomacy as eighteenth-century military-fiscal policies rather than as a modern development program.
  7. Distinguish among patronage, ceremonial legitimation, punishment, forced displacement, and conquest in the contested record of Tipu Sultan's relations with religious communities.
  8. Explain how Marthanda Varma consolidated Travancore and how the Battle of Colachel and the dedication to Sri Padmanabha fit into a wider process of state-building.
  9. Analyze the social and economic foundations of Jat power at Bharatpur and the military networks and genealogical claims that supported Rohilla authority in Rohilkhand.
  10. Compare the military systems of the Sikh formations, Mysore, Travancore, the Jats, and the Rohillas, and evaluate the claim that innovation came through adaptation and coordination rather than imported weapons alone.
  11. Interpret primary sources from Mughal, Sikh, Persian, British-edited, and Dutch traditions, identifying the perspective and limits of each.
  12. Explain why the weakening of Mughal authority produced multiple pathways of regional state formation rather than an institutional vacuum or a single successor model.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did the weakening of Mughal central authority create opportunities for new forms of sovereignty rather than simple disorder?
  2. How did the Sikh community move from the defeat and execution of Banda Singh Bahadur to collective control of territory within five decades?
  3. Was the Sikh political order of the eighteenth century a confederacy, a set of territorial chieftaincies, or something else, and what evidence decides the question?
  4. In what sense was Haidar Ali's rise a transformation of existing Mysorean institutions rather than the achievement of a lone adventurer?
  5. How did Mysore under Haidar Ali and Tipu Sultan finance its armies, and who bore the costs?
  6. What does the record of Tipu Sultan's patronage of temples alongside documented episodes of coercion suggest about the limits of explaining his policy through a single religious motive?
  7. Why did Travancore, a relatively small kingdom, become one of the most effective states of the south, and what role did religion play in its legitimacy?
  8. How did the Jats of Bharatpur and the Rohillas of Rohilkhand build states, and why should they not be treated merely as rebels or as military opponents of their neighbors?
  9. How did military innovation in these states depend on revenue, logistics, local recruitment, and political coalitions rather than on weapons alone?
  10. How have colonial and nationalist narratives shaped the way these regional states are remembered, and what does a comparative approach correct?

Historical Context

The political landscape from which these states emerged was shaped by the long reign of Aurangzeb (Chapter 54) and the contested succession that followed his death in 1707 (Chapter 57). The Mughal empire did not vanish in 1707. Its emperors continued to confer titles, its administrative vocabulary continued to structure revenue and rank, and its legitimacy remained an asset that regional rulers were reluctant to abandon. What changed was the capacity of the imperial center to coordinate its provincial governors, to control the assignment of revenue (jagirs) to its nobility, and to protect its heartland from external attack. The invasions of Nadir Shah in 1739 and of Ahmad Shah Durrani in the following decades, treated in Chapter 57, demonstrated that weakness vividly.

Conditions in the regions differed. In Punjab, a fertile agricultural zone watered by five rivers and lying astride the invasion route from Afghanistan, the Mughal provincial government was repeatedly challenged by internal revolt and external invasion. In the south, the old Vijayanagara successor kingdoms such as Mysore, discussed in Chapter 45, had survived as small but resourceful states, now confronting the shifting claims of the Marathas (Chapter 58), the Nizam of Hyderabad, the Nawabs of the Carnatic, and the European trading companies (Chapter 61). In the Malabar region of the western coast, a mosaic of small chieftaincies faced the pressures of the pepper trade. Around Delhi and Agra, and in the Doab and Rohilkhand to the east, agrarian communities and military entrepreneurs had access to rich land and a plentiful supply of mounted soldiers.

Two structural features recur across all of these cases. The first is the central importance of what historians call the military labor market. Eighteenth-century South Asia contained a large population of skilled and mobile soldiers, including Afghan, Rajput, Maratha, Hindustani, and Turani cavalry and a growing body of musketeers and gunners, who sold their services to those able to pay them. The state that could secure regular revenue could hire soldiers and compete. The second feature is the persistence of Mughal political forms. Even rulers who had ceased to obey the emperor often continued to use his titles, seals, and administrative vocabulary, a pattern more fully explored in Chapter 60. Haidar Ali, Najib ud-Daula, and Suraj Mal each combined independent action with some reference to imperial authority, though the Sikh polity and, increasingly, Tipu Sultan chose to emphasize alternative sources of legitimacy.

Students should also note the limits of the evidence. Eighteenth-century political history was written, in the first instance, by court chroniclers, Persian-language historians, European merchants and officials, and later by colonial and nationalist authors. Much of what is known about Sikh institutions comes from texts composed in the late eighteenth and nineteenth centuries. Much of what is known about Mysore comes from Persian chronicles and from British accounts written by the adversaries of Haidar Ali and Tipu. The chapter therefore insists on the distinction between contemporary documents, retrospective traditions, and modern scholarship, and it flags the points at which the factual record remains contested.

Sikh Militarization and the Emergence of Political Power

From the defeat of Banda Singh Bahadur to the Sikh seizure of Sirhind and Lahore

Section 59.1

Punjab after Guru Gobind Singh and the Rise of Banda Singh Bahadur

When Guru Gobind Singh died at Nanded in the Deccan in 1708, the Sikh community he left behind was already a layered and politically experienced body. Chapter 48 traced the teachings of Guru Nanak and the formation of the early Sikh Panth, and Chapter 54 introduced the conflicts between the Sikh Gurus and the Mughal state. Between the late seventeenth century and 1708, the tenth Guru had established the Khalsa, a community of initiated Sikhs bound by common discipline and distinctive outward marks, and had fought several engagements against hill rajas and imperial officers in the Shivalik foothills. Punjab itself was a province with a dense agrarian population, many of them Jat cultivators, in which Sikh congregations (sangats) existed in villages and towns and where the memory of conflict with provincial authorities was fresh. It was also a province whose Mughal administration was becoming harder to run, as the prolonged Deccan wars had drained resources and officers from the north.

The man who transformed these circumstances into an organized rebellion is known as Banda Singh Bahadur. Born in the Pir Panjal region of the Jammu hills, he had lived as an ascetic before meeting Guru Gobind Singh at Nanded, where, according to the Sikh tradition, he accepted the Guru's mission and was sent to Punjab. Details of his early life, including his earlier name, come from later narratives, and historians treat them with care. What is better established is that by late 1709 he was in Punjab at the head of a rapidly growing force, and that the movement drew substantial support from rural communities, among whom Jat and other cultivating groups were prominent, as well as from Sikh followers who rallied to the Guru's name.

The campaign moved quickly. Banda's forces attacked and plundered Sonepat and Kaithal in the Delhi region, defeated and killed the faujdar of Samana, a town associated with the execution of the Guru's family members and with the earlier execution of Guru Tegh Bahadur's associates, and gathered strength for the decisive confrontation with Wazir Khan, the Mughal governor at Sirhind. At Chappar Chiri, near Sirhind, in May 1710, Wazir Khan was defeated and killed, and Sirhind was taken and sacked. The symbolic weight of this event was considerable in Sikh memory, since Sirhind was identified with the execution of the Guru's young sons. Banda then began to build a territorial base. He established a stronghold at Lohgarh in the Shivalik foothills, appointed officials to hold captured districts, and, according to tradition and to some surviving evidence, issued seals and coin legends in the names of the Gurus.

Historians disagree about how revolutionary the social program of this movement was. Some accounts emphasize the redistribution of land and the displacement of intermediary landholders (zamindars) and revenue officials in the districts Banda controlled, and see the rising as an agrarian upheaval as well as a religious one. Others note that the evidence for systematic land reform is thin and mostly retrospective, and that the movement's practices of revenue collection, plunder, and appointment may have resembled those of other contemporary rebellions. The sensible position for a student is that cultivators and local communities participated in large numbers, that the movement challenged existing landholding and administrative relationships in the territories it held, and that the details of its institutions remain uncertain because most surviving descriptions come from its enemies or from much later traditions.

The Mughal response was substantial. Emperor Bahadur Shah, returning from the Deccan and Rajasthan, led imperial forces northward in 1710, and Lohgarh fell in December of that year, although Banda escaped into the hills. After Bahadur Shah's death in 1712 and the contested succession that followed, Farrukhsiyar's government continued the campaign, and a series of imperial and provincial forces, including those of the governor Abdus Samad Khan, steadily reduced Banda's support. In 1715, Banda and a large body of followers were besieged at Gurdas Nangal, in the Gurdaspur region, and surrendered after enduring a prolonged blockade. He was taken to Delhi and, together with many of his companions, was executed in 1716.

The significance of Banda's career lies less in its immediate success than in what it demonstrated. It showed that a Sikh force could defeat a Mughal governor, hold fortified towns, and claim revenue. It also demonstrated the vulnerability of a centralized movement dependent on a single leader. The Sikh polity that eventually emerged after 1748 would be built on a different organizational principle, one of dispersed and collectively recognized leadership. That later development should be understood as a response to the experience of 1710–1716, and the next section examines how Sikh communities survived and reorganized in its aftermath.

Section 59.2

Sikh Communities, Military Bands, and Survival after 1716

The years after 1716 are sometimes remembered as a time of unbroken persecution, and the Mughal government of Punjab did conduct sustained campaigns against Sikh fighters. Yet the period is better understood as one of reorganization under pressure, in which Sikh communities developed forms of mobility, shared authority, and negotiation that would make later territorial power possible. Persecution and accommodation both formed part of the record.

The characteristic military unit of this period was the jatha, a band of mounted fighters, often a few hundred strong, led by a recognized commander and sustained by a combination of plunder, protection arrangements, and support from sympathetic villages. Jathas could disperse into the Shivalik hills, the Malwa tracts south of the Sutlej, and the semi-arid country of the Lakhi Jungle when pressed, and reassemble when pressure eased. This mobility was a strategic answer to the superior resources of the provincial government. Fighters could not hold fortresses against the Mughal artillery, but they could avoid decisive engagements, attack revenue convoys and isolated posts, and withdraw. The tactical lesson drawn from Banda's defeat at Gurdas Nangal was to avoid being besieged.

Religious centers, above all Amritsar with the Harmandir, provided points of assembly. Sikhs gathered there at the festivals of Vaisakhi and Diwali, and the practice of gathering as a community to deliberate, discussed in the vocabulary box below, gave the dispersed bands a common forum. The Mughal governors of Lahore, among them Abdus Samad Khan (in office until 1726) and his son Zakariya Khan (in office from 1726 to 1745), alternated between repression and negotiation, depending on the strength of the Sikh bands and the demands of other military problems. Contemporary and later accounts also describe divisions within the Sikh community in this period, including debates over the appropriate relation of the community to the state and over the succession of leadership after Banda.

The most revealing episode of negotiation occurred in 1733, when Zakariya Khan offered the Sikhs the title of Nawab and a jagir, a revenue assignment often reported in Sikh historical writing as worth about a lakh of rupees annually, in an attempt to bring the bands into the imperial system. The nominal valuation is not independently established: published secondary accounts disagree, and the actual revenue collected cannot be inferred from a figure attached to the grant. After deliberation, the offer was accepted and Kapur Singh, a respected leader, received the title, from which he is known as Nawab Kapur Singh. The arrangement did not last, and within a few years the jagir was withdrawn and fighting resumed, but the episode matters for two reasons. It shows that the governor regarded Sikh leaders as potential imperial clients and not only as enemies, and it shows that the Sikh community was willing to consider incorporation into the existing revenue system while preserving its own deliberative authority. Kapur Singh's later career is associated with organizational change, including the traditional account of the consolidation of the many jathas into two larger groupings, the older and more seasoned Budha Dal and the younger Taruna Dal, around 1734. The details of that arrangement rest largely on later tradition.

Zakariya Khan's death in 1745 was followed by factional conflict within the Lahore government, and by the establishment of Ahmad Shah Durrani's Afghan polity in 1747 and the invasions of Punjab beginning in 1748, which are discussed in §59.4. In the interval, the Sikh bands grew in size and acquired additional resources, notably the practice later called rakhi, the collection of a payment from villagers in return for protection, which turned military capacity into a recurring source of income. Villages that paid rakhi received protection from other raiders and, in effect, became part of the political economy of the bands, an arrangement that bridged plunder and revenue. Historians continue to debate how voluntary these arrangements were, but they were a crucial step toward governing territory.

Historical Vocabulary
Gurmata and Sarbat Khalsa

The Sarbat Khalsa ("the whole Khalsa") refers to the assembly of Sikhs, traditionally held at Amritsar before the Akal Takht at festivals such as Vaisakhi and Diwali, to consider matters affecting the community. A gurmata (literally "the Guru's counsel") is a resolution adopted by such an assembly, understood as expressing the collective authority of the community under the Guru's guidance. In the eighteenth century these practices gave dispersed military bands a shared forum for deciding on common action, resolving disputes, and naming commanders.

Students should avoid assuming that these institutions functioned like a parliament with formal membership and fixed procedures. Sources composed later sometimes describe them in idealized terms, and their actual authority varied with circumstances, but they established the principle that sovereignty belonged to the community and not to one individual, which marks a decisive contrast with Banda's centralized leadership and with the monarchies of Mysore and Travancore.

Section 59.3

The Dal Khalsa, the Misls, and Sikh Political Organization

By the late 1740s the Sikh bands had consolidated into a more formal military body. The traditional account holds that at the Vaisakhi gathering of 1748 at Amritsar, the many jathas were merged into a single army, the Dal Khalsa, divided into a number of large groups, and placed under the overall leadership of Jassa Singh Ahluwalia. The date 1748 is the one given in the later Sikh tradition, and modern historians generally accept that a reorganization of this kind occurred in the late 1740s while treating the specific number of constituent groups and the neat annual setting with caution.

The constituent formations were called misls, a term of Arabic origin meaning something like "equal" or "alike," adopted in Punjabi for a military group of comrades. The conventional list names twelve, though sources differ about their number and composition. Prominent among them were the Ahluwalia misl, associated with Jassa Singh Ahluwalia, who became the community's leading commander after Kapur Singh; the Bhangi misl, which became the dominant power in Lahore and Amritsar for a time under leaders such as Hari Singh, Jhanda Singh, and Ganda Singh; the Ramgarhia misl, linked to Jassa Singh Ramgarhia and to the fort Ram Rauni, which the Sikhs rebuilt at Amritsar as Ramgarh around 1748; the Kanhaiya misl under Jai Singh Kanhaiya, whose territory lay in the Gurdaspur region; and the Sukerchakia misl under Charat Singh, whose later leadership would produce Ranjit Singh. Another notable group was the Singhpuria or Faizalpuria misl, associated with Kapur Singh, and the Karorsinghia misl, linked with Baghel Singh, who led Sikh forces to Delhi in 1783 and negotiated the right to build gurdwaras there.

Distinct from these in important ways were the Phulkian chiefs of the Malwa region south of the Sutlej. Ala Singh of Patiala, whose territory was consolidated in the 1760s, was recognized by Ahmad Shah Durrani with a title and a robe of honor, and the Phulkian houses are not always included in the traditional list of misls. Their position illustrates how the Sikh political world was not a single unified entity: some leaders were willing to negotiate with Afghan or Mughal authority, while others resisted.

Each misl was led by a sardar, whose authority rested on personal prowess, kinship, and the loyalty of his followers, who attached themselves by choice and could, in principle, move to another leader. Misl leaders took shares of the plunder and, increasingly, of the revenue of territories they controlled. These territories were held by what historians describe as a combination of conquest, the rakhi system, and the grant of villages to followers as shares of the leader's holdings. By the 1760s and 1770s, the leaders of the larger misls had become, in effect, territorial lords controlling districts, collecting revenue through their own agents, and building forts. Often the territory of a single misl was scattered, with the holdings of different misls interspersed, a pattern that reflected the conquest process, in which each leader seized what he could.

The relationship between collective and individual authority is the central question about this system, taken up in Historical Debate 1 below. What the evidence does show is that the Sarbat Khalsa and the gurmata continued as a form of collective legitimacy even as individual sardars acquired territorial power. The Khalsa's collective identity was sufficiently strong to permit common action against external threats, such as the Afghan invasions, while individual misls competed, and sometimes fought each other, over territory and revenue.

Historical Vocabulary
Misl

The word misl, from an Arabic term for likeness or equality, was used in Sikh eighteenth-century practice for a confederate military group whose members regarded one another as comrades under a chosen leader. In time it came to denote the territorial formations into which Sikh power was divided.

Students should be wary of treating "the twelve misls" as a fixed constitutional list. Different sources enumerate them differently, some groups merged or split, and leadership and territorial claims changed across the century. The misl is better understood as an evolving form of organization that shifted from mobile military bands to territorial households.

Context: Kesar Singh Chhibbar belonged to a Brahman family that had served the Sikh Gurus, and his work, composed around 1769, offers a genealogical history of the ten Gurus and of the Sikh community, together with an account of the formation of the Khalsa and of events in his own lifetime. Its perspective, however, is not that of all Sikhs. This makes the work unusually instructive about internal disagreement as well as collective memory: its narrator does not speak for every Khalsa warrior or Sikh agrarian household. The author's family background, genealogical interests, and judgments about caste and authority shape his account. Its narrative of earlier decades also depends on oral and family tradition rather than on contemporary documents.

Section 59.4

Sikh Territorial Power, Afghan Invasions, and the Changing Punjab

The Sikh bid for territory unfolded against a background of invasion and political fragmentation. Ahmad Shah Durrani, who founded the Durrani state in Afghanistan after Nadir Shah's death in 1747, invaded northern India repeatedly, and Chapter 57 traced the broader effects, including his defeat of the Marathas at Panipat in 1761 (Chapter 58). For Punjab, the invasions meant a contest over who would govern the province. After his early incursions, the Mughal government in Delhi ceded Lahore and Multan to Ahmad Shah in 1752 in an effort to buy peace, and the Mughal governor Mu'in al-Mulk, known as Mir Mannu, who held Lahore from 1748 to 1753, served as a link between the older and newer authorities. Mir Mannu's death in 1753 left the province unstable.

The Sikhs acted as both opponents and opportunists within this contest. They plundered Afghan baggage trains and attacked isolated detachments, and they allied, when advantageous, with other powers. In 1758, for example, Sikh forces cooperated with the Maratha commanders Raghunath Rao and Malhar Rao Holkar and with the Mughal officer Adina Beg Khan in pushing Ahmad Shah's son Timur Shah out of Lahore, an episode reminding students that the Marathas of Chapter 58 extended their reach into Punjab. The Afghan response was severe. In 1757 the Afghan commander Jahan Khan attacked Amritsar and desecrated the Harmandir, and Baba Deep Singh, remembered in Sikh tradition as a martyr, died in the fighting. Ahmad Shah's army returned in 1761 for the campaign that ended at Panipat, and again in 1762.

The event of 1762 that Sikh tradition calls the Vadda Ghallughara, the "Great Holocaust," occurred in February of that year. Ahmad Shah's forces, aided by local Muslim chieftains of Malerkotla and other allies, surprised a large body of Sikhs, including many non-combatants, near Kup in the Malwa region, and killed a large number of them. Estimates of the dead range widely, from around ten thousand to considerably higher figures, because the sources are later and partisan. The Afghan army also blew up the Harmandir in Amritsar later that year. Yet the Sikh recovery was swift. By October, at Diwali, Sikh forces gathered at Amritsar and defeated an Afghan detachment, and the gathering underlined the resilience of their collective institutions.

The decisive Sikh gains came in the following years. In January 1764 Sikh forces defeated and killed Zain Khan, the Afghan governor of Sirhind, and captured and sacked the town, which was identified in Sikh memory with the execution of Guru Gobind Singh's sons. The conquered districts were divided among the misl leaders and their allies, including Ala Singh of Patiala, whose territory expanded. Within a year, Sikh chiefs, notably Lehna Singh, Gujjar Singh, and Sobha Singh of the Bhangi group, entered Lahore, and coins were struck there in 1765 with a legend celebrating the community's collective power. The coin tradition emphasized the Guru and the community, rather than any individual ruler or the emperor, which indicates the kind of legitimacy that Sikh leaders claimed.

Ahmad Shah returned in 1764, 1765, 1767, and 1769, and Sikh control was not uninterrupted, but the Afghan state, beset by troubles at home, was unable to sustain a lasting occupation. After his death in 1772 and under his successor Timur Shah, Afghan authority in Punjab receded. By the 1770s and 1780s, the Sikh misls controlled most of the Punjab plains. Their raids reached Delhi in 1783, and in the same decades the political center of gravity of the community shifted, as the powerful Bhangi leadership gave way to the rise of the Sukerchakia and Kanhaiya groups. The Sukerchakia leader Charat Singh and his son Mahan Singh laid the foundations of what Mahan Singh's son Ranjit Singh would build. This chapter does not narrate that later consolidation. Ranjit Singh's seizure of Lahore in 1799, the formation of his kingdom, and its administration belong to Chapter 65.

For the purposes of this chapter, the important point is how Sikh political power in these decades combined religious solidarity with competing chieftaincies. The shared identity of the Khalsa enabled collective action and gave legitimacy to resistance. At the same time, the conquest of territory created rivalries over revenue and boundaries that sometimes led to conflict among the misls themselves. The tension between collective sovereignty and territorial lordship is the subject of the debate below.

Historical Debate 1

Were the Sikh Misls a Confederacy, a Collection of Territorial Principalities, or an Evolving Network?

The question: How should historians characterize the political order of the Sikhs between the 1740s and the end of the eighteenth century?

Interpretation A — A confederacy under collective authority: Some historians, drawing on the Sikh tradition and on accounts of the Sarbat Khalsa and the gurmata, emphasize the Khalsa's common identity and its deliberative institutions. They see the Dal Khalsa as a real federal body in which sardars acknowledged a collective authority, particularly in the period of common struggle against the Afghans.
Interpretation B — Territorial principalities in competition: Others stress that, once territory was seized, individual misl leaders acted as independent lords, building forts, collecting revenue, and sometimes fighting one another. On this view the collective institutions had limited practical authority after the 1760s, and the label "confederacy" overstates their coherence.

Evidence: The two views weigh the same evidence differently. Retrospective Sikh narratives tend to stress collective institutions. Records of land revenue, village arrangements, and military campaigns tend to show leaders acting for their own households and followers. Differences among the misls complicate any generalization. The Phulkian chiefs negotiated with Afghan authority, while the Bhangis dominated Lahore and Amritsar for a time before declining.

Why it matters: The answer affects how historians understand the origins of Ranjit Singh's kingdom. If the system was a confederacy with real collective institutions, his rule may be seen as a centralization of an existing political body. If it was a set of competing principalities, it appears closer to a conquest and subordination of rivals. Many scholars today adopt a middle position, describing an evolving network whose character changed between the 1740s, when collective action predominated, and the 1780s and 1790s, when territorial competition did.

Student task: Using §§59.3–59.4, identify two pieces of evidence that support collective authority and two that support territorial independence, and explain which better describes the period before and after 1764.

The Kingdom of Mysore and the Rise of Haidar Ali

How an established South Indian kingdom became a military-fiscal state

Section 59.5

Mysore before Haidar Ali: Wodeyar Kingship and Regional Institutions

Haidar Ali did not create the state he came to govern. The kingdom of Mysore had existed for well over a century when he rose to prominence, and its institutions, resources, and military traditions provided the foundation for what followed. Chapter 45 traced the history of Vijayanagara and the political fragmentation that followed its defeat at Talikota in 1565. In the decades after that defeat, the Vijayanagara governors at Srirangapatna, on an island in the Kaveri River, held the region on behalf of the declining imperial line. In 1610, Raja Wodeyar, the head of a ruling family based at Mysore, took Srirangapatna from the Vijayanagara viceroy, and from that point the Wodeyar dynasty made the island fortress its capital.

The kingdom's geography shaped its development. The Kaveri basin offered fertile alluvial land, which could be irrigated by channels drawn from the river, in a region of relatively modest rainfall on the leeward side of the Western Ghats. To the west lay the forested ghat country, the pepper and spice districts of the coast, and the ports of Canara and Malabar; to the east and south lay the plateau and the routes to the Carnatic and Tamil country. Control of Srirangapatna gave the Wodeyars command of the river's crossing, and the fort's position on an island made it very difficult to besiege. Kanthirava Narasaraja I, who ruled from 1638 to 1659, expanded the kingdom's territory and issued a coinage, and Chikka Devaraja, who reigned from 1673 to 1704, was a still more notable builder of institutions. His reign saw the purchase of Bangalore from the Mughal government in 1687, an expanded revenue system, the organization of departments of government, and a system of communication and espionage. Tradition credits him with a regular organization of eighteen departments (athara kacheri), though details of the arrangement come from later accounts.

Within this royal state, several categories of power coexisted. At the local level, hereditary village headmen and accountants managed rural society and revenue. Between the village and the court stood the palegars (the Kannada and Telugu forms of polygar), hereditary military chiefs who held forts and districts and owed tribute and military service to the ruler but frequently acted with considerable independence, a pattern inherited from the Nayaka system of Vijayanagara. The court itself comprised the royal household, the great ministers, and military commanders. The principal executive officers were the dalavayi, the commander-in-chief, and the sarvadhikari, the chief administrator and minister.

After Chikka Devaraja's death in 1704, the kings of the dynasty were mostly weak, and power moved to ministers and military officers. By the 1730s, the brothers Devaraja (the dalavayi) and Nanjaraja (the sarvadhikari), of a family from Kalale, dominated the government under the nominal rule of Krishnaraja II, who ruled from 1734 to 1766. This division of the monarch's symbolic status from the actual conduct of government was not unique to Mysore, since a comparable pattern appeared in the Maratha polity, where the Peshwas governed in the name of the Chhatrapati (Chapter 58). In Mysore, it meant that the machinery of the state, including its army, its revenue collectors, and its fortress network, could pass into the hands of a capable commander without the destruction of the dynasty.

The mid-eighteenth century also drew Mysore into the wider contests of southern India. The kingdom was involved in the struggle for Trichinopoly in the Carnatic in the 1740s and 1750s, during which it sent forces and incurred heavy debts, and it faced Maratha demands for tribute and the pressure of the Nizam of Hyderabad. These pressures strained the revenues of Mysore and created an environment in which military ability was richly rewarded. An experienced officer who could command troops, secure revenue, and negotiate with neighbors held a very strong position, as the career of Haidar Ali would show.

Section 59.6

Haidar Ali: Military Advancement and the Acquisition of Power

Haidar Ali was born around 1720, though the date varies in different accounts, into a military family. His father Fath Muhammad served as a commander (naik) in the service of Mysore's neighbors and later of Mysore itself, and Haidar began his career as a soldier, rising by ability through the Mysore army serving under Nanjaraja and Devaraja. Contemporary British sources suggest that he was not formally lettered, though he developed strong skills of command and administration, and in this he resembled many successful military figures of his time. His rise should not be understood simply as the career of a talented adventurer. It occurred within, and in part reshaped, the framework of the Mysore state.

His first major position of independent responsibility came in the mid-1750s, when he was appointed commander (faujdar) of Dindigul, a fort and district to the south of Mysore, in 1755. At Dindigul he built up his own force, drew revenue from the district, and, according to the standard accounts, established contacts with French military specialists who helped develop his artillery and the training of his infantry. This relationship with the French, who were then competing with the British in the Carnatic wars (Chapter 61), would remain important to his military organization. Control over troops who looked to him for pay, and over revenue that financed them, was the foundation of his later power.

His political ascent followed from the crises of the Mysore court. In the late 1750s, Maratha invasions and unpaid soldiers created a political emergency. Haidar's military successes in defending Mysore and quelling mutinies won him titles and increasing responsibility. The ministers who had previously dominated the court found their authority challenged, and in 1760 a faction led by the dewan Khande Rao, with support from elements of the royal household, moved against Haidar and forced him to flee. His position was strong enough, however, that he soon returned and, in 1761, defeated Khande Rao and took control of the government. From about that time Haidar was the effective ruler of Mysore, though he formally continued to act in the name of the Wodeyar raja.

This relationship between actual and nominal authority was carefully maintained. The Wodeyar raja remained the symbolic sovereign, and successive members of the family occupied the throne, while the executive power, the army, and the treasury were in Haidar's hands. He held the title of Nawab, and, in dealings with other powers, behaved as an independent sovereign, while maintaining the ceremonial fiction of royal service. This approach resembles that of other eighteenth-century rulers who sought to consolidate power without breaking with the older sources of legitimacy that their subjects recognized. It also meant that he needed to cultivate both the ministers and military officers around him and the Hindu religious and landed elites who looked to the Wodeyar dynasty.

What made Haidar's rise a transformation, and not merely a personal ascent, was his use of the existing state apparatus. He took over a kingdom that had an established revenue system, hereditary officials, fortresses, and a tradition of employing mercenary and regular soldiers. He applied to it a new intensity of control, drawing the army more firmly under his direct authority, paying soldiers through the central treasury as far as he could, and subordinating or removing the intermediate chiefs who stood between the center and the land. The sources of his strength were as much administrative as military, and the following section examines them.

Section 59.7

Haidar Ali's Government, Revenue System, and Regional Consolidation

The central problem of Haidar Ali's government was financial. To maintain a large standing army that included cavalry, infantry, and artillery, he needed a steady and growing flow of revenue, and this requirement drove most of his administrative decisions. His approach combined the extension of territory, which brought in new revenue and plunder, with efforts to tighten collection within the territory he held. The two reinforced each other. New conquests financed the army, and the army made new conquests possible.

The expansion was rapid. In the early 1760s he captured Sira, a Mughal-held district to the north, and in 1763 took the rich kingdom of Bednur (Ikkeri) in the western hills, whose treasury was reportedly considerable and which he renamed Haidarnagar. He pressed northward toward the Maratha-held lands around Dharwar and Savanur, and in 1766 he invaded Malabar on the western coast, subduing the Zamorin of Calicut and other chiefs and gaining control of the pepper and spice districts and access to the sea. The conquest of Chitradurga, whose palegar had resisted for years, was completed in 1779. Control of these areas, from the Kaveri plains to the coastal belt, gave Mysore both agricultural revenue and the commercial income of the ports and the spice trade.

Within these territories Haidar sought to strengthen the central authority. He relied on revenue officials known as amils, who administered districts and collected revenue on the state's behalf, supplemented by hereditary local officials. He drew upon a corps of capable administrators, many of whom were Hindu Brahmans from families with long experience in Mysore's revenue service, among them Purnaiya, who later served Tipu Sultan and then the restored Wodeyar government. At the same time, Haidar worked to reduce the independence of the palegars, who controlled forts and troops and whose tribute payments were often irregular. Some he defeated, as at Chitradurga, and others he made accept stricter obligations. Reducing their autonomy increased the revenue available to the state and removed potential rivals.

The administration of the military was closely tied to that of revenue. Haidar paid many of his troops directly from the treasury rather than through land assignments, which gave him greater control over his soldiers but also meant that delays in payment could cause serious unrest. He recruited from diverse backgrounds, including Muslim cavalry from the north, local infantry, and, according to reports, units known as chelas, who were raised from captured or recruited boys and trained as soldiers loyal to the state. The details and numbers of such units are reported by observers who were often hostile, and they should be treated with caution, but they point to a general effort to build a standing army whose loyalty was to the ruler rather than to intermediate lords.

The balance between inherited office and new military authority also shaped the state's resilience. Local landholders, revenue accountants, and temple institutions possessed knowledge of fields, customary payments, and communities that soldiers newly appointed by Haidar could not instantly acquire. Administration therefore depended as much on supervising or accommodating existing specialists as on creating new commands. A district that yielded revenue in an ordinary season might not meet its obligations when troops requisitioned grain or rival forces passed through; military urgency had to be reconciled with the longer-term survival of cultivation. This dependence helps explain both the government's achievements and the recurring resistance of the palegars.

The effects on local communities were mixed. For cultivators, the greater regularity of central administration could mean more predictable assessments but also more systematic collection, and in wartime the state's demands for grain, transport animals, and labor were heavy. For merchants and bankers, the state offered protection and opportunities, especially in the revenue-rich coastal districts, while also exacting loans and contributions. For the palegars and other local power-holders, consolidation meant a loss of autonomy, in some cases accompanied by forced removal or the transfer of populations. The costs of Haidar's expansion also fell on the territories he invaded; the devastation of the Carnatic in 1780, which contemporary accounts connect with the severe famine that followed, is one example, and it is a reminder that military state-building brought suffering to many communities. Students should keep both the achievements and costs in view as they assess the developing Mysorean state.

Section 59.8

Haidar Ali's Military Power and South Indian Diplomacy, 1761–1782

Haidar Ali's diplomacy was as active as his campaigning. Throughout his rule, Mysore sat at the intersection of several powers: the Marathas to the north and west, the Nizam of Hyderabad to the northeast, the Nawab of the Carnatic and his English protectors at Madras to the southeast, and the French and Portuguese on the coast. Haidar's goal was to maintain Mysore's independence and expand its holdings by exploiting divisions among these powers, a strategy that depended on a flexible willingness to change alliances.

His relationship with the Marathas was both hostile and entangled. The Maratha polity, discussed in Chapter 58, claimed tribute from the territories of the south and campaigned repeatedly against Mysore. In 1771, a Maratha army under the Peshwa Madhav Rao defeated Haidar and forced him to pay tribute and cede territory. Madhav Rao's death in 1772 and the political turmoil in Pune that followed gave Haidar the opportunity to recover territory, and he did so over the following years. The pattern of the relationship, with alternating Maratha pressure and Maratha internal weakness, is an example of how events in one regional polity shaped the opportunities of another.

With the East India Company the relationship was shaped by the Company's growing involvement in the Carnatic. The First Anglo-Mysore War (1767–1769), in which Haidar faced a combination of the Company and the Nizam, ended in a treaty signed at Madras in 1769 after Haidar's cavalry advanced to the vicinity of the city. In 1780, Haidar entered the Carnatic in what became the Second Anglo-Mysore War, and his forces won an important victory over a British detachment at Pollilur in September 1780, though he suffered a defeat at Porto Novo in July 1781. The campaigns and their outcomes are treated in Chapter 64. For present purposes, their importance is what they reveal about Haidar's position: he could field an army capable of defeating Company troops in open battle, and he sought, with limited success, to build a coalition of Indian powers and the French against the Company.

His military organization was crucial to his diplomatic weight. His forces combined cavalry, which provided mobility and had been the traditional arm of the Mughal and Deccan armies, with disciplined infantry and a substantial artillery. He employed European and especially French officers and gunners to train and supply his troops, but he also drew on local craftsmen and on his own arsenals. He organized units of rocket-men, whose use is discussed in §59.17. The effectiveness of this force depended on logistics: the supply of grain, draught animals, and fodder, which required cooperation from grain merchants, transporters such as the Banjara carriers, and the local population, and which also set limits on how far and how long his armies could operate.

Haidar Ali died on 7 December 1782 at his camp near Chittoor, during the course of the Second Anglo-Mysore War, apparently of a malignant growth that had afflicted him for some months. His son Tipu Sultan, who was then commanding forces on the western coast, returned to take up the succession, and the army and the ministers accepted him. By then Haidar had built a state that extended across much of the southern plateau and the western coast, with a standing army, an effective revenue administration, and an established position in the diplomacy of the region. The next division examines how Tipu developed this inheritance.

Tipu Sultan, State Reform, and Political Legitimacy

The chapter's fullest analysis of institutional state-building

Section 59.9

Tipu Sultan's Accession and the Consolidation of Sovereignty, 1782–1784

Tipu Sultan was born in November 1750 and had received a careful education and extensive military training under his father. He had commanded forces in the Maratha and Company wars, and at the time of Haidar's death he was operating on the Malabar coast. The first challenge was a practical one: securing the army and the treasury without a succession crisis. Haidar's ministers, the commanders, and the troops accepted Tipu, and within weeks he had returned to the main theater of the war. Contemporary accounts emphasize both that the transition was smoother than might have been feared and that Tipu had to assert his authority over officers who had served his father.

The immediate military problem was the continuing Second Anglo-Mysore War. With the Company's attention divided and its resources strained, and with the arrival of French support limited, both sides were ready for a settlement. The Treaty of Mangalore, concluded in March 1784, restored conquests and provided for the exchange of prisoners. The terms were, in general, a return to the pre-war position, and the significance of the treaty lay in its recognition of Tipu as an independent ruler negotiating on equal terms with the Company. Chapter 64 discusses the war and its diplomacy in detail. The point to underline here is that the treaty confirmed, to Tipu's advantage, the sovereign status of the state.

Having secured his position, Tipu moved to articulate a more explicit form of royal authority than his father had used. Haidar Ali had ruled as Nawab, formally under the Wodeyar raja. Tipu retained the Wodeyar house in a subordinate and ceremonial position, while the mechanisms of government and the language of rule became increasingly his own. In the mid-1780s—variously dated within about 1786–1787 in modern studies—he advanced the title Padshah, the Persian term for emperor, and experimented with coin legends and the Friday sermon (khutba) as signs of independent sovereignty. This was not an uninterrupted or universally acknowledged severance from Mughal suzerainty: Tipu had accepted a Mughal title in 1784, and at times continued to accommodate imperial forms. The chronology of his titulature and coinage must be reconstructed from dated documents and surviving specimens rather than assigned to one unambiguous declaration. The meaning of this step is discussed by historians such as Kate Brittlebank as part of a deliberate search for legitimacy, in which Tipu drew on Islamic conceptions of kingship, on the prestige of the Prophet's family and the Sufi tradition, and on his own claim to sovereignty rather than on delegation from Delhi.

Other features of his rule, discussed further below, support this interpretation. He introduced a new calendar, called the Mauludi era, beginning from the birth of the Prophet and using new month names, and revised the system of weights, measures, and coinage, giving them new names in Persian and Arabic. He reorganized the court and the ministries, appointing officers to supervise departments. He also extended the use of Persian as the language of administration and renamed several towns, giving, for example, new names to Calicut and Mysore. These were not merely cosmetic changes; they were intended to mark a new political order and to centralize the symbols and practices of authority.

A revealing contrast is the use of imperial correspondence and court documents as evidence. The title awarded in Shah Alam II's name in 1784 treated Tipu as an eminent but formally subordinate servant of the imperial system. Later assertions of autonomous dignity were therefore political choices made within a competitive repertoire of titles, not proof that Mughal forms had lost all meaning in southern India. Coins, seals, oaths, and ceremonial language could send different signals to imperial officials, regional commanders, and overseas interlocutors. Historians should ask which audience a particular claim addressed and whether it corresponded to a change in military or fiscal control.

The transition from Haidar's government to Tipu's can therefore be described as a shift from a powerful military commandership that operated under a nominal king, to a more explicitly articulated royal system. The underlying continuity was strong. Tipu relied on the same army, the same revenue base, and much of the same administrative personnel. What changed was the degree of formal organization and the ideological emphasis, and it is this combination of continuity and change that makes his reign useful for the comparative argument of the chapter.

Section 59.10

Administrative Centralization, Land Revenue, and Economic Policy under Tipu Sultan

Tipu's government sought to bring the apparatus of the state under closer control. The sources for his administration include the regulations attributed to him, the Persian chronicles of the period, and the letters collected and translated by William Kirkpatrick, whose Select Letters of Tippoo Sultan appeared in 1811. These sources are valuable but must be used with care, because they present an ideal of administration, the regulations as the ruler wished them to operate, while the actual functioning of the system depended on officials in the districts.

The central administration was divided into a number of boards or departments, each headed by a named officer, covering functions such as revenue, the army, the treasury, commerce, and, from the 1790s, a navy. The organization of such boards reflected a desire to systematize and supervise government business. In the provinces and districts, the state relied on officials appointed by the ruler and answerable to the center, and it sought to supplement local intermediaries with salaried officers. The extent to which this system functioned as the regulations envisage is hard to determine, and a recurring theme in the scholarship is the gap between the regulatory ideal and the practical capacity of officials to implement it.

Land revenue was the base of the system. Tipu's government attempted to assess the land more systematically, to supervise the collection of revenue, and to reduce the share taken by intermediaries. A critical element was the continued pressure on the palegars and other hereditary chiefs whose independence Haidar had begun to reduce. The policy was one of centralization: the more revenue that flowed directly to the treasury, the more the state could maintain its salaried army. Tipu's government also made loans to cultivators and encouraged the extension of cultivation and irrigation, and inscriptions and later accounts associate his reign with plans or works for a Kaveri irrigation embankment near Kannambadi. A Persian inscription associated with these works is dated to 1794 in some modern accounts, while an older epigraphic catalogue describes construction or raising an earlier dam around 1797. These records should not be conflated with the much later completed Krishna Raja Sagara dam; whether the eighteenth-century undertaking was a new foundation, improvement, or plan requires caution. Alongside these efforts, he pursued measures to introduce or expand products such as sericulture, which developed in parts of the kingdom.

The documentary record makes clear why administrative centralization must be treated as a program rather than a completed fact. An order to a district officer is evidence that the court sought information or compliance; a subsequent complaint or repeated order may reveal that implementation was difficult. Kirkpatrick's published translations preserve specific requests for expenditure returns and responses to shortages of military pay, while his notes often interpret such acts through a hostile Company perspective. A historian must therefore distinguish the ruler's effort at supervision, reports by officials, and the results in particular districts. The distinction also matters when evaluating state monopolies and the degree of direct land assessment.

The limits of these reforms were substantial. The state depended on local agricultural production, which was vulnerable to drought and to the damage caused by war. The revenue demands were heavy, and the sources record complaints and resistance. The officials charged with assessment and collection were often drawn from local networks with interests of their own, and some of them resisted or evaded the regulations. The army's pay depended on the regularity of revenue, so shortfalls created political risks. Centralization also reduced the autonomy of local chiefs, some of whom looked for opportunities to resist, an issue that the Company would later exploit.

Scholars have used the concept of military fiscalism, associated particularly with the historian Burton Stein, to interpret this system, arguing that the Mysorean state organized its fiscal institutions around the needs of the army, building on practices that long preceded Haidar and Tipu. This approach has the advantage of reading Tipu's measures as institutional developments rather than as expressions of a personal ambition, and it connects them to the Nayaka-period practices of the south. The following vocabulary box explains the term, and Historical Debate 2 below considers how far the Mysorean state was innovative.

Historical Vocabulary
Military Fiscalism

Military fiscalism is a term used by historians for a form of state in which the organization of revenue, taxation, and administration is shaped above all by the need to raise, equip, and pay armies. Burton Stein applied the idea to southern Indian states from the Vijayanagara period onward, including Mysore under Haidar Ali and Tipu Sultan.

The term does not mean that these states were merely war machines. It directs attention to how rulers extracted resources from agriculture and commerce, how they paid soldiers, and how fiscal institutions evolved under military pressure. It also avoids the misleading assumption that these states were attempting something like a modern program of national development. Students should note that historians differ over how far the concept explains specific Mysorean practices.

Section 59.11

Mysore's Commercial Ambitions, Diplomacy, and International Connections

Tipu's government took a more active role in commerce than most contemporary South Asian states. Mysore controlled important coastal districts and ports, including Mangalore, and had access to valuable commodities such as pepper, cardamom, sandalwood, and, within the plateau, the products of agriculture and sericulture. The scholarship describes a policy by which the state established its own trading establishments, sometimes with monopoly rights over particular goods, and sought to channel commerce through them. A board of trade was created, state commercial houses were set up in the kingdom's ports, and Tipu's government also established agencies overseas, with the Persian Gulf and the Red Sea as targets, in places including Muscat, with the aim of reaching Arabian and other markets.

These initiatives need to be understood in their own context. The logic was fiscal and strategic, not developmental in a modern sense. Revenue from state trading supplemented land revenue, and control over coastal trade provided access to supplies such as military stores, along with foreign exchange to buy them, and also the opportunity to deprive rivals, including the Company and the Malabar chiefs, of income. A navy planned in the 1790s was meant to protect coastal commerce and project power, though its practical impact appears to have been limited. It would be anachronistic to describe Tipu's program as industrialization or national economic planning, since no such categories existed, and the historian's task is to evaluate it within the military-fiscal and commercial conditions of the eighteenth century.

His diplomacy was extensive and sought allies against the Company, the Marathas, and the Nizam. Missions were dispatched to Constantinople in the mid-1780s to seek the support of the Ottoman Sultan, to Iran, to Afghanistan, and, notably, to France. The embassy to the court of Louis XVI in 1788 was a high point, and its reception at Versailles aroused considerable public interest in France, but the French monarchy, soon engulfed in revolution, was unable to offer military assistance. In the late 1790s, the revolutionary context gave rise to contacts between Tipu's government and French officers at Srirangapatna, including the formation of a short-lived club of French sympathizers, and an exchange of letters connected to the French expedition to Egypt, which the Company's leadership used to justify its final war on Mysore. How much help Tipu could actually have obtained from France remains questionable.

The distinction between intention and implementation is especially important in the commercial field. A directive to establish a factory or regulate a commodity demonstrates the state's ambition and organizational language; it does not show that every agency became profitable, that all private merchants were excluded, or that distant officers complied consistently. Merchants already possessed regional credit, shipping, and information networks that the government needed but could not simply replace. A sustainable account therefore asks which goods were effectively controlled, which agents advanced the ruler's interests, and where trade continued by negotiation or evasion. The port and overland markets were political resources as well as economic ones, and disruptions from war could defeat commercial plans even when the orders were carefully drafted.

These diplomatic and commercial efforts show a ruler who understood Mysore as a participant in a wider world of Indian Ocean commerce and Islamic and European politics. Tipu sought legitimacy and recognition from Muslim rulers, including the Ottomans and the Mughal court's neighbors, and military assistance from the French. The relationship between these aims and his policies at home forms the subject of the next section.

Section 59.12

Religious Patronage, Political Authority, and the Contested Legacy of Tipu Sultan

No part of this chapter requires more care than this one. Tipu Sultan's relationship with Hindu and Christian communities and with his Muslim subjects has been the subject of heated debate since his own lifetime, and both British colonial writers and modern political commentators have used him to make arguments that have little to do with eighteenth-century evidence. The responsible approach is to separate the types of evidence and the distinct categories of action: religious ideology, royal ceremony, patronage, punishment, military strategy, forced displacement, and the politics of conquest.

As a Muslim ruler, Tipu employed the language and ceremony of Islamic kingship, as §59.9 described. He used the title Padshah, adopted the Mauludi calendar, and sought recognition from other Muslim powers. Brittlebank's study argues that these steps were part of a wider search for legitimacy that drew on varied sources, including Sufi and devotional traditions as well as claims about the Prophet and his lineage. At the same time, Tipu's administration relied extensively on Hindu officials, including Purnaiya and Krishna Rao, and the majority of his subjects were Hindu. The state continued to patronize Hindu temples and religious institutions, among them the Ranganatha temple at Srirangapatna, adjacent to his palace, and others in the kingdom, with grants documented in the records.

The correspondence with the Sringeri monastery is among the most important evidence of this patronage. In 1791, Maratha troops raided Sringeri, an important center of the Advaita Vedanta tradition, and damaged the temple and displaced the image of the goddess Sharada. The head of the monastery wrote to Tipu, and letters preserved at Sringeri and published in the early twentieth century record Tipu's expressions of concern and his provision of funds and support for the restoration of the images and the monastery. The letters are valuable evidence of the relationship between a Muslim ruler and a prominent Hindu religious institution and of the political interest Tipu had in cultivating that relationship, particularly against the Marathas. They should be read as evidence of patronage and political calculation, and not as proof that Tipu held any particular attitude toward Hinduism in general.

The record of coercion is also substantial and cannot be omitted. Sources, including Tipu's own correspondence, Persian chronicles, and the accounts of British officials and local traditions, describe episodes of forcible conversion, deportation, and punishment, particularly during his campaigns in Coorg (Kodagu) in the mid-1780s and in Malabar from 1788 onward. The conquest of Coorg is reported to have led to the removal of large numbers of Kodavas to captivity at Srirangapatna, though estimates of the numbers vary widely. In Malabar, the Nair chiefs and landed Brahmans, who resisted Mysorean rule, suffered confiscations, flight, and in some cases forced conversion, and many fled to Travancore. Accounts from Mangalore describe the removal of large numbers of Christians of the coast to captivity at Srirangapatna in 1784, a controversy over numbers that still divides writers. A frequently cited later account relates the killing of Mandayam Iyengar Brahmans at Melkote, though its dating and details are contested.

Population figures repeated in accounts of the campaigns require particular scrutiny. The frequently reported tens of thousands transported from Coorg and Canara are not counts from a neutral contemporary census, and some totals are inconsistent with what historians can establish about local populations. That problem does not erase the documented removals or the suffering they caused; it requires a distinction between the occurrence of coercion, its geographic scope, the number affected, and the reliability of a particular estimate. The same method applies to the later Melkote narrative, which should not be used to date or quantify an episode without corroborating contemporary evidence.

Historians draw different conclusions from this evidence. Some, such as Mohibbul Hasan in his 1951 biography, tended to downplay or explain such episodes as military measures against rebellion. Others, including Brittlebank and scholars writing in the volume edited by Irfan Habib, have examined both the patronage and the coercion within the context of Tipu's pursuit of authority, the rebellions he faced, and the politics of conquest. In the Malabar case, Tipu's measures should be read against the long-standing local struggle between the Mappila Muslim traders and the Nair and Brahman landholding elite, and against Mysorean attempts to establish direct revenue administration. Not all of the reported actions can be reduced to a single religious motive, and the sources for many of them were produced by interested parties, whether British officials who wanted to portray Tipu as a tyrant or local communities with grievances against Mysore. The fair summary is that Tipu engaged in documented patronage of Hindu institutions and also imposed severe coercive measures, including captivity, population transfers, and reported forced conversions, on specific populations; the scale and motives of individual episodes must be evaluated separately. Political and military explanations may clarify the context of coercion, but they do not negate the experiences of those subjected to it. The evidence does not justify either a blanket acquittal or the reduction of all his policies to a single religious objective.

Historical Debate 2

Was Mysore under Haidar Ali and Tipu Sultan an Innovative Centralized State or an Intensification of Earlier Military-Fiscal Institutions?

The question: Did Haidar Ali and Tipu Sultan create a new kind of state in southern India, or did they intensify practices that already existed?

Interpretation A — Innovation and centralization: Mohibbul Hasan's detailed study of Tipu, and many later writers, emphasize the range of his initiatives in administration, commerce, the navy, calendar and coinage reform, and military organization, and portray him as an exceptionally energetic reformer. On this view the Mysorean state was distinctive, with a more systematic central government than most of its contemporaries.
Interpretation B — Intensification of earlier institutions: Burton Stein and others stress that Mysore's institutions drew on Wodeyar and Vijayanagara-Nayaka practices of military fiscalism, and that the notable features of the Haidar-Tipu period, including heavy revenue demands, the reduction of palegar autonomy, and a standing army, were an intensification of long-standing trends rather than a sharp break. Kate Brittlebank's work on legitimacy adds that Tipu's innovations in kingship were drawn from existing Islamic and regional repertoires.

Evidence: The debate rests on the interpretation of regulations, which show intent, and of revenue and military records, which are less abundant. The gap between regulation and practice limits claims for either interpretation. Evidence of coercion, particularly in recently conquered regions, is relevant to assessing how far the center truly controlled the localities.

Why it matters: The issue affects how Mysore is placed in the wider history of the century. If it was uniquely innovative, it may be viewed as a precocious state cut short by British conquest. If it was an intensification of earlier forms, it becomes one example, among others, of regional military-fiscal states. The two positions are not mutually exclusive. Political judgments about Tipu, whether admiring or hostile, are not substitutes for institutional analysis of how the state worked.

Section 59.13

The Formation of Travancore under Marthanda Varma

The kingdom of Travancore took shape in the southern part of what is now Kerala, in a region where political authority had been fragmented among many small chieftaincies. The coastal strip between the Western Ghats and the Arabian Sea was divided among royal houses such as Venad, Attingal, Quilon (Desinganad), Kayamkulam, Purakkad, and Cochin to the north, together with the dominion of the Zamorin of Calicut farther up the coast. These rulers were linked to temples, to hereditary noble houses, and to the matrilineal Nair warrior-landholding communities that supplied soldiers and local officials. The region's wealth rested on rice cultivation in the lowlands, coconut and other garden crops, and, above all, on the export of pepper and spices to the Indian Ocean and European markets, in which the Dutch East India Company was a major buyer (Chapters 49 and 61).

Marthanda Varma, who succeeded to the throne of Venad in 1729 and ruled until 1758, transformed this small southern principality into a larger and more centralized kingdom. His accession was disputed, and he faced opposition from the powerful Nair noble houses, known collectively as the Ettuveettil Pillamar (the "lords of the eight houses"), and from a council of local magnates and temple authorities, the Ettara Yogam, which had long limited the authority of the ruler. In the early years of his reign he moved against these groups, breaking the power of the noble houses, in some cases violently, and reducing the autonomy of the council. This effort to subordinate the intermediate nobility is comparable, in logic if not in form, to Haidar Ali's reduction of the palegars, although its context was a matrilineal Nair society rather than the Nayaka tradition of the Mysore plateau.

Having secured his base, Marthanda Varma expanded northward. Over the following decades he absorbed or subordinated neighboring principalities, including Desinganad, Attingal, and, after a series of campaigns that culminated in the 1740s, Kayamkulam. The conquest of Purakkad and other territories on the northern frontier extended the kingdom to the vicinity of Cochin, whose ruler remained a rival. The new kingdom therefore stretched along much of the southern coast, and its geographic compactness, with the sea to the west and mountains to the east, made it easier to defend than the more open territories of its northern neighbors.

Administrative consolidation accompanied expansion. The ruler relied heavily on a trusted minister, Ramayyan Dalawa, who served as chief minister (dalawa) and helped reorganize the revenue system, strengthen record-keeping, and tighten control over the districts. The state asserted a growing monopoly over valuable commodities, most notably pepper, whose trade had previously been shared among local intermediaries, and it extended direct royal control over land, including by increasing the extent of lands under the ruler's own administration. These measures raised revenue that financed the army and the building of fortifications.

The sources for this period include local chronicles and family histories, the records of temples and royal households, and, importantly, the documentation of the Dutch East India Company, which had pepper contracts in the region and recorded political developments closely. They show a kingdom in which royal power grew at the expense of older noble and corporate bodies. The next section examines the military and ideological dimensions of this transformation.

Section 59.14

Travancore's Military Transformation, the Dutch, and Temple-Centered Kingship

The military test that has come to symbolize Marthanda Varma's reign was the confrontation with the Dutch East India Company in 1741. The Dutch, whose pepper trade in the region was threatened by the expansion of Travancore, supported rival principalities, notably Kayamkulam, and a Dutch force sent from Ceylon landed on the coast of southern Travancore. The Battle of Colachel, fought at the port of that name, resulted in the defeat of the Dutch. The event is traditionally commemorated on 10 August 1741. Archival reconstructions differ over how to date the capitulation and the capture: a contemporary account by the weaver Rengas, identified in Dutch Company records, describes the capture on 16 August, while another reconstruction uses 7 August for the surrender. These dates may refer to different stages or reflect disagreements in the surviving sources. The confrontation unfolded over an extended siege, and the precise day should not be presented as unanimously established.

The victory is significant, but its importance should be assessed carefully. It was among the earliest cases in which an Asian ruler defeated a European trading company's forces in a major engagement. It did not by itself eliminate the Dutch presence on the coast, which continued for some years until the Treaty of Mavelikkara in 1753 settled relations between the company and Travancore and constrained the company's interference in local politics. What made the victory consequential was its effect on Travancore's own development. Among the Dutch captured was a naval officer of Flemish origin, Eustachius De Lannoy, who, after being taken prisoner, entered Marthanda Varma's service. De Lannoy, known locally as Valiya Kappithan ("Great Captain"), helped to reorganize the Travancore army, introducing European drill, the use of firearms, and the manufacture of cannon and ammunition, and supervised the construction of fortifications, including the northern defensive works that became known as the Travancore Lines.

The Dutch encounter was a component of the wider state-building process and not its sole cause. The military reorganization rested on the revenue reforms described in §59.13, which provided the funds, and on the political victories over the local nobility, which removed rivals who could have blocked the creation of a standing force. It also drew on the Nair warrior tradition, with Nair soldiers forming a substantial part of the army, now organized under the central government rather than under noble houses. Coastal defense and fortifications reflected an understanding of the threats to the kingdom from the sea and from the north.

The ideological counterpart to this military and administrative transformation was an extraordinary act of political theology. In January 1750, Marthanda Varma dedicated his kingdom to the deity Sri Padmanabha, the form of Vishnu worshipped at the temple in Thiruvananthapuram, in a ceremony known as the Trippadidanam. He declared himself and his successors to be servants of the deity (Padmanabha Dasa), ruling in the god's name. The practice of ruling as a representative of a deity has parallels elsewhere in South Asia, including the Maratha rulers' relation to the Chhatrapati and Mysore's retention of the Wodeyar kingship, but the Travancore arrangement was distinctive in the explicit transfer of sovereignty to the temple deity.

The dedication served several political purposes. It gave the new and heavily centralized monarchy a form of legitimacy rooted in religious tradition and in the allegiance of the temple's networks and devotees, which was especially valuable after a reign marked by conflict with the older nobility. It also tied the ruler to the temple's considerable landed resources and to the Brahman communities associated with it, and it provided a framework in which succession disputes could be subordinated to a higher authority. The enduring importance of the Padmanabha temple to the kingdom's identity is evident in the later history of the state.

Travancore's strength was tested later in the century. After Marthanda Varma's death in 1758, his successor Karthika Thirunal Rama Varma, known as Dharma Raja, ruled until 1798 and maintained the kingdom through the period in which Mysore dominated Malabar to the north. Tipu Sultan's attack on the Travancore Lines in 1789–1790 became one of the triggers of the Third Anglo-Mysore War, treated in Chapter 64, and Travancore's alliance with the Company in the 1790s reflected the changed balance of power. For this chapter the key point is that Travancore, a relatively small kingdom, had by mid-century built an administratively and militarily effective state on the basis of royal centralization and temple-centered legitimacy.

Section 59.15

The Jats and the Consolidation of Bharatpur

The Jats of the Braj region, around Mathura and Agra, were a community of cultivators and local landholders whose political importance grew out of the agrarian strength of the lands between the Yamuna and the Chambal. The Mughal state had long relied on revenue from this fertile and densely settled region, and had also repeatedly contended with its rural leaders. Jat uprisings against Mughal authority had occurred in the late seventeenth century, including a revolt around Mathura in 1669 and further risings under Raja Ram and others, and these set the stage for the emergence of durable Jat chieftaincies in the following century.

The first of the founders of Jat state power was Churaman, a landholder of the Sinsini village group who rose to prominence in the early eighteenth century. He built fortified positions, gathered an armed following, and exploited the instability that followed Aurangzeb's death, at times raiding traffic on the roads near Agra and at times negotiating with Mughal officials, who sought to contain him. His strategy combined military force with political bargaining, and, although he did not create a lasting state, he established the basis on which his successors built.

His nephew and successor Badan Singh (who led the community from about 1722 until his death, variously dated 1755 or 1756) was a decisive figure. He formed an alliance with Sawai Jai Singh II of Jaipur, the powerful Kachhwaha Rajput ruler, who conferred on him a title (Brajraj) and supported his claims, and he gained recognition from the Mughal government. Badan Singh developed Deeg as a fortified and palatial center and extended Jat control over the surrounding region. The alliance with Jaipur illustrates how Jat state-building depended on relationships with other regional powers, and that a cultivating community could act as a partner of a Rajput kingdom rather than simply as an adversary.

The state reached its height under Suraj Mal, who ruled from 1756 until his death in 1763. He constructed or expanded the fortress of Bharatpur, which, with its massive mud-and-earth walls and surrounding moats, proved very resistant to artillery, and he greatly extended the kingdom's holdings. His forces captured Agra in 1761, after the Third Battle of Panipat (Chapter 58), when the collapse of Maratha power in the north created a vacuum. He also showed political skill in the period around Panipat: he had been allied with the Marathas but disagreed with their strategy, and after their defeat he was able to offer shelter to survivors, a gesture that contemporary sources and later memory treated as notable. His revenues were reported by observers to be very large for a state of its size, a testimony to the productivity of the region he controlled and to the effectiveness of its collection. In December 1763 he was killed in a skirmish near Delhi in a conflict with the forces of Najib ud-Daula, the Rohilla commander discussed in §59.16, an episode that connects the two states.

It would be a mistake to reduce Jat political history to Suraj Mal's biography or to see the Jats as a merely rebellious rural population. The state rested on social and economic foundations: village-based lineages and landholding groups that supplied soldiers and local leadership, the agricultural wealth of the Braj plains, and fortified mud-walled villages and strongholds that were hard to reduce. The rulers established revenue systems, built forts and palaces, and employed diverse soldiers, including infantry and, in later decades, units trained by European and European-trained officers. After Suraj Mal's death, his successors, beginning with his son Jawahar Singh, continued to expand and then to struggle with internal rivalries and external pressures, but Bharatpur remained a significant regional power in the late eighteenth century and later.

The Jat experience adds a distinctive pattern to the comparison. Like the Sikh formations, the Jat state emerged from agrarian communities of cultivators, and, like Travancore, it relied on fortification and dynastic continuity. But it did not develop the collective religious institutions of the Sikh Panth, nor the temple-based sovereignty of Travancore, and its legitimacy rested on military success, alliances, and recognition by larger powers. Its history should be read as one of state-building from the village up.

Section 59.16

The Rohillas: Afghan Military Networks and State Formation in Rohilkhand

The Rohillas were Afghan soldiers and settlers, drawn largely from the Pashtun tribal areas of the northwest, who established control over the region east of Delhi that came to be known as Rohilkhand. The term "Rohilla" derives from "Roh," a name for the Afghan highlands, and it was applied in India to Afghans who migrated into the plains seeking employment as soldiers. The Mughal state and its provincial governors had long recruited Afghan cavalry, and in the early eighteenth century the weakening of central authority gave these military networks an opportunity to turn service into territorial power.

The founder of Rohilla authority in the region was Ali Muhammad Khan, who rose from the leadership of a band of Afghan soldiers in the 1720s and 1730s to become the dominant figure in the territory between the Ganga and the Himalayan foothills, an area subsequently called Rohilkhand. He accumulated lands through military service, through grants, and through the absorption of the holdings of local landholders. His growing power troubled the Mughal government, which in 1745 sent an army against him; he surrendered and was held for a time at Sirhind. During the disturbances of the late 1740s, and with the Afghan invasion of Ahmad Shah, he was able to return and re-establish himself, and he died in 1748. His young sons were placed under the guardianship of a regent, Hafiz Rahmat Khan, who became the leading figure in Rohilla politics for the next quarter century.

Hafiz Rahmat Khan governed, in effect, a confederation of Rohilla chiefs, among them Dundi Khan and others, who held districts within Rohilkhand, with Bareilly, Moradabad, and Pilibhit as important centers. His authority was one of leadership among peers rather than absolute rule, a structure that made the Rohilla polity comparable, in a limited way, to the Sikh misl system. The Rohillas' relations with neighbors were complex. They faced pressure from the Nawabs of Awadh, whose territory lay to the east, and in the early 1750s Awadh forces together with Maratha allies fought against Afghan chiefs in the region. They were also exposed to the Marathas, who demanded tribute, and to the Jats of Bharatpur, with whom their interests sometimes collided. After the Third Battle of Panipat in 1761, in which Rohilla leaders were allied with Ahmad Shah Durrani against the Marathas, the Rohillas' position strengthened.

A second figure of great importance was Najib ud-Daula, an Afghan commander of Yusufzai origin who had begun in Rohilla service and rose to dominate Delhi in the decades after Nadir Shah's invasion. As the Mughal emperor's chief military officer after 1761, Najib effectively controlled the capital's affairs until his death in 1770, and his estates around Najibabad linked him to Rohilkhand. His career shows that Afghan military networks operated at several levels, from local chieftaincies to the imperial court. After his death his son Zabita Khan inherited a weaker position.

It is important to distinguish Rohilkhand from the other Afghan-established center to its south, Farrukhabad, which was founded by Muhammad Khan Bangash in 1714. The Bangash Afghans were a separate community with their own leadership and history, though they, too, were part of the northern Afghan military world. Treating all Afghans of the Gangetic plain as a single "Rohilla" group would obscure these distinctions.

In 1772, Hafiz Rahmat Khan and the Nawab of Awadh, Shuja ud-Daula, entered an agreement under which Awadh was to protect the Rohillas against Maratha attack in exchange for a large payment. When the payment was not made, Awadh, with the support of a British East India Company force, invaded Rohilkhand in 1774. Hafiz Rahmat Khan was killed in battle in April of that year, and the Rohilla confederation was broken up, though a remnant principality survived at Rampur under Faizullah Khan. The episode is significant for the history of the Company's relationship with Awadh and became a subject of controversy in Britain (Chapter 63). It also marks the end of independent Rohilla power in Rohilkhand.

Recent scholarship has drawn attention to the Rohillas as active participants in regional state-building and not merely as the military opponents of their neighbors. Their authority depended on military recruitment among Afghan kin and clients, on the acquisition of land and revenue rights, on alliances and rivalries with Awadh, the Marathas, and the Jats, and on the construction of a claim to legitimacy through Afghan lineage and prestige. Hafiz Rahmat Khan himself, an educated man with an interest in Persian letters, took part in this construction of legitimacy through his writing, as the source window below shows.

Military Innovation, Regional State-Building, and Historical Synthesis

Comparing five pathways of regional power

Section 59.17

Regional Military Innovation: Cavalry, Infantry, Artillery, Fortifications, and Rockets

The regional states of the eighteenth century developed distinct military systems adapted to their terrain, resources, and political organization. A comparison shows that innovation took the form of adaptation, institutional coordination, and the mobilization of resources, and it was not simply a matter of acquiring European weapons.

The Sikh formations relied principally on mounted fighters, whose mobility in the plains and in the semi-arid tracts of Punjab suited the strategy of raids, ambushes, and rapid withdrawal developed after 1716. Their cavalry was organized in jathas and later in misls, with each rider commonly owning his horse and equipment and following a leader of his choice. The Sikh polity was slower to build large infantry or artillery formations, and its forts were mainly local strongholds. The Sikh advantage lay in the combination of mobility, local knowledge, and the cohesion that a shared religious identity provided, which allowed dispersed forces to assemble quickly. Purnima Dhavan's work on the period emphasizes the development of a Sikh military culture in the decades after 1699, and it is a useful corrective to the idea that these formations were simply bands of raiders.

Mysore under Haidar Ali and Tipu Sultan built a more balanced standing army, with cavalry, trained infantry armed with muskets, and a substantial artillery. Haidar drew on French officers and gunners, and he and Tipu developed arsenals and foundries that produced cannon and small arms within the kingdom. They also established a system of paying and supplying troops through the central treasury as far as possible. A manual of military regulations associated with Tipu's reign attests to the aim of systematic discipline. The state's capacity to field such an army depended on the revenue arrangements discussed in §§59.7 and 59.10, and on the control of fortified positions, of which Srirangapatna was the greatest.

The most distinctive Mysorean innovation was the organized use of iron-cased rockets. Rockets were not new to South Asia or to the world, and earlier uses are recorded in China, the Islamic world, and the subcontinent, including under the Mughals, so Mysore did not invent rocketry. What distinguished the Mysorean rockets was the use of iron tubes, which could hold higher pressures than the cardboard or bamboo casings used elsewhere and gave the weapon greater range and destructive force, and the organization of dedicated rocket units, with their own officers and supply, within the army. Observers recorded the effect of rocket fire on British forces during the wars, including the explosion of ammunition wagons at Pollilur in 1780. After the fall of Srirangapatna in 1799, captured rockets were sent to Britain, where William Congreve's work in the following years developed a British rocket. The Mysorean case is thus significant as an example of the organized military application of an existing technology, and not as a unique invention.

Travancore's army was built on the Nair warrior tradition but reorganized under Marthanda Varma with the help of De Lannoy, who introduced European drill, firearms, and the manufacture of cannon, and the kingdom invested in fortifications such as the northern lines. Here the adaptation was explicit: a local military tradition combined with imported specialist knowledge, funded by the royal revenue system. Jat power at Bharatpur rested on fortification above all, with mud-walled strongholds that absorbed artillery fire, supplemented by cavalry and infantry drawn from the community, and in later decades by units trained by European and European-trained officers. The Rohillas, drawing on the Afghan military labor market, fielded notable cavalry and, as their power grew, infantry and artillery in a pattern similar to that of the Mughal successor states, but their strength depended on the cohesion of their chiefs and on their ability to pay soldiers.

A common lesson emerges. In every case, military capacity depended on money, supply, and political backing. Cavalry required horses and fodder; artillery required foundries, powder, and trained gunners; fortification required labor and local cooperation; and all required soldiers whose loyalty could be sustained. The states that built the most effective armies were those that could combine revenue, political coalition, and organizational discipline. Weapons mattered, but institutions determined how effectively they could be used.

Section 59.18

Revenue, Military Service, Local Society, and the Costs of State-Building

Each of the regional states faced the same fundamental task of securing money, recruits, food, animals, weapons, and local cooperation, and each solved it differently. The Sikh formations developed the rakhi system, which turned protection into a recurring payment, and, after the conquests of the 1760s, collected revenue from the districts they held, often by dividing villages among the leaders and their followers. Haidar Ali and Tipu Sultan relied on a centralized revenue administration headed by salaried officials and aimed to reduce the share taken by intermediaries, with additional income from state trade. Travancore drew on a reorganized land revenue system and on its monopoly over pepper and other commodities. The Jats depended on the productivity of the Braj plains and on efficient collection by officials of the ruling house. The Rohillas relied on revenue from the lands they had acquired and on military service that could be rewarded with grants.

These arrangements affected different groups in different ways. Cultivators bore the heaviest burden, whether as payers of land revenue, rakhi, or special levies, and as providers of labor and transport during campaigns. Their experience varied: a village that paid a predictable protection fee in Punjab might consider itself better off than one subject to repeated plunder, and a cultivator in Mysore or Travancore might benefit from the suppression of petty exactions by local chiefs while facing heavier central demands. Merchants and bankers, who provided credit and facilitated the movement of money, profited from state demand and were also liable to forced loans and contributions. Hereditary officers and local chiefs, whose position depended on negotiated relationships with the center, saw their autonomy diminished where states centralized and their influence preserved where authority was shared. Religious establishments, including temples, monasteries, and Sufi shrines, received grants and protection but also became pawns in political competition, as the cases of Sringeri and the Padmanabha temple show. Soldiers gained income and status, but their pay was often irregular, and soldiers' mutinies over arrears were a persistent risk.

The costs of state-building were considerable. War devastated agricultural districts, and the movement of armies stripped grain and fodder from the countryside. Coercive recruitment and the removal of populations, whether the transfer of subjects after conquest or the flight of those who resisted, disrupted communities. The case of the Carnatic in the early 1780s, where devastation by armies was followed by famine, shows the human consequences of military competition, and the Sikh community's losses in the Afghan invasions show the vulnerability of non-combatants. Whether these costs were balanced by gains in security, administration, or economic activity cannot be decided by general statement, and it differed by region and by social group. The responsible historical stance is to acknowledge both the achievements of these states and the suffering involved in building them.

Everyday Life and Social History

Villages, Armies, and Supply in the Eighteenth Century

Ordinary people experienced the politics of the century mainly through the seasonal rhythms of cultivation and the arrival of armies. A village in Punjab might pay rakhi to a Sikh band at harvest time, shelter Sikh riders in the sangat of a local gurdwara, and hide its grain when an Afghan force approached. In the Kaveri plain, village headmen and accountants prepared the assessment records on which Mysorean revenue officials depended, while cultivators were required to supply bullocks and carts for the movement of the army. Grain was carried over long distances by specialist transporters such as the Banjaras, whose caravans were essential to military logistics and whose cooperation armies could not command by force alone.

In Travancore and Malabar, the matrilineal households of the Nair community supplied soldiers and local officials, while the pepper trade tied cultivators of the hills to the merchants of the ports. In the Braj and Rohilkhand country, fortified villages provided refuge in time of war and recruits in time of peace. Women's experience is poorly documented, but sources hint at their roles in managing households and property during men's absences, in the loss and captivity that accompanied conquest, and, in some cases, as figures of political influence at courts. These everyday dimensions, though less visible in the chronicles, were the foundation on which the political and military history of the chapter rested.

Section 59.19

Competing Models of Sovereignty and the Historiography of Regional Power

Placed side by side, the five cases illustrate distinct models of sovereignty. The Sikh polity located legitimate authority in the collective Khalsa, expressed through the Sarbat Khalsa and the gurmata, even while individual sardars acted as territorial lords. Mysore centralized authority in a ruler, first as the effective master behind a nominal king, then as an openly sovereign Padshah, and built administrative and fiscal institutions to support the claim. Travancore vested sovereignty in a deity and governed through the king as the deity's servant, using religious legitimacy to anchor a centralized monarchy. The Jat state grounded authority in lineage leadership, fortification, and alliances with larger powers. The Rohillas combined chiefly leadership among Afghan peers with claims of Afghan descent and prestige.

These models also differed in the relationship between formal sovereignty and practical control. Tipu's regulations and titles asserted a very extensive authority, but the capacity of officials to enforce it was uneven. The Sikh misl leaders held no formal royal titles but controlled villages and revenue effectively. Travancore's ruler held the formal sovereignty of a king and, in practice, had the administrative apparatus to match, but depended on the legitimating role of the temple. Centralized institutions and negotiated local authority coexisted in all of these states, in different proportions.

The historiography of these states has been shaped by later concerns. Colonial writers, such as Kirkpatrick and Wilks, interpreted Mysore through the lens of its conflicts with the Company, and often portrayed Tipu as a fanatic or tyrant, while nationalist and later political writers have alternately celebrated him as an anticolonial hero or condemned him as an oppressor of Hindus. Both approaches treat the eighteenth century as a prelude to British rule and use the past for contemporary argument. Sikh historiography, likewise, has been shaped by colonial categories, by the Singh Sabha reform movement of the late nineteenth century, and by later political developments, which influenced how the misls and the period of persecution were remembered. For the Jats and Rohillas, colonial narratives often reduced them to rebels or marauders. Scholars such as Burton Stein, Kate Brittlebank, Irfan Habib, Purnima Dhavan, and others have sought to read these states on their own terms, attending to their institutions, their legitimating strategies, and their relationships with local societies.

A comparative approach corrects these tendencies by showing that no single outcome, and certainly not British conquest, was inevitable, and by focusing on what each state was trying to accomplish. It also reveals the variety within the category of "religious" legitimacy: Islam, Sikh collective identity, and devotion to Padmanabha were used for different political purposes, and each was mixed with practical considerations of revenue, alliances, and control of territory.

Section 59.20

Review and Historical Synthesis

This chapter has examined five groups of regional states and military formations that competed for power in eighteenth-century South Asia. The Sikh community moved from the suppression of Banda Singh Bahadur's movement to the collective seizure of territory, creating institutions, the jathas, the Dal Khalsa, the gurmata, and the misls, that suited a population under pressure and eventually able to claim land and revenue. Mysore, under Haidar Ali and Tipu Sultan, transformed an established Wodeyar kingdom into a centralized military-fiscal state, drawing on earlier institutions while intensifying the extraction of resources and, under Tipu, articulating a more explicit royal ideology. Travancore combined the subjugation of its nobility, military reorganization, and temple-centered legitimacy. The Jats built a state from fortified villages and the agrarian wealth of the Braj plains. The Rohillas turned Afghan military networks into territorial chieftaincies and constructed an identity that supported their authority.

Several common threads connect these histories. Military development required fiscal resources, and fiscal resources required political coalitions with local elites, merchants, and cultivators. The weakening of Mughal authority created opportunities but not a vacuum: Mughal titles, administrative vocabulary, and forms continued to shape politics, and regional states built their own institutions in the space the empire left. Legitimacy, whether religious, genealogical, or military, was essential to each state, and it was constructed in different ways. And in every case, the costs of state-building fell heavily on ordinary people, even as some benefited.

The chapter also underlines that these states were not interchangeable. Their different pathways reflect different geographies, social structures, and political circumstances. Chapter 58 showed how the Marathas built the largest of these networks; Chapter 59 shows how others built smaller but significant ones. Their later fates diverged. Mysore was defeated in the wars treated in Chapter 64, the Sikh formations were consolidated under Ranjit Singh and then absorbed into the Company's dominions (Chapter 65), and the Jat, Rohilla, and Travancore states entered subordinate relationships with the Company. These outcomes were, however, the result of specific military, fiscal, and diplomatic developments, and not the inevitable consequence of the eighteenth century's structure.

Chapter 60 turns to a different kind of transformation: the provincial regimes of Bengal, Awadh, and Hyderabad, which retained the forms and personnel of the Mughal system while becoming effectively autonomous. Together with the states studied here, they complete the picture of an eighteenth century in which South Asia was a field of competing, creative, and sometimes brutal experiments in sovereignty, and in which the European companies, examined in Chapter 61, became one participant among many before they became the dominant one.

Cause and Effect

The Emergence of Regional States in the Eighteenth Century

Long-term causes

The size and diversity of the Mughal empire, the strain of the Deccan wars, the growth of regional elites and of a mobile military labor market, and the commercial wealth of regions such as Punjab, the Braj plains, and the western coast.

Short-term causes

The succession crises after 1707, the invasions of Nadir Shah and Ahmad Shah Durrani, the weakening of provincial control, and local conflicts over revenue, land, and succession.

Event or transformation

The rise of Sikh misls, Mysore under Haidar Ali and Tipu Sultan, Travancore, Bharatpur, and the Rohilla chieftaincies as autonomous powers with distinct institutions.

Immediate effects

Competition among regional powers, new patterns of revenue extraction and military recruitment, the displacement of older intermediaries, and the suffering associated with war and conquest.

Long-term consequences

A political landscape of varied successor states, which the East India Company confronted in the following decades, and a set of historical memories that were later used in colonial and nationalist narratives.

Historical complexity

The pathways differed by region, and no single cause, whether Mughal decline, European intervention, or religious identity, explains them. Decline at the center did not mean institutional vacuum.
Continuity and Change Over Time

Sovereignty and Military Organization, 1708–1799

What Changed

  • Sikh leadership moved from the centralized command of Banda to dispersed bands, then to territorial misls.
  • Mysore passed from Wodeyar kingship with powerful ministers to Haidar Ali's commandership and then to Tipu Sultan's explicit sovereignty.
  • Travancore centralized power at the expense of the older nobility.

  • Dependence on agricultural revenue and on negotiation with local elites.

Who experienced change most directly: Hereditary chiefs and nobles displaced by centralization, and populations in conquered territories, experienced the sharpest change. Village communities in regions less directly affected by conquest experienced greater continuity in agricultural life. Why the pattern matters: It shows that eighteenth-century change was real but uneven, and varied by region and social position.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that the weakening of Mughal central authority in the eighteenth century produced not simple disorder but a variety of new forms of sovereignty, military organization, and state-building. It compared five groups of polities, each with its own institutions and sources of legitimacy, and showed that regional states were creative participants in a competitive political environment rather than diminished copies of the Mughal empire.

In Punjab, the death of Guru Gobind Singh in 1708 and the campaigns of Banda Singh Bahadur from 1709 to 1716 produced the first Sikh assertion of territorial and revenue authority, including the capture of Sirhind in 1710. Banda's defeat at Gurdas Nangal and his execution in 1716 ended that phase, but the experience taught the Sikh community the dangers of centralized leadership and sieges. Mughal court newsletters record the imperial response, though they cannot reveal the motives of Sikh participants.

After 1716, Sikh survival depended on mobile bands (jathas), gatherings at Amritsar, and a mixture of conflict and negotiation with Mughal governors, as the offer of a nawabship and jagir to Kapur Singh in 1733 shows. The collection of protection payments (rakhi) gave the bands a recurring income. In the late 1740s, the traditional reorganization of the Dal Khalsa, and the formation of the misls, created an institutional framework combining collective deliberation through the Sarbat Khalsa and gurmata with the leadership of individual sardars.

The Sikh formations faced the invasions of Ahmad Shah Durrani, including the devastating Vadda Ghallughara of 1762, yet recovered rapidly, took Sirhind in 1764 and Lahore in 1765, and came to control most of the Punjab plains. The character of this system, whether a confederacy, a set of territorial principalities, or an evolving network, remains debated, and the answer changed between the 1740s and the end of the century. Ranjit Singh's later kingdom, treated in Chapter 65, grew from this environment.

In the south, Mysore under the Wodeyars possessed institutions, resources, and military traditions before Haidar Ali. Haidar rose through the army, controlled revenue and troops at Dindigul, and took effective control of the kingdom around 1761 while retaining the Wodeyar raja as nominal sovereign. His government built a military-fiscal state that expanded into Bednur, Malabar, and Chitradurga, reduced the autonomy of the palegars, paid a standing army, and balanced diplomacy among the Marathas, the Nizam, the Carnatic, and the Company. The costs of his expansion fell heavily on conquered and devastated regions.

Tipu Sultan, who succeeded in December 1782, secured recognition of his sovereignty at the Treaty of Mangalore in 1784 and articulated a more explicit royal system, with the title Padshah, a new calendar, revised coinage, and a reorganized administration. His reforms in revenue, state commerce, and diplomacy, including embassies to France and the Ottoman world, are best understood as eighteenth-century military-fiscal policies and not as a modern development program. The limits of reform, in the gap between regulation and practice, are important.

Tipu's religious record is complex and contested. He used the language of Islamic kingship, relied on Hindu officials, and patronized temples, as the Sringeri correspondence shows. He also oversaw documented coercion, deportation, and forced conversion in Coorg, Malabar, and on the Canara coast. A fair analysis distinguishes ideology, patronage, punishment, strategy, and conquest, and treats the sources, produced by interested parties, with care. The debate between interpretations that stress innovation and those that stress intensification of earlier institutions remains open.

Travancore under Marthanda Varma (1729–1758) subdued the older nobility, expanded northward, reorganized its administration under Ramayyan Dalawa, and, after the defeat of the Dutch at Colachel in 1741, employed European specialists such as De Lannoy to modernize its army. The dedication of the kingdom to Sri Padmanabha in 1750 joined royal authority to temple-centered legitimacy. The Jats of Bharatpur, under Churaman, Badan Singh, and Suraj Mal, built a state from fortified villages and agrarian resources, and the Rohillas of Rohilkhand, under Ali Muhammad Khan, Hafiz Rahmat Khan, and, at Delhi, Najib ud-Daula, converted Afghan military networks into territorial power and constructed legitimacy through genealogy until the Rohilla War of 1774.

Comparing the states shows that military innovation, whether in Sikh cavalry, Mysorean artillery and rockets, Travancorean drill and fortification, or Jat strongholds, depended on adaptation, revenue, logistics, and political coalitions rather than on weapons alone. The Mysorean rocket corps was significant as an organized application of an existing technology, not as the invention of rocketry. The states differed in their models of sovereignty, from collective Khalsa authority to centralized kingship, deity-centered monarchy, lineage-based leadership, and chiefly confederation.

Finally, the chapter has emphasized that these states should not be read only through their later encounters with British power or through present-day political arguments. Their histories show that the eighteenth century contained many possible futures, and that the people who lived in these states, cultivators, merchants, soldiers, religious communities, and rulers, experienced them in unequal and varied ways. Chapter 60 turns to the provincial successor regimes of Bengal, Awadh, and Hyderabad.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it corrects a common picture of the eighteenth century as a time of decline and drift preceding colonial conquest. The people who built Sikh institutions, Mysorean administration, Travancore's temple monarchy, the Jat strongholds of Bharatpur, and Rohilla chieftaincies were not waiting for the British. They were responding to real problems of security, revenue, and legitimacy with real institutional inventions, some of them durable and some of them destructive.

The chapter also reveals processes that continued beyond its dates. The Sikh experience of collective deliberation and territorial lordship shaped the Punjab that Ranjit Singh would unify (Chapter 65). The Mysorean state's military-fiscal methods and its rivalry with the Company framed the wars of Chapter 64. The memory of these states, especially of Tipu Sultan, was reshaped by colonial writers and by later political debate, a reminder that historical interpretation is itself part of history.

Within the six-volume sequence, the chapter links the Maratha expansion of Chapter 58 with the provincial regimes of Chapter 60 and the European companies of Chapter 61, helping to show an eighteenth century of many centers rather than one. That multiplicity is the condition that makes the colonial conquests of Volume IV historically intelligible, and it is the reason why those conquests should not be told as the triumph of a single power over a stagnant land.
