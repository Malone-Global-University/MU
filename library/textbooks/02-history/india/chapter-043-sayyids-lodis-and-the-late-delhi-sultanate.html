Part VIII — Islamic Frontiers and the Delhi Sultanate

Sayyids, Lodis, and the Late Delhi Sultanate

Indian History: South Asia from the Indus Civilization to the Present

Chapter Opening

In May 1414 a governor from the Punjab borderlands entered Delhi and took control of a city that had been sacked by Timur sixteen years earlier, fought over by rival nobles, and left without an undisputed ruler. He did not call himself sultan. He took a more modest title, acknowledged a distant Timurid overlord, and began the work of governing a state whose effective reach extended little beyond the walls of the capital and a handful of surrounding districts. Just over a century later, in April 1526, the last ruler of the dynasty that eventually replaced his lay dead on a plain north of Delhi, and a new power from Kabul held the city. Between those two moments lies the story of the Sayyid and Lodi dynasties: the attempt to put the Delhi Sultanate back together, and the reasons that attempt ended as it did.

Chapter 42 traced the Tughlaq dynasty from the ambitious experiments of Muhammad bin Tughlaq to the invasion of Timur in 1398 and the collapse of central authority that followed. This chapter begins where that collapse leaves off. It treats the Sayyid dynasty (1414–1451) and the Lodi dynasty (1451–1526), together the final period of the Delhi Sultanate. The period is sometimes dismissed as an epilogue, a long decline between the achievements of the Khaljis and Tughlaqs and the glories of the Mughals. This chapter argues instead that it was a distinct and consequential phase in its own right, in which Delhi first shrank to a regional principality, then recovered a large part of the Ganges plain, and finally lost its ruling house.

The central question is how the Sayyid and Lodi rulers tried to reconstruct the Sultanate after its fragmentation, and why the Lodi recovery did not produce a stable political order capable of surviving the crisis of 1526. The chapter's principal interpretive thread is the changing relationship between royal authority and the military nobility. Khizr Khan governed as the nominal deputy of a Timurid overlord and depended on nobles whose loyalty he could not command. Bahlul Lodi, the first Lodi sultan, built a coalition of Afghan chiefs and ruled as something close to the leader of his own supporters. Sikandar Lodi worked to raise the sultan above that coalition without dismantling it. Ibrahim Lodi pressed the same project harder and met resistance that fragmented his support just as an external invader approached. The chapter keeps returning to the gap between what a sultan aspired to and what he could actually command: nobles, revenue, soldiers, and provinces.

The chapter also insists that the late Sultanate was not a story of Delhi alone. The Sharqi sultans of Jaunpur, the sultans of Malwa, Gujarat, and Bengal, the Rajput lords of Gwalior and Mewar, the Khokhar chiefs of the Punjab, and the Timurid rulers of Kabul and Central Asia were all independent actors whose decisions shaped what Delhi could and could not do. Their full histories belong to Chapters 45 to 47 and, for Babur, to Chapter 50 and the Mughal chapters that follow. They appear here wherever their relations with Delhi explain the course of events.

A word about the people who gave the Lodi dynasty its character is also needed. The Lodis were Afghans, and the Afghan military families who moved into northern India during the fifteenth century formed the political society on which Lodi rule rested. The label "Afghan," however, concealed tribal, kinship, regional, and factional differences, and it did not imply an unchanging tradition of equality among chiefs. Understanding how Afghan military networks actually worked, through service, patronage, marriage, and iqta assignments, is essential to understanding both the Lodi achievement and the Lodi failure.

The evidence for this period is uneven. For the Sayyids, historians rely on Yahya bin Ahmad Sirhindi's Tarikh-i Mubarak Shahi, a chronicle written during the period it describes. For the Lodis, the chronicles are mostly later: Afghan histories composed in the late sixteenth and early seventeenth centuries, when memories of the dynasty had been shaped by the Mughal and Sur eras that followed. Contemporary coins, inscriptions, and surviving tombs and mosques supplement the texts, and for the final campaign historians have the extraordinary advantage of Babur's own memoir, the Baburnama, which is a contemporary account but also a partisan one. Part of the work of this chapter is learning how to weigh these kinds of evidence against one another.

By the end of the chapter, students should be able to explain why Delhi recovered under the Lodis and why that recovery was fragile; to distinguish four phases of royal-noble relations across the period; to place Jaunpur, Agra, Gwalior, and Punjab on the political map of fifteenth- and early sixteenth-century northern India; and to explain the First Battle of Panipat as the outcome of political and military causes rather than of a single technological advantage.

Learning Objectives

  3. Analyze the formation and internal diversity of the Afghan military elite on which Lodi rule depended.

Historical Context

The Delhi Sultanate had reached its greatest territorial extent under Muhammad bin Tughlaq in the early fourteenth century, when Delhi claimed authority across much of the subcontinent from the Punjab to the Deccan (Chapters 41 and 42). That claim proved impossible to sustain. During the 1340s and 1350s the Deccan broke away to form the Bahmani Sultanate, and Vijayanagara emerged in the south (Chapters 45 and 46). Bengal became independent under its own sultans, as did Gujarat and Malwa in the years after Timur's invasion, and Jaunpur became an independent Sharqi kingdom in 1394 (Chapter 47). Timur's sack of Delhi in 1398 did not itself cause these separations, but it accelerated a process already well advanced and destroyed what remained of Delhi's prestige.

The Delhi that Khizr Khan entered in 1414 was therefore a much smaller state than the Delhi of earlier centuries. Its core lay in the Punjab, the Doab between the Ganges and Yamuna rivers, and neighboring districts such as Mewat, Katehar (in modern Rohilkhand), and Bayana. These were fertile or strategically important zones but also zones of local chiefs and zamindars who paid revenue only when pressed by force. Distances were short, but control was thin. A sultan who wanted revenue from a district often had to lead an army there in person, and a campaigning army could not be in two places at once.

The military and fiscal systems of the Sultanate also shaped what a ruler could do. Soldiers and commanders were typically supported through iqta assignments, rights to collect revenue from particular territories in return for maintaining troops and sending a portion of revenue to the center (Chapter 40). When the center was strong, it could rotate iqtadars, audit accounts, and punish defaulters. When it was weak, iqta holders became hereditary regional magnates. In the fifteenth century a large part of northern India's military resources therefore lay in the hands of nobles, many of them of Afghan, Turkic, and regional origin, whose willingness to obey was conditional.

Readers should keep in mind two cautions about evidence and framing. First, much of what is known about the Lodis comes from chronicles composed decades after the events, and these often reflect the concerns of the Mughal and Afghan worlds of their own time. Second, the term "decline" can obscure more than it clarifies. The late Sultanate saw genuine recovery under Bahlul and Sikandar, vigorous urban growth in Delhi and Agra, and significant architectural and literary activity. The story is better understood as a struggle between a recurring project of centralization and the structural limits that repeatedly frustrated it. Chapter 44 examines broader social and cultural developments across the whole Sultanate period, while this chapter concentrates on political and institutional change.

Section 43.1 · Division I — The Sayyid Dynasty and the Struggle for Survival, 1414–1451

Delhi after the Tughlaqs: A Sultanate among Rival Powers

The political landscape in 1414 was one of plural centers. Delhi was no longer the capital of northern India but one of several rival courts. To the east, the Sharqi sultans ruled Jaunpur and much of the middle Ganges plain, and their court attracted scholars, poets, and builders. To the south and southwest, Malwa under its own sultans looked toward the Chambal and the routes to Delhi. Gujarat, wealthy from Arabian Sea commerce, was building its own regional power. Bengal, far to the east, was a self-governing sultanate with its own traditions. Each of these courts claimed Muslim royal legitimacy and could and did intervene when Delhi's weakness invited it. Chapter 47 treats them in detail; here they matter as constraints on what Delhi could attempt.

Closer to Delhi, the political map was a patchwork of local power-holders. Chiefs of Mewat, whose territory lay immediately south of the capital, raided and negotiated by turns. Rajput and other local lords held Katehar, parts of the Doab, and the fortified towns around Etawah and Bayana. In the Punjab, the Khokhar chiefs of the Salt Range area were capable of launching major raids, and Muslim governors in Multan, Lahore, Dipalpur, and Sirhind held the western approaches in conditions of near autonomy. Delhi's revenue base was correspondingly narrow and uncertain.

Timurid influence added a further layer. Timur's invasion had been a raid rather than a conquest, and he left no occupying army. But he and his successors claimed a lordship that some local governors found useful to acknowledge, and Timur's son Shahrukh, who ruled from Herat, was recognized as overlord by the first Sayyid ruler. Whether this recognition was a meaningful subordination or a convenient claim to legitimacy is one of the questions §43.2 examines.

Section 43.2

Khizr Khan and the Founding of Sayyid Rule, 1414–1421

Khizr Khan had been associated with Multan and the Punjab frontier for years before 1414. He had served the Tughlaqs, lost his position in the turmoil that followed, and had links with Timur's campaign of 1398 and with the Timurid court afterward. In the years of confusion following Timur's departure, Delhi's government passed through the hands of nobles, including Daulat Khan Lodi, who held the city for a short time in 1413–1414. Khizr Khan advanced on Delhi from the northwest and took the city in May 1414, after a siege that wore down his rival.

The new ruler did not take the title of sultan. Contemporary and near-contemporary accounts say that he styled himself Rayat-i Ala, roughly "exalted standard," and treated himself as a governor acting on behalf of the Timurid house. He sent tribute and acknowledgments to Shahrukh, and for a time the khutba, the Friday sermon invoking the ruler's name, and the coinage reflected a nominal Timurid overlordship. Historians draw an important distinction here. Titles, coins, and the khutba tell students what a ruler claimed; they do not by themselves tell what he could command. In practice Khizr Khan's authority depended on the loyalty of the commanders around him, and Timurid overlordship never involved Timurid armies or officials in Delhi. The recognition may have provided a form of legitimacy useful to a ruler whose own claim to rule rested on little else.

The dynasty's name, Sayyid, derives from a claim that Khizr Khan's family descended from the Prophet Muhammad. This was a dynastic claim, reported in the chronicles, and not an established genealogical fact. Claims of sayyid descent carried real prestige in Muslim societies, and the claim served the dynasty's legitimacy, but a historian cannot confirm it and should not state it as settled. Khizr Khan's reign was spent largely in campaigns to collect revenue and enforce submission in places such as Mewat, Katehar, the Doab, and the Punjab. These expeditions often yielded tribute and temporary obedience rather than durable control, and local chiefs would revert to defiance when the army withdrew.

Khizr Khan died in May 1421. His reign is best seen as the establishment of a defensible core: a ruler in Delhi with a modest army, a narrow revenue base, and an unstable but functioning relationship with the nobles who surrounded him.

Section 43.3

Mubarak Shah: Restoration, Military Campaigns, and Court Politics, 1421–1434

Khizr Khan was succeeded by his son Mubarak Shah, who dropped the more limited style of his father and adopted the royal title in his own name. Coins and the khutba followed. This shift marked a decision to present the Sayyids as an independent dynasty rather than as Timurid agents, and it came at a time when the Timurid connection could no longer protect Delhi. The most detailed account of his reign comes from Yahya Sirhindi, who wrote while Mubarak Shah was alive and named his chronicle after him.

Much of Mubarak Shah's reign was occupied by military problems. In the Punjab, the Khokhar chief Jasrath, who at times moved across the frontier and at times entered the Delhi sultan's orbit, posed a recurring threat and at one point became involved in struggles over Lahore. In the Doab and nearby regions, Mubarak Shah campaigned against local chiefs at Etawah, Bayana, Mewat, and Katehar, attempting to extract revenue and obedience. He also confronted outside intervention, including pressure from Malwa and from Jaunpur. These expeditions kept the sultanate alive but did not transform its basic position, because each campaign solved a local problem for a season.

Mubarak Shah patronized urban building, and the chronicler credits him with founding a new city on the Yamuna called Mubarakabad, begun in the early 1430s. This points to a ruler still thinking in terms of royal capital-building even in a diminished state. At court, however, rival factions contested influence. The wazir, Sarwar al-Mulk, rose to great power, and conflict between him and other officials and nobles produced a conspiracy in which Mubarak Shah was assassinated in 1434 while attending a building site. The murder, carried out by court insiders, demonstrated how fragile royal authority was when high officers could treat the throne as an instrument of their own factions.

Primary Source Window

Yahya Sirhindi, Tarikh-i Mubarak Shahi

Source type: Persian court chronicle · Author: Yahya bin Ahmad Sirhindi · Composed: during the reign of Mubarak Shah, completed in the 1430s · Audience: the Sayyid court and educated Persian-reading elites · Coverage: a general history of Muslim rule in India leading to the Sayyid period, with its fullest and most contemporary treatment reserved for the Sayyids

Context: Yahya Sirhindi wrote at a court whose ruler he praised and whose family he had reason to serve. His work is the main surviving chronicle for the Sayyid dynasty and the main source for the political events of 1414–1434. Later chroniclers, including Nizam al-Din Ahmad and Firishta, drew on it heavily.

Paraphrase (not a direct quotation): The chronicle presents Khizr Khan as a ruler who accepted the overlordship of the Timurid house and governed as its deputy, sending tribute and acknowledging Timurid authority in public forms. It then presents Mubarak Shah as a more fully sovereign king who took royal titles, issued coins in his own name, and conducted a series of campaigns against refractory chiefs, with the work ending shortly after the sultan's assassination.

Analysis: This source gives historians chronology, names of officers, the sequence of campaigns, and the vocabulary of court legitimacy. As a contemporary work, it is closer to events than any other narrative of the period. It also has limits. It was written under royal patronage, so it tends to treat Sayyid claims favorably and to describe failures as temporary. It is concerned mostly with the court and the army, not with villagers, merchants, or non-Muslim communities. Its picture of "obedient" and "rebellious" chiefs reflects the view of the center. Historians therefore test its claims against coins, inscriptions, and the later chronicles, and treat the reported titles as claims rather than as a measure of real authority.

Questions for students:

  1. What does the chronicle say about the relationship between Khizr Khan and the Timurids?
  2. Who wrote it, for whom, and why might he have described Sayyid rule as he did?
  3. What can historians learn from the work about the structure of the court and army?
  4. What can they not establish from this source alone?
Section 43.4

Muhammad Shah and the Weakening of Sayyid Authority, 1434–1445

Mubarak Shah's murderers placed his nephew on the throne as Muhammad Shah, and for a time the new ruler was controlled by the very ministers who had engineered the succession. The dominant figure was Sarwar al-Mulk, who held the office of wazir and attempted to rule through the young sultan. Muhammad Shah moved against him, and a struggle followed in which the sultan and his supporters destroyed the conspirators, but the effort consumed time and energy that might have gone toward strengthening the state. After these events, power gravitated toward other high officers, and ministerial power remained a persistent weakness.

Muhammad Shah's reign also exposed Delhi's dependence on provincial commanders. In the early 1440s, Mahmud Khalji of Malwa attacked toward Delhi, and the sultan lacked the strength to repel him unaided. Bahlul Lodi, governor of Sirhind, with extensive forces and a powerful Afghan following, came to the sultan's aid and played a decisive part in turning the Malwa army back. The sultan rewarded him with higher rank and additional territories, including holdings in the Punjab, and Bahlul gained prestige and resources in the process. The episode shows an important pattern: the throne's defenders were often its potential successors. A sultan who relied on a powerful commander to preserve his position made that commander more powerful.

The wider limits on Sayyid power were fiscal and military. A state that could not reliably collect revenue from outlying iqtas could not support a large standing force, and a small force could not compel obedience. The combination of tribute-collecting campaigns, noble factionalism, and pressure from neighbors left the Sayyids constantly reacting to events rather than shaping them. Relations with Jaunpur and Malwa remained tense and at times hostile, and neither required a decisive victory over Delhi to benefit from its weakness.

Section 43.5

Alam Shah, Badaun, and the Transfer of Power in 1451

Muhammad Shah died in 1445 and was succeeded by his son Alam Shah, the last Sayyid ruler. Alam Shah's reign was weak from the outset. Powerful governors increasingly acted independently, ministerial intrigue continued, and the sultan's authority did not extend much beyond the city and its immediate surroundings. Around 1447 he withdrew to Badaun, a city to the east, which he seems to have preferred to Delhi, leaving the capital in the hands of his officers. This withdrawal was a symptom of how little the sultan could do in Delhi itself.

In Delhi, the wazir Hamid Khan held effective control, but his relations with nobles and with the sultan were unsettled. Bahlul Lodi, who held large territories in the Punjab and enjoyed the support of numerous Afghan commanders, watched this situation and advanced on Delhi more than once. Eventually he gained the city, and in April 1451 he was proclaimed sultan. Alam Shah is reported to have agreed to cede the throne while retaining Badaun, where he lived on, and the chronicles describe an arrangement more like a political settlement than a violent conquest.

Historians ask why the transfer of power produced so little prolonged resistance. Several explanations fit the evidence. The Sayyids had no independent army large enough to resist, and the nobles on whom they depended saw little reason to defend a ruler who could not protect their interests. Bahlul already commanded substantial resources and followers and could offer nobles a more promising patron. The Sayyid claim to sovereignty rested more on custom than on any strong institutional foundation, and so it dissolved easily when challenged. The change of dynasty therefore tells students less about a dramatic conquest than about a government that had already lost the practical means of ruling. It also sets up the central problem of the Lodi period: a new dynasty whose power came from a coalition of nobles would have to decide how far it could ever stand above them.

Section 43.6 · Division II — Bahlul Lodi and the Reconstruction of Delhi's Power, 1451–1489

Afghan Military Migration and the Emergence of the Lodi Elite

To understand how Bahlul Lodi could take Delhi, students must understand the world of the Afghan military families of northern India. The Afghans of the late medieval period came from the mountainous region west of the Indus, including the Sulaiman range, the Kabul valley, and neighboring uplands. Afghan soldiers had been serving in Indian armies for generations, some entering as mercenaries, some as cavalry under Delhi's sultans, and some as settlers on lands granted for service. By the fifteenth century Afghan military families such as the Lodis, Surs, Lohanis, Farmulis, and Sarwanis held positions and territories across the Punjab and the Ganges plain.

Migration was gradual and continuous rather than a single event. Young men, often relatives and clients of men already in service, came in search of employment and found it. Chiefs recruited followers from their own kin and region, and followers who prospered brought more relatives. This pattern produced strong personal and kinship ties, with a chief expecting loyalty from his dependents and rewarding it with grants, offices, and protection. The Lodis themselves had risen through service in the Punjab, and Bahlul's family had held Sirhind under the Sayyids.

The label "Afghan" requires caution. It covered a wide range of tribes, clans, and families with different histories, interests, and rivalries. Chiefs of different groups competed for office and rank, and alliances shifted. The Afghan elite also lived alongside, and often cooperated with, Turkic, Persian, and Indian Muslim nobles, as well as Hindu local chiefs and officials. Treating "the Afghans" as a single bloc, or as a people with a unified political culture, would hide the divisions that played such a large part in the Lodi story. It would also hide how Afghan identity was continually shaped by political circumstance, including the memory of earlier migrations and later claims in the chronicles.

Afghan political culture should also not be described as a uniform tradition of equality among chiefs. Later chroniclers, writing in a period when Afghan nobles of the Mughal era had their own stake in the past, sometimes projected an image of Lodi kings as egalitarian leaders among peers. Chiefs certainly expected consultation and honor, and in the early Lodi period such expectations shaped royal behavior. But chiefs also differed in rank, wealth, and following, and the supposed equality was in practice a hierarchy of influence.

Section 43.7

Bahlul Lodi's Accession and the Consolidation of His Throne

Bahlul Lodi began his career in the Punjab. His uncle Islam Khan had served as governor of Sirhind under the Sayyids, and Bahlul succeeded to a position of considerable power there. By the 1440s he had come to control Sirhind, Lahore, Dipalpur, and neighboring territories, and he had assembled a force of loyal Afghan commanders. His support to Muhammad Shah against Malwa raised his prestige, and the political vacuum around Alam Shah gave him the opening to seize Delhi.

Bahlul's accession in 1451 posed problems of legitimacy. He had no royal ancestry, no claim of descent from a prestigious house, and no clear mandate from an overlord. His authority rested on the force he could muster and on the acquiescence of the nobles and religious leaders of Delhi. He needed to show that he could defend the city, give nobles a share in the government, and maintain the forms of Muslim kingship, including the khutba and the coinage, which he issued in his own name. Reconciling the fact of his rule with the habit of treating the throne as the property of an established house was a continuing task.

His early years as sultan were spent in securing control of Delhi's immediate region. He had to deal with nobles who resented his rise, local chiefs who took advantage of the change of regime, and the pretensions of neighboring rulers. His chosen approach, described in §43.8, combined force with negotiation. He campaigned where necessary but sought to bind commanders to him through shared interests, kinship, and rewards rather than through simple command. The result was not a centralized state but a coalition under a sultan skilled at managing it.

Section 43.8

Afghan Chiefs, Noble Alliances, and the Structure of Lodi Kingship

The Lodi sultanate under Bahlul depended on a web of agreements with Afghan commanders. Bahlul gave chiefs offices, military commands, and revenue assignments, and in return they supplied troops and support. Marriage ties, gifts, and personal attention strengthened these bonds. Chronicles composed in later generations stress Bahlul's informality: they report that he addressed nobles with respect, did not insist on sitting apart on a throne in their presence, and treated them almost as colleagues. The details come from sources written long after his reign and may idealize the early Lodi court, but they reflect a real feature of his rule, which was the need to treat powerful nobles as partners.

Historians have often summarized this situation by describing Bahlul as a "first among equals." That phrase is useful because it captures the dependence of the sultan on the goodwill of his nobles. It should not be treated as a rigid constitutional principle or as proof that Afghan custom required kings to be mere peers. Bahlul still led armies, appointed and removed officers, collected revenue, and punished some opponents. The nobles themselves differed in power, and he could favor some over others. The relationship was a negotiated one, subject to constant adjustment, rather than a fixed rule of equal status.

The practical significance of this structure was both strength and weakness. It allowed Bahlul to mobilize large forces quickly and at low cost, because commanders brought their own followers. It also meant that he could not easily discipline an important noble or reassign his territories, since to do so risked defection. Revenue assignments tended to become hereditary in practice, and the center did not always know what resources were actually available. When a strong king ruled, such a system could work well. When a king tried to change it, the consequences could be dangerous, as the reigns of Sikandar and Ibrahim would show.

Historical Vocabulary
Afghan, Patronage, and Political Allegiance

In fifteenth-century northern India, Afghan referred to a broad set of military and political families of Afghan origin serving as soldiers, commanders, and landholders. The term was a flexible social and political label, not a fixed ethnic identity, and it covered groups with distinct tribal affiliations and conflicting interests. Patronage was the exchange of material support and protection for loyalty and service. In the Lodi context, it ran through offices, iqta assignments, gifts, and marriages, and it connected the sultan to nobles and nobles to their own followers. Allegiance in this system was personal and conditional, tied to the continued benefit of the relationship rather than to an abstract state.

These terms matter because they help explain why Lodi government worked through negotiation, and why attempts to rely on formal command alone often failed. Students should avoid modern equivalents such as "nationality" or "loyalty to the state," which do not describe how political allegiance worked in this period.

Historical Debate

Was Bahlul Lodi's Authority Primarily Negotiated or Centralized?

The question: Did Bahlul rule as the leader of a coalition of Afghan chiefs, or did he build a more centralized kingship that used the chiefs as instruments?

Interpretation A — A negotiated kingship: Bahlul's authority rested on alliances with Afghan commanders who brought their own followers. The Afghan chronicles describe his informal style, the limited ceremonial distance between him and his nobles, and his reluctance to override their claims. On this view, he governed by consensus and personal management, and the Lodi state was in effect a confederacy under a sultan.
Interpretation B — A kingship with centralizing aims: Bahlul commanded armies, appointed officers, campaigned against both rivals and subordinates, and extended Delhi's territory through the conquest of Jaunpur. He used coinage, the khutba, and the apparatus of Sultanate government to assert sovereignty. On this view, the informal style was a tactic, and Bahlul laid the foundation for Sikandar's more assertive rule.

Evidence: Both views draw on the same chronicles, which are late and shaped by later Afghan memory, as well as on coins and administrative practice. The informal manners reported by later authors may be partly idealization. The record of campaigns and territorial expansion shows an active ruler, but it does not settle how far he could direct his nobles.

Why it matters: The answer affects how historians understand the later conflicts. If Bahlul ruled by consensus, Sikandar and Ibrahim were changing the rules and creating opposition. If Bahlul already exercised substantial authority, the later conflicts look more like arguments over its degree. Most scholars today combine the two views, seeing a ruler who negotiated skillfully from a position of growing strength.

Section 43.9

Consolidating the Punjab, Doab, Mewat, and Neighboring Territories

Before Bahlul could confront Jaunpur, he had to make the area around Delhi dependable. This meant campaigns in the Punjab, the Doab, Mewat, and neighboring districts, where chiefs had grown used to ignoring the capital. The sultan led expeditions to Mewat, to towns in the Doab such as Etawah and Sambhal, and to chiefs in the region of Bayana and Gwalior's approaches. In most cases he secured submission and tribute, and in some cases he replaced local rulers or installed trusted commanders.

Geography shaped these operations. Roads from Delhi ran along river valleys and across fords, and control of crossings on the Yamuna and Ganges determined whether a force could reach its objective in a season. Armies moved with the harvest and the monsoon in mind, since the rainy season made rivers difficult and fodder essential. Local chiefs, who knew the terrain, could withdraw to forests or fortified villages and wait. A sultan who marched through their territory could collect tribute but could not hold it without leaving garrisons or granting land to trusted retainers.

This is why campaigning alone could not impose lasting authority. A defeated chief could resume defiance as soon as the army left, and a sultan's reach was limited by the distance his troops could travel and the supplies they could carry. The durable solution was to give a trusted noble an iqta in the region, with the right to collect revenue and the obligation to maintain order. That in turn increased the noble's power. The cycle of conquest, assignment, and growing noble autonomy helps explain both the successes and the limits of Lodi consolidation.

Section 43.10

Delhi and Jaunpur: The Struggle against the Sharqi Sultanate

The Sharqi sultanate was the most formidable rival Delhi faced. Its founder, Malik Sarwar, had been a high officer of the Tughlaqs who established an independent government at Jaunpur in 1394, and his successors ruled the middle Gangetic plain for most of the fifteenth century. The Sharqi capital was a major center of learning and architecture, with imposing mosques that blended regional and Sultanate styles, and the court attracted scholars and poets. The Sharqis controlled fertile land, a large revenue base, and important river routes, and at their height they posed a serious threat to Delhi itself.

The conflict was in part a contest over legitimacy. The Sharqis claimed to be heirs to the Sultanate's authority and at times sought to supplant Delhi as the leading Muslim power in the north. Husain Shah Sharqi, the last ruler, was ambitious and at one point advanced toward Delhi with a large army. The Lodis, for their part, regarded the Sharqis as usurpers of territory that belonged within the Sultanate. Both courts therefore treated the war as a struggle for supremacy and not merely a border dispute.

Strategically, the middle Gangetic plain gave access to Bihar, Awadh, and routes to Bengal, and its control strengthened any ruler who held it. The wars between Bahlul and the Sharqis stretched over roughly a quarter of a century and consisted of repeated invasions, truces, and renewed fighting, accompanied by diplomacy and shifting alliances among local chiefs. The Sharqis had real advantages in resources, but they were also affected by dynastic quarrels and by the dangers of fighting on several fronts, and as the conflict continued the balance moved gradually toward Delhi. Chapter 47 examines Jaunpur's own history and culture more fully.

Section 43.11

The Conquest of Jaunpur and the Achievements of Bahlul's Reign

The decisive Lodi advance into Jaunpur came around 1479, though sources differ about exact dates and the Sharqi ruler Husain Shah was able to recover parts of his territory afterward. By the early 1480s Lodi control of the city and much of the kingdom was established, and Husain Shah withdrew eastward, eventually finding refuge in Bengal, where he lived under the protection of its sultan. This was the greatest territorial gain of the restored Sultanate and transformed Delhi's position, giving it the rich lands of the middle Ganges and a base from which to pressure Bihar and the east.

The arrangements for governing the recovered territories followed the established pattern. Bahlul entrusted Jaunpur to his son Barbak Shah, who ruled it as a kind of viceroy, and he assigned other territories to Afghan nobles. This preserved the coalition and gave family members a stake in the conquest, but it also created a semi-independent power center and the possibility of dynastic rivalry, which would emerge after Bahlul's death. The new regime also had to win over local landholders, administrators, and scholars who had served the Sharqis, and it did so partly by retaining many existing local arrangements.

When Bahlul died in 1489, the Sultanate he left was larger and more stable than the one he had received. Delhi again held the Punjab, the Doab, Mewat, and the middle Ganges plain, and its sultan could once more claim to be the leading Muslim ruler of the north. But the achievement rested on the personal skill of the sultan and on a network of noble alliances that had not been institutionalized. The question that Sikandar inherited was whether this recovery could be made more durable.

Cause and Effect

Why Jaunpur Was Incorporated

Long-term causes

Delhi's growing military resources under a coalition of Afghan commanders; the Sharqi sultanate's dependence on a single dynasty and on shifting regional alliances; and the strategic and economic value of the middle Gangetic plain.

Short-term causes

Prolonged warfare that exhausted Sharqi resources; internal divisions among Sharqi supporters; and Bahlul's effective use of Afghan commanders and kinship ties to sustain a long campaign.

Immediate effects

Delhi acquired Jaunpur and much of its territory; Husain Shah Sharqi lost his kingdom and took refuge in the east; and Barbak Shah was installed as ruler of the recovered region.

Long-term consequences

The Lodis became the dominant northern power and gained a base for pressure on Bihar and Bengal; but the creation of a semi-autonomous Jaunpur under a Lodi prince also created a new site of dynastic competition.

Historical complexity: The conquest cannot be explained by military superiority alone. Resources, diplomacy, patronage, and the internal condition of both states all contributed, and the Sharqi collapse looks less inevitable when viewed from the perspective of the long preceding stalemate.

Section 43.12 · Division III — Sikandar Lodi: Centralization and Territorial Recovery, 1489–1517

Sikandar Lodi's Accession and the Reorganization of Royal Authority

On Bahlul's death in 1489, the succession was not automatic. Several sons and relatives had claims, and powerful nobles had their own preferences. Bahlul's son Nizam Khan, who took the name Sikandar, was enthroned at Jalali in July 1489 with the support of a number of leading nobles, while his brother Barbak Shah held Jaunpur and other relatives held important positions. In the years that followed, Sikandar had to settle his relations with his brothers and with nobles who had backed other candidates, and he did so partly by negotiation and partly by force, ultimately weakening Barbak Shah's autonomous position at Jaunpur.

Sikandar is remembered as a ruler determined to raise the sultan above his nobles. The Afghan chronicles say that he insisted on greater court ceremony and that nobles were expected to show deference they had not shown his father. These reports may be exaggerated, but they fit a real pattern of efforts to supervise nobles, audit their accounts, and make them answerable. At the same time, Sikandar did not abandon the system of Afghan alliances. He kept the nobility in royal service, rewarding loyal commanders and punishing disobedient ones selectively. The sultan sought to strengthen the throne within the existing structure, not to replace it.

This combination of assertion and accommodation was one of the key features of his reign and, for much of it, a successful one. It allowed him to expand and administer territory more effectively than Bahlul had, while avoiding the open confrontation that would later undermine Ibrahim.

Section 43.13

Jaunpur, Bihar, and the Eastern Frontier

Sikandar's first major tasks were in the east. Jaunpur needed to be secured, and the remnants of Sharqi power, including Husain Shah Sharqi, who still had supporters and could count on shelter in Bihar and Bengal, remained a threat. In the mid-1490s Sikandar defeated Husain Shah's forces, brought Jaunpur more fully under direct control, and advanced into Bihar, where local chiefs and officers accepted his overlordship in varying degrees. Husain Shah himself finally found refuge with the sultan of Bengal.

The extent of Lodi authority in Bihar should be distinguished from direct rule. In some areas Sikandar installed Lodi governors and collected revenue; in others he exercised pressure through campaigns and diplomacy and accepted formal submission from local lords who retained their territories. Bihar remained a region where local power-holders, including Afghan nobles with their own followings, had considerable freedom, a point that matters for the later rebellions under Ibrahim and the rise of Afghan power in Bihar in the 1520s.

The eastern frontier was also shaped by Bengal. Bengal's sultan, Ala al-Din Husain Shah, ruled a prosperous and well-organized kingdom, and the two sultans reached an agreement around 1499 recognizing a boundary and pledging not to harbor each other's enemies. The treaty indicates that Sikandar preferred to fix a stable frontier in the east rather than pursue open-ended war there, freeing resources for campaigns in other regions. It also reflects Bengal's strength and the limits of Delhi's reach.

Section 43.14

Gwalior, Narwar, Dholpur, and the Southern Frontier

South of the Delhi region lay a belt of fortified states and strongholds that controlled the routes between northern India, Malwa, and central India. Chief among them was Gwalior, ruled by the Tomar dynasty under Raja Man Singh, whose great hilltop fort commanded the region. Other fortified places included Dholpur, Narwar, Mandrayal, and Utgir. The Chambal river region was difficult terrain, and control of its forts meant control of the southern corridor and of access to Malwa.

Sikandar campaigned in this region repeatedly in the first decade of the sixteenth century. He captured Dholpur and other forts, took Mandrayal, and later took Narwar and Utgir, and his armies forced Man Singh into tribute and defensive postures. These campaigns involved siege warfare, careful logistics, and negotiations with local rulers. They did not, however, result in the capture of Gwalior itself. Sikandar's operations pressed Gwalior and weakened it, but the fort remained in Tomar hands until after Man Singh's death, and its conquest was achieved only in 1518 under Ibrahim Lodi, when Man Singh's successor surrendered after a siege. This distinction matters because later accounts sometimes blur Sikandar's pressure on Gwalior with Ibrahim's capture.

Rajput resistance in this region was not simply a matter of hostile religion or culture. It was a contest over control of strategic routes, revenue, and the survival of local dynasties, and it involved diplomacy and temporary alliances alongside combat. The Tomars, like many local rulers, accepted tribute arrangements when they had to and resisted when they could. Control of the southern frontier would remain a persistent problem for Delhi and for the Mughals afterward.

Section 43.15

The Rise of Agra as a New Political Center

Around 1504–1505, Sikandar made Agra his principal residence and base. The decision reflected strategy. Agra lies on the Yamuna south of Delhi, and from it a sultan could supervise the Doab, press on Gwalior and Dholpur, watch the routes to Malwa and Rajasthan, and stay closer to his eastern territories. A capital at Delhi was well placed for the Punjab and the northwest, but Sikandar's main military problems lay to the south and east.

Agra was not created by Sikandar. It was an existing settlement with a fortress and a long history of earlier occupation, and chroniclers refer to it before his reign. His achievement was to transform it into a major royal center by building, settling nobles and merchants there, and making it the seat of the court for extended periods. The sources report that an earthquake struck Agra around 1505, a reminder that urban development in this period was vulnerable to natural hazards as well as political change. Over the following decades, Agra's importance would continue to grow, and it became a principal capital of the Mughals.

This example shows the relationship between political geography and urban development. The location of a ruler's residence reflects the location of his problems, and the growth of a city reflects the political and commercial activity that follows the court. Delhi did not lose its importance, and the Lodi sultans continued to use it, but the emergence of Agra marks a shift in Delhi's political center of gravity toward the Yamuna south and the Doab.

Section 43.16

Revenue Administration, Military Organization, and Provincial Supervision

Sikandar's reputation as an administrator rests largely on later Afghan chronicles that praise his supervision of nobles and officials. They report that he required accounts to be submitted, that nobles who failed to render a proper reckoning could be punished, and that he maintained a network of informants who reported on officers and provinces. These accounts may exaggerate, but they fit what the sultan needed to do: ensure that revenue assigned to nobles actually produced the troops and payments they were supposed to supply.

Revenue was drawn from agricultural land, assessed in the form of a share of the crop or its value, with local officials, village headmen, and zamindars playing essential roles in collection. The Sultanate's revenue system was layered. Some territories were kept as crown land, or khalisa, with revenue going directly to the treasury, while others were assigned as iqtas to nobles. The balance between them was a crucial political variable, because every iqta given away reduced direct royal income and increased a noble's independence.

One measure associated with Sikandar is the gaz-i Sikandari, a standard yard for measuring land and building, which later Mughal sources continued to refer to. Historians accept that a standard associated with his name existed and was used for some time, but the details of its length and the extent of its use are known mostly from later accounts, which require careful treatment. The gaz fits a broader aim of standardization, but it does not prove a uniform empire-wide revenue survey in his reign. Military organization remained based on the retinues of nobles supplemented by troops directly under royal control, with cavalry as the core and elephants and infantry as support.

Historical Vocabulary
Iqta, Khalisa, and Gaz

An iqta was an assignment of the right to collect revenue from a territory in return for military and administrative duties, held by an iqtadar. Originally revocable and transferable, it tended to become hereditary in practice when central control weakened. Khalisa referred to land whose revenue went directly to the royal treasury rather than to a noble. A gaz was a unit of linear measurement, and the Sikandari gaz was a standard associated with Sikandar Lodi and used in measuring land and construction.

These terms help explain the political economy of the Sultanate. The proportion of land held as khalisa and as iqta shaped the strength of the throne, while measurement standards helped, in principle, to assess and verify revenue.

Section 43.17

Religious Policy, Court Learning, and Intellectual Patronage

Sikandar's religious policy has been a subject of disagreement, because the surviving evidence is limited and was written mostly by authors with their own viewpoints. As a Muslim sultan in a society where most subjects were not Muslim, he worked within a framework of Islamic political legitimacy that expected patronage of the ulama, respect for Islamic law, and the maintenance of mosques and religious institutions. He consulted religious scholars, appointed qazis, and supported pious foundations. Contemporary and later accounts describe him as devout and as a patron of learned men.

His treatment of non-Muslim communities is reported more variably. Later chronicles, including those compiled in the Mughal period, describe the destruction of temples in places such as Mathura, restrictions on pilgrim bathing, and punishment of a Brahman accused of claiming that Hinduism and Islam were both valid paths. Other reports, and the continued participation of Hindu officials and local chiefs in Lodi government, suggest that policy was uneven. Historians should not conclude from these reports that his rule was uniformly intolerant, nor that it was uniformly tolerant. Policy varied with region, circumstance, and the sultan's political needs, and the source texts are late and may reflect the concerns of their own times.

Sikandar was also a patron of learning. His court supported Persian literature and scholarship, and the tradition holds that he composed verse under the pen name Gulrukhi. Medical and scholarly works were compiled under his patronage, including a Persian medical compilation completed in the early sixteenth century. This patronage belonged to the broader Persianate culture of the Sultanate, which Chapter 44 examines.

Section 43.18

Cities, Architecture, Commerce, and the Lodi Political Landscape

Delhi and Agra grew during the late Sultanate as centers of administration, military recruitment, and trade. Markets served the court and the nobles' households, craftsmen produced goods for urban consumers, and merchants moved grain, textiles, horses, and other commodities along the roads and rivers. Horses were imported from the northwest, a reminder that the military economy depended on links with Central Asia and Afghanistan. The political fragmentation of northern India did not stop commerce, but it complicated it, since tolls, local chiefs, and uncertain security affected trade routes.

The Sayyid and Lodi periods left a distinctive architectural record, mostly tombs and mosques in and around Delhi. Octagonal tombs, raised on platforms and topped with domes, are characteristic of Sayyid and early Lodi building, while later Lodi work includes square tombs and mosques with decorated facades and enameled tile work. Many of these monuments stand in what is now Delhi's Lodi Gardens, but students should remember that the present park setting and its restoration history are later developments, not the original context of the monuments. Sikandar's tomb, built after his death in 1517 under his successor, is a notable example of a walled garden tomb that anticipates later Mughal practice.

This section describes political and urban developments. The broader social, religious, and cultural life of Sultanate society, including conversion, Sufi communities, and Hindu-Muslim interaction, is treated in Chapter 44.

Everyday Life and Social History

Living under Late Sultanate Government

For most people in the Ganges plain and the Punjab, the Sultanate meant revenue demands and the behavior of local officers and chiefs, not the doings of the court. Cultivators paid a share of the harvest, and the identity of the collector, whether a royal officer, iqtadar, or zamindar, varied. In times of campaign, armies drew supplies from the countryside, and villages near roads or forts could be burdened by billeting and foraging. Craftspeople and traders lived in towns, producing goods for local use and for the households of nobles, and moved with the political fortunes of their patrons. The chroniclers rarely describe these lives directly, so historians must infer them from revenue practices, monuments, and incidental references, and should avoid assuming that the elite's experience represented everyone's.

Section 43.19 · Division IV — Ibrahim Lodi and the End of the Delhi Sultanate, 1517–1526

Ibrahim Lodi's Accession and the Conflict with Afghan Nobles

Sikandar died in November 1517, and the nobles gathered at Agra enthroned his eldest son Ibrahim. The succession was not undisputed, since some nobles favored a division of the realm that would have given Ibrahim's brother Jalal Khan a separate kingdom centered on Jaunpur, a solution consistent with the Lodi tradition of family appanages. An initial arrangement along these lines was made and then reversed, and the reversal itself became a source of conflict.

Ibrahim's reign is conventionally described as a confrontation between a king bent on royal supremacy and an aristocracy used to partnership. The Afghan chronicles say that he insisted on a stiffer court ceremonial, that nobles were required to stand in his presence, and that he imprisoned or removed commanders whom his father and grandfather had treated as partners. Some of these reports reflect the viewpoint of Afghan authors writing in later generations, who looked back on his reign as a time when the Lodi order broke down. But the underlying pattern is plausible: Ibrahim continued Sikandar's policy and applied it with less tact, at a time when nobles were more powerful and numerous than ever.

The explanation that Ibrahim fell because he was personally arrogant is too simple. His position was structurally difficult. He had inherited a state whose power depended on commanders who held large territories and followings, and he had to either accept their autonomy or try to reduce it. A policy of reduction, pursued during a succession dispute and without a large standing army under direct royal command, was dangerous. The question for historians is less whether Ibrahim was harsh than whether any sultan could have achieved Sikandar's goals under these conditions. Sikandar succeeded partly because he moved gradually and had a long reign. Ibrahim did not have such time.

Section 43.20

Rebellion, Rival Claimants, and the Fragmentation of Lodi Authority

The first serious challenge came from Jalal Khan, who held Kalpi and Jaunpur and claimed royal status in the east. Ibrahim moved against him, compelled him to flee, and Jalal eventually died after being captured while seeking refuge in Malwa and elsewhere, according to the usual accounts. The episode eliminated a rival claimant but also displayed the Lodi house's divisions and strained relations with commanders who had backed Jalal or who feared for their own positions.

Rebellions followed in several regions. Powerful nobles such as Azam Humayun Sarwani, who held commands in the east, came into conflict with the sultan and, after periods of campaigning and imprisonment, their families and followers contested the royal government. In Bihar, the Afghan governor Darya Khan Lohani held effective power and, after his death around 1522, his son Bahar Khan declared himself sultan under the name Muhammad Shah, with the support of numerous Afghan nobles, effectively setting up a rival sultanate in the east. Ibrahim's campaigns against these opponents were costly and often inconclusive.

At the same time Ibrahim carried on the southern campaigns. Gwalior fell to Lodi forces in 1518, ending Tomar independence in the fort and finishing the effort begun under Sikandar. Relations with the Rajput state of Mewar under Rana Sanga were hostile, and Rajput tradition records clashes in which Lodi forces suffered reverses, though the details are better documented in later tradition than in contemporary evidence. Taken together, these developments were not separate problems but interconnected strains on the sultan's resources: the same armies and revenues were needed against eastern rebels, southern rivals, and domestic factions, and a defeat or diversion in one region encouraged defiance in another.

Section 43.21

The Punjab Crisis: Daulat Khan Lodi, Alam Khan, and the Politics of Intervention

The Punjab was the most exposed part of the Lodi realm, since it lay on the route from Kabul and was governed by Daulat Khan Lodi, a veteran noble with long service and a large following. Daulat Khan held Lahore and the surrounding region with great autonomy. Relations between him and Ibrahim worsened as the sultan sought to bring powerful governors to heel. The chronicles report that Ibrahim summoned him to court and that Daulat Khan, fearing for his safety, kept his distance and sent a son, Dilawar Khan, who found himself unwelcome. Whatever the details, Daulat Khan came to regard the sultan as a danger and began to look to the northwest for support.

Another actor was Alam Khan, a relative of the Lodi house, often identified as Ibrahim's uncle and a son of Bahlul, who had a claim to the throne that he thought the nobles might support. He went to Kabul to seek Babur's help. His motives differed from Daulat Khan's. Daulat Khan wanted to protect his position in the Punjab and secure his territories from the sultan, while Alam Khan wanted the throne itself. Babur had his own ambitions, which went beyond serving as an instrument of either.

Students should therefore avoid describing the appeals to Babur as a single coordinated invitation. They were separate approaches by men with different aims, and their relationships with each other and with Babur were unstable. Daulat Khan at times cooperated with Babur and at times resisted him, and Alam Khan's own attempts to take Delhi with Babur's backing failed. The episode illustrates how a divided Afghan elite offered a foreign ruler opportunities that a united one would not have.

Section 43.22

Babur, the Northwestern Frontier, and the Campaigns Leading to 1526

Babur, born in 1483 in the Ferghana valley, was a Timurid prince through his father and, through his mother, a descendant of Chinggis Khan's house. He spent his youth in a struggle for Central Asian territory, twice taking Samarkand and twice losing it, and in 1504 he seized Kabul, which became his principal base. From Kabul he looked toward India, partly because the Timurid heritage, including Timur's own campaigns, gave him a precedent for claiming parts of the subcontinent, and partly because his resources in Afghanistan were limited and the plains beyond the Indus were rich.

From about 1519 onward Babur conducted expeditions into the Punjab, taking places such as Bajaur and Bhera and probing the region's defenses. In 1524 he advanced to Lahore and Dipalpur, then withdrew, having found the political situation more complicated than he had anticipated. In the winter of 1525 he marched again from Kabul with a larger force, and Daulat Khan, who had by then failed in his own attempts to control events, submitted to him. Babur's own writings show that he understood the movement as the recovery of a Timurid inheritance as well as a prospect of conquest.

This chapter gives only enough background to explain the fall of the Delhi Sultanate. Babur's later government, his campaign against the Rajputs, and the creation of the Mughal state belong to Chapter 50 and following chapters.

Section 43.23

The First Battle of Panipat and the Fall of the Lodis, April 1526

Babur's army advanced from the Punjab in the early months of 1526, and Ibrahim marched out from Delhi and Agra to meet him. The two forces came together on the plain near Panipat, a town on the historic route from the northwest to Delhi, about eighty kilometers north of the capital. Panipat lay on terrain that suited Babur's preparations: the open plain allowed an army to fortify a front, and the town and its surrounding fields offered anchors for its flanks.

Babur described the arrangements in his memoir. He drew up his army with a line of wheeled carts, tied together with ropes of rawhide, with protective screens placed between them to shelter matchlock gunners. His right flank rested on the town of Panipat, and his left was protected by a ditch with cut branches of trees. Gaps were left between the carts for cavalry to sally out. Artillery pieces, handled by gunners including Ustad Ali Quli, were positioned behind the screens. This arrangement drew on Ottoman and Central Asian practices that combined firearms, wagons, and mounted archers. Babur also used the flanking maneuver known as the tulughma, in which detachments on the wings swung around the enemy's sides and rear.

Ibrahim's army was reported by Babur to be vastly larger than his own, with figures of a hundred thousand men and a thousand elephants. Historians treat these numbers cautiously, since a memoirist often magnified an enemy's strength, and modern estimates are lower, though the Lodi army probably did outnumber Babur's. After several days of facing one another, the Lodi army attacked. The sultan's forces pressed against Babur's center, where they were exposed to gunfire and could not easily break the line. Babur's flanking units wheeled around the Lodi wings and attacked from the rear, compressing the army. The fighting lasted several hours and ended with the Lodi forces broken. Ibrahim was killed on the field, and so was the Tomar ruler of Gwalior, Vikramaditya, who had joined the Lodi side. Babur reported that many thousands of Lodi troops fell.

Babur dispatched columns to occupy Delhi and Agra, and the khutba was read in his name. The traditional date of the battle is given in Gregorian terms as 20 or 21 April 1526 in different reputable references, a variation that arises from converting the Islamic date reported in the sources into the Western calendar. Students who assign a precise day should note the variation. The victory ended the Delhi Sultanate as a political system, but Babur still faced rival Afghan commanders and Rajput opponents, and the establishment of Mughal rule was not complete.

Primary Source Window

Babur's Account of Panipat in the Baburnama

Source type: Autobiographical memoir · Author: Zahir al-Din Muhammad Babur · Language: Chagatai Turkic, with later Persian translation under Akbar · Composed: in the years leading up to Babur's death in 1530, from notes and daily records · Audience: Babur himself, his family, and a Timurid readership

Context: Babur wrote candidly about military events, landscape, and people, and his memoir is the most important contemporary source for the battle. The surviving text has gaps and later translations and manuscripts introduce variants.

Paraphrase (not a direct quotation): Babur describes how he arranged carts and screens, how he positioned his gunners and cavalry, how the enemy hesitated and then advanced into a space too narrow for its numbers, and how his flanking detachments completed the encirclement. He portrays Ibrahim as an inexperienced young commander whose army was poorly managed and who fought without proper planning.

Analysis: The memoir provides detail unavailable elsewhere on tactics, preparations, and the reactions of Babur's commanders. It is, however, the account of the victor, who has reasons to emphasize his own judgment and his opponent's faults. His numerical estimates are not independently verified, and his depiction of Ibrahim may reflect partisan judgment. Afghan chronicles composed later offer a different emphasis, one that reflects Afghan concerns about noble loyalty and betrayal.

Historical Debate

What Principally Explains the Lodi Defeat at Panipat?

The question: Did Babur win chiefly because of firearms and artillery, because of superior tactics and discipline, or because the Lodi state was politically divided?

Interpretation A — Military technology: Babur's gunpowder weapons, deployed behind wagons, gave him an advantage that Lodi forces, who relied on cavalry and elephants, could not overcome.
Interpretation B — Tactics and cohesion: The decisive factors were the defensive arrangement, the flanking maneuver, and the cohesion of a veteran army under a single commander, set against a large but poorly coordinated Lodi force.
Interpretation C — Political division: The Lodi army was weakened by the alienation of nobles, the defection or absence of important commanders, and the long strain of rebellion. A united Afghan aristocracy might have fought more effectively or might have negotiated rather than fought.

Evidence: Babur's memoir stresses preparation and tactics; Afghan chronicles stress noble disunity; the earlier conflicts described in this chapter show how the Lodi state had been weakened. Firearms were present but their battlefield effect was one part of a combined arrangement.

Why it matters: A technological explanation can make the outcome seem inevitable, while political and tactical explanations restore contingency. Most historians see the result as arising from several causes working together.

Student task: Explain how each interpretation would predict a different result had the Lodi nobility been united, and identify what evidence would help you decide.

Section 43.24 · Division V — Historical Synthesis

Review and Historical Synthesis

The chapter has traced 112 years in which four Sayyid and three Lodi rulers tried, with differing methods and different success, to rebuild the political authority of Delhi. Four developments stand out. The Sayyids struggled for political survival, holding a small domain through tribute-collecting campaigns and acknowledging a Timurid overlordship that gave them little practical help. Bahlul Lodi rebuilt Delhi's power through a coalition of Afghan commanders, a style of rule that allowed him to conquer Jaunpur but left authority dependent on negotiation. Sikandar Lodi consolidated territory, supervised nobles more closely, and shifted the center of operations toward Agra, increasing royal authority without breaking the coalition. Ibrahim Lodi tried to impose greater royal authority on a divided aristocracy at a time when rebellions, rival claimants, and the Punjab crisis were already straining the state.

Across all these phases, the decisive problem was the gap between ambition and capacity. A sultan could claim sovereignty, issue coins, and demand obedience, but he could not command nobles, collect revenue, and field armies unless he could persuade or compel the people who controlled those resources. When rulers were able to combine patronage with force, as Bahlul and Sikandar did, the Sultanate recovered. When the combination failed, as in the 1520s, the state fragmented. The Lodi recovery was real, but it was a recovery of the political arrangement that had made the Sultanate fragile, not a replacement of it by a more durable institutional order.

The wider context also matters. Delhi's rivals, Jaunpur, Bengal, Malwa, Gujarat, and the Rajput states, shaped its options at every stage, and the Timurid world beyond the Hindu Kush eventually supplied the ruler who ended the dynasty. The environment of rivers, roads, and distances shaped how armies moved and where capitals developed, and the shift from Delhi toward Agra shows geography and politics working together.

Panipat ended the Lodi dynasty but not Afghan political power in India. Afghan nobles remained numerous and armed, and the Afghan political world of Bihar and the east continued under leaders such as Bahar Khan and later Sher Shah of the Sur family, who would drive Babur's son Humayun from India within two decades. The Mughals, for their part, built on many Sultanate foundations in revenue and administration, even as they introduced new elements. Chapter 44 turns to the social and cultural history of the Sultanate period as a whole, Chapters 45 to 47 to the regional powers that coexisted with Delhi, and Chapter 50 to Babur, Humayun, and Sher Shah.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Sayyid and Lodi Approaches to Kingship

What Changed

  • From a deputy of a distant Timurid overlord (Khizr Khan) to a fully sovereign sultan (Mubarak Shah onward)
  • From dependence on a ministerial and noble elite to a coalition of Afghan chiefs under Bahlul
  • From negotiated partnership to attempted royal supremacy under Sikandar and Ibrahim
  • From a capital centered on Delhi to a double center including Agra

What Stayed the Same

  • Reliance on iqta assignments and noble retinues for military force
  • The use of Sultanate forms of legitimacy: khutba, coinage, patronage of the ulama
  • Local zamindars and village officials as the real collectors of revenue
  • The persistent problem of controlling provincial governors

Who experienced change? Nobles and court officials felt the shifts in royal style most directly. Cultivators and townspeople experienced greater continuity, mainly in the form of revenue demands and local authorities. Why it matters: The pattern shows that institutions persisted even as the balance of power within them shifted.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the Delhi Sultanate's final century, from Khizr Khan's entry into Delhi in 1414 to Ibrahim Lodi's death at Panipat in 1526. Its central question was how the Sayyid and Lodi rulers attempted to reconstruct the Sultanate after its fragmentation, and why the Lodi recovery did not produce a stable order capable of surviving the crisis of 1526. The answer lay in the changing relationship between royal authority and the military nobility.

Khizr Khan, who took Delhi in 1414, ruled as the nominal deputy of the Timurids and did not claim the title of sultan. His authority depended on a small domain, a narrow revenue base, and nobles whose loyalty was conditional. Mubarak Shah adopted full royal titles and campaigned energetically, but he was murdered in 1434 by court insiders. Under Muhammad Shah and Alam Shah, ministers and provincial commanders became more powerful than the throne, and in 1451 the dynasty yielded to Bahlul Lodi with little resistance.

Bahlul's rise rested on the Afghan military networks that had grown in northern India through migration and service. These networks were diverse, divided by clan and faction, and bound by patronage and kinship rather than by a shared state. Bahlul governed through negotiation with chiefs who brought their own followers, a method that gave him large forces but limited his freedom to discipline them. His conquest of Jaunpur, around 1479 and consolidated in the early 1480s, was the great achievement of his reign and restored Delhi to the middle Ganges plain.

Sikandar Lodi built on this base. He settled his brothers' claims, defeated the Sharqi remnant, extended Lodi influence into Bihar, fixed a frontier with Bengal, and pressed the fortified states on the southern frontier, although Gwalior itself fell only in 1518 under Ibrahim. He made Agra a principal royal center, supervised nobles and revenue more closely, patronized Persian learning, and reportedly adopted a standard measure of length. His religious policy was uneven and is known mostly from later sources.

Ibrahim Lodi continued the effort to increase royal authority but met resistance from nobles who were powerful, numerous, and accustomed to partnership. Rival claimants, rebellions in the east, the rise of a rival sultanate in Bihar, and conflict on the southern frontier strained the state's resources. In the Punjab, Daulat Khan Lodi and Alam Khan, acting from different motives, turned to Babur, whose own ambitions were rooted in his Timurid inheritance and his base in Kabul.

At Panipat in April 1526, Babur's army, arranged behind carts and screens with gunners and cavalry and using flanking maneuvers, defeated a larger but less cohesive Lodi force. Ibrahim was killed. Historians weigh technology, tactics, and political division in explaining the result, and most see all three as contributing. The evidence for the battle depends heavily on Babur's own memoir, whose numbers and judgments must be treated critically.

The chapter has stressed the gap between ambition and capacity. A sultan could claim sovereignty, but he could exercise it only if he could command nobles, collect revenue, supply soldiers, and control provinces. This helps explain both the recovery under Bahlul and Sikandar and the breakdown under Ibrahim.

Finally, the chapter has argued that Panipat ended the Lodi dynasty but not Afghan power or the institutions of the Sultanate. Afghan nobles continued to play major roles, as the later rise of Sher Shah would show, and Mughal rule inherited much from the Sultanate even as it changed the political order of northern India.

Chapter Reflection

The late Delhi Sultanate is easy to overlook, sandwiched between the dramatic expansion of the Khaljis and Tughlaqs and the imperial grandeur of the Mughals. This chapter has shown why it deserves attention. It reveals what political authority looked like when the ambitions of a capital outran its resources, and how rulers worked, with partial success, to close that gap.

The chapter also shows how politics, geography, and society intersected. Rivers and roads shaped campaigns, the location of capitals followed the location of problems, and the networks of Afghan soldiers and local landholders determined what a sultan could do. The experience of cultivators, townspeople, and religious communities was different from that of the court, and the sources capture it only partially.

Within the six-volume series, the chapter links the high Sultanate of Volume II's earlier chapters to the regional kingdoms of Chapters 45 to 47 and to the Mughal world that opens Volume III with Chapter 50. The fall of the Lodis did not end the history of Afghan power, of Sultanate institutions, or of the Persianate culture that had shaped the court, all of which continued under new rulers.

Students who understand why a recovering state could still collapse quickly will be better prepared to study later transformations in which new rulers inherit old structures, and to ask each time not only who won, but what resources, alliances, and constraints made victory possible.
