Part X — The Mughal Empire

Jahangir and Shah Jahan

Indian History: South Asia from the Indus Civilization to the Present

Chapter Opening

In March 1626, as the imperial camp was crossing the Jhelum river on its way to Kabul, the emperor of Hindustan was seized by one of his own generals. Mahabat Khan, among the most experienced commanders of the empire, had surrounded the camp with his Rajput troops while much of the army was still on the far bank, and for some months afterward Jahangir traveled as a nominal ruler under the watch of his captor. The coup failed, and the emperor regained his freedom. But the episode showed, with unusual clarity, two things at once: how much the empire depended on the person of the emperor, and how easily that dependence could be exploited when the court was divided against itself.

Chapter 52 examined the administrative machinery and political ideology that Akbar left behind: a bureaucratic empire of ranked nobles, provincial governors, and revenue officials, held together by a vision of imperial kingship that sought to draw Muslim, Hindu, Jain, and other communities into a shared political order. This chapter asks how that inheritance performed under his two successors. Jahangir (r. 1605–1627) and Shah Jahan (r. 1628–1658) ruled for more than half a century between them, a period often described as the high noon of Mughal prosperity and display. The empire was extensive and wealthy, its court ceremonial grew more elaborate, and its patronage of painting and architecture produced works, including the Taj Mahal, that remain among the best-known monuments in the world.

The chapter's central argument is that this grandeur and the empire's vulnerability were not opposites but companions. The same institutions that concentrated authority in the emperor's person, and rewarded a vast court nobility with ranks, estates, and honors, also made the transfer of power from one ruler to the next a moment of extreme danger. Mughal custom recognized no fixed rule of primogeniture. Adult princes commanded provinces and armies, and each had allies among the nobility and within the imperial household. Jahangir came to the throne after his own armed defiance of his father; his reign was troubled by the rebellion of his son Khusrau and then by the long rebellion of his son Khurram; and the reign of Khurram, as Shah Jahan, ended in a war among his four surviving sons that destroyed one of them, humiliated another, and brought a third to the throne while the old emperor remained alive in confinement.

The chapter also attends to people who are sometimes lost behind the emperors. Nur Jahan, Jahangir's powerful wife, and Jahanara Begum, Shah Jahan's eldest surviving daughter, were significant political actors within what historians call the imperial household. Nobles such as Asaf Khan, Mahabat Khan, and Malik Ambar, the Ethiopian-born regent of Ahmadnagar who resisted Mughal armies for two decades, shaped events as much as the emperors did. Artists such as Mansur and Abu'l-Hasan, calligraphers, architects, and the thousands of unnamed workers who raised the buildings of Agra and Delhi were equally part of the world these reigns created. Their lives are recoverable only unevenly, and the chapter is candid about where the evidence is rich and where it is thin.

The sources for these reigns are voluminous but not neutral. The Jahangirnama, the emperor's own memoir, is a vivid and sometimes disarming document, but it is also a carefully shaped account of imperial authority. The Padshahnama, the official chronicle of Shah Jahan's reign, was written by court historians whose task was to present the emperor as a model of just and splendid kingship. European visitors such as Sir Thomas Roe, Peter Mundy, François Bernier, and Jean-Baptiste Tavernier left observations of real value, but they wrote from the vantage point of foreign observers with their own interests and prejudices. Court paintings, coins, inscriptions, and the buildings themselves offer a further body of evidence that must be read as representation rather than as photographic record.

By the end of the chapter, students should understand how imperial government functioned in these two reigns, how royal women and powerful nobles shaped politics, why the empire's ambitions in the Deccan and the northwest met limits, how architecture served political purposes, and why the illness of a single ruler in 1657 could unleash a war that reshaped the dynasty. The chapter ends in 1658, at the threshold of Aurangzeb's long reign, which Chapter 54 examines. The social and economic structures beneath these reigns are treated in Chapter 55, and the broader history of Mughal art, architecture, and intellectual life in Chapter 56. The present chapter confines itself, as far as possible, to the political history that connects them.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  2. Analyze the rebellion of Prince Khusrau and the death of Guru Arjan, distinguishing among Mughal, Sikh, and later interpretive accounts.
  3. Evaluate the Jahangirnama as historical evidence, distinguishing the ideals of imperial justice it projects from the practice of government.
  4. Assess Nur Jahan's political role within the structures of the imperial household while recognizing the limits of surviving evidence.
  5. Trace the course of Mughal relations with the Rajput kingdoms, Ahmadnagar, Bijapur, Golconda, the Safavids, and Central Asian powers across the two reigns.
  6. Interpret Mughal court painting and natural-history illustration as expressions of imperial self-presentation and intellectual curiosity.
  7. Explain the causes and consequences of the factional crisis of 1622–1628, including Khurram's rebellion, Mahabat Khan's coup, and the succession of Shah Jahan.
  8. Analyze how court ceremony, the new capital of Shahjahanabad, and the Taj Mahal functioned as representations of imperial authority, and distinguish contemporary evidence from later legend.
  9. Compare the arguments that Shah Jahan's reign represented the culmination of Mughal strength with those that identify structural overextension.
  10. Explain why the succession war of 1657–1658 arose from Mughal political structures rather than from a simple religious division among the princes.
  11. Connect the developments of 1605–1658 to the questions examined in Chapters 54 through 57.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How did an empire that reached new heights of wealth, prestige, and architectural grandeur remain vulnerable to court rivalry, regional resistance, military overextension, and disputes over succession?
  2. Why did Mughal custom, which recognized no fixed law of succession, make the transfer of power so dangerous, and what restrained violence in 1605 but not in 1658?
  3. What can an emperor's own memoir tell historians about government, and what can it not tell them?
  4. In what ways did royal women and the imperial household participate in political life, and how should historians evaluate evidence that was written largely by men?
  5. Why did Mughal armies succeed in some regions and meet stubborn resistance or costly failure in others?
  6. How did painting, architecture, and ceremony help create and communicate imperial authority?
  7. How should historians distinguish contemporary evidence about monuments such as the Taj Mahal from later legends?
  8. Was Shah Jahan's reign the culmination of Mughal imperial power or the beginning of structural strain, and what evidence bears on the question?

Historical Context

The empire that Akbar bequeathed in 1605 stretched from Kabul and Kashmir in the northwest to Bengal in the east and from the Himalayan foothills to the northern Deccan, where the Mughal grip was still incomplete. It was governed through the system examined in Chapters 51 and 52: nobles holding numerical ranks (mansabs) that fixed their status, pay, and military obligations; assignments of revenue (jagirs) that supported them; provincial governors and revenue officers who administered the provinces (subas); and a court culture in which loyalty to the emperor was expressed through ritual, service, and gift exchange. Akbar had also cultivated the nobility's diversity, incorporating Rajput lords, Persian and Central Asian immigrants, and Indian Muslims into a single imperial elite.

The empire's economy rested on agriculture, whose surplus the state claimed through land revenue, and on a vigorous commerce in textiles, indigo, saltpetre, and other goods, linked by overland and maritime routes to Iran, Central Asia, the Arabian Sea, and increasingly Europe. Silver flowing into South Asia from the Americas and elsewhere supported a heavily monetized economy. Chapter 55 treats these structures in detail; for the present chapter it is sufficient to note that the revenue assigned to imperial nobles and the wealth of the court rested on the productivity of the countryside and the pace of commerce, and that famine, war, or reductions in revenue could therefore have political consequences.

Geography shaped Mughal strategy. The northwestern frontier ran through Kabul and Kandahar, a corridor that connected Hindustan to Iran and Central Asia and that supplied the war horses on which Mughal cavalry depended. Kandahar lay on a contested border with the Safavid empire of Iran, while the Hindu Kush and the cities of Balkh and Badakhshi lay beyond it, in the lands the Mughal dynasty remembered as the ancestral Timurid heartland. In the south, the Deccan Plateau offered a different challenge: a land of fortified hill-forts, monsoon-dependent campaigning seasons, and a set of regional sultanates, Ahmadnagar, Bijapur, and Golconda, whose rulers were as capable of making and breaking alliances with the Mughals as of resisting them. In the east, Bengal's riverine landscape and the pacification of its chiefs required a different mode of warfare again.

The ruling dynasty's own traditions also mattered. The Mughals descended from Timur and, through Babur's mother, from Chinggis Khan, and they inherited a Central Asian conception of royal authority in which sovereignty belonged to the whole ruling family rather than passing automatically to an eldest son. The empire's founders, Babur and Humayun, had experienced this at first hand in struggles among brothers. By Akbar's time the dynasty had grown wealthy and institutionally complex without having adopted any new rule of succession. This is the context in which Jahangir's accession, and the succession crises that followed, must be understood.

Jahangir's Accession, Government, and the Imperial Household

This division establishes the political character of Jahangir's reign and shows how imperial government depended on the emperor, powerful nobles, princes, and the royal household.

Section 53.1

Jahangir's Accession and the Inheritance of Akbar's Empire, 1605

Prince Salim, the future Jahangir, was born in 1569, the long-awaited son of Akbar, whose earlier sons had not survived infancy. His birth was associated in court tradition with the blessing of the Chishti saint Shaikh Salim of Sikri, after whom he was named. As the eldest surviving son he was educated with care, given a high rank, and married into leading families, including that of the Kachhwaha rulers of Amber. His relationship with his father, however, deteriorated in the closing years of Akbar's reign. Akbar spent long periods in the Deccan, leaving Salim restless and, in the view of the court, impatient for authority. Around 1599–1600 the prince established an independent court at Allahabad, issued his own appointments and even coins, and in 1602 became associated with the murder of Abu'l-Fazl, Akbar's closest intellectual companion and chronicler, who was killed on the road by Bir Singh Bundela, a Bundela chief who later received favor from Salim. Reconciliation came in 1603–1604, partly through the mediation of the senior women of the household, but the affair left a lasting mark on the court.

By 1605 Akbar's other adult sons, Murad and Daniyal, had died, both reportedly through heavy drinking. This left two serious candidates: Salim, the eldest son, and his own eldest son, Khusrau, born in 1587. Khusrau, then about eighteen, was the child of a Rajput mother, the sister of Raja Man Singh of Amber, one of Akbar's most senior commanders. Man Singh, together with Khusrau's father-in-law, Mirza Aziz Koka, another powerful noble, reportedly favored Khusrau as a candidate who might avoid the tensions between Akbar and Salim. Against them stood nobles including the Barha Sayyids and Shaikh Farid Bukhari (Murtaza Khan), who supported Salim and who controlled important military and administrative positions at Agra.

As Akbar lay ill in October 1605, these rivalries came to a head. According to Jahangir's own account, Akbar on his deathbed acknowledged Salim as his successor by a gesture of investiture. The reliability of this tradition cannot be judged independently, since it comes from the beneficiary of the outcome. Some later accounts add that conservative nobles pressed Salim for pledges to defend Islam as the price of support, but the evidence for such conditions is thin. Akbar died in late October 1605, and Salim was enthroned at Agra within days, taking the title Nur-ud-din Muhammad Jahangir Padshah Ghazi: "World-Seizer," as the regnal name Jahangir was understood. Coins and proclamations announced the new reign.

Why did the succession not become an immediate full-scale civil war, as the events of 1658 would? Several factors worked together. Salim was in physical control of the capital; his supporters held the Agra fort and its treasury, which in Mughal practice was a decisive advantage. Khusrau was young, and his chief backers were divided and, in Man Singh's case, ready to compromise once Salim's position seemed secure. Jahangir, for his part, moved quickly to reassure potential opponents. He confirmed Man Singh's position, forgave Mirza Aziz Koka, and kept Khusrau at court under supervision. He also promulgated a set of regulations, summarized in his memoir as twelve edicts, that included the abolition of certain taxes, measures to secure roads and protect the property of deceased persons, and restrictions on practices such as the sale of alcohol and the mutilation of offenders. Whether or not every edict was carried out, the package presented the new ruler as a careful and just sovereign.

Continuity was a central feature of the transition. Akbar's officers, with few exceptions, retained their ranks and often received increases; the administrative machinery of provincial governors, diwans, and judicial officers continued to function; and policies on matters such as the treatment of non-Muslim communities were not reversed. This continuity partly reflected the difficulty of governing a large empire through a change of ruler; it also reflected Jahangir's own interests, since the nobles whom Akbar had appointed were his instruments of rule. But it did not mean that the accession settled the political tensions behind it. Khusrau remained a living rival, and his supporters among the nobility had not been fully reconciled. Within months, the question would again be put to the test.

Section 53.2

The Rebellion of Prince Khusrau and the Crisis of 1606

In April 1606, only months into the reign, Khusrau left Agra at night with a small party, on the pretext of visiting the tomb of Akbar at Sikandra. He rode northwest toward Punjab, collecting followers as he went. His supporters included nobles who had favored his claim, soldiers drawn by the prospect of rewards, and local horsemen who joined the prince's column along the road through Mathura and Panipat. The numbers, which the sources report in the thousands, grew considerably before his force reached Lahore and attempted to take the city, whose governor, Dilawar Khan, held the fort. Khusrau's commanders included Hussain Beg Badakhshi, an experienced officer of Central Asian origin.

Jahangir responded personally. Leaving Agra, he advanced in pursuit with the main army and with senior nobles, while Khusrau failed to take Lahore and withdrew. The decisive encounter occurred near Bhairowal on the Beas river, where Khusrau's forces were defeated. The prince himself attempted to cross the Chenab and was captured. He was brought before his father in humiliating circumstances. Several of his chief supporters were executed, some in public, and Khusrau himself was imprisoned and, according to the sources, subjected to blinding or damage to his sight. He remained in custody for years, was transferred to the care of Asaf Khan and eventually to Prince Khurram, and died in 1622 at Burhanpur, officially of illness, though many contemporaries suspected that he had been killed with Khurram's knowledge. The truth cannot be established from the surviving evidence.

Khusrau's revolt matters for several reasons. It demonstrated that princely rebellion was not an aberration but a recurrent feature of Mughal politics, and Jahangir, who had himself defied his father, was acutely aware of the precedent. It showed the importance of nobles' choices: Khusrau failed in part because the great majority of senior officers remained with Jahangir. And it revealed the severity with which the emperor was prepared to punish challengers, a severity that would be echoed by his successors. Provincial elites also made calculations: local chiefs and officials who had supported Khusrau faced punishment, while those who aided the imperial pursuit were rewarded.

The crisis of 1606 also affected a religious community outside the court. Guru Arjan, the fifth Guru of the Sikhs, had expanded the community's institutions at Amritsar, compiled the Adi Granth in 1604, and gathered considerable influence and revenue. Jahangir's memoir states that as Khusrau passed through Punjab, the Guru met him and applied a mark to his forehead, an act the emperor regarded as support for a rebel. Jahangir ordered the Guru's arrest, the confiscation of his property, and punishment, and the Guru died in Mughal custody at Lahore in 1606 following Jahangir’s order for his execution.

Interpretations of these events differ and must be distinguished. Jahangir's account presents the matter as a political offense and mentions, with evident hostility, the Guru's influence among Hindus and many Muslims. Sikh tradition, recorded in later generations, describes the Guru's torture and execution and often involves other figures at the Mughal court, including a Lahore official called Chandu Shah, who is assigned a leading role in the Guru's persecution; the contemporary Mughal sources do not mention him. Historians generally agree that Arjan died in Mughal custody as a result of an imperial order connected with the Khusrau affair, but disagree about the extent to which religious hostility, fiscal interest, or a wish to restrain a growing institution contributed. The consequences are clearer. The event deepened Sikh anxieties about Mughal power and contributed to the military and institutional development of the community under Guru Hargobind. Later Mughal–Sikh relations are examined in Chapter 54 and the emergence of eighteenth-century Sikh military power in Chapter 59. It would be a mistake, however, to read the whole later history of Mughal–Sikh relations backward from 1606; the relationship fluctuated considerably in the decades that followed.

Section 53.3

Jahangir's Court, Justice, and the Practice of Imperial Government

Jahangir governed from a series of capitals, including Agra, Lahore, and Fatehpur Sikri, and spent years in motion with the imperial camp, including long residences at Ajmer (1613–1616) and visits to Kashmir, whose gardens and climate he loved. The emperor's day followed a structured routine. He appeared at a window (jharokha) to be seen by the public, held public audiences in the hall of the Diwan-i Am, received senior nobles in more private gatherings, and spent the evening in conversation, reading, and in the sessions of drinking and opium use that he does not conceal in his memoir. Contemporary and later observers noticed his dependence on these substances, which became more severe over time and affected his health.

It would be a mistake, however, to conclude that Jahangir was merely a passive ruler. His memoir shows an emperor who personally approved appointments, reviewed promotions, inquired about provincial affairs, and intervened in matters of punishment and reward. He remembered nobles' families and records, and his comments on individuals' qualities show considerable personal knowledge. Government in this period was highly personal, and imperial attention was itself a resource that nobles competed to attract.

Jahangir cultivated the image of a ruler who dispensed justice. Persian political tradition, which the Mughals inherited, regarded justice as the principal duty of a king, and the story of the legendary Sasanian ruler Anushirvan, who was said to be accessible to the oppressed, provided an ideal. The Jahangirnama describes how, at his accession, Jahangir ordered a chain of gold to be hung from the Agra fort to a post by the river, with bells attached, so that anyone whose petition had been neglected could shake it and bring the matter directly to the emperor. Whether it was often used, we cannot know. The surviving record does not document petitioners ringing the bells, and the chain functioned above all as a symbol, one that appears repeatedly in court painting. Real access to imperial justice ran through a network of officials: qazis in the cities, provincial diwans and governors, and petitions presented at court. Distance from the capital, wealth, and connections strongly shaped who could obtain a hearing.

The administrative system itself was substantially that of Akbar. Provinces (subas) were held by governors (subahdars), who commanded military forces and were responsible for order, while the provincial diwan, appointed from the center, supervised revenue. The wazir presided over the empire's finances, and orders issued by him on the emperor's authority, known as hasb-ul-hukm documents, conveyed imperial decisions to officials. Appointments and transfers circulated nobles around the empire, preventing the entrenchment of local power but also generating ceaseless competition for the most valuable assignments. In Bengal, Jahangir's governor Islam Khan Chishti moved the provincial capital to Dhaka around 1610, renaming it Jahangirnagar, and directed campaigns against the zamindars and chiefs of the delta. The memoir of one of his officers, Mirza Nathan's Baharistan-i Ghaybi, records the grueling warfare in the region's rivers and marshes, a reminder of how much of imperial authority was won through local campaigns that left little trace in the court chronicles.

The Jahangirnama is the key source for these themes and must be read with care. Jahangir wrote his memoir in Persian through the seventeenth regnal year, having circulated a separate manuscript covering the first twelve years. As his health declined, the historian Mu'tamad Khan continued the account into the nineteenth regnal year; Muhammad Hadi later supplied a further continuation based largely on other court histories. It is candid about the emperor's tastes, curiosity, and temper, but it is also a work of self-presentation. It treats the Khusrau affair and the punishment of enemies from the emperor's perspective, silently omits embarrassments, and describes the practice of justice in terms of the ideals Jahangir wished to project.

Historical Vocabulary
Darbar and Jharokha

The darbar (Persian, "court") was both the assembly of the emperor and his nobles and the institution of royal presence through which rank and favor were visibly confirmed. Nobles were arranged according to precedence, performed prescribed gestures of obeisance, and presented gifts. The jharokha, a balcony or window of appearance, allowed the emperor to be seen daily by a gathered public, an observance (jharokha darshan) that Akbar had adopted and that his successors maintained. Its significance is political: it made the emperor visible, offered a channel for petitions, and supported the claim that the ruler was accessible to his subjects. The modern word "durbar" is a later colonial-era spelling of the same Persian term, and the Mughal institution should not be confused with colonial ceremonies that borrowed its name.

Primary Source Window

The Jahangirnama: Regulations and the Chain of Justice

Source type: Imperial memoir in Persian · Author: Emperor Jahangir, continued by Mu'tamad Khan · Date: Jahangir’s own narrative through his seventeenth regnal year, with subsequent continuations

Context: The opening regnal-year account survives in the Tuzuk-i Jahangiri (also called Jahangirnama), for example in Alexander Rogers’s translation edited by Henry Beveridge (1909), volume I, opening pages, including the Chain of Justice and Twelve Decrees. The work was copied and illustrated for the court and circulated among nobles and foreign envoys.

Editorial paraphrase (not a direct quotation): Jahangir reports that, after taking the throne, he issued a series of regulations, abolishing certain duties and cesses, forbidding officials to seize the property of deceased persons, establishing hospitals and securing the roads, and prohibiting the sale of liquor. He then describes the gold chain he ordered to be hung so that anyone seeking justice could appeal directly to him.

Analysis: The passage is a statement of royal intent written by the king himself. It tells historians what policies the emperor wished to be associated with and how he wished to be seen. It cannot tell us how consistently the measures were enforced, whether petitioners actually used the chain, or how provincial officials responded. Corroboration must come from other sources, including documents, European observers, and the record of local administration. Those who sought justice and failed, and those whose property was seized despite the regulations, are absent.

Section 53.4

Nur Jahan: Marriage, Political Influence, and the Imperial Household

Mehr-un-Nisa was born around 1577 to Mirza Ghiyas Beg, a Persian who, having fallen on hard times in Iran, traveled to Hindustan and entered Akbar's service, rising to hold the post of diwan in Kabul. In 1594 she was married to Sher Afgan, an officer of Persian background who received a jagir in Bengal. Sher Afgan died in 1607 in a confrontation with the provincial governor, Qutb-ud-din Koka, and Mehr-un-Nisa came to the imperial court as a widow, where she served in the household of one of Akbar's widows. In May 1611 she married Jahangir. Later tradition, with great narrative appeal, claimed that the emperor had fallen in love with her years earlier and had Sher Afgan killed to obtain her. Contemporary sources, including Jahangir's own memoir, give no support for this story, which developed in later accounts.

She was first titled Nur Mahal ("Light of the Palace") and in 1616 Nur Jahan ("Light of the World"). Her family rose with her. Her father received the title Itimad-ud-Daula ("Pillar of the State") and served as chief minister until his death in January 1622. Her brother Asaf Khan became one of the leading nobles of the empire; his daughter Arjumand Banu, later Mumtaz Mahal, married Prince Khurram in 1612, linking the empress's family to the heir. Nur Jahan's daughter by her first marriage, Ladli Begum, married Prince Shahryar, Jahangir's youngest surviving son, in 1620. These alliances formed a network of family connections that spanned the throne, the senior ministry, and the princely households.

Nur Jahan's influence developed visibly. She received a mansab-equivalent income and large assignments of revenue, owned property, and participated in trade; she sponsored the construction of caravanserais and gardens, including the Nur Afshan garden at Agra and the tomb of her father, whose delicate marble inlay would influence later Mughal architecture. She accompanied the emperor on hunts, and sources record her skill with the gun. As Jahangir's health declined, she took a growing part in managing the daily business of the court. Farmans bearing her seal were issued, coins were struck bearing her name together with the emperor's, and she made recommendations for appointments. Such activity was extraordinary in degree but not wholly without precedent: senior women of the Mughal household, such as Akbar's foster mother Maham Anga, Hamida Banu Begum, and Mariam-uz-Zamani, who was engaged in maritime trade, had long possessed property, issued orders, and influenced politics. The imperial household was itself a political institution.

Nur Jahan's alliance with Prince Khurram was close in the early years, but it broke down around 1621–1622, as questions of succession and the emperor's failing health sharpened. She increasingly favored Shahryar, her son-in-law, as the likely heir, while Asaf Khan remained loyal to his own son-in-law Khurram. This split in her family illustrates that the idea of a monolithic "faction" of the empress is misleading; the interests of her relatives were not identical. After Jahangir's death, Nur Jahan was placed under a pension by Shah Jahan, withdrew to Lahore, where she lived quietly until her death in 1645, and was buried near her husband's tomb at Shahdara.

Treating Nur Jahan as a historical actor requires cautious handling of the evidence. Much of what is said about her comes from authors who were hostile, or from European observers who misunderstood household politics, or from later writers who inherited the tradition of the scheming empress and the incompetent emperor. Her own voice appears primarily in material remains: seals, coins, farmans, and buildings, along with a few poems attributed to her. Modern historians, including Ellison Findly and Ruby Lal, have emphasized the way in which she exercised power within and through the established forms of Mughal household politics rather than overturning them.

Historical Vocabulary
Mahal, Pardah, and the Imperial Household

The mahal (or haram, in the language of the sources) was the imperial household: the emperor's wives, mothers, daughters, sisters, and their servants, officers, and dependents. Pardah refers to practices of seclusion that limited women's visibility in public settings. In the Mughal context, pardah did not remove elite women from political life; secluded women received property and income, corresponded, commissioned buildings, conducted trade through agents, and negotiated between rivals. The term "harem," as used by many European travelers, often conveyed an image of idleness and sensuality that distorted a complex institution that was also a center of administration and patronage.

Division II · Sections 53.5–53.8 · 1605–1628

Jahangir's Empire: Military Campaigns, Court Politics, and Cultural Patronage

This division explains how Jahangir's reign combined administrative continuity, military challenges, artistic innovation, and increasingly dangerous competition among imperial princes.

Section 53.5

Rajput Relations, the Deccan, and the Limits of Mughal Expansion

Among the Rajput kingdoms, Mewar had long stood apart. Its rulers, the Sisodiyas, had not accepted the service Akbar demanded, and Rana Pratap's resistance after the battle of Haldighati in 1576 had become central to Rajput memory. Jahangir revived campaigns against Rana Amar Singh, Pratap's son, in the first years of his reign. After several indecisive expeditions, the emperor placed Prince Khurram in command in 1613. Khurram's campaign was a war of attrition, targeting the countryside and the Rana's supply, and by early 1615 Amar Singh accepted terms.

The settlement was carefully balanced. Amar Singh acknowledged Mughal suzerainty but was not required to attend the imperial court in person; his son Karan Singh went in his place and received a high mansab. Chittorgarh and the Rana's territories were restored, though the fortress was not to be rebuilt. The Rana was not compelled to send a daughter into the imperial household, a practice that other Rajput houses had accepted. The terms allowed the Sisodiyas to preserve their claim to special dignity while entering Mughal service, and they fit a pattern in which Rajput lords, including the Kachhwahas of Amber, the Rathors of Marwar, the Hadas of Bundi, and the Bundelas, served the empire as commanders and governors while retaining their local rule. Rajput service was not uniform: the terms differed by house, by moment, and by the individual relationship with the emperor, and the Rajput presence in the nobility depended on continuing negotiation.

The Deccan presented harder problems. Akbar had taken Ahmadnagar's fort in 1600, but the Nizam Shahi state had not ended. Under the regency of Malik Ambar, an Ethiopian-born military leader who had been enslaved in his youth and risen through service in the Deccan, the Nizam Shahi regime reconstituted itself with a new capital and organized resistance. Malik Ambar is remembered as a gifted organizer: he built an army that combined heavy Deccani cavalry with light mounted raiders who harassed Mughal supply lines, and he is credited with a revenue arrangement that supported his state's finances. His success rested on geography as much as on tactics. The Deccan's terrain, broken by hills, forts, and rivers, favored defenders who knew the country, while Mughal armies depended on long supply lines, the seasons of the monsoon, and the cooperation of local chiefs.

Mughal campaigns in the Deccan in the first two decades of the reign had mixed results. Armies advanced and were forced back. Prince Khurram's expedition in 1616–1617, which obtained the submission of Bijapur and the surrender of territory by Ahmadnagar, was celebrated as a triumph: Jahangir conferred on him the title Shah Jahan, "King of the World," and a rank of unprecedented height. But the gains were fragile, and in the early 1620s Malik Ambar recovered much of what had been lost. He died in 1626, at an advanced age, still in command of a state that had never been fully subdued. His successors were less capable, and the Mughal conquest of Ahmadnagar was completed only under Shah Jahan.

The meaning of Malik Ambar's resistance was contested even at Jahangir's court. A famous painting by Abu'l-Hasan, now in the Freer Gallery of Art, depicts Jahangir shooting an arrow into the severed head of a figure labeled Malik Ambar. The scene is allegorical: Jahangir never defeated his adversary in battle. The image offers a visual equivalent of what the campaigns had failed to accomplish and illustrates how art could compensate for the limits of military power. The Deccan frontier, which Chapter 58 treats from the perspective of the Maratha chiefs who emerged from it, thus emerges in this reign as a region that Mughal arms could cross but that Mughal administration struggled to hold.

Section 53.6

Imperial Diplomacy, Kandahar, and Foreign Commercial Contacts

Kandahar was the most important point of friction between the Mughal and Safavid empires. Akbar had taken it from Safavid control in 1595, and the city, which commanded the road between Iran and the Indus plain and stood at the head of the horse trade from Central Asia, remained a prize that Iranian rulers wished to recover. Relations between the two courts were nonetheless cordial in form: embassies, gifts of horses and textiles, and elaborate courtesies marked the diplomacy, and Jahangir dispatched the noble Khan Alam as ambassador to Shah Abbas I in 1613. The painter Bishndas, who accompanied the embassy, was instructed to produce portraits of the Safavid ruler and his court.

In 1622 Shah Abbas besieged Kandahar and captured it. The Mughal response failed in part because the court was distracted. Jahangir had ordered Prince Khurram, the empire's most successful commander, to lead a relief expedition, but Khurram, wary of leaving the court and of the growing hostility of Nur Jahan's circle, refused to go and rebelled instead (see §53.8). The loss of Kandahar was thus a consequence of political division as much as of Safavid power, and it deprived the empire of a symbolically and strategically valuable position. It also shows that the northwestern frontier and the politics of the court were closely linked: a reverse on the frontier could result from, and intensify, rivalries at the center. Relations with Central Asia, where the Ashtarkhanid rulers of Bukhara and Balkh controlled the lands the Mughal dynasty remembered as its ancestral homeland, were conducted through embassies and exchanges of gifts, and the Mughal court did not seek major campaigns beyond the Hindu Kush during this reign.

The court also received European merchants and diplomats. Portuguese Jesuit missions were a feature of Akbar's reign, and Jahangir continued to receive Jesuits, whose European prints and paintings influenced court artists. The English East India Company, founded in 1600, sought commercial privileges at Surat, the principal port of western India. Its first envoy, William Hawkins, arrived in 1609 and remained at court for a time, but failed to obtain what the Company wanted, in part because of Portuguese opposition. In 1615 the English crown sent Sir Thomas Roe as ambassador. Roe arrived at Surat in September 1615 and met Jahangir at Ajmer in January 1616, remaining at the court for about three years. His journal is a major source for court ceremonial and for the way foreign merchants were regarded.

Roe was frustrated. He sought a formal treaty guaranteeing English trading rights, and the emperor was unwilling to enter into a treaty as between equals. Jahangir issued farmans permitting trade, but treated the English as one group of merchants among many. The scale of the English trade also needs to be kept in proportion. The Company's ships brought silver, cloth, and some European goods and exported indigo, cotton textiles, and other commodities, but the commerce of Gujarat and the Arabian Sea was dominated by Indian, Arab, and Persian merchants and shipowners, and the Mughal economy of the early seventeenth century was only marginally affected by English trade. Roe's depiction of himself as a decisive player in the court's policy is the perspective of an ambassador who believed his own importance; Mughal sources hardly mention him. Competition among Europeans, notably Portuguese attempts to control Indian Ocean shipping and the arrival of the Dutch, was nonetheless significant for Indian ports, a subject examined in Chapter 61.

Section 53.7

Mughal Painting, Natural History, and Jahangir's Intellectual Interests

Jahangir's reign is among the most important periods in the history of Mughal painting. Trained in the imperial atelier established by his father, the emperor had formed strong views about art and took an active part in directing his painters. His memoir records his claim that he could recognize the hand of individual artists, even in a work to which several had contributed, naming, for instance, which painter had executed the faces and which the eyes. Whether or not the boast was literally true, it illustrates the emperor's self-presentation as a connoisseur, and the cultivation of connoisseurship was itself a form of royal authority.

Two features distinguish the painting of the reign. The first is portraiture. Court artists produced individual likenesses of nobles, scholars, and holy men, of the emperor himself, and of foreign rulers, with an attention to personality and physical detail that was new in the Mughal tradition. Allegorical portraits placed the emperor within symbolic settings: Bichitr's well-known painting of Jahangir preferring a Sufi shaikh to kings, for example, shows the emperor seated on an hourglass throne and turning away from rulers, including the Ottoman sultan and the English king, to offer a book to a holy man. Abu'l-Hasan's image of Jahangir embracing Shah Abbas of Iran imagines a relationship of equality, or in fact the emperor's superiority, that diplomacy did not produce.

The second is natural history. Jahangir had a long-standing curiosity about animals and plants, and his memoir records careful descriptions of species, the gift of rare animals, and observations of their behavior and anatomy. He commissioned painters to record unusual specimens. Ustad Mansur, whom Jahangir honored with the title "Wonder of the Age" (Nadir-ul-Asr), produced studies of birds, animals, and flowers, such as the famous painting of a turkey cock, whose species had recently been introduced to India through Goa, and the study of a hornbill in the Metropolitan Museum of Art, which carries an inscription attributing it to imperial patronage. Abu'l-Hasan, titled "Wonder of the Time" (Nadir-uz-Zaman), painted subjects including squirrels on a plane tree with a precision that suggests direct observation. These images reflect a convergence of interests: Persian traditions of painting, an Indian tradition of animal and plant imagery, the arrival of European prints through missionaries and merchants, and the emperor's own curiosity.

Paintings were collected in albums (muraqqa), in which pictures were paired with calligraphy and framed by decorated borders, and which served as a kind of personal and dynastic museum. Painters of this period included Govardhan, Manohar, and Bichitr, many of them from families of artists trained in imperial workshops, whose individual identities are better known than in earlier periods. Jahangir's cultural interests extended beyond painting to gardens, particularly in Kashmir, where the Shalimar Bagh was laid out in 1619, and to the company of religious figures, whom he visited at shrines such as Ajmer and, with varying degrees of sympathy, at Hindu pilgrimage centers. His attitude to religious authority was not uniform: he honored some holy men, and imprisoned the Naqshbandi reformer Shaikh Ahmad Sirhindi at Gwalior for a time, as well as, in different circumstances, the Sikh Guru Hargobind.

Court paintings must be used with care. They are representations produced for the emperor, and the allegorical scenes do not record events but express claims about power, as the image of Malik Ambar's head makes plain. Natural-history studies, by contrast, seem to reflect genuine observation, but they also formed part of a program of imperial self-presentation, a ruler who could collect, examine, and command knowledge of the world. The full history of Mughal painting belongs to Chapter 56; here it is considered as part of how the court represented itself.

Section 53.8

Court Factions, Mahabat Khan, Prince Khurram, and the Succession of 1627–1628

Prince Khurram, born in 1592, grew to prominence through military success. His campaigns against Mewar and in the Deccan, the title Shah Jahan, and his marriage to Mumtaz Mahal made him by around 1620 the most likely heir. His elder brother Khusrau was in confinement; Parviz, a prince of limited ability and strong drinking habits, held commands in the Deccan; and Shahryar, the youngest, was favored by Nur Jahan after his marriage to her daughter in 1620.

The break came in 1621–1622. Jahangir, whose health was now fragile, summoned Khurram to lead the expedition to recover Kandahar; Khurram, fearing that his absence would leave the court open to his rivals, delayed and made demands, and the emperor sent Shahryar instead. By then the fall of Kandahar to the Safavids was imminent, and in 1622 it fell. Khurram rebelled. He marched north from the Deccan but was defeated near Delhi at Balochpur in 1623, and for the next two years moved through the Deccan, Orissa, and Bengal, collecting what forces he could but unable to establish a base. In 1625 he submitted, sending two of his sons, Dara Shukoh and Aurangzeb, to the imperial court as hostages for his good conduct. The events shaped both princes' early lives, for they spent months in the household of Nur Jahan, a fact that later became part of the tangled memory of the family.

Meanwhile the empress's circle clashed with Mahabat Khan, the most distinguished commander in the empire, who had commanded imperial forces against Khurram in 1623–1625 and whom Nur Jahan's circle now wished to curb. Summoned to account for his conduct and finances, and perceiving humiliation, Mahabat Khan resolved to act. In March 1626, as the imperial camp crossed the Jhelum on the road to Kabul, he surrounded the emperor with his Rajput troops and took him into custody. Nur Jahan, who had been on the far side, joined in an unsuccessful attempt to rescue Jahangir, reportedly directing a counterattack from an elephant, and then joined her husband in captivity. For some months the court traveled under Mahabat Khan's control. Nur Jahan's diplomacy, along with the coup leader's inability to command broader support, enabled the emperor to regain freedom, and Mahabat Khan withdrew and eventually allied himself with Khurram. The episode revealed how personal Mughal authority was: control of the emperor's person was nearly equivalent to control of the state, yet the coup failed because no significant group of nobles accepted it.

Jahangir died on 28 October 1627, on the road from Kashmir to Lahore, aged about fifty-eight. What followed was a brief and decisive struggle. Shahryar, at Lahore, proclaimed himself emperor, seized the treasury, and distributed money to his supporters. Khurram was far away in the Deccan. Asaf Khan, Khurram's father-in-law and the leading noble at the court, acted decisively: he supported the elevation of Dawar Bakhsh, Khusrau's son, as a temporary nominal ruler, to keep the throne from Shahryar until Khurram could arrive, and sent a message to Khurram urging him to come with speed. Asaf Khan's forces defeated Shahryar's, who was captured and blinded. Khurram reached Agra in January 1628 and was crowned in the following weeks as Shah Jahan. In the aftermath, Dawar Bakhsh, Shahryar, and the sons of Prince Daniyal, who might serve as rallying points for opposition, were put to death.

It would oversimplify the sequence to describe it as a contest between Nur Jahan and Shah Jahan. Nur Jahan supported Shahryar, but her own brother supported Khurram; the coup of Mahabat Khan was directed against her faction and ended with him joining Khurram; and many nobles shaped the outcome by shifting allegiance in line with their own prospects. The succession was determined by control of the center, by the choices of leading commanders, and by the readiness of the winning side to eliminate rivals.

Historical Debate

Was Nur Jahan's Power an Unusual Concentration of Authority or an Example of Established Household Politics?

The question: Did Nur Jahan's prominence in the later years of Jahangir's reign represent an unprecedented concentration of power in the hands of a woman and her family, or was it a visible instance of a political role that elite Mughal women, and royal households generally, had long exercised?

Interpretation A — A distinctive concentration of power: Older scholarship, influential in the early twentieth century, described a "junta" of Nur Jahan, her father, her brother Asaf Khan, and Prince Khurram that effectively governed while Jahangir's attention wandered. On this view, Nur Jahan's seal, coinage, and appointments demonstrated an unusual and, to some observers, destabilizing political dominance, and her feuds with Khurram and Mahabat Khan helped to produce the instability of the 1620s.
Interpretation B — An expression of established household politics: More recent historians argue that this portrait inherits the prejudices of hostile chroniclers and European observers. They stress that Mughal women of rank had long held property, issued orders, and brokered political arrangements; that Nur Jahan's actions, including patronage, building, and trade, resemble those of earlier royal women, though conducted on a larger scale; and that the family alliances of the period were typical of how nobles built alliances through marriage. Her prominence reflects the emperor's declining health and her own abilities more than an extraordinary breach of norms.

Evidence: Both views depend on the same body of evidence: the Jahangirnama and other chronicles, European accounts, farmans, coins, and buildings. The debate concerns how to weigh sources written by men with interests in the factions, and how to interpret the symbolic significance of her seal and name on coinage.

Why it matters: The question affects how historians understand the structure of Mughal politics. If Nur Jahan's power was exceptional, it was a personal aberration; if it was an instance of the household's normal role, political history must treat women and the household as part of the machinery of government.

Division III · Sections 53.9–53.12 · 1628–1657

Shah Jahan's Government, Military Ambitions, and Imperial Power

This division shows that Shah Jahan governed an extraordinarily powerful empire whose ambitions nevertheless exceeded its capacity in important frontier regions.

Section 53.9

Shah Jahan's Accession and the Consolidation of Dynastic Authority

Shah Jahan was crowned at Agra in early 1628, taking the title Shihab-ud-din Muhammad Sahib-i Qiran-i Sani, "Second Lord of the Auspicious Conjunction," a deliberate echo of Timur. He was thirty-six, experienced in war and administration, and his accession followed the elimination of rival claimants. Asaf Khan, whose swift action had secured the throne, was appointed chief minister (wazir) with the title Yamin-ud-Daula and became one of the wealthiest men in the empire until his death in 1641. Mahabat Khan, who had joined Khurram after the coup, was restored to favor and given high commands, including the position of Khan-i Khanan. Nur Jahan was provided with a pension and withdrew from politics. The reshuffling of ranks, jagirs, and offices allowed the new emperor to reward supporters and reshape the nobility around his own network.

The new reign was soon tested. Khan Jahan Lodi, an Afghan noble who had governed the Deccan, grew uneasy about the new regime, fled with his followers toward Ahmadnagar, and was pursued for more than a year before being killed in 1631 near Kalinjar. Jujhar Singh Bundela of Orchha rebelled in 1628 and again in 1635; the second revolt was crushed, and Jujhar Singh was killed by Gond tribesmen in flight. In 1632 Mughal forces besieged and captured the Portuguese settlement at Hughli in Bengal, an action that curbed Portuguese influence in the region. In the east, Mughal pressure along the borders of Assam and Cooch Behar culminated in a war with the Ahom kingdom (1636–1639) that ended with a negotiated boundary. Each campaign extended or secured imperial authority and offered Shah Jahan occasions to display discipline and to reward loyal nobles.

Shah Jahan's religious policy has been the subject of debate. He retained many of Akbar's institutions and employed Hindu nobles, Rajput commanders, and officials in large numbers. Yet the official history of the reign records measures that signal a more conservative tone than Akbar's: orders in 1632 to halt the construction of newly begun Hindu temples at Banaras and elsewhere, modification of ritual forms of obeisance at court that some regarded as incompatible with Islamic practice, and displays of piety connected to his Sunni identity. The measures were limited and uneven; they did not amount to a systematic change of policy, and the extent to which they were enforced is uncertain. Historians generally describe Shah Jahan's approach as a pragmatic shift in emphasis within the inherited framework rather than a break with it.

The reign's official history, the Padshahnama, was first entrusted to Muhammad Amin Qazvini and then completed in successive volumes by Abdul Hamid Lahori and later Muhammad Waris. Its authors presented the emperor as a model of just, pious, and magnificent rule. The text and the surviving illustrated first-decade manuscript (Royal Collection Trust, RCIN 1005025, copied in 1656–1657) supply detail about court events but must be read as authorized imperial representations. The Windsor copy covers the first decade, not the complete reign, and its illustrations are neither contemporaneous eyewitness drawings nor neutral records.

Historical Vocabulary
Padshah and Sahib-i Qiran

Padshah (Persian, "emperor" or "great king") was the standard title of the Mughal ruler. Sahib-i Qiran, "Lord of the Auspicious Conjunction," originally referred to rulers born under a fortunate alignment of planets and was associated in particular with Timur, who used it as a mark of world-conquering destiny. Shah Jahan adopted the title Sahib-i Qiran-i Sani ("the second") to link himself to his Timurid ancestor. The title expresses an ambition to reclaim the dynasty's Central Asian legacy and helps to explain why Shah Jahan later committed resources to campaigns beyond the Hindu Kush (§53.12).

Section 53.10

The Imperial Court, Royal Household, and Government under Shah Jahan

Court ceremony under Shah Jahan became more formal and more lavish. Daily routines followed the pattern of earlier reigns: the emperor appeared at the jharokha, held public audience in the Diwan-i Am, and received senior nobles in the more private Diwan-i Khas. The great festivals, including Nauruz, the Persian new year, and the emperor's solar and lunar birthdays, when he was weighed against gold, silver, and other goods that were then distributed as charity, were celebrated with great expense. Forms of obeisance were regulated, and nobles' positions in the assembly were fixed by rank. The emperor's authority was expressed through spectacle.

The most famous object of this display was the Peacock Throne (Takht-i Taus), constructed over several years and unveiled by 1635. Described in court chronicles as encrusted with gems and costing an immense sum, the throne was a work of the court's goldsmiths and jewelers and expressed the wealth of the empire in concentrated form. It would be carried off by the Iranian ruler Nadir Shah in 1739, an event treated in Chapter 57.

Relations among nobles and princes changed. Shah Jahan kept his sons at court or granted them commands under close supervision, conferring extremely high ranks on his favorite, Dara Shukoh, who held a rank far above that normally given to a prince. The nobility, which had grown in size and in the number of Persian and Deccani members, depended on the emperor's favor for jagirs. One administrative adjustment of the period, the practice of making jagirs payable for fewer than twelve months of the year, widened the gap between a noble's nominal rank and the revenue he could actually count upon, with consequences for the relationship between nobles, their troops, and the peasantry, which Chapter 55 considers.

The imperial household remained politically significant. Mumtaz Mahal had been Shah Jahan's most trusted companion and held the imperial seal until her death in 1631. After her death, the emperor's eldest daughter, Jahanara Begum, born in 1614, assumed the position of the first lady of the empire, with the title Padshah Begum, control of the seal, large assignments of revenue, including income from the port of Surat, and extensive patronage. She commissioned buildings, including a major mosque at Agra and a garden-caravanserai in the new capital that gave rise to Chandni Chowk, and she was a devotee of the Qadiri Sufi order, maintaining ties to Sufi teachers. Her sister Roshanara Begum (born 1617) was also influential. In 1644 Jahanara was severely burned in an accident, and her recovery was celebrated by the court with elaborate charity. The princesses' influence in the succession struggle of 1657–1658 is discussed in §53.17.

Did the splendor reflect effective government? The Padshahnama's picture is of an emperor deeply involved in administration, who worked long hours and inspected the empire's affairs, and the revenue and military resources of the empire reached unprecedented levels. Nobles' rankings and the administrative machinery continued largely as under Akbar. Yet the cost of display, the demands of campaigns, and the pressure on the countryside were considerable. The great famine of 1630–1632 in Gujarat and the Deccan, described by Dutch and English merchants, including Peter Mundy, who traveled in the region, and recorded in the official chronicle, showed both the state's capacity for relief measures such as remissions of revenue and free kitchens, and the scale of suffering when harvests failed.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From Akbar's Court to Shah Jahan's

What Changed

  • Ceremony became more formal and more costly, with elaborate festivals and objects such as the Peacock Throne
  • The court's architecture shifted toward marble, symmetry, and monumental urban planning
  • Princes held very high ranks and commanded distant provinces, increasing the stakes of succession
  • Imperial religious tone became somewhat more conservative, though not systematically so

What Stayed the Same

  • The mansab-jagir system and the provincial administration established by Akbar
  • The inclusion of Rajput and Hindu nobles in the imperial elite
  • The absence of a fixed rule of succession
  • The importance of the imperial household in politics and patronage

Who experienced change most directly? Nobles, who faced greater costs of display and tighter competition for jagirs, and the princes, whose position grew more precarious as the stakes rose. Who experienced greater continuity? Villagers and artisans, for whom the practical demands of the state changed less visibly. Why the pattern matters: The reign shows how an institution can become more imposing while its underlying tensions remain unresolved.

Section 53.11

Shah Jahan's Deccan Policy and the Settlement of 1636

The Deccan remained the empire's most persistent military problem. After Malik Ambar's death in 1626, the Nizam Shahi state of Ahmadnagar lost its coherence, and Shah Jahan, who knew the region from his own campaigns, determined to end its independence. Mahabat Khan took command in the early 1630s, and in 1633 the great hill-fortress of Daulatabad fell after a siege. Fath Khan, Malik Ambar's son, surrendered, and the reigning Nizam Shahi ruler Husain Shah was imprisoned in the fortress of Gwalior; other claimants were subsequently raised in resistance. Mughal sovereignty over Ahmadnagar was thereby established.

The fall of Daulatabad and the capture of the reigning Nizam Shahi prince did not end resistance. Shahaji Bhonsle, a Maratha military leader who had served the Nizam Shahi state, installed a boy of the Nizam Shahi line as a nominal ruler and continued to resist, supported at times by Bijapur. The Mughals sent large forces, and in 1636 the emperor himself came south. Faced with this pressure, Bijapur and Golconda reached terms. The two settlements differed materially. Golconda agreed to recognize Mughal paramountcy publicly, including through the Friday khutba and coinage in Shah Jahan’s name, and accepted annual tribute. Bijapur recognized Mughal superior power and territorial arrangements, accepted a one-time indemnity rather than annual tribute, and received part of the former Nizam Shahi territory. Both sultanates retained their own rulers and substantial political autonomy. Shahaji, defeated, entered the service of Bijapur.

The settlement of 1636 should be understood as a diplomatic and military arrangement rather than annexation. It extended Mughal paramountcy while leaving two substantial states in place, a pattern consistent with the Mughal practice in Rajasthan of accepting subordination without direct rule. This distinction is important because it differs from the fate of the same two sultanates three or four decades later, when Aurangzeb annexed Bijapur in 1686 and Golconda in 1687, an outcome examined in Chapter 54.

Prince Aurangzeb governed the Deccan from 1636 to 1644, working to organize the administration of the newly won Mughal territories, and he returned as viceroy in 1652. During this second tenure he was severely strapped for revenue, since the Deccan provinces yielded less than their nominal assessments, and he asked for relief and reform, working with officers such as Murshid Quli Khan to introduce more systematic revenue measures. In 1656–1657, in conflict with the courts of Golconda and Bijapur and encouraged by his advisor Mir Jumla, a Golconda minister who had gone over to the Mughals, Aurangzeb launched attacks on both. Shah Jahan, influenced by Dara Shukoh and by the sultans' appeals, restrained him, and the campaign was halted short of conquest. The episode strained the relationship between father and son and fed the rivalry among the princes that would erupt in 1657.

Section 53.12

Central Asia, Kandahar, and the Limits of Mughal Military Ambition

In the northwest, Shah Jahan sought to recover what Jahangir had lost and to reassert the Timurid claim to Central Asia. The first part of this effort succeeded by opportunity rather than force. In 1638 Ali Mardan Khan, the Safavid governor of Kandahar, who had fallen out with his court, surrendered the city to the Mughals and entered Mughal service, where he became a distinguished noble and an engineer of canals and buildings. The Mughals thereby regained Kandahar without a siege.

Encouraged, Shah Jahan turned to the north. In 1646 the Mughal government sent an army across the Hindu Kush against Balkh and Badakhshi, whose Ashtarkhanid ruler, Nazr Muhammad Khan, was embroiled in a family struggle. Prince Murad Bakhsh led the initial advance and Balkh was occupied, but Murad, discontented with the harsh conditions, abandoned his command, and Prince Aurangzeb was sent to replace him. Aurangzeb negotiated with Nazr Muhammad and performed a famous act of piety by dismounting for the evening prayer in the middle of a battle, but could not secure the region. Supplies were costly to transport across the mountains, the winter was harsh, local resistance persistent, and the Mughals found no basis for lasting rule. In 1647 the army withdrew with heavy losses, at a cost the sources suggest ran to many millions of rupees. The Balkh campaign gained no permanent territory.

Kandahar was then lost. In early 1649 the Safavid ruler Abbas II besieged and captured it, in a winter campaign that surprised the Mughal garrison. Shah Jahan sent three major expeditions to retake the city, the first and second under Aurangzeb (1649 and 1652) and the third under Dara Shukoh (1653). Each failed. The Mughals fielded substantial forces, but they could not reliably bring enough effective heavy siege artillery over the long supply line, the defenders were well entrenched, and the Safavid army could contest the surrounding country. Chroniclers attributed the failures to the strength of the defenses and sometimes to the rivalries of the princes, whose reputations were at stake. The effort drained the treasury and left lasting resentment between the commanders. Kandahar remained in Safavid hands.

The northwestern campaigns illustrate that Mughal power, however formidable in the plains, depended on logistics, terrain, and political conditions that were harder to command at the periphery. They also show that the emperor's ambitions were shaped by dynastic memory: the pursuit of Balkh and Samarkand was not simply a calculation of strategic advantage but an assertion of the Timurid legacy. The costs and failures were borne by soldiers, by peasants who funded the campaigns, and by the reputations of princes who were soon to become rivals.

Historical Debate

Was Shah Jahan's Reign the Culmination of Mughal Strength or the Beginning of Structural Overextension?

The question: Should the reign be understood as the height of Mughal power, or did its expenditures, campaigns, and administrative pressures sow the problems that later weakened the empire?

Interpretation A — Culmination: The empire's revenue was at its highest, its territory was extensive, its administration functional, and its court culture brilliant. Setbacks at Kandahar and Balkh did not threaten the core, and the Deccan settlement strengthened Mughal paramountcy. On this view, the later difficulties, including the succession war and the pressures of the Deccan, arose from contingent decisions after 1658 rather than from structural flaws before it.
Interpretation B — Structural overextension: Other historians, drawing on analyses of the mansab-jagir system, argue that rising expenses for the nobility, a growing gap between nominal ranks and available revenue, and increasing pressure on agricultural producers were visible by the 1650s. Costly and unproductive frontier campaigns, combined with the heavy expense of monumental construction, strained resources, and the concentration of princely military power in provinces made succession struggles unusually dangerous.

Evidence: Both readings depend on revenue statistics, chronicles, and European accounts, which are incomplete and hard to compare across periods. The interpretation of the jagir system and of agrarian conditions remains debated, as Chapter 55 discusses.

Why it matters: The answer shapes how the later decline or transformation of the empire is understood (Chapter 57): as the outcome of long-developing structural problems, or as the consequence of later contingencies and decisions.

Division IV · Sections 53.13–53.16 · Principal focus 1630–1653

Shah Jahan's Architectural Program and the Representation of Imperial Grandeur

This division explains the relationship between architectural patronage and the political representation of Mughal kingship, without allowing the Taj Mahal to displace the dynastic and military history of the reign.

Section 53.13

Shahjahanabad, the Red Fort, and the Architecture of Imperial Government

Agra had been the principal imperial residence since Akbar's time, but by the 1630s it had drawbacks: the court and its dependents had outgrown the city's constricted fort and bazaars, the heat of the summer was oppressive, and the capital's layout allowed little room for the large ceremonies the emperor wished to stage. Delhi, on the Yamuna, offered open land, a long association with earlier Muslim rulers, and a site on the river that could support a palace-fortress and a city. The foundation of the new city, called Shahjahanabad, was laid in 1639, and the work was substantially complete in 1648. The court entered it in a ceremonial procession in the spring of that year.

The core of the project was the Red Fort (Qila-i Mubarak), a palace-fortress of red sandstone on the river's edge. Its plan followed a hierarchy of space. Visitors passed through gates and a covered market street into an outer courtyard, then to the Diwan-i Am, where the emperor sat in a marble pavilion to hear public business; further within lay the Diwan-i Khas for private audiences and the apartments of the imperial household, arranged along a channel of water, the Nahr-i Bihisht ("Stream of Paradise"), which ran through the pavilions. The sequence moved from public to private, and from outer to inner, expressing in architecture the gradations of access that defined the court.

Shahjahanabad itself stretched west from the fort. A broad avenue, Chandni Chowk, ran from the fort's Lahori Gate to a central market, with a canal down the middle drawn from the Yamuna by an engineering project that restored and extended an older canal. Mansions of leading nobles were built along principal streets, with bazaars, caravanserais, and gardens, and a city wall enclosed the whole. The great Jama Masjid, begun in 1650 and completed in 1656, stood on a rocky rise facing the fort, forming a second pole of the city: the palace of the emperor and the mosque of the community. Historians such as Stephen Blake have interpreted the city as the sovereign's household made permanent in stone, in which the urban layout organized a society of nobles, merchants, and artisans around the emperor.

The new capital had political functions. Nobles were required to maintain residences near the court, which brought them under imperial observation and tied them to the city's economy. Architecture and ceremony created a setting in which rank was visible. The move also marked a break from Akbar's court and from Agra's associations, and located the dynasty in the city that had been the seat of earlier sultanates, an implicit claim of continuity with a long tradition of Muslim sovereignty in Hindustan. The cost, in labor and money, was substantial, and the city's growth drew artisans, laborers, and traders from far afield.

Section 53.14

Architecture, Gardens, Mosques, and the Imperial Aesthetic

Shah Jahan's building program extended far beyond Delhi and the Taj Mahal. In the Agra fort, where Akbar's red sandstone palaces had stood, he ordered pavilions rebuilt in white marble, among them the Diwan-i Khas and the Khas Mahal, with the Moti Masjid ("Pearl Mosque") nearby. At Lahore, he added to the fort and developed the Shalimar Gardens (1641–1642), laid out in terraces with water channels and fountains. In Kashmir, nobles of the court, including Asaf Khan, laid out gardens such as the Nishat Bagh. The Jama Masjid of Delhi and a major mosque at Agra sponsored by Jahanara were great congregational structures; and in Lahore the Wazir Khan Mosque, built in the mid-1630s under a provincial governor, shows the richly tiled decoration of the Punjab tradition.

A recognizable imperial aesthetic emerged. Marble, quarried at Makrana in Rajasthan, replaced much of the sandstone favored earlier, and was inlaid with semi-precious stones in the technique known as pietra dura. Buildings were conceived in symmetrical plans; multifoil arches, slender columns, and curved cornices created a refined, controlled elegance. Gardens, laid out on the charbagh plan, a quadripartite division by water channels, evoked the paradise of Islamic tradition and also the Persian and Central Asian garden heritage of the Timurids. Water was a central feature, drawn from rivers and canals at great expense.

These buildings served political purposes. They announced the emperor's wealth, piety, and cultural authority; they provided settings for ceremonies; they offered employment and patronage to craftsmen; and they associated the dynasty with ideals of order and paradise. Mosques established Shah Jahan's identity as a patron of Islamic worship, while palaces and gardens provided stages for the display of kingship. Buildings were also a form of competition: nobles and royal women commissioned their own structures within the network of imperial patronage, so that architecture was spread across the elite. Chapter 56 provides a full survey of Mughal architecture and art; the emphasis here is on how building expressed and supported imperial authority.

Section 53.15

Mumtaz Mahal and the Construction of the Taj Mahal

Arjumand Banu Begum, daughter of Asaf Khan and niece of Nur Jahan, was born in 1593, betrothed to Prince Khurram in 1607, and married to him in 1612. She received the title Mumtaz Mahal ("Chosen of the Palace"). The marriage was a political alliance, connecting the prince to one of the empire's most powerful families, but the sources, including the official chronicle, portray it as a close partnership. She accompanied Khurram on his campaigns, including the years of rebellion, and bore him many children, among them Jahanara, Dara Shukoh, Shuja, Aurangzeb, and Murad Bakhsh. After his accession she was granted the highest honors and the imperial seal.

Mumtaz Mahal died on 17 June 1631 at Burhanpur, in the Deccan, shortly after giving birth to their fourteenth child, while the emperor was campaigning against Khan Jahan Lodi. Her body was buried temporarily in a garden at Burhanpur and, some months later, transported to Agra. The emperor, according to the chronicle, was inconsolable, and the court observed mourning. The decision to build a great mausoleum for her, on a site by the Yamuna south of the Agra fort, was part of a broader practice of royal tomb-building but surpassed earlier examples in scale and ambition.

Construction began in 1632. The complex consists of a raised marble mausoleum with a central dome, flanked by a mosque and an identical building known as the mihman khana, set within a walled charbagh garden, with a monumental gateway and ancillary courtyards. The principal tomb was substantially complete by about 1648, and the associated buildings and work continued until about 1653, as UNESCO's modern assessment records. The architectural direction is traditionally credited to a team that included Ustad Ahmad Lahori, though the precise division of roles among designers, master masons, and supervisors cannot be reconstructed with certainty, and the calligraphic program was designed by Amanat Khan, a Persian calligrapher whose named and dated inscriptions survive in the mausoleum interior; the execution of individual gateway inscriptions is treated differently in the specialist literature.

The materials came from across Asia. White marble was brought from the quarries of Makrana, nearly 400 kilometers away, in carts drawn by animals; red sandstone came from the region around Fatehpur Sikri. For the inlay, semi-precious stones such as lapis lazuli, carnelian, jasper, and turquoise were obtained through trade and tribute from regions including Afghanistan, Arabia, and beyond. Building required scaffolding, lifting devices, and ramps, the labor of many workers, and the coordination of supplies. Estimates of the size of the workforce and of cost come largely from later European travelers and later tradition, and they vary; figures of twenty thousand laborers and large sums in rupees appear in the literature but should be treated as approximate and uncertain.

Everyday Life and Social History

Labor, Materials, and the Making of a Monument

Behind the Taj Mahal lay the work of thousands: quarrymen at Makrana, cart drivers and animal handlers who moved the stone across the countryside, masons and carvers who shaped it, inlay specialists who cut and set tiny pieces of colored stone, gardeners, carpenters who built scaffolding, water carriers, overseers, and accountants. Some were highly skilled specialists who could command significant recognition and pay; others were unskilled laborers whose wages and working conditions were far less favorable. Wages were paid in money, and the project generated demand for food, tools, and housing in Agra, where a settlement grew around the site.

The surviving record gives little direct voice to these workers. Historians reconstruct their lives from chronicle references, from the evidence of the building itself, and from the logistics of the supply of materials. Their experience was shaped by the same conditions that shaped other Mughal labor: seasonal cycles of agricultural work, vulnerability to famine such as that of 1630–1632, and the demands of the state. The monument was made by people whose names mostly were not recorded.

Section 53.16

The Taj Mahal as Imperial Monument: Meaning, Resources, and Historical Interpretation

The Taj Mahal is often described as a monument to love, and the emperor's grief was real in the evidence of the chronicles. But historians generally interpret the complex as operating on several levels at once. It was a memorial to an empress, linking the emperor to the memory of a wife who had been central to his household. It was a dynastic statement, placing the mausoleum within a lineage of Mughal tombs, including those of Humayun and Akbar, and asserting the dynasty's permanence. And it was a monument of imperial power, whose scale, symmetry, and use of costly materials signaled wealth and mastery over resources.

Its religious symbolism has attracted particular scholarly attention. The calligraphic inscriptions, drawn largely from the Qur'an, include passages concerned with the Day of Judgment and paradise, and the charbagh garden alludes to the paradise promised to the faithful. The art historian Ebba Koch has argued that the complex, with the tomb set at the end of the garden on the river terrace, evokes the throne of God on the Day of Judgment; other scholars treat this reading with caution, noting that it rests on interpretation of symbolism rather than on any explicit contemporary statement of intent. What can be said with confidence is that the architecture participates in the imagery of paradise that Mughal gardens and funerary buildings regularly invoked.

The monument also invites questions about resources. Constructing it required the skilled labor of many specialists, the transport of stone over long distances, and large expenditure, which came from the imperial treasury. The scale of this expense must be set against the empire's resources, which were extensive; it did not by itself exhaust them. But it formed part of a larger pattern of costly building, campaigns, and display, which contemporaries and modern historians have connected to the broader fiscal pressures of the reign (§53.12).

Popular legends have gathered around the Taj, and historians distinguish them from documented evidence. The best known is the story that Shah Jahan had the hands of the craftsmen cut off so that they could not build another such work. No contemporary source mentions this, and it appears to belong to later storytelling. Another is the idea that the emperor intended to build a matching mausoleum in black marble across the river. The seventeenth-century French traveler Jean-Baptiste Tavernier mentions such a plan, but archaeological investigation has found no firm evidence for it, and most scholars treat it as unproven. Such stories show how the monument's fame has attracted narrative, and the task of the historian is to separate what the sources attest from what later imagination supplied.

The Succession Crisis and the Historical Legacy of the Two Reigns

This division establishes the political transition from Shah Jahan to Aurangzeb and prepares the historical questions examined in Chapter 54.

Section 53.17

The Imperial Princes: Dara Shukoh, Shuja, Aurangzeb, and Murad

Four of Shah Jahan's sons survived to adulthood and became principal contenders for the throne. Dara Shukoh, born in 1615, was the eldest and his father's evident favorite. He was kept at court, given an extraordinarily high rank and a chair beside the throne, and entrusted with commands, though he rarely took the field himself with success. Dara was also a serious intellectual. A follower of the Qadiri Sufi tradition, associated with teachers such as Mian Mir and Mulla Shah Badakhshi, he wrote works on Sufism and, in his treatise Majma-ul-Bahrayn ("The Confluence of the Two Oceans," c. 1655), explored parallels between Sufi and Vedantic thought. In 1657 he completed, with the help of scholars, a Persian translation of a number of Upanishads. His patronage of Hindu scholars and his interest in the common ground between traditions have made him a symbol in modern debates, though his contemporaries saw him chiefly as a prince with strengths and weaknesses of temperament and political management.

Shah Shuja, born in 1616, governed Bengal from 1639, with a base at Rajmahal, and held that province, with its rich revenue, for most of two decades. He had a well-organized administration and an army of his own, and he was a patron of arts and building. Aurangzeb, born in 1618, had shown courage as a youth, notably in 1633, when he faced a maddened elephant in public. He governed the Deccan from 1636 to 1644, fell out of favor in 1644 and lost his rank and office for a time, then served in Gujarat (1645–1647), on the Balkh and Kandahar campaigns, and again in the Deccan from 1652. He had a reputation for discipline, caution, and pious observance, and he had gained administrative and military experience that other princes lacked. Murad Bakhsh, born in 1624, the youngest, governed Gujarat and Malwa, was brave, impulsive, and fond of hunting and pleasure, and was often underestimated by his brothers.

Each prince had allies among the nobility and the household. Jahanara, the eldest daughter and first lady of the empire, supported Dara; Roshanara Begum, who held lesser rank and was reportedly resentful of her sister's dominance, aided Aurangzeb, reportedly sending him information from court. Nobles aligned with princes for reasons of kinship, patronage, regional interest, and calculation of advantage. The Rajput commanders, including Jai Singh of Amber and Jaswant Singh of Marwar, and Muslim nobles of Persian, Central Asian, and Indian origin, were found on more than one side, and alliances shifted.

The historical sources require careful handling. The Padshahnama's later volumes and the Alamgirnama, written under Aurangzeb, present the events from the perspective of the eventual victor. European writers such as François Bernier, who arrived in India in 1658 and served at one time in Dara's circle, and Niccolao Manucci, whose memoirs mix observation with rumor, offered colorful accounts. Dara's own writings, preserved from before the crisis, illuminate his thought. Historians such as Munis Faruqui have emphasized that the princes should be understood in light of the institutions of the Mughal state, in which princely command of provinces and armies and the absence of a fixed rule of succession shaped their ambitions, rather than primarily in terms of personal character or ideology.

Section 53.18

Shah Jahan's Illness in 1657 and the Beginning of the Succession War

In September 1657 Shah Jahan, then about sixty-five, fell seriously ill at Delhi. The nature of his illness is described in sources as a urinary complaint, and the severity and duration of his condition were uncertain even to those at court. Dara Shukoh, who was at his father's side, took charge of access to the emperor, and, according to hostile accounts, restricted the passage of news and letters from the provinces. The emperor's incapacity was the signal for the brothers to mobilize, and rumors spread in the provinces that he was dead, or had been poisoned, or was being kept from view.

Shuja, in Bengal, declared himself emperor and marched west; Murad, at Ahmedabad, did the same, seized the fortified port of Surat and its treasury, and prepared to advance; Aurangzeb, in the Deccan, did not at first claim the throne but moved with caution, presenting himself as acting to protect the emperor and the empire against the presumption of Dara. He allied with Murad, offering him a share of power, and corresponded with nobles throughout the empire. Dara's response was to send forces against each brother: his son Sulaiman Shukoh, with Raja Jai Singh, against Shuja in the east, and Jaswant Singh Rathor and Qasim Khan against Aurangzeb and Murad in Malwa.

The conflict was conditioned by Mughal conventions. The Timurid tradition recognized that any male descendant of the ruling house, if he could muster sufficient support, had a claim to the throne, and the Mughals had not replaced it with a rule of primogeniture. Princes came of age commanding armies and managing provincial revenues, so each had the resources for a military bid. The usual outcome, as in 1627–1628, was that victory determined legitimacy and the defeated were eliminated or confined, because a surviving rival was a danger. A phrase often quoted to summarize the stakes, that the prince's alternatives were the throne or the grave, expresses a logic that the princes understood.

The situation in 1657 was particularly dangerous because power was dispersed among capable princes, each with a base: Bengal, Gujarat and Malwa, the Deccan, and the court. Shah Jahan had recovered by late 1657, but by then the events had gone beyond his control. Efforts at his command to halt the princes were ignored by those who suspected that the emperor was incapacitated or under Dara's influence, or who judged that withdrawing would be fatal. The war that followed did not arise primarily from differences of religious conviction, though later accounts often framed it as a struggle between a liberal Dara and an orthodox Aurangzeb. Each side included nobles of many backgrounds, and Rajputs and Muslims fought on both sides.

Section 53.19

The Battles of Dharmat and Samugarh, 1658, and the Fall of Shah Jahan

The eastern campaign ended first. In February 1658 Sulaiman Shukoh and Jai Singh defeated Shuja's forces at Bahadurpur, near Banaras, and Shuja withdrew to Bengal. That victory, however, left Dara's forces divided at the critical moment. Aurangzeb and Murad joined armies in Malwa in April 1658, where Jaswant Singh and Qasim Khan blocked their route near Dharmat, not far from Ujjain. In the battle on 15 April, the imperial force, whose commanders were divided and Qasim Khan's contingent uncertain, was defeated after fierce fighting, in which Jaswant Singh's Rathor troops suffered heavy casualties. Jaswant Singh withdrew to Marwar. Aurangzeb followed his victory by advancing rapidly to the Chambal and the road to Agra.

Dara, now under pressure, assembled an army and advanced to meet his brothers. The armies met at Samugarh, about eight miles east of Agra, on 29 May 1658. Dara's force was large and included Rajput contingents, imperial regiments, and artillery, but the command was uneven and some nobles hesitated. The battle was hard-fought, with the left and right wings of both sides engaged. At a critical moment Dara's elephant was reportedly struck, and he dismounted to continue on horseback. Seeing the empty howdah, many of his soldiers concluded that he was dead or captured, and the army disintegrated. Dara fled toward Agra and then to Delhi and Lahore, while Aurangzeb and Murad held the field.

Aurangzeb then moved on Agra. Shah Jahan, who had not formally yielded power, negotiated and sent messages, with Jahanara acting as an intermediary, but Aurangzeb was in no mood to restore his father's control. He sent his eldest son, Prince Muhammad Sultan, to take the fort, and in June 1658 the emperor was confined to the palace, where he would remain, cared for by Jahanara, until his death in January 1666. Murad, who had been Aurangzeb's ally and had been promised a share of power, was arrested and sent to Gwalior fort, where he was later executed. Aurangzeb left Agra for Delhi, and in July 1658 he proclaimed himself emperor, taking the title Alamgir. A more formal coronation followed the next year. Dara's flight, the pursuit of Shuja, and the consolidation of Aurangzeb's authority belong to Chapter 54.

The war illustrated the logic of Mughal succession. Control of the capital, the treasury, and the emperor's person gave legitimacy; battlefield victory settled questions that law and custom did not; and the winner needed to neutralize his rivals. Aurangzeb's success depended on his army's cohesion, his alliances, his political caution, and the failures of Dara's coordination, as well as on circumstances such as the divided command of Dara's forces. It would be wrong to treat the outcome as inevitable. Other outcomes were conceivable, and contemporaries noted how narrowly the battles were won.

Cause and Effect

Why the Succession War of 1657–1658 Occurred

Long-term causes

The Timurid tradition that all princes of the house had claims; the absence of a fixed rule of succession; princely command of provinces and armies; precedents of rebellion in 1599–1604, 1606, and 1622–1626.

Short-term causes

Shah Jahan's illness in 1657; uncertainty and rumor about his condition; Dara's control of access to the emperor; the princes' mutual distrust; frictions over the Deccan campaigns of 1656–1657.

Event

Mobilization by Shuja, Murad, and Aurangzeb; the battles of Bahadurpur, Dharmat, and Samugarh; the capture of Agra.

Immediate effects

Shah Jahan's confinement; the arrest of Murad; Dara's flight; Aurangzeb's proclamation as emperor.

Long-term consequences

The reign of Aurangzeb (Chapter 54); lasting memory of the war; debate over its meaning for Mughal politics and for later interpretations of religion and kingship.

Historical complexity

The war cannot be reduced to a clash of religious outlooks, to personalities alone, or to the emperor's illness alone: structure, contingency, and individual choices combined.
Section 53.20

Review and Historical Synthesis

The two reigns examined in this chapter present a paradox that organizes their history. Under Jahangir and Shah Jahan, the Mughal Empire enjoyed extraordinary wealth, a functioning administrative system inherited from Akbar, a brilliant court culture, and monumental architecture that remains admired. Yet each reign was disturbed by princely rebellion, factional struggle at court, and costly military ventures, and the second ended in a war among the emperor's sons that deposed him while he lived.

The threads developed in the preceding sections converge on a single explanation. Imperial government in this period was strongly personal. Authority flowed from the emperor and was exercised through nobles whose rank, income, and prospects depended on his favor, and through an imperial household whose senior women, such as Nur Jahan and Jahanara, handled property, patronage, and political negotiation. This arrangement produced remarkable stability when the emperor was vigorous and the court united, as under Shah Jahan in the 1630s and 1640s, and acute vulnerability when the center was divided or the emperor was incapacitated, as in 1622–1626 and 1657.

The empire's military record was uneven. Rajput service was consolidated by negotiated settlements such as that of 1615; the Deccan was brought under Mughal paramountcy by 1636 but not stably integrated; and in the northwest Kandahar was lost, regained, and lost again, while the Balkh campaign cost heavily without gain. These outcomes reflected the geographic and logistical limits of Mughal power. Cultural patronage, meanwhile, served political ends: Jahangir's painters and naturalists projected the image of a ruler of intellect and discernment, while Shah Jahan's architects, in Shahjahanabad, the Red Fort, and the Taj Mahal, fixed the image of a ruler of order and permanence. Neither reduced to display alone; both were part of how kingship was constructed.

The chapter also invites attention to what these reigns do not by themselves explain. The tensions of the 1650s do not by themselves account for the later history of the empire; Chapter 54 considers Aurangzeb's long reign, with its religious policies, Deccan wars, and relations with the Marathas and Sikhs; Chapter 55 examines the economy and society that bore the weight of imperial expenses; Chapter 56 treats Mughal art and intellectual life in full; and Chapter 57 reconsiders whether the eighteenth century was a period of decline or transformation. The question with which the chapter began, why a powerful and wealthy empire remained politically vulnerable at the moment its ruler became incapacitated, finds its answer in the interplay of structures and choices: an inheritance system without a rule, armies and provinces in princely hands, a nobility whose loyalties were negotiated, and a court that concentrated both the glory and the risk in one person.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the Mughal Empire under Jahangir (1605–1627) and Shah Jahan (1628–1658), focusing on how the institutions inherited from Akbar performed and where they strained. It has argued that the empire's grandeur and its political vulnerability were connected rather than opposed, because the concentration of authority in the emperor's person made the transfer of power a moment of danger.

Jahangir's accession in 1605 occurred without a full civil war because he controlled Agra, because senior nobles supported him, and because his rival Khusrau was young and his backers divided. Continuity of personnel and policy eased the transition. Khusrau's rebellion in 1606, however, showed that princely revolt was a recurrent feature of Mughal politics, and the death of Guru Arjan in Mughal custody had consequences for Mughal–Sikh relations that historians interpret differently.

Jahangir governed in a personal style, projecting an ideal of justice, and the Jahangirnama is both a vital source and a carefully constructed self-portrait. The imperial household played an important role, and Nur Jahan emerged as a significant political actor, issuing farmans, appearing on coinage, patronizing buildings, and exercising influence, in ways that recent scholarship interprets as an expression of established household politics rather than a simple aberration.

Militarily, Jahangir's reign saw the negotiated incorporation of Mewar in 1615, persistent resistance in the Deccan led by Malik Ambar, and the Safavid capture of Kandahar in 1622. Diplomacy brought Safavid, Central Asian, and European envoys to court, among them Sir Thomas Roe, whose importance to Mughal policy was far less than his journal suggests. Court painting flourished, with portraiture and natural-history studies by artists such as Mansur and Abu'l-Hasan, which served both curiosity and imperial self-presentation.

The final years of the reign were disturbed by Prince Khurram's rebellion (1622–1626), by Mahabat Khan's seizure of the emperor in 1626, and by the succession contest of 1627–1628, from which Khurram emerged as Shah Jahan, supported by Asaf Khan and at the cost of the lives of rival claimants.

Shah Jahan consolidated authority through the suppression of rebels, an enhanced court ceremonial, and a program of display symbolized by the Peacock Throne. His government maintained Akbar's institutions, drew on the political roles of Mumtaz Mahal and then Jahanara, and presided over a high point of imperial revenue, even as the demands of display and campaigns put pressure on resources. The settlement of 1636 extended Mughal suzerainty over Bijapur and Golconda without annexation, while campaigns in the northwest, including the failed Balkh expedition and the repeated unsuccessful attempts to retake Kandahar after 1649, showed the limits of Mughal capacity.

The building program of the reign, including Shahjahanabad, the Red Fort, the Jama Masjid, gardens, and the Taj Mahal, formed part of the representation of imperial authority. The Taj Mahal is best understood as a memorial, a dynastic statement, and an expression of imperial power, with religious symbolism whose interpretation is debated; legends such as the severing of craftsmen's hands lack contemporary support.

The succession war of 1657–1658 arose from the Timurid tradition of shared claims, the absence of a fixed rule, and the princes' command of provinces and armies, triggered by Shah Jahan's illness. The battles of Dharmat and Samugarh brought victory to Aurangzeb and Murad, followed by the confinement of Shah Jahan and the seizure of power by Aurangzeb. The war cannot be explained as a simple clash between religious outlooks.

Finally, the chapter has shown that the historical record for these reigns is rich but shaped by the interests of those who produced it, and that careful attention to the distinction between official representation, foreign observation, material evidence, and later legend is necessary. The questions it leaves open, about the empire's structural strengths and weaknesses, are taken up in the chapters that follow.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has asked students to hold two images together: the brilliance of the Mughal court under Jahangir and Shah Jahan, and the fragility of the political order that produced it. The paintings of Mansur and Abu'l-Hasan, the gardens of Kashmir, the Red Fort, and the Taj Mahal are achievements of a society of remarkable skill and resources. The seizure of an emperor on the Jhelum and a war among brothers at Samugarh belong to the same history.

The reigns reveal how states work when authority is personal. Institutions inherited from Akbar kept the empire running, yet the absence of a rule of succession, the princes' command of armies, and the dependence of nobles on imperial favor meant that the health or authority of one person could determine the fate of the whole. They also reveal that political life extended beyond the emperor: royal women, ministers, commanders, artists, and laborers made the history of these decades, even where their voices survive only in fragments.

The developments of 1605–1658 shaped what followed. Aurangzeb's reign, which Chapter 54 examines, inherited the Deccan frontier, the strains on the nobility, and the memory of a war that placed the throne in the hands of a ruler who had defeated his brothers. The eighteenth-century transformation examined in Chapters 57 through 61 grew in part from these structures. Within the six-volume sequence, the chapter marks the high point of Mughal display and the moment at which its vulnerabilities became visible, preparing students to ask, in the chapters ahead, how far the empire's later history was determined by those vulnerabilities and how far by the choices that individuals made in response.
