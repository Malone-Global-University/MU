Part VII — Regional Kingdoms and the Early Medieval Order
34

Palas, Pratiharas, and the Politics of Northern India

Indian History: South Asia from the Indus Civilization to the Present

Chapter Opening

Around the end of the eighth century CE, a king of Bengal and Bihar did something that tells us how northern India was governed. Having marched into the upper Ganges plain, he did not simply annex the ancient city of Kannauj. According to his own copper-plate charter, he installed a ruler of his choosing there, in the presence of a gathering of subordinate rulers who acknowledged the ceremony. Kannauj mattered because whoever conferred its throne claimed a kind of superiority that went beyond the city itself. Yet within a few years the arrangement was undone by a rival from the west, and that rival was in turn confronted by an army from the Deccan.

Chapter 30 traced the end of Gupta supremacy and the career of Harsha of Kannauj, whose death in the middle of the seventh century left no successor capable of holding together the political space he had assembled. This chapter picks up the story from there. It follows northern and eastern India across roughly four centuries, from the middle of the seventh century to the eleventh, and asks how political power was organized in a world without a single dominant empire, yet with several rulers who aspired to be one.

Three dynasties dominate the narrative. The Gurjara-Pratiharas built a state from bases in western India and Rajasthan and eventually made Kannauj their capital. The Palas, rising in Bengal and Bihar, became the great patrons of eastern Indian Buddhism and the principal rivals of the Pratiharas along the Ganges. The Rashtrakutas, whose Deccan empire was examined in Chapter 31, repeatedly sent armies northward and for a time shaped the politics of the Ganges plain from more than a thousand kilometers away. Historians have long grouped their rivalry under the label “Tripartite Struggle,” a phrase this chapter will use but also interrogate.

The central argument is that post-Harsha northern India should not be described as a period of simple fragmentation or decline. Regional states developed durable political bases, and their rulers repeatedly tried to convert regional strength into supraregional supremacy. Kannauj became the symbolic and strategic center of that competition. But neither the Pratiharas, the Palas, nor the Rashtrakutas could turn military predominance into uniform, permanent control. Their power rested on regional cores, alliances, subordinate rulers, mobile armies, and negotiated political networks. Chapters 31 through 33 have already shown that early medieval South Asia contained powerful regional centers capable of imperial expansion. Chapter 34 shows the same phenomenon in the north and east.

The evidence is unusually revealing about royal self-presentation and unusually thin about everyday administration. Much of it consists of royal inscriptions and copper-plate land charters, which record victories, genealogies, and grants in elevated Sanskrit. These are praise texts, and they claim far more than they prove. They can be set beside Arabic travel accounts, Tibetan Buddhist histories compiled centuries later, Jain and Kashmiri literary works, coins, and the excavated remains of great monastic complexes. Reading them together, and against one another, is the skill this chapter asks students to practice.

The chapter also takes the Palas seriously as more than a military dynasty. Their kingdom sustained monastic centers such as Nalanda and Vikramashila, whose scholars and manuscripts reached Tibet, Nepal, and Southeast Asia. The political history of Bihar and Bengal cannot be separated from the religious and intellectual networks that Pala patronage helped build, though Chapter 36 retains the political history of the Himalayan kingdoms themselves.

The chapter keeps firm boundaries with its neighbors. Chapter 35 owns the problem of Rajput origins and the detailed histories of the Chauhans, Chandellas, and Paramaras. Chapter 36 owns Kashmir, Nepal, and the Himalayan trade networks. Chapter 37 owns the comparative analysis of land grants, temple economies, jati, and the “feudalism” debate. Here those subjects appear only where they explain how Pala and Pratihara power worked. The chapter ends in the eleventh century, when Pratihara supremacy had collapsed, the Palas were contracting and reviving by turns, and new regional houses were taking the stage that Chapter 35 will describe.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why the period after Harsha is better understood as regionalization and state formation than as simple political decay.
  2. Analyze why Kannauj held strategic and symbolic importance and distinguish possessing Kannauj from controlling northern India.
  3. Trace the rise of the Gurjara-Pratiharas from western Indian bases to imperial power under Nagabhata II and Mihira Bhoja.
  4. Trace the rise of the Palas from Gopala to Dharmapala and Devapala, and evaluate the limits of the evidence for Gopala's accession.
  5. Describe the Rashtrakuta interventions in northern politics and explain why Deccan armies could win victories without governing the Ganges plain.
  6. Evaluate the phrase “Tripartite Struggle” as a modern label and explain what it clarifies and what it obscures.
  7. Assess royal inscriptions and copper-plate charters as evidence, identifying their purposes and limitations.
  8. Describe the political and agrarian basis of Pala power in Bengal and Bihar.
  9. Explain the role of Nalanda, Vikramashila, Odantapuri, and Somapura in Pala patronage and Buddhist intellectual life, avoiding anachronistic comparison with modern universities.
  10. Analyze how subordinate rulers (samantas) and local elites shaped both the strength and the limits of Pala and Pratihara rule.
  11. Explain the fragmentation of Pratihara supremacy and the later fortunes of the Palas, and identify the successor powers that Chapter 35 examines.
  12. Connect this chapter's regional empires to the patterns of early medieval state power seen in Chapters 31 through 33.

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  2. Why did three powerful dynasties from different regions fight over one city in the upper Ganges plain?
  3. How reliable are royal inscriptions that claim sweeping victories, and how can historians test those claims?
  4. Why could rulers repeatedly win imperial supremacy without creating permanent, uniform control?

Historical Context

Northern India in the seventh century had already experienced a long cycle of imperial formation and regional reassertion. The Gupta Empire (Chapters 26 through 30) had at its height held the Ganges plain together, but by the sixth century its authority had been broken by regional dynasties and by the Huna invasions. Harsha, ruling from Kannauj in the first half of the seventh century, built a large but personal and unstable overlordship that the Chinese pilgrim Xuanzang described and that Harsha's own court poet Banabhatta celebrated. When Harsha died around 647, that structure did not outlast him.

The setting for what followed is geographic. The Ganges and its tributaries, especially the Yamuna, create a densely populated alluvial heartland. The upper plain, the Doab between the Ganges and the Yamuna, is fertile and strategically placed between the Punjab and Rajasthan to the northwest and west, and Bihar and Bengal to the east. Malwa and the Rajasthan frontier lie to the southwest, and the Vindhya and Narmada barrier separates the north from the Deccan. Bengal and Bihar are a wetter, riverine world of rice agriculture, navigable channels, and monsoon floods. Horses, essential to elite armies, came largely from the northwest and Central Asia, whereas eastern kingdoms were famed for elephant forces. These facts shaped the kinds of armies each region could field.

Politically, the period inherited institutions that were already well developed. Kings issued land grants, usually on copper plates, to Brahmins, temples, and Buddhist monasteries, and these grants created local landed and administrative intermediaries. Titles such as maharaja, maharajadhiraja, and paramabhattaraka were used to claim overlordship, and subordinate rulers (samantas) were expected to attend the court, pay tribute, and furnish troops. The Deccan powers examined in Chapter 31 and the Pallavas and Cholas of Chapters 32 and 33 operated within similar political conventions, so the north was not unusual in its mixture of royal ambition and layered authority.

The evidence presents particular difficulties. Inscriptions glorify their patrons and routinely claim conquests that may have been raids or ceremonial submissions. Chronologies for the dynasties differ by decades, depending on how regnal years in the plates are interpreted. Arabic geographers and merchants such as Sulayman (writing in the mid-ninth century) and al-Masudi (writing in the tenth) offer outside views of the kingdoms they call Juzr, Ruhmi, and Balhara, but they are often secondhand and their identifications are debated. Tibetan monastic histories such as Taranatha's, composed in the seventeenth century, preserve valuable traditions about Pala patronage but cannot be treated as contemporary records. This chapter therefore marks approximate dates with “c.” and flags where a claim rests on a single partisan source.

Finally, the chapter belongs to a wider early medieval political world. Arab forces in Sindh (Chapter 38) pressed on the western frontier. Kashmir under the Karkotas (Chapter 36) intervened briefly in the Ganges plain. The Tibetan empire and the Pala kingdom had contacts through Buddhism and sometimes through politics. Far to the south the Cholas reached the Ganges in the early eleventh century (Chapter 33). Northern India, in other words, was never politically sealed off from the powers around it.

Section 34.1

North India after Harsha: Regionalization and Political Opportunity

Harsha died around 647 without an heir able to hold his overlordship together. Chinese sources report that a minister seized the throne and attacked a Chinese diplomatic mission led by Wang Xuance, who then raised Tibetan and Nepalese troops and defeated him. The episode is brief and comes from an outside source, but it captures the situation: Harsha's authority had depended on his personal prestige, his armies, and a web of obligations to subordinate rulers, and none of these passed automatically to a successor.

What followed was not a void. The seventh and eighth centuries saw the growth of regional courts and military houses across the Ganges plain, Rajasthan, Malwa, Bengal, and Bihar. In Magadha the Later Guptas, among whom Adityasena is the best attested, ruled in a reduced form of the old imperial tradition. In Bengal several local kingdoms competed. In Rajasthan and Malwa new warrior houses were forming. In Kashmir the Karkota dynasty rose to a power that, under Lalitaditya in the eighth century, reached into the Ganges plain (Chapter 36). Meanwhile Arab forces established themselves in Sindh from 711 (Chapter 38) and made probing attacks eastward.

Historians increasingly describe this as regionalization: the consolidation of durable political cores around fertile districts, reliable revenue, and loyal military followings. Each core had its own court language of power, its own grant-making practices, and its own network of local chiefs. The old imperial center in the Ganges plain no longer monopolized prestige. Instead, several centers competed to claim the title of universal overlord.

This opened opportunity. A ruler who could gather a strong army, secure his home region, and win a spectacular victory over a rival could claim imperial status without needing to dismantle every local power. That is the political logic behind the contests of the next three sections. It also explains why ambition and fragmentation coexisted: the same regional resource bases that made conquest possible also made permanent unification difficult. The question, then, is not why the north fragmented after Harsha, but why the regional kingdoms that formed there kept trying to become more than regional, and why their efforts so often fell short.

Section 34.2

Kannauj and the Political Geography of Northern India

Kannauj, anciently Kanyakubja, lies on the Ganges in the upper Doab, in the modern state of Uttar Pradesh. Its importance had several layers. It was set in some of the most productive agricultural land of the upper plain, which supported dense settlement and a substantial revenue base. It lay where routes from the Punjab and the northwest, from Rajasthan and Malwa to the southwest, and from Bihar and Bengal along the Ganges converged. And it carried historical prestige: Harsha had ruled from it, and the earlier Maukhari dynasty had held it, so that possession of Kannauj evoked the memory of the last imperial court in the north.

That prestige turned Kannauj into a political symbol. Court poets of the period, including Rajashekhara at the Pratihara court, referred to it by the honorific name Mahodaya. The Pala charter discussed in Section 34.7 treats the installation of a king at Kannauj as an act that demonstrated superiority over a gathering of other rulers. For a dynasty from Bengal, the western desert fringe, or the Deccan, holding or conferring Kannauj was a way of saying that its king stood above regional rivals.

It is nevertheless important to distinguish possessing Kannauj from controlling northern India. The city could be taken by an army that had marched hundreds of kilometers, but holding it required sustained logistical support, cooperative local elites, and the ability to meet new challenges from several directions at once. Kannauj was a magnet for ambition and a hard place to keep. It was also not the economic center of all northern India: Bengal's rice lands, Malwa's agricultural districts, and the Punjab frontier each had their own resource bases. Treating Kannauj as the capital of an integrated North Indian state would therefore be misleading. It was better understood as a ceremonial and strategic center within a competitive political field.

Section 34.3

The Rise of the Gurjara-Pratiharas

The Gurjara-Pratiharas present a difficult origins problem, which this chapter treats cautiously and in outline only. Their inscriptions, notably the later Gwalior prashasti of Mihira Bhoja, claim descent from Lakshmana, the brother of the epic hero Rama, who in the story served as a doorkeeper (pratihara). Rashtrakuta records and Arabic sources, by contrast, call them Gurjaras (Arabic Juzr). Whether Gurjara denotes a people, a clan, or a region (the area of Gurjaratra in Rajasthan and Gujarat) is debated. What can be said is that the term was applied to the dynasty by outsiders, that it was linked to a western Indian homeland, and that the dynasty cultivated a Sanskrit lineage claim that reflected its royal ambitions. The wider question of warrior-lineage origins belongs to Chapter 35.

The dynasty's earliest well-attested ruler is Nagabhata I, active around 730 to 760. The later Gwalior prashasti credits him with defeating a Mleccha army, a term generally taken to refer to Arab forces advancing from Sindh. An eighth-century charter of the Chalukya rulers of Lata in southern Gujarat likewise records a successful stand against an Arab attack. These accounts suggest that western Indian rulers collectively blunted Arab expansion beyond Sindh. The Pratiharas used this success to build prestige, while Sindh itself, as Chapter 38 shows, followed its own path under Arab rule.

The Pratihara base lay in the semi-arid country of Rajasthan and the plains of Malwa, with centers such as Ujjain and the Jalor-Bhinmal region regularly cited by historians, though scholars differ on their earliest capital. This was not rich rice country, but it offered something valuable: horses, mobile pastoral and warrior populations, and trade routes linking Gujarat's ports with the Ganges. A separate Pratihara line also ruled from Mandor in Rajasthan, a reminder that the name referred to several related ruling houses, not one unified state.

The state Nagabhata I helped found was therefore a regional power with a military orientation toward cavalry and a vantage point between the Indus and Ganges systems. This helps explain both its later successes, when it could strike quickly into the Doab, and its later difficulties, since a state built on mobile warfare and negotiated loyalties found it hard to administer a vast agrarian interior directly.

Section 34.4

Vatsaraja, Nagabhata II, and the Contest for Kannauj

The Pratiharas first became central actors in the Kannauj problem under Vatsaraja, who ruled around 775 to 805. Rashtrakuta inscriptions record that he defeated a king of Gauda (Bengal), seizing from him two white umbrellas, an emblem of sovereignty. The same records then report that the Rashtrakuta king Dhruva defeated Vatsaraja and drove him into the desert, taking the umbrellas as trophies, and that Dhruva also defeated the Pala king Dharmapala in the Doab. These are partisan claims, but the Jain scholar Jinasena, completing the Harivamsa in 783 or 784, independently names Vatsaraja as ruling in the west, Indrayudha at Kannauj in the north, and a Rashtrakuta king in the south. His casual reference shows contemporary awareness of a multipolar situation.

At Kannauj itself, the Ayudha rulers were weak. Dharmapala (Section 34.7) enthroned Chakrayudha there. Soon after, Nagabhata II, Vatsaraja's son, who ruled around 800 to 833, drove Chakrayudha out and took the city. His successors made it their capital. The Gwalior prashasti also claims that Nagabhata II defeated Dharmapala near Munger, deep in Pala territory, and a long list of other rulers across India, though how far these assertions reflect lasting conquest rather than raids is uncertain.

Rashtrakuta power then intervened again. Govinda III, who ruled from 793 to 814, campaigned north in the first decade of the ninth century. His records claim that Nagabhata II fled and that Dharmapala and Chakrayudha submitted. Whatever the details, the campaign did not leave Rashtrakuta garrisons in the north. The Rashtrakutas returned to the Deccan, and the Pratihara hold on Kannauj survived. Nagabhata II's lasting achievement was the transformation of Kannauj from a prize to a capital, giving his dynasty a claim to the heritage of Harsha and a central position from which to dominate the middle Ganges.

Section 34.5

Mihira Bhoja and Pratihara Imperial Power

After the brief reign of Ramabhadra, whose rule appears to have been troubled, Mihira Bhoja (c. 836–885) became the greatest Pratihara king. His inscriptions, among them the Gwalior prashasti of 876 and charters from Rajasthan and the Doab, show the dynasty issuing grants across a wide area and styling itself in imperial terms. His coinage, the silver or billon “Adivaraha” drachms bearing a boar incarnation of Vishnu, circulated widely, and their imagery suggests a king who wished to be seen as the protector of order in a Vaishnava idiom.

Bhoja's reach extended across much of Rajasthan, the Doab, and into parts of the Punjab fringe, Malwa, and Saurashtra, but its borders fluctuated. He faced the Palas on the east, particularly under Devapala and Narayanapala, and the Rashtrakutas on the south. Rashtrakuta sources, including the Gujarat branch, record that Bhoja was checked on the Narmada frontier. His power was thus considerable but contested.

Arabic sources describe the kingdom from outside. The merchant Sulayman, writing in the mid-ninth century, depicted the ruler of Juzr as the owner of a formidable cavalry and as hostile to the Arabs, while noting that his kingdom lay along a frontier from which merchants feared little. Later, al-Masudi described a Pratihara king with four large armies stationed in the cardinal directions. These accounts support the picture of a military state with a significant cavalry arm, though they are second-hand and should not be treated as precise accounts.

Bhoja's reign illustrates both strength and structure. His court drew on Sanskrit culture, and his successors included Mahendrapala I (c. 885–910), whose court housed the poet and critic Rajashekhara. The Pratihara state held together through military mobility, royal grants, and the loyalty of subordinate rulers (Section 34.15), not through a centralized bureaucracy. At its height it probably deserved the label empire in the sense of an overlordship over many lesser kings. It never stopped depending on them.

Section 34.6

Bengal before the Palas and the Rise of Gopala

Bengal in the centuries before the Palas was politically varied. In the early seventh century Shashanka, based at Karnasuvarna in Gauda, had contended with Harsha and with the king of Kamarupa; later Buddhist sources hostile to him accuse him of persecuting Buddhists, a claim that must be read with caution. Following the end of his rule, regional dynasties such as the Khadgas in the southeast held parts of Bengal, while the Later Guptas controlled Magadha, and powers from outside, including the ruler of Kannauj in the early eighth century, sent armies in. Kashmiri tradition recorded in the twelfth-century Rajatarangini even recounts an adventurous Kashmiri king in Pundravardhana. The result was a period remembered later as one of disorder.

Historical Vocabulary
Matsyanyaya

Matsyanyaya, literally “the law of the fishes,” is a Sanskrit political metaphor for a state of anarchy in which the strong devour the weak, as large fish do small ones. Political thinkers used it to justify the existence of kingship: without a ruler to enforce order, society would collapse into predation.

In the context of Bengal, the term appears in Pala-era documents to explain how the dynasty began. It is a rhetorical and legitimizing device, not a neutral description of social conditions. Historians therefore treat it as evidence for how the Palas wished their origins to be understood, and as a pointer to genuine instability, but not as a measure of exactly how chaotic the region was.

The Pala account of its own beginning comes in the Khalimpur copper plate of Dharmapala (examined in Section 34.7). It says that the people, or leading men, of the realm made Gopala king in order to end matsyanyaya. Much later, the seventeenth-century Tibetan historian Taranatha elaborated the story and claimed that Gopala was chosen by leaders of the country. Modern writers sometimes summarize this as an “election,” but the evidence does not support a formal democratic process. The plate is a royal document, written to legitimize an established dynasty. The word translated as “people” or “subjects” (prakriti) may refer to ministers and leading men, and there is no description of a process or electorate. What can safely be said is that Gopala's rise was presented as a consensual response to disorder, and that he probably drew on support from regional elites.

Gopala, who ruled around 750 to 770, probably came from the Varendra region of north Bengal, which a later Pala-era poem, the Ramacharita, calls the ancestral homeland of the dynasty. He held Bengal and began to extend influence into Bihar. According to later Tibetan tradition he also patronized Odantapuri in Bihar, though the evidence for this attribution is late. Gopala's real importance was to consolidate a base from which his son could contend for the north.

Section 34.7

Dharmapala and the Expansion of Pala Power

Dharmapala, who ruled for roughly four decades beginning around 770, turned the Pala kingdom from a regional state into a contender for supremacy. His power rested on control of Bengal and Magadha, and his inscriptions use the full imperial titles. He expanded into Bihar, strengthened the dynasty's hold on the middle Ganges, and engaged in the Kannauj contest. He also made a political marriage: the Mungir copper plate of Devapala records that Dharmapala married Rannadevi, daughter of a Rashtrakuta prince named Parabala. A ruler of that name is also known from the Pathari pillar inscription, though whether the two are the same person is uncertain.

Dharmapala's campaigns met reversals. As Section 34.4 described, Vatsaraja and Dhruva both defeated him according to rival records. Yet his charter claims a triumph at Kannauj, where he installed Chakrayudha and received acknowledgment from a group of neighboring rulers. Historians interpret this as a ceremony of overlordship: Dharmapala gave Kannauj a king, rather than ruling it directly. The distinction matters. Influence over a city's ruler was a cheap and portable form of supremacy, but it left the real administration in local hands and evaporated when a stronger rival arrived, as happened when Nagabhata II took Kannauj.

Dharmapala's religious patronage had lasting effects. He is credited with founding Vikramashila and the great monastery at Somapura (Paharpur), which Section 34.13 treats. In the royal documents he is called paramasaugata, a devout Buddhist, but he also made grants to Brahmins and respected Brahmanical institutions. The Palas, like many early medieval kings, were not religious exclusivists.

Primary Source Window

The Khalimpur Copper-Plate Charter of Dharmapala

Source type: Royal land-grant charter inscribed on copper · Findspot: Khalimpur, in the Malda region of north Bengal · Date: issued in Dharmapala's thirty-second regnal year (early ninth century) · Language: Sanskrit

Context: The plate records a grant of land in Pundravardhana, a major region of north Bengal, made by Dharmapala. Like other Pala charters, it opens with an elaborate genealogy and praise of the king before turning to the grant itself and listing the officials and local persons who must respect it.

Paraphrase of content: The charter says that the realm, afflicted by anarchy, made Gopala king to end it. It praises Dharmapala's victories, and states that he installed a king at Kannauj, an act acknowledged with approval by rulers of neighboring regions, who are listed by name or region. It then records the donation of land and the obligations of local officials and residents toward the grantee.

Analysis: The plate is a primary source for the dynasty's self-image and for the form of Pala government, since it names the offices and categories of people addressed in the grant. It is not a neutral narrative. It was written to glorify the king and legitimize the line, it describes the Kannauj event in the most favorable terms, and it does not mention the defeats recorded in rival Rashtrakuta and Pratihara sources. The list of acknowledging rulers could reflect real attendance, a ceremonial formula, or an assertion of claimed influence. The plate says nothing about how long the arrangement lasted.

  2. Who created it, and for what purpose?
  3. What can historians learn from it about Pala government and landholding?
  4. What can historians not establish from it alone, and how might they test its claims?
Section 34.8

Devapala and the Pala Imperial Zenith

Devapala, who ruled around 810 to 850, is usually regarded as the high point of Pala power. His own records and those of his officials make sweeping claims. The Badal pillar inscription, set up in the time of Narayanapala, is primarily a praise text for the ministerial lineage of Guravamisra, whose ancestors served several Pala kings. Its verses credit Devapala with humbling the ruler of Utkala (Odisha), breaking the pride of the Hunas, and subduing Dravida and Gurjara rulers. The submission of the ruler of Pragjyotisha (Assam) comes from a different source, the Bhagalpur grant of Narayanapala, which recounts the campaigns of Devapala's general Jayapala in retrospect. A Munger charter of Devapala similarly describes an immense army.

These claims need to be read as royal praise. Utkala and Kamarupa were adjacent kingdoms that could have been compelled to accept Pala overlordship or to pay tribute for a time without being absorbed. Victories over the “Gurjara” could refer to actual battles with Pratihara forces under Nagabhata II or Bhoja, or to rhetorical conventions. Historians generally accept that Devapala was the most powerful ruler in eastern India and that he influenced the Ganges plain, but they do not accept the sweeping claims as a political map.

One of the best-documented signs of Devapala's international standing is the Nalanda copper-plate charter. It records that Balaputradeva, a ruler of the Shailendra dynasty of Suvarnadvipa (the Indonesian archipelago), built a monastery at Nalanda and asked Devapala for the grant of villages to support it, and that Devapala agreed. The document is evidence of maritime contact between Bengal and island Southeast Asia, of the prestige of Nalanda abroad, and of Pala willingness to endow a foreign king's foundation. It shows that the Pala political world extended across the Bay of Bengal in cultural and religious ways even where it did not extend in military ones.

Devapala's reign also set the pattern that later Pala rulers would find hard to maintain. The kingdom's revenue came from the Bengal and Bihar plains, its military from elephants and infantry mobilized through landed elites, and its external prestige from patronage. Under his successors, including Narayanapala (c. 854–908), territorial reach contracted.

Section 34.9

The Rashtrakutas Enter Northern Politics

Chapter 31 has examined the Rashtrakuta state in the Deccan. Its role in northern politics can be told briefly. Dhruva (c. 780–793) was the first Rashtrakuta king to campaign decisively in the Ganges plain, defeating the Pratihara king Vatsaraja and the Pala king Dharmapala in succession, if the Rashtrakuta claims are accepted. His son Govinda III (793–814) repeated the exercise on a larger scale, as the Radhanpur and Sanjan plates record, and claimed that rulers of the north bowed to him. Later, in the early tenth century, Indra III struck north again, and Krishna III, in the middle of that century, pushed into central India, as Rashtrakuta charters report.

The strategic logic was clear. The Rashtrakutas ruled from the Deccan plateau with a powerful mounted army and substantial revenue. Their rulers sought both plunder and recognition as universal sovereigns, and a victory over the kings at Kannauj was the surest way to earn it. The Malwa and Narmada routes offered a corridor, and the Rashtrakutas also held Gujarat through a collateral branch, which gave them a base on the northern flank.

Equally clear was the limit. Having defeated northern rulers, the Rashtrakutas did not stay. The reasons were practical. The distance from their core to Kannauj ran well over a thousand kilometers, the Doab required garrisons, and affairs in the Deccan, including rebellions, wars with the Eastern Chalukyas and the Pallavas, and succession disputes, called armies home. Rashtrakuta victories thus produced humiliation, tribute, and prestige for the winner, and temporary disruption for the loser, but not durable governance. This shows a pattern central to the chapter: military superiority over a region and administrative control of a region were different things.

Section 34.10

The Tripartite Struggle for Kannauj

With the three powers introduced, the sequence can be assembled. It is best understood not as one continuous three-sided war but as a series of campaigns across about a century and a half, with changing alignments.

First phase (c. 780s). Vatsaraja of the Pratiharas defeats a Gauda ruler and the Rashtrakuta Dhruva then defeats him and Dharmapala in the Doab. Dharmapala recovers sufficiently to install Chakrayudha at Kannauj. Second phase (c. 800–810). Nagabhata II dislodges Chakrayudha, makes Kannauj Pratihara, and claims victory over Dharmapala. Govinda III then marches north and compels the northern rulers to concede his superiority. Third phase (mid-ninth century). With the Rashtrakutas withdrawn, Pala and Pratihara rivalry continues under Devapala and Mihira Bhoja, in contests along the middle Ganges and into Bihar, with neither achieving a permanent decision. Rashtrakuta pressure on Bhoja's southern flank continues. Fourth phase (early tenth century). Pratihara power under Mahendrapala I extends into Magadha and parts of north Bengal, as inscriptions in those areas show, but then, around 916, Rashtrakuta Indra III attacks Kannauj and Mahipala I flees. The damage to Pratihara prestige is severe.

The overall pattern is episodic. Each campaign gave a ruler a moment of dominance and a set of inscriptions to prove it. None eliminated the other two. By the time Indra III struck Kannauj, the Rashtrakutas were the only one of the three without a continuous presence in the Ganges plain, and yet they inflicted the most damaging blow, which is one of the paradoxes of this history.

Multiple Perspectives

The Contest for Kannauj, Seen from Different Positions

Note: these are analytical reconstructions grounded in the kinds of evidence discussed in this chapter, not quotations from individuals.

Ruling dynasties: For the Pratiharas, Palas, and Rashtrakutas, Kannauj was a means to demonstrate sovereignty. Inscriptions treated its capture or the enthroning of its king as proof of universal rule, which explains why each dynasty recorded its involvement and minimized its setbacks.
Subordinate rulers and local magnates: For samantas, the contest created opportunities. They could switch allegiance, receive honors, or negotiate for autonomy, and the acknowledgement of a distant overlord cost little if that overlord did not stay. The charter evidence suggests such rulers attended ceremonies without surrendering their local authority.
Cultivators and townspeople of the Doab: Marching armies required supplies, and campaigning near cities would have affected local communities through requisitions and disruption, although the sources, written by and for courts, say little about these experiences. The sack of a major city also meant loss of property and, for its elite, loss of patronage.
Buddhist monastic communities and Brahmin scholars: For monasteries in Bihar and Bengal, the Pala-Pratihara conflict affected the security of their patrons and endowments, while court Brahmins such as the Pratihara poet Rajashekhara, or the Brahmin ministers who served the Palas, linked their own prestige to the fortunes of their kings.
Outside observers: Arab merchants and geographers described the kingdoms as large, wealthy, and militarily distinctive, and saw them in the context of trade and frontier relations rather than the Kannauj contest itself.
Section 34.11

What the “Tripartite Struggle” Actually Meant

Historical Vocabulary
The “Tripartite Struggle”

The Tripartite Struggle (also called the Tripartite Conflict or Triangular Contest) is a modern historians' label for the repeated competition among the Gurjara-Pratiharas, Palas, and Rashtrakutas for control of Kannauj and the Ganges plain, roughly from the late eighth to the early tenth century. No medieval source uses this term.

The label is useful as a shorthand but potentially misleading. It suggests a single structured war among three equal states, whereas the evidence shows intermittent campaigns, shifting alliances, and unequal involvement. The term also centers Kannauj, which was important but not the only focus of any of the three powers.

The label implies more coherence than the record supports. First, the three dynasties were never all fighting at once. The Rashtrakutas appeared in the north in discrete campaigns separated by decades. The Pala-Pratihara rivalry was more continuous, but even it included periods of calm and of cooperation against other enemies. Second, each dynasty had concerns elsewhere. The Pratiharas faced the Arab frontier, Rajasthan chiefs, and later the Rashtrakutas in Gujarat. The Palas dealt with Kamarupa, Utkala, and internal rivals. The Rashtrakutas fought the Eastern Chalukyas, Pallavas, and Gangas in the south and east.

Third, the sources require skepticism about victory. Each side claimed success against the others, and the claims often conflict. The same event could be a triumph in one inscription and a defeat in another, or be omitted entirely. Historians rely on triangulation: when independent sources from rival courts agree that a particular defeat happened, as with Dhruva's victory over Vatsaraja, confidence rises. When only one court reports a conquest, caution is required.

Fourth, control was episodic and indirect. A victorious king could demand tribute, install a client, or ravage a region, but the logistics of the period made sustained occupation difficult. Armies relied on foraging and local supply, rivers could be barriers or aids, monsoon rains limited campaigning seasons, and the distance between Kannauj and the Deccan or Bengal made reinforcement slow. These conditions help explain why no single dynasty created permanent pan-northern rule. The struggle, therefore, reveals less a failure of will than the structural characteristics of early medieval state power: strong regional cores, layered authority, and ceremonial rather than bureaucratic forms of imperial supremacy.

Section 34.12

Bengal and Bihar under Pala Rule

Turning from campaigns to the regional state, the Pala kingdom's durability becomes the central puzzle. The Palas ruled parts of Bengal and Bihar, with changing frontiers, for around four centuries, a longer span than most early medieval dynasties. They did so from a regional core in Varendra and Magadha, with administrative and military centers that shifted over time. Munger (Mudgagiri) on the Ganges appears in Pala charters as a camp of victory, Pataliputra remained an important site, and Karnasuvarna and Pundravardhana were regional centers. Under the later king Ramapala, a new city, Ramavati, was founded in Varendra.

The basis of Pala power was the agricultural productivity of the plains, tied together by rivers such as the Ganges, Karatoya, and the channels of the delta. These waterways supported rice cultivation and boat transport, enabling revenue collection and the movement of goods and troops. Land charters, the most abundant source, show kings granting villages and plots to Brahmins, temples, and Buddhist monasteries. Such grants transferred revenue rights and often some local authority to the recipient. They also encouraged agricultural expansion and bound religious institutions to the crown. The comparative analysis of this practice across South Asia belongs to Chapter 37; here it is enough to note that, in Pala territory, grants were an instrument of political integration.

Pala charters list a range of officials and local figures to whom the grant is addressed, from high ministers and military commanders to village headmen and cultivators. The lists suggest a hierarchy of offices, though the exact functions are not always clear. The administrative divisions known as bhuktis and mandalas organized the kingdom, with local magnates taking part in governing. Royal power thus worked through intermediaries, who both extended the king's reach and could form independent bases.

Everyday Life and Social History

Village, Market, and Monastery in Pala Bengal

Most inhabitants of Pala territory were cultivators living in villages, working rice fields watered by monsoon rains and river floods, with communities organized around landholding families and local chiefs. Charters name village boundaries, fields, groves, and ponds, giving a glimpse of a landscape divided into plots and tied to specific rights and obligations. Artisans, including potters, weavers, and metalworkers, served villages and towns, and the large monasteries required labor for construction, copying, and provisioning.

The monetary economy is debated. Coin finds from Pala Bengal are comparatively sparse, and some historians have linked this to a decline in monetized exchange, while others caution that the evidence may simply be limited. Elsewhere in the region, the Pratiharas issued the Adivaraha drachms, which circulated widely. Textual and sculptural evidence suggests that trade continued along the Ganges and across the Bay of Bengal, but the scale of commercial activity relative to earlier centuries remains a matter of scholarly argument. Evidence about women and families is thin and mostly comes from donative inscriptions, which show women as patrons of images and monasteries. The experience of the poor, the landless, and groups outside the court's concerns is largely invisible.

Section 34.13

Nalanda, Vikramashila, Odantapuri, and Somapura

The great Buddhist centers of Bihar and Bengal are often called universities, but the term can mislead. They were mahaviharas, large monasteries that housed communities of monks and supported advanced study, teaching, ritual, and the copying of manuscripts. They had no degree system, no corporate independence, and no separation from religious life comparable to a modern university. They depended on royal and lay endowments, usually in the form of revenue from villages, and on the goodwill of rulers.

Nalanda, in south Bihar, was the oldest and most famous. It flourished in the Gupta and post-Gupta eras, as the accounts of Xuanzang and Yijing attest, and continued under Pala patronage, with inscriptions and clay seals identifying monastic communities and royal gifts. The Nalanda charter of Devapala (Section 34.8) shows its international reach. Vikramashila, associated with Dharmapala and located near modern Bhagalpur on a hill above the Ganges, became a leading center of tantric Buddhist learning. Odantapuri, in Bihar, is traditionally linked to Gopala by Tibetan sources, though these are late. Somapura, at Paharpur in northern Bangladesh, has a vast quadrangular monastery with a central cruciform temple, and is among the best-preserved Buddhist monastic ruins in South Asia. A later foundation, Jagaddala in Varendra, belongs to the final Pala period.

Evidence for these institutions comes from excavations, seals, inscriptions, manuscripts, Chinese pilgrim accounts, and Tibetan histories. Each has limits. Excavation can reveal plans and objects but not teaching content; seals show corporate identity; Tibetan accounts preserve detailed lists of scholars and rules but were compiled centuries later, with their own devotional aims. Combined, they portray communities of monks and teachers, some of them scholars of high reputation, who studied philosophy, logic, grammar, medicine, and ritual, and who relied on Pala kings and wealthy lay supporters.

The relationship with the Palas was reciprocal. Monasteries gained security and revenue, and the Palas gained prestige, a reputation as defenders of the Dharma, and links to a transregional religious network. The Palas also patronized Brahmanical temples and learned Brahmins, so monastic prominence should not be read as an exclusive Buddhist state. In the early thirteenth century, as Chapter 39 and later chapters describe, Turkic military campaigns under Bakhtiyar Khalji devastated Bihar's monasteries, but that belongs to a later political context.

Section 34.14

Pala Buddhism and Transregional Intellectual Networks

The Pala centers sat at the junction of several religious and cultural networks. Buddhism in the Pala period was increasingly shaped by Mahayana and, especially, Vajrayana (tantric) practices, which emphasized ritual, meditation, and the role of accomplished teachers. Poetry and songs associated with Buddhist tantric practitioners, such as the Charyapada, preserve early forms of Bengali and neighboring languages and show that the tradition had a vernacular side.

Links with Tibet were especially significant. Tibetan tradition holds that the Tibetan emperor Trisong Detsen invited Indian scholars, including Shantarakshita of Nalanda, in the eighth century to help establish Buddhism, and that the monastery of Samye was inspired by Odantapuri. In the eleventh century, the scholar Atisha (Dipamkara Shrijnana, c. 982–1054), associated with Vikramashila, traveled to Tibet at the invitation of its western rulers and became a major influence on later Tibetan Buddhism. The details of these traditions come from Tibetan sources, but the broader pattern of exchange is well supported. The political history of Tibet and Nepal itself, however, belongs to Chapter 36.

Connections extended eastward too. The Shailendra rulers of the Indonesian archipelago, who patronized Buddhism and built great monuments, maintained contact with Nalanda, as the Devapala charter shows, and Buddhist sculpture and iconography from eastern India influenced Southeast Asia. Pala-style stone and bronze images, often in black stone, along with illustrated palm-leaf manuscripts, traveled widely. Palas thus had an impact beyond their frontiers disproportionate to their military reach, a form of cultural power rather than territorial control.

Section 34.15

Regional Political Networks and Subordinate Powers

Historical Vocabulary
Samanta

Samanta (and the higher rank mahasamanta) denotes a subordinate ruler or local magnate who acknowledged the overlordship of a stronger king, attended court, provided troops, and paid tribute, while retaining local authority and often hereditary lands. In origin the word referred to a neighboring chief; in early medieval inscriptions it became a formal rank.

The term does not map neatly onto European “vassal.” Samanta relationships varied widely in strength and could shift quickly, as the history of Pratihara and Pala subordinates shows. The question of whether such relationships amounted to “feudalism” is a major debate taken up in Chapter 37.

Both Pala and Pratihara kingdoms were networks of such relationships. Charters from each address samantas, rajanyakas, and other local rulers, and royal courts hosted them at ceremonies. A strong king could mobilize their forces for campaigns, as armies in this period were assembled from royal troops and the contingents of subordinates. A weak king saw them withdraw.

Marriage and tribute were the instruments of these networks. Dynasties intermarried, as Dharmapala did with the Rashtrakutas, and royal women appear in inscriptions as links among families. Tribute flowed upward in the form of gifts, soldiers, and sometimes revenue, and honors flowed downward in the form of titles, robes, and recognized authority. For subordinates, these relationships were negotiable. A chief might serve a Pratihara king in one generation and claim independence in the next, or accept a Rashtrakuta overlord when a Rashtrakuta army arrived.

Within the Pratihara orbit were houses that would later become important in their own right, including the Chahamanas (Chauhans) of Rajasthan, the Guhilas, the Kalachuris, the Chandellas of Bundelkhand, and the Paramaras of Malwa, the last initially connected with the Rashtrakutas. This chapter notes only that these houses grew within the larger political networks of the ninth and tenth centuries. Their origins, politics, and culture belong to Chapter 35. The point here is structural: the subordinate rulers who made Pratihara and Pala power possible were also the seeds of the successor states that followed.

Section 34.16

The Limits of Pratihara and Pala Imperial Control

The preceding sections allow the central problem to be stated directly. Why did kings with large armies, great prestige, and genuine victories fail to build permanent, uniform control over northern India? Several constraints interacted.

Distance and communication. Messages and armies moved at the speed of foot, horse, and boat. The distance between the major regions meant that a king who left his core to campaign was vulnerable at home. Military mobility. Cavalry-heavy Pratihara armies could strike quickly, while Pala forces emphasized elephants and infantry, and Rashtrakuta forces had their own composition. Each was strong in particular settings, and none was decisive across all of them. Local elites. Landed families, local chiefs, and religious institutions controlled revenue and manpower, and royal officials relied on them. Succession. Disputes within dynasties, which occurred in all three, repeatedly interrupted campaigns. Rival dynasties. Every ruler had enemies on several frontiers, as the Pratiharas did with Arabs and Rashtrakutas, and the Palas with Kamarupa and Utkala. Titulature versus reach. Imperial titles were an assertion of ideal rank. They do not map administrative reach, which was far narrower.

Northern India's pattern resembles the layered imperial systems of the south, including the Chola state in Chapter 33, but should not be forced into the same model. The north does not preserve a comparably documented equivalent of the Chola system of localized assemblies and temple-centered administration, and its political culture emphasized ceremonial overlordship and subordinate chiefs. Both regions show that early medieval empire was a negotiated and graduated condition rather than a uniform territory.

Cause and Effect

Why Imperial Victories Did Not Produce Permanent Control

Long-term causes

Regional resource bases and political cores inherited from the post-Gupta period; a political culture of layered overlordship and subordinate rulers; the physical scale of the Ganges plain and its connections.

Short-term causes

Contemporary rivalries on multiple frontiers; succession disputes; campaign-season limits; the ability of rivals to retreat and return.

Immediate effects

Temporary tribute and prestige for victors; damage to cities and populations; repeated shifts in control of Kannauj; inscriptions recording victory.

Long-term consequences

Regional courts and subordinate houses retained autonomy; the Pratihara collapse opened space for successors, and the Palas remained a regional power with wide cultural influence.

Historical complexity: These causes reinforced one another. It would be wrong to reduce the outcome to the personal weakness of particular kings or to a single military or institutional failure.

Historical Debate

Was Post-Harsha Northern India a Period of Decline or of Regional State Formation?

The question: Should the centuries after Harsha be understood as political decay following a classical imperial age, or as the creation of durable regional states?

Interpretation A — Fragmentation and decline: Older narratives emphasized the loss of imperial unity after Harsha, seeing the era as one of constant warfare, weakened central authority, and political disintegration. Many accounts connected this to wider claims about economic contraction and the decline of urban life, and some scholars working within the “Indian feudalism” framework, associated especially with R. S. Sharma, have argued that the proliferation of land grants and local intermediaries reflected a structural change in state power and the economy.
Interpretation B — Regionalization and integrative state formation: Other historians stress that regional kingdoms were stable, sophisticated, and culturally productive, and that their rulers expanded agriculture, patronized learning and art, and created durable administrative networks. Scholars such as Hermann Kulke have emphasized processes of integrative state formation, in which royal courts incorporated local elites and cults. On this view, the political order was not a failure to achieve unity but a different form of state power.

Evidence: Both sides use the same copper-plate grants, inscriptions, coin finds, and literary evidence. They interpret grants differently (as signs of weakness or as tools of integration), and they read the scarcity of coins differently as well.

Why it matters: The question affects how students judge the entire early medieval period. This textbook does not treat the era as a falling-off from an imperial golden age, and Chapters 31 through 33 and this chapter show regional states of considerable power, but it also recognizes that the debate over the economy continues and is taken up in Chapter 37.

Section 34.17

Fragmentation, Successor Powers, and the Political Legacy of the Ninth to Eleventh Centuries

Pratihara supremacy did not end in a single event. After Mahendrapala I, the dynasty faced succession disputes and external pressure. The Rashtrakuta attack on Kannauj under Indra III around 916 forced Mahipala I to flee and dealt a heavy blow to the dynasty's prestige. The Rashtrakuta king Krishna III later campaigned in central India. Over the tenth century, subordinate houses asserted their independence. By the time Mahmud of Ghazni attacked Kannauj in 1018–1019 (Chapter 39), the reigning Pratihara ruler, Rajyapala, is reported by Arabic and Persian sources to have fled, and the dynasty's authority was already narrowly confined. Later, the Gahadavala dynasty, beginning with Chandradeva around the 1090s, took over Kannauj.

The Palas declined and revived by turns. After Devapala's successors, Pala power contracted in the later ninth and tenth centuries, and a line of Kamboja rulers appears in northern Bengal inscriptions in the tenth century. Mahipala I, reigning from the end of the tenth century into the 1040s, restored much of the kingdom and revived Buddhist foundations. The expedition of the Chola king Rajendra to the Ganges around 1021 to 1022 (Chapter 33) reached the Pala frontier, though it did not leave a lasting hold. In the later eleventh century a revolt in Varendra led by Divya, a leader of the Kaivarta community, seriously threatened the dynasty, and Ramapala recovered the region, a campaign celebrated in the Ramacharita. By the middle of the twelfth century the Senas had begun to displace the Palas in Bengal.

These developments produced a political landscape of smaller regional houses. Some had begun as Pratihara subordinates and would become the dominant warrior dynasties of the next era. The next chapter, Chapter 35, takes up the question of Rajput origins and the Chauhans, Chandellas, and Paramaras. What this chapter has shown is that these later powers inherited political practices, titles, and networks developed under the Pratiharas and Palas, and that the fall of imperial supremacy did not return the north to a blank slate.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From Harsha's Kannauj to the Eleventh Century

What Changed

  • Political authority shifted from Harsha's single overlordship to competing regional dynasties with firm territorial bases
  • The Palas and Pratiharas emerged as new powers, and Kannauj passed from the Ayudhas to the Pratiharas and then to the Gahadavalas
  • Buddhist monastic centers gained the patronage of an eastern dynasty and extended their reach abroad
  • Subordinate rulers developed into regional dynasties, changing the political map

What Stayed the Same

  • Kannauj retained symbolic prestige throughout, until after the eleventh century
  • Kings continued to rely on land grants, ceremonial overlordship, and subordinate chiefs
  • Agricultural revenue, horses, elephants, and river routes continued to shape power
  • Sanskrit prashasti remained the dominant form of royal self-presentation

Who experienced change most directly? Courts, cities such as Kannauj, and monastic communities tied to royal patronage. Who experienced more continuity? Most village cultivators, whose agricultural routines continued under successive overlords, though they bore the costs of war and taxation. Why it matters: This shows that political turbulence at the top did not mean uniform disruption below.

Section 34.18

Review and Historical Synthesis

The synthesis question for this chapter is: why could early medieval rulers repeatedly win imperial supremacy without creating permanent uniform control over northern India? The preceding sections supply the elements of an answer.

Geography set the stage. The Ganges plain was large and rich, but it was also divided among regions with distinct resources: the horse-and-cavalry zones of Rajasthan and Malwa, the rice and river economy of Bengal and Bihar, and the Deccan plateau beyond the Narmada. Kannauj stood where these converged, and for that reason it became the prize. Yet a prize that could be reached by several armies could be held by none permanently.

The regional resource bases made rulers strong at home and ambitious abroad. The Pratiharas, with their western cavalry, the Palas, with their agricultural plains, elephants, and monastic prestige, and the Rashtrakutas, with the revenue and armies of the Deccan, were each capable of campaigns that shocked their rivals. Royal ideology encouraged this, since inscriptions rewarded victory and universal sovereignty. But victory was recorded as a ceremony of submission more than as conquest and administration.

Political institutions explain the rest. Early medieval kings governed through subordinate rulers, grants to religious institutions, local magnates, and military leaders whose loyalty could be won but not guaranteed. This system allowed rapid expansion and resilience, but it left authority dispersed, and it meant that when a king's army left, local power reasserted itself. These institutions also created the successor houses discussed in Chapter 35. Chapters 31 through 33 showed similar layered systems in the Deccan and the south, which is why this chapter should be read as part of a wider pattern.

Finally, the legacy of the period was significant. The Palas left a monastic and artistic tradition that shaped Buddhism across Asia. The Pratiharas left a model of imperial overlordship centered on Kannauj and a set of subordinate houses that would shape the next era. And the Tripartite Struggle itself left a lesson about evidence: royal claims of conquest, however grand, must be tested against rival sources, material remains, and the structure of political power.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined northern and eastern India from the death of Harsha to the eleventh century, arguing that the period is better understood as one of regional state formation and imperial competition than as simple fragmentation. Harsha's overlordship did not survive him, but the political space he had occupied was contested by regional dynasties with durable bases.

Kannauj stood at the center of that competition. Its fertile Doab setting, its position at the meeting of routes, and its memory of Harsha made it the symbolic prize of northern politics. The chapter distinguished possessing Kannauj from controlling northern India, a distinction that explains much of the period's history.

The Gurjara-Pratiharas rose from western Indian bases, building a cavalry-based state; Nagabhata I was credited with defeating a mleccha army, generally identified with Arab forces advancing from Sindh. Under Nagabhata II they took Kannauj, and under Mihira Bhoja they reached the peak of their power, as shown by inscriptions, the Adivaraha coinage, and Arabic accounts. Their origins remain debated, and Chapter 35 treats the wider question of warrior lineages.

The Palas rose in Bengal and Bihar after Gopala, whose accession was presented as an end to anarchy though not as a formal election. Under Dharmapala and Devapala, they became imperial contenders, intervened at Kannauj, and patronized the great monastic centers. Their inscriptions, including the Khalimpur and Nalanda plates, are rich but partisan sources.

The Rashtrakutas, whose empire Chapter 31 examined, repeatedly defeated northern rulers under Dhruva, Govinda III, and Indra III, but did not stay to govern. The comparison of their campaigns with Pala and Pratihara efforts shows that military superiority and administrative control were different things.

The chapter argued that the label “Tripartite Struggle” is a useful modern shorthand but can imply more coherence than the sources show. The three powers did not fight constantly or simultaneously, victory claims conflict, and control was episodic, limited by distance, logistics, local elites, and succession.

Pala rule in Bengal and Bihar rested on agricultural plains, river networks, land grants, and a hierarchy of officials and local intermediaries. The monasteries of Nalanda, Vikramashila, Odantapuri, and Somapura received royal patronage and became centers of learning with links to Tibet and Southeast Asia, though they should not be equated with modern universities.

Both dynasties governed through subordinate rulers (samantas), whose loyalty sustained and constrained royal power. These networks explain both the empires' reach and their weakness, and they produced the successor houses of later chapters. Pratihara supremacy collapsed after the attack of 916 and the pressures of the tenth and eleventh centuries, while the Palas contracted and revived until the Senas replaced them. The chapter's lessons about regional cores, layered authority, and the testing of royal claims against other evidence apply across early medieval South Asia.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows that political history without a single empire is still political history of great consequence. The Pratiharas, Palas, and Rashtrakutas built powerful states and shaped the destiny of the Ganges plain, even though none created lasting unification.

It also reveals processes that run through the rest of the series: the dependence of kings on subordinate rulers, the role of land grants and religious institutions, and the gap between royal claims and administrative reality. These processes shaped the Rajput polities of Chapter 35 and the political landscape that Turkic and Afghan armies would encounter in Chapter 39 onward.

The chapter connects regions as well. Bengal and Bihar were linked to the Himalayas, Tibet, and Southeast Asia through Buddhist networks, while Rajasthan and the Doab were linked to Gujarat, Sindh, and the Arab world. Reading these connections together prevents the political history of the north from being reduced to a sequence of dynastic battles.

Placed within Volume II, it completes a trio of case studies, with Chapters 31 to 33, in how early medieval South Asia organized power. Students should come away understanding that supremacy and control were different things, and that the strength of regional states lay in the very networks that limited their empires.
