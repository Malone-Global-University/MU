Farming Villages and the Foundations of Indus Civilization

Indian History: South Asia from the Indus Civilization to the Present

Chapter Opening

Long before any city rose beside the Indus, before any seal was carved, generations of people in the hills of Balochistan were already doing something quietly revolutionary: staying in one place. At a site now called Mehrgarh, on the eastern edge of the Kachi plain where the Bolan River emerges from the mountains onto the plain below, families built houses of sun-dried mud brick, planted barley and wheat in the surrounding soil, kept herds of goats, sheep, and cattle, and buried their dead with care and with grave goods. Successive communities rebuilt mudbrick structures on the same ground over generations. Nothing about this was inevitable, and nothing about it happened quickly. It was the product of a much longer transformation — beginning far earlier, in the deep Pleistocene past, and unfolding unevenly across a huge and varied landscape — that this chapter now traces.

Chapter 1 established South Asia as a historical region and explained the kinds of evidence, the geographic frame, and the interpretive cautions that this textbook uses throughout. This chapter turns to the beginning of the human story within that region: how people first came to inhabit the subcontinent, how they adapted across many thousands of years to a changing and varied environment, and how, in scattered and largely independent ways across different parts of South Asia, some communities eventually took up farming, herding, and permanent settlement. It ends at the threshold of a further transformation — the emergence of the large, planned cities that will occupy Chapters 3 and 4 — but it does not yet cross that threshold. The Mature Harappan cities of Mohenjo-daro, Harappa, and Dholavira belong to the next chapter. This chapter is about everything that had to happen first.

It is tempting to imagine the deep past as empty space before "real" history begins, a long silence broken only when writing, cities, or empires appear. That temptation should be resisted. Well over a million years of hominin presence, tens of thousands of years of modern human occupation, and many centuries of increasingly settled village life are not merely a prelude to be rushed past on the way to the Indus cities. They are a substantial part of South Asia's history in their own right, recoverable — imperfectly, but genuinely — through archaeology, and essential for explaining why urban civilization emerged in the Indus region when and where it did rather than somewhere else or at some other time.

This chapter also resists a second temptation: the assumption that South Asia experienced a single, sudden "Agricultural Revolution" analogous to a textbook diagram, in which foragers everywhere abandoned hunting and gathering at once for farming. The evidence instead shows a patchwork process. The northwestern borderlands preserve the best-documented early farming sequence forming part of the long-term regional antecedents to the later Indus trajectory, a region connected to areas farther west and northwest where the wild progenitors of wheat, barley, sheep, and goats were native and from which domesticated forms reached the northwestern subcontinent. This does not necessarily mean farming began earlier here than everywhere else in the subcontinent, only that its sequence here is unusually well documented and closely relevant to the developments this chapter traces toward Chapter 3. Elsewhere in the subcontinent, food production developed later, more gradually, sometimes independently, and sometimes not at all for a very long time, as communities continued to forage, fish, and hunt successfully in environments that rewarded those strategies. South Asia's transition to farming was not one event. It was many overlapping regional stories.

At the center of this chapter's account stands Mehrgarh, the single best-documented early farming settlement anywhere in South Asia and one of the most important sites for understanding the origins of food production anywhere in the world. Excavated across several decades beginning in the 1970s, Mehrgarh preserves a long sequence — from an aceramic (pre-pottery) farming and burial phase through later phases with increasingly elaborate pottery, crafts, and long-distance exchange — that allows archaeologists to watch a farming community grow in scale and complexity. As discussed further in Section 2.5, the precise chronology of this sequence has recently been substantially revised by new radiocarbon evidence, and this textbook presents that revision explicitly rather than relying on older excavator chronologies alone. Mehrgarh did not, by itself, become the Indus Civilization; its own occupation declined and activity shifted toward nearby sites such as Nausharo around, or shortly before, the beginning of the Mature Harappan period, rather than ending long in advance of it. But the kinds of activities visible at Mehrgarh — cultivation, herding, permanent architecture, craft specialization, and expanding exchange networks — are exactly the ingredients that, combined and intensified across the wider Indus and Ghaggar-Hakra zone over the following centuries, eventually made urban civilization possible.

This chapter also introduces a methodological challenge distinct from those Chapter 1 raised about texts, inscriptions, and scripts: for most of the period this chapter covers, there is no writing at all, and often no imagery that can be confidently interpreted. Historians must reconstruct diet, technology, settlement, exchange, health, and even, cautiously, aspects of belief and social organization entirely from material remains — stone tools, plant and animal bones, house foundations, burials, pottery, and ornaments — recovered through careful excavation and analyzed with methods ranging from radiocarbon dating to the microscopic study of plant remains and, increasingly, ancient DNA. This evidence is rich, but it answers some questions far better than others, and historians must be honest about where confident interpretation ends and informed inference begins.

By the close of this chapter, students should understand not only what happened — the movement from foraging to farming, from mobile camps to permanent villages, from simple tool kits to specialized crafts and long-distance exchange — but why these changes mattered, how unevenly they occurred across South Asia's many environments, and how the increasingly large, interconnected, and materially rich village societies of the fourth and early third millennia BCE created the conditions from which the Mature Harappan urban system would emerge, the subject of Chapter 3.

Historical Context

Chapter 1 established that South Asia's physical geography — the Himalayan arc, the Indus and Ganges river systems, the monsoon, the Thar Desert, the Deccan Plateau, and the subcontinent's many ecological regions — has repeatedly shaped where and how people have lived across more than five thousand years of documented history. This chapter reaches back before documentation of any kind, into a period measured not in centuries but in hundreds of thousands and, at its most distant point, well over a million years, when the same physical geography was already shaping human life, though in ways historians must reconstruct entirely from material remains rather than texts.

The northwestern borderlands of the subcontinent — the hills of Balochistan, the Bolan Pass, and the plains where the Kachi region meets the Indus system — occupy a particularly important place in this chapter's narrative. This region sits at the eastern edge of a much larger zone, stretching westward across the Iranian plateau toward Mesopotamia and the Fertile Crescent, within which some of the world's earliest experiments in plant and animal domestication took place. Early farming communities in this borderland region developed in close, though not fully understood, relationship with this wider western Asian world, a relationship examined closely in Section 2.5 and in the Historical Debate feature below. At the same time, this chapter emphasizes that South Asia's story of early food production cannot be reduced to a simple westward-to-eastward diffusion. Communities across the Gangetic plain, central India, and peninsular South Asia developed their own trajectories, at different times and often independently, discussed in Section 2.6.

Historians studying this period depend overwhelmingly on archaeological evidence — excavated settlements, stone tools, plant and animal remains, human skeletal remains, and, increasingly, ancient DNA — because no South Asian society of this period left any form of writing. This places particular demands on archaeological method: dating relies on techniques such as radiocarbon dating of organic material and, for portions of the Paleolithic record, on other geological and sedimentary dating methods whose margins of uncertainty can be substantial. Identifying domesticated plants and animals requires careful comparison of bone and seed morphology with wild ancestors, a process that has been refined considerably by modern archaeobotanical and archaeozoological research. Readers should understand that dates and interpretations offered in this chapter, especially for the earliest periods, often carry wider ranges of uncertainty than the more securely anchored chronologies available for the urban Indus Civilization examined in Chapters 3 and 4, let alone the epigraphically documented empires of later chapters.

This chapter's account also connects directly to themes introduced in Chapter 1: the danger of anachronism, the incompleteness of the evidentiary record, and the importance of resisting simple, single-cause narratives. Just as Chapter 1 warned against imagining "India" as an unbroken civilizational unity stretching back to remote antiquity, this chapter warns against imagining farming, sedentism, or "civilization" itself as a single achievement reached at one moment by one people. The following sections trace, instead, a genuinely plural and regionally varied process — one that nonetheless converges, by the chapter's end, on the specific set of developments in the greater Indus zone that Chapter 3 will show flowering into South Asia's first cities.

Section 2.1

The Earliest Human Presence in South Asia

The human history of South Asia begins long before farming, long before permanent settlement, and long before anything resembling the "historical" societies most textbooks treat as their starting point. Hominins — the broader group of upright-walking primates that includes modern humans and several extinct related species — occupied parts of the subcontinent for an immense span of time, documented chiefly through stone tools rather than through skeletal remains, which are far rarer and more difficult to date securely in South Asian contexts than in parts of Africa or Europe.

The most widespread and long-lasting early stone-tool tradition in South Asia is generally described as Acheulian, associated elsewhere in the world with early hominin populations and characterized by carefully shaped hand-axes and cleavers produced through a controlled process of removing flakes from a stone core. Acheulian tools have been recovered from numerous sites across the subcontinent, including the Soan valley in the Potohar Plateau of present-day Pakistan — one of the regions where South Asian prehistoric archaeology first developed systematically in the twentieth century, though the earliest Soan dates carry considerable chronological uncertainty — and from Attirampakkam in Tamil Nadu, South India, where careful, modern excavation and dating have produced the most securely documented early evidence anywhere in South Asia.

Dating this evidence is genuinely difficult, and readers should treat any single number with appropriate caution. Modern dating at Attirampakkam has established Acheulian activity extending back to approximately 1.51 (± 0.07) million years ago, a figure that places South Asia among the regions with very early securely dated Acheulian occupation outside Africa. This should not be read as evidence of uninterrupted population continuity across that entire span: securely dated evidence at separated sites and stratigraphic layers documents recurring hominin presence, not a single, unbroken population history. Other South Asian sites have yielded disputed or less securely dated claims for even earlier activity, and archaeologists continue to debate both the reliability of specific dating methods applied to particular sedimentary contexts and the broader question of when and by what route hominin populations first reached the subcontinent. This textbook presents these dates as the current, actively researched state of the evidence rather than as settled fact, consistent with the methodological caution introduced in Chapter 1.

What can be said with more confidence is that hominin populations were present in South Asia across an enormous span of the Pleistocene epoch, adapting stone-tool technology gradually over hundreds of thousands of years, and that this presence long predates, by a factor of many hundreds, the roughly five-thousand-year documented history that occupies most of this textbook. Understanding this deep timescale is not merely a matter of antiquarian interest. It establishes that South Asia's human history did not begin with agriculture, with cities, or with writing, but with a very long process of adaptation to a changing subcontinental environment — an environment whose broad features, discussed in Chapter 1, were already exerting influence on where and how hominin populations could live.

Section 2.2

Paleolithic Lifeways and Environmental Change

Across the long Paleolithic period, South Asian hominin and, eventually, early modern human populations lived as mobile foragers, moving across the landscape in response to the seasonal and long-term availability of plant foods, game, and water. Archaeologists divide this long span into broad technological phases — commonly described as Lower, Middle, and Upper Paleolithic — based chiefly on changes in stone-tool manufacturing technique, though the precise chronological boundaries between these phases, and their relationship to population movements and biological changes, remain subjects of ongoing research rather than fixed certainties.

The Pleistocene environment these populations inhabited was not static. Glacial and interglacial cycles affected global climate and, in turn, South Asian rainfall patterns, vegetation, and the distribution of game animals, although the subcontinent, positioned largely outside the areas directly covered by continental ice sheets, experienced these changes chiefly through shifts in monsoon intensity, river behavior, and vegetation zones rather than through glaciation itself, except in the high Himalaya. Foraging populations had to adapt their strategies as these conditions shifted, exploiting river valleys, rock shelters, and resource-rich ecotones — the boundary zones between different environments, such as forest and grassland, which often offer especially diverse and abundant food resources.

Middle Paleolithic technology in South Asia, generally associated with more refined flake-based tool production, appears considerably earlier than older textbook summaries once assumed. At Attirampakkam, the transition to Middle Paleolithic technological traditions has been dated to approximately 385,000 (± 64,000) years ago, with this technology continuing at the site until roughly 172,000 (± 41,000) years ago. The biological identity of the hominins who produced these Middle Paleolithic tools, and their relationship to later dispersals of Homo sapiens across Asia, are not established by lithic technology alone and remain actively debated in global paleoanthropology. South Asian evidence has become increasingly important to these wider debates as excavation and dating methods have improved, though this remains an active and evolving area of research rather than one with settled conclusions appropriate for confident textbook summary beyond the general pattern described here.

By the Upper Paleolithic, generally associated with more standardized blade-based tool technologies, South Asian populations show increasing regional diversification in toolkits, suggesting growing adaptation to specific local environments and resources. This trajectory — from broadly similar early technologies toward increasingly regionalized and specialized later technologies — anticipates a pattern this chapter will trace repeatedly across the following millennia: as South Asian populations grew more numerous and more settled, their material cultures increasingly reflected the specific environments and resources of particular regions rather than a single, subcontinent-wide pattern.

Section 2.3

Mesolithic South Asia

Small, carefully shaped stone tools known as microliths — often only a few centimeters in length and frequently hafted in combination onto wood or bone shafts to create composite tools such as barbed arrows, sickles, or knives — did not originate with the Holocene Mesolithic. Microlithic technologies are attested in South Asia considerably earlier, within the Late Pleistocene: evidence from Jwalapuram, in South India, indicates microlithic production by around thirty-five thousand years ago, and evidence from Dhaba, in central India, suggests microlithic technology by roughly forty-eight thousand years ago. What changes beginning around ten thousand years ago, in the aftermath of the last major glacial period and its associated global climatic reorganization, is not the invention of microliths but their especially widespread and intensive use across many South Asian rock-shelter and open-air sites. This Mesolithic horizon represents neither a sudden invention nor a simple continuation of earlier Paleolithic technology, but a significant intensification and diversification of how foraging communities exploited their local environments, building on microlithic techniques with a much longer history in the subcontinent.

Bhimbetka, a large complex of sandstone rock shelters in central India, provides one of the most thoroughly documented sequences for this period, with occupation layers spanning the Paleolithic through the Mesolithic and beyond, alongside an extraordinary body of rock art. Some Bhimbetka paintings have been assigned to Mesolithic phases on stylistic and archaeological grounds — including superposition, association with datable occupation layers, and pigments recovered from Mesolithic contexts — but the paintings themselves have not been directly and securely dated, and claims about their precise age should be treated with particular caution. The site illustrates a broader pattern: Mesolithic communities frequently returned repeatedly to favorable rock-shelter and riverine locations, exploiting a widening range of plant foods, small game, fish, and shellfish, in patterns that in some regions edge toward increasingly intensive and semi-sedentary resource use even before the adoption of agriculture.

This intensification matters directly to the chapter's larger argument. Mesolithic communities were not simply waiting to discover farming; in many regions they had already developed sophisticated, highly effective strategies for exploiting local resources, strategies that in some cases persisted for millennia after farming had begun elsewhere in the subcontinent. Where farming eventually took hold, it typically did so gradually and partially, often coexisting with continued foraging, fishing, and hunting for extended periods, rather than immediately and completely displacing older subsistence strategies. This pattern of gradual, partial, and regionally uneven adoption recurs throughout Sections 2.4 through 2.6.

Section 2.4

The Origins of Food Production

Before tracing South Asia's specific evidence, this section defines several terms historians use carefully rather than interchangeably. Domestication refers to a long-term genetic and morphological process by which a plant or animal population becomes distinguishable from its wild ancestors as a result of sustained human management and selection — for example, domesticated wheat typically retains its seeds on the stalk rather than shattering and scattering them, a trait that makes harvesting far more efficient but that would disadvantage the plant without human cultivation. Cultivation refers to the human practice of deliberately planting, tending, and harvesting plants, which may occur before full domestication has taken place and does not by itself prove that a plant population had yet become genetically distinct from its wild relatives. Pastoralism refers to economies substantially organized around the herding of domesticated animals, which may be combined with cultivation, practiced independently, or practiced by mobile or semi-mobile communities rather than fully settled ones. Sedentism refers to the practice of remaining in one location, typically evidenced archaeologically by substantial architecture, accumulated refuse, and repeated use of the same structures over time; sedentism often accompanies farming and herding but is not strictly identical to either, since some foraging communities in resource-rich environments also became substantially sedentary, and some early farming or herding communities remained partly mobile.

These distinctions matter because South Asia's evidence does not support a simple, single-step story in which foragers "discovered" agriculture and then everyone became settled farmers. Instead, the archaeological record shows a variable and often gradual layering of these different developments — cultivation preceding full domestication in some contexts, pastoralism developing independently of, or well before, plant cultivation in others, and increased sedentism sometimes preceding rather than following the adoption of farming, particularly in resource-rich riverine and coastal environments where intensive foraging alone could support settled life for extended periods.

One of South Asia's best-documented early transitions to food production occurred in its northwestern borderlands, examined closely in Section 2.5, where the wild progenitors of wheat, barley, sheep, and goats were native principally to regions west and northwest of Balochistan, and whose domesticated forms reached the northwestern subcontinent through connections with the Iranian plateau and wider Southwest Asia. Elsewhere in South Asia, food production developed on different schedules and, in some cases, around different crops and animals altogether, including, in later periods examined in subsequent volumes, indigenous cultivation of crops better suited to monsoon-dependent and tropical environments. This chapter concentrates on the northwestern farming frontier and the developments that fed most directly into the emergence of Indus civilization, while acknowledging that this was only one, though historically pivotal, regional story among several unfolding across the subcontinent during this era.

Section 2.5

Mehrgarh and the Northwestern Farming Frontier

No single site illuminates the origins of South Asian farming more thoroughly than Mehrgarh, located on the Kachi plain in Balochistan, present-day Pakistan, near the point where the Bolan Pass descends from the Iranian plateau toward the Indus lowlands. Excavated in a series of major campaigns beginning in the 1970s under French archaeological leadership working with Pakistani institutions, Mehrgarh preserves a long occupation sequence, allowing archaeologists to trace the development of a farming community from its earliest aceramic phase through later periods marked by pottery, expanding crafts, and growing exchange.

Excavators originally dated the earliest occupation at Mehrgarh, belonging to what archaeologists term the aceramic Neolithic — a farming community that had not yet adopted pottery — to approximately the eighth or seventh millennium BCE. That older chronology has recently been substantially revised. A major 2025 radiocarbon reassessment, based on direct dating of tooth enamel from twenty-three Period I burials, places the beginning of Mehrgarh's aceramic cemetery occupation between roughly 5223 and 4914 cal BCE, its end between roughly 4769 and 4679 cal BCE, and its total duration at only about 186 to 531 years — substantially shorter and later than earlier excavator estimates. Because this reassessment is recent, this textbook presents it as an important, still-developing revision to the field's understanding rather than treating either the older or the newer chronology as beyond question; readers encountering older scholarship on Mehrgarh should expect to see the earlier, longer chronology, while more recent work increasingly reflects this shortened and lowered dating. The same 2025 study argues that pottery did not appear in this part of present-day Pakistan before the mid-fifth millennium BCE, a point taken up further below.

Excavated house remains from Mehrgarh's earliest phase show rectangular, multi-roomed structures built of sun-dried mud brick, a technology that would remain characteristic of South Asian architecture for millennia afterward, discussed further in Chapters 3 and 4. Archaeobotanical evidence — the recovered remains of seeds, chaff, and plant impressions preserved in mud brick and hearths — documents the cultivation of barley, along with wheat, crops whose wild ancestors are not native to this immediate region but whose cultivated forms closely resemble varieties known from sites farther west in the Iranian plateau and the broader Fertile Crescent zone. The revised, later chronology described above is generally considered by its proponents to strengthen the case that this crop package, along with sheep and goats, reached the Kachi plain through the eastward movement of already-farming communities or already domesticated stock and seed from the west, rather than developing independently in Balochistan itself, a question examined further in the Historical Debate feature below.

Alongside cultivation, Mehrgarh's earliest community practiced herd management of goats and sheep, animals whose wild ancestors trace to regions west and northwest of the Indus borderlands and whose strong connection to West Asian domestication traditions is not seriously in dispute. Archaeozoological evidence — the study of recovered animal bones — shows a shift over time in the size and form of these herd animals consistent with intensifying human management. Cattle were also present from an early point in the Mehrgarh sequence and became increasingly important over time; notably, South Asian humped cattle (zebu) show morphological evidence consistent with a local domestication history in or near the Indus region itself, distinct from the West Asian sheep and goat pattern. This local cattle history indicates that Mehrgarh's early economy combined externally derived crops and herd animals with what appears to be an independent, local process of cattle domestication — a genuinely mixed picture that complicates any simple story of agriculture being either entirely imported from the west or entirely home-grown.

Mortuary evidence from Mehrgarh's earliest phases is similarly informative. Burials, generally placed within or near the settlement itself, include grave goods such as ornaments made of shell and stone beads, and in some cases additional deposited goods, patterns that suggest deliberate, socially structured practice surrounding death, though historians caution against inferring specific religious beliefs from burial goods alone, since similar material practices can accompany very different belief systems, and the absence of texts means Mehrgarh's inhabitants cannot speak for themselves about what such practices meant to them.

Across later phases — beginning, on the revised chronology described above, from around the mid-fifth millennium BCE — Mehrgarh's material culture grows markedly more elaborate. Pottery appears and develops increasingly refined and decorated forms. Craft production expands to include finely made stone and shell ornaments, increasingly sophisticated bead-making — including, in later phases, the use of drilling techniques for hard stones that anticipate craft traditions visible in the later Mature Harappan world — and evidence for early copper working appears, marking the beginnings of metallurgy in the region, a technology examined further in Section 2.8. Settlement size and population density also appear to increase across these phases, and evidence for exchange with more distant regions, including materials such as marine shell likely originating from the Arabian Sea coast and semi-precious stones from sources well outside the immediate Kachi plain, indicates that Mehrgarh's community was embedded within widening regional and interregional exchange networks well before the emergence of Mature Harappan cities.

In its latest significant phases, Mehrgarh's importance as the region's primary settlement appears to decline, with population and activity shifting toward other locations in the wider region, including the nearby site of Nausharo, occupied from around, or shortly before, the beginning of the Mature Harappan period. Mehrgarh itself, in other words, is not the Indus Civilization's direct ancestor in a simple, unbroken line, but it demonstrates, with unmatched clarity, the kind of long developmental process — cultivation, herd management, permanent architecture, craft specialization, mortuary practice, and exchange — through which South Asia's northwestern borderlands moved from mobile foraging toward the settled, materially complex village societies from which urban civilization would eventually emerge.

Section 2.6

Agriculture, Herding, and Regional Diversity

Mehrgarh's sequence, however exceptional in its documentation, was not the only trajectory by which South Asian communities took up cultivation, herding, and settled life. To the west and northwest, other Balochistan sites show related but locally distinct patterns of early farming, while further afield, the adoption of food production followed different timelines and, in some cases, involved different crops and animals altogether.

Within the wider Indus borderlands and Sindh, communities in subsequent centuries continued to combine wheat and barley cultivation with goat, sheep, and increasingly cattle herding, gradually expanding the geographic range of farming settlement toward the Indus floodplain itself, a movement examined further in Sections 2.9 and 2.10 and in Chapter 3. In Gujarat and other parts of western India, farming and pastoral communities developed on a somewhat later schedule, with local adaptations reflecting the region's distinct rainfall and vegetation patterns.

Elsewhere in the subcontinent, the picture diverges more sharply from the northwestern pattern. In parts of the Gangetic plain and eastern India, archaeological evidence indicates that rice cultivation and other crops adapted to wetter, monsoon-dependent conditions developed on their own regional timelines, in some cases considerably later than the northwestern Neolithic and through processes not directly derived from the West Asian domestication package of wheat, barley, sheep, and goats; the details of these eastern and southern developments belong more fully to later chapters as they intersect with the Vedic and early historic periods, but their existence is essential to keep in view here, because it underscores this chapter's central methodological point: South Asia did not experience one Agricultural Revolution, but several, unfolding on different schedules, with different crops and animals, in response to different environments, and not necessarily connected to one another by direct transmission.

This regional diversity matters for how students should understand the eventual emergence of the Indus Civilization in Chapter 3. That civilization arose specifically within the greater Indus and Ghaggar-Hakra zone, building on the particular sequence of farming, herding, and village development traced through Mehrgarh and related sites in this chapter — not because this was the only place in South Asia where food production developed, but because this was the specific region where the combination of fertile alluvial floodplain, expanding population, craft specialization, and exchange networks converged, over the following centuries, into urban civilization. Other South Asian regions, developing food production on their own timelines, would produce their own distinct historical trajectories, some of which intersect with the Indus world and many of which this textbook examines in later chapters devoted to those regions and periods.

Section 2.7

Village Society and Everyday Life

What was daily life like for the people who lived at Mehrgarh and related early farming settlements? Archaeological evidence permits a partial, carefully qualified reconstruction, though students should understand that much about early village social organization — kinship structures, leadership, gender roles, and belief — remains genuinely uncertain and subject to ongoing scholarly interpretation rather than settled fact.

Houses at Mehrgarh, built of sun-dried mud brick and organized into multi-roomed rectangular structures, suggest household units that stored grain, prepared food, and carried out craft activities within or near their residences; some structures identified by excavators as having unusually small, subdivided compartments have been interpreted as storage facilities, though the precise function of every excavated structure is not always certain. Repeated rebuilding on the same house foundations across generations indicates a strong attachment to particular residential locations, consistent with the sedentism this chapter has traced as a defining feature of the transition from mobile foraging life.

Diet combined cultivated barley and wheat with the meat, milk, and other products of managed goats, sheep, and cattle, supplemented by continued hunting and gathering of wild resources, particularly in the community's earlier phases, when farming had not yet become the exclusive or even necessarily dominant subsistence strategy. This mixed economy — combining new domesticated resources with continued reliance on wild foods — appears to have been common among early farming communities generally, not only in South Asia, and it cautions against imagining an abrupt, total switch from foraging to farming.

Skeletal evidence from Mehrgarh burials has allowed researchers to draw some conclusions about health and physical labor, including evidence of dental wear patterns and, in some individuals, indications of physically demanding labor, though drawing firm conclusions about the overall health consequences of the shift to farming — a question debated more broadly in global archaeology, where some researchers have argued that early farming populations in various parts of the world experienced worse nutrition and health than preceding foraging populations — requires caution given the specific and partial nature of the Mehrgarh skeletal sample.

Grave goods, including ornaments and, in some burials, goods interpreted as offerings, indicate that death was marked by deliberate, socially meaningful practice, though, as noted in Section 2.5, historians cannot confidently reconstruct specific religious beliefs from these remains alone. Variation among burials in the elaborateness of grave goods has led some researchers to suggest the possibility of emerging social distinctions even within these early farming communities, though such interpretations remain necessarily tentative given the limited sample sizes typical of archaeological excavation and the difficulty of inferring social meaning from material remains alone.

Evidence for the gendered division of labor in these communities is limited and should be treated with particular caution; skeletal and material evidence can sometimes suggest patterns in activity or diet correlated with biological sex, but historians should avoid projecting later, better-documented South Asian gender norms backward onto communities separated from those later periods by thousands of years and by evidence of an entirely different, non-textual character.

Section 2.8

Craft Production, Technology, and Exchange

One of the clearest trends visible across the Mehrgarh sequence and related sites is the steady growth of craft specialization — the development of increasingly skilled, often standardized production of particular categories of goods, frequently by individuals or households who devoted significant labor and expertise to a single craft rather than producing every needed good themselves.

Pottery, absent in Mehrgarh's earliest aceramic phase, appears in later phases and develops increasingly refined forms, including painted decoration, indicating both growing technical skill in firing and shaping clay and, potentially, the use of pottery styles to express regional or community identity, a pattern that becomes considerably more elaborate in the Early Harappan period examined in Section 2.10.

Bead-making represents one of the most striking areas of craft development. Excavated evidence from Mehrgarh's later phases shows increasingly sophisticated techniques for shaping and drilling beads from materials including shell, turquoise, lapis lazuli, and other semi-precious stones, some of which — lapis lazuli in particular — originate from sources well outside South Asia, most notably from mines in present-day Afghanistan, indicating that Mehrgarh's craftspeople were already embedded in long-distance exchange networks reaching across a substantial portion of Southwest and Central Asia. These bead-making traditions anticipate, on a smaller scale, the sophisticated stone-bead technologies that will become a hallmark of Mature Harappan craft production, discussed in Chapter 3.

Early copper working also appears in the Mehrgarh sequence, representing among the earliest evidence for metallurgy in South Asia. This early copper technology remained limited in scale and application compared to the more extensive later Bronze Age metallurgy of the Mature Harappan period, but its presence indicates that the technological foundations for later metalworking were already being laid during this long formative era.

Exchange networks evidenced by materials such as marine shell, turquoise, and lapis lazuli demonstrate that these early farming communities were not isolated. Goods, and very likely the people, ideas, and techniques that accompanied them, moved across considerable distances, connecting the Kachi plain to the Arabian Sea coast, to sources in Afghanistan and Central Asia, and, more distantly, to the broader Iranian plateau world from which domesticated wheat, barley, sheep, and goats likely arrived in the first place. This growing web of exchange, expanding steadily across the centuries traced in this chapter, provided one of the essential preconditions for the more intensive, longer-distance trade networks that would characterize the Mature Harappan world, examined in Chapter 3.

Section 2.9

From Villages to Regional Traditions

Across the fifth and fourth millennia BCE, farming and pastoral communities across Balochistan, Sindh, and the wider Indus borderlands grew in number, size, and material complexity. Settlement surveys across this region document an increasing number of village sites, some considerably larger than others, suggesting the beginnings of settlement hierarchy — differences in size and, plausibly, in function or status among neighboring settlements, though the precise administrative or political significance of these size differences, if any existed at this stage, remains difficult to determine from archaeological evidence alone.

In at least some parts of this region, communities may also have practiced small-scale water management, such as floodwater farming or local runoff control, to extend and stabilize agricultural production beyond what rainfall alone could support, particularly relevant given the relatively low and variable rainfall characteristic of much of Balochistan and Sindh. The archaeological evidence for such practices at this early date remains limited and site-specific rather than firmly established as a general regional pattern, and this textbook presents it as a plausible but not yet fully demonstrated feature of this period's agricultural development, connecting to the environmental themes introduced in Chapter 1.

Regional cultural traditions become increasingly distinguishable in the archaeological record during this period, identifiable through characteristic pottery styles, craft techniques, and settlement patterns that differ somewhat from one sub-region to another even as certain broader technological and economic features — cultivation of wheat and barley, herding of sheep, goats, and cattle, craft specialization, and long-distance exchange — remain shared across the wider zone. This combination of shared broad features and distinct regional expressions is an important pattern for students to recognize, because it recurs, in a more developed form, in the Early Harappan period examined in the next section, and again, differently, in the regional diversity that persists even within the shared framework of Mature Harappan civilization discussed in Chapters 3 and 4.

By the close of the fourth millennium BCE, the greater Indus and Ghaggar-Hakra zone had developed a dense network of interconnected farming and pastoral communities, increasingly linked by exchange, sharing important technological and economic features, and showing signs of growing settlement size and complexity. This is the immediate context within which the more clearly defined Early Harappan cultural traditions, examined next, took shape.

Section 2.10

The Early Harappan Transition

Archaeologists use the term Early Harappan, or "Regionalization era," to describe a set of related regional cultural traditions that developed across the greater Indus zone at different times from the later fourth millennium BCE onward, immediately preceding the Mature Harappan urban transformation examined in Chapter 3. These traditions did not all begin or end simultaneously across the whole zone; at Harappa itself, for example, a Ravi-phase occupation, dated in different chronological schemes to roughly 3700–2800 BCE or, more narrowly, 3300–2800 BCE, depending on the chronology used, precedes a Kot Diji-phase occupation, dated to roughly 2800 to 2600 BCE, which leads directly into the Mature Harappan phase beginning around 2600 BCE. Elsewhere in the zone, related but locally distinct traditions developed on their own local timelines. A single overview range of approximately 3300 to 2600 BCE can be used for the Early Harappan period as a whole, provided students understand that this is an approximate, regionally variable range rather than a single synchronized period. These traditions are named, in the usual archaeological practice, after key sites where they were first identified or are especially well documented.

The Kot Diji tradition, named for a site in Sindh, is associated with growing settlement size, the construction of substantial mudbrick and stone fortification walls around some settlements, and increasingly standardized pottery and craft production, patterns that many archaeologists interpret as evidence of growing social complexity, possibly including competition or conflict among communities, though the precise causes of fortification — whether defense against other human communities, protection from flooding, or another purpose — remain debated. The Amri tradition, also centered in Sindh, represents a related but locally distinct regional culture visible in its own characteristic pottery and material assemblage. Further east and north, communities associated with the Hakra river system, today largely dry but historically a significant watercourse, and with the Ravi river in the Punjab, show related developments extending the Early Harappan cultural zone across a wide geographic range encompassing much of what would become the later Mature Harappan world.

Several features distinguish the Early Harappan period as a genuine transitional phase rather than simply "the Indus Civilization, but smaller." Settlements, while growing in size and number, had not yet reached the scale of the great Mature Harappan cities examined in Chapter 3. Evidence for standardization — in weights, in craft production, and eventually in features such as brick proportions — was developing but had not yet reached the striking, widespread uniformity that characterizes the Mature Harappan period across an enormous geographic area. Long-distance exchange networks, already well established by this period as this chapter has traced, continued to expand, connecting the greater Indus zone to Central Asia, the Iranian plateau, the Arabian Sea coast, and, increasingly, to Mesopotamia, a connection examined further in Chapter 4.

Historians and archaeologists generally understand the transition from Early Harappan regional traditions to the highly integrated and remarkably standardized Mature Harappan urban system as a genuine transformation — involving increased settlement size, new levels of craft and administrative standardization, and the emergence of the great planned cities of Mohenjo-daro, Harappa, and Dholavira — but not as an abrupt rupture disconnected from what came before, and not as proof of a single centralized political authority, a question examined further in Chapter 4. The craft traditions, exchange networks, agricultural base, architectural technologies, and regional cultural interactions built up across the many centuries this chapter has traced provided the essential foundation on which that later transformation rested. Chapter 3 picks up this story directly, examining how and why, around 2600 BCE, this long process of regional development gave way to South Asia's first urban civilization.

Section 2.11

Review and Historical Synthesis

This chapter has traced an immense span of South Asian history — from the earliest securely attested hominin presence, well over a million years ago, through the Early Harappan cultural traditions immediately preceding the rise of Mature Harappan urbanism around 2600 BCE. Several threads developed separately across the preceding ten sections converge here into a single interpretive foundation for Chapters 3 and 4.

The deep-time argument of Sections 2.1 through 2.3 establishes that South Asia's human history vastly predates farming, permanent settlement, or anything resembling "civilization" in the conventional sense, and that this long Paleolithic and Mesolithic past is not merely a prelude but a substantial, if necessarily partial, chapter of South Asian history in its own right, recoverable through careful archaeological method even in the total absence of texts. The changing technologies traced across this period — from Acheulian hand-axes through Middle Paleolithic flake technologies, overlapping with microlithic traditions already attested by the Late Pleistocene and intensified during the Holocene Mesolithic — reflect a long, uneven process of adaptation to South Asia's varied and changing environments, not a single tidy sequence of stages, and this process set essential preconditions, though not a predetermined destiny, for the developments that followed.

The food-production argument of Sections 2.4 through 2.6 establishes this chapter's central methodological caution: South Asia did not experience a single, uniform "Agricultural Revolution." Domestication, cultivation, pastoralism, and sedentism developed as related but distinct processes, unfolding on different schedules in different regions, chiefly through diffusion from the wider Southwest Asian world of early domestication in the case of wheat, barley, sheep, and goats, and through a locally distinct, independent process in the case of South Asian cattle domestication. Mehrgarh, examined in unusual depth in Section 2.5 because of its exceptional documentation, illustrates this combination directly: externally derived crops and herd animals alongside what the evidence suggests was a locally distinct cattle-domestication process, developing together, on the revised chronology, across several centuries into an increasingly elaborate farming and craft-producing community.

The social and economic argument of Sections 2.7 and 2.8 establishes that early farming communities were not simple or undifferentiated. Household architecture, mixed subsistence strategies combining new domesticates with continued wild resources, differentiated mortuary practice, and increasingly sophisticated and specialized craft production, especially in beadwork, pottery, and early metallurgy, all point toward communities of real social and economic complexity, embedded within exchange networks reaching across hundreds of kilometers, even though many specific questions about their social organization, leadership, and belief remain, appropriately, subjects of continued scholarly caution rather than confident assertion.

The regional and developmental argument of Sections 2.9 and 2.10 establishes the chapter's culminating point: across the fifth through early third millennia BCE, farming and pastoral communities across the greater Indus and Ghaggar-Hakra zone grew increasingly numerous, increasingly large, increasingly interconnected, and increasingly marked by shared technological and craft features expressed through distinguishable regional traditions such as Kot Diji, Amri, and the Hakra/Ravi cultures. This Early Harappan world was not yet the Mature Harappan civilization of great planned cities, standardized weights, and extensive inscribed seals bearing the undeciphered Indus script/sign system that Chapter 3 examines, but it supplied that later civilization's essential foundations: an agricultural and pastoral economic base, mudbrick architectural technology, craft specialization, long-distance exchange networks, and a degree of settlement hierarchy and regional cultural coherence built up gradually across many centuries rather than invented suddenly.

Chapter 3 builds directly on this foundation, turning to the specific transformation, beginning around 2600 BCE, through which this long developmental process gave rise to the great cities of Harappa, Mohenjo-daro, and Dholavira, to unprecedented standardization across an enormous geographic area, and to South Asia's first urban civilization. Students should carry forward from this chapter a central lesson that will recur throughout this textbook's treatment of every subsequent transformation: major historical changes, however dramatic they may appear in retrospect, are almost always the visible culmination of long, often gradual, and frequently uneven processes that deserve careful study in their own right.

Material Evidence from Mehrgarh

Evidence type: Excavated architecture, burials, plant and animal remains, and craft objects · Site: Mehrgarh, Kachi plain, Balochistan · Date range: aceramic Period I now dated (2025 radiocarbon reassessment) broadly to c. 5250–4650 cal BCE, with later phases extending into the fourth millennium BCE · Recovered through: Systematic archaeological excavation beginning in the 1970s, with chronology refined by subsequent scientific dating

Context: Because no South Asian society of this period left any form of writing, historians studying Mehrgarh must reconstruct its inhabitants' lives entirely from material remains: the mudbrick foundations of houses, the seeds and chaff preserved in ancient hearths and brick, the bones of herded and hunted animals, the skeletal remains and grave goods of the dead, and the ornaments, pottery, and tools left behind by generations of craftspeople. Unlike the Primary Source Window in Chapter 1, which examined a written text, this feature deliberately examines unwritten material evidence, reinforcing the methodological point that historians of the deep past must read objects and contexts as carefully as later historians read documents.

Description of the evidence: Excavators at Mehrgarh recovered rectangular multi-room mudbrick houses rebuilt repeatedly on the same foundations; carbonized barley and wheat remains; goat and sheep bones documenting herd management and changing livestock profiles, together with cattle remains relevant to the development of South Asian zebu domestication; burials containing shell and stone ornaments; and, in later levels, painted pottery, finely drilled stone and shell beads, and evidence of early copper working.

Analysis: This body of evidence allows historians to reconstruct, with reasonable confidence, the basic outlines of Mehrgarh's economy, architecture, and craft production, and, more cautiously, aspects of its inhabitants' health, diet, and mortuary practice. It does not allow historians to reconstruct language, personal names, political leadership, specific religious beliefs, or many aspects of social organization with the same confidence. Historians must therefore distinguish carefully between what this material evidence can support directly and what would require speculative inference beyond what the evidence itself demonstrates.

Historical Debate

Did Agriculture Spread into South Asia from Southwest Asia, Develop Locally, or Result from Both Processes?

The question: Did farming and herd management reach South Asia's northwestern borderlands chiefly through the eastward movement of already-farming communities, or already domesticated crops and animals, from the Iranian plateau and the wider Fertile Crescent, did they develop through locally independent processes, or did both processes combine?

Interpretation A — Diffusion from the west: Mehrgarh's earliest cultivated crops, wheat and barley, and its earliest herd animals, sheep and goats, all have wild ancestors native to regions west and northwest of Balochistan, in and around the Iranian plateau and the Fertile Crescent, where domestication occurred earlier. A major 2025 radiocarbon reassessment of Mehrgarh's Period I cemetery, which places the aceramic occupation later and considerably shorter than older excavator chronologies suggested, has been read by its authors as most consistent with a comparatively late diffusion of farming communities, or of already domesticated crops and stock, into the Indus borderlands from the west, rather than a long, slow, locally independent development of the entire economic package.
Interpretation B — Local adaptation and independent development: At the same time, morphological evidence for South Asian humped cattle (zebu) points to a domestication history local to, or near, the Indus region itself, distinct from the western sheep-and-goat pattern, and the specific architectural, craft, and mortuary traditions visible at Mehrgarh do not simply replicate patterns known from sites farther west. On this view, even if wheat, barley, sheep, and goats arrived through contact with, or migration from, the west, South Asian communities actively adapted this package, added their own locally domesticated cattle, and developed distinctive local traditions rather than passively receiving a uniform package from outside.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on the same body of archaeobotanical, archaeozoological, and now radiocarbon evidence from Mehrgarh and related sites, together with comparative evidence from sites farther west, but they weigh that evidence differently: the revised, shortened chronology strengthens the case for relatively late external diffusion of the crop-and-herd package, while the distinct zebu cattle history and locally specific material culture continue to support a meaningful role for local adaptation and innovation.

Why it matters: This textbook treats the evidence as no longer supporting equal weight for a fully independent local origin of the entire farming and herding package. The wheat, barley, sheep, and goat complex is best explained, on current evidence, chiefly by diffusion from the west, while cattle domestication and much of Mehrgarh's material culture reflect genuine local development. The overall picture, in other words, is not a simple either/or, but it is also not a fully symmetrical fifty-fifty combination.

Student task: Using the evidence described in Section 2.5, explain how the 2025 radiocarbon reassessment changes the balance of the debate, and identify which specific pieces of evidence continue to support a meaningful role for local innovation even under the revised chronology.

Historical Vocabulary
Domestication, Cultivation, Pastoralism, and Sedentism

Domestication describes a long-term biological process in which a plant or animal population becomes genetically and morphologically distinct from its wild ancestors as a result of sustained human management, typically identified archaeologically through changes such as seed size, seed-retention traits in plants, or bone size and shape in animals. Cultivation describes the human practice of deliberately planting, tending, and harvesting plants, which can occur before domestication is complete. Pastoralism describes economies substantially organized around herding domesticated animals, which may or may not be combined with settled cultivation. Sedentism describes the practice of remaining in one location over an extended period, evidenced by durable architecture and accumulated occupation deposits.

These four terms are often loosely conflated in popular usage, but historians and archaeologists use them carefully because South Asia's evidence, like that from many world regions, shows them developing at different rates, in different combinations, and sometimes independently of one another, rather than as a single, unified package that appeared all at once.

Historical Vocabulary
Neolithic and the Myth of a Single "Agricultural Revolution"

The term Neolithic, literally "New Stone Age," was originally coined to describe a technological stage characterized by polished, rather than merely flaked, stone tools, but it has come, in common usage, to refer more broadly to the period associated with early farming and settled village life. Twentieth-century popular accounts sometimes described the shift to agriculture as an "Agricultural Revolution," implying a rapid, decisive, and near-universal transformation.

Archaeological evidence from South Asia and many other world regions has substantially complicated this picture. As this chapter has shown, farming developed on different schedules in different parts of the subcontinent, sometimes over many centuries rather than in a single decisive break, often alongside continued foraging and hunting, and with locally variable combinations of imported and independently developed domesticates. This textbook therefore uses "Neolithic" descriptively, to refer to the period and general technological horizon associated with early farming, while avoiding language that implies a single, sudden, worldwide "revolution."

Multiple Perspectives

Living in an Early Farming Village: Reconstructed Perspectives

Note: because no written sources survive from this period, the perspectives below are reconstructed analytical illustrations grounded in archaeological evidence, not quotations or accounts from any named historical individual.

A household managing cultivated fields and stored grain: Archaeological evidence for storage structures and repeated house rebuilding suggests households that had to plan across agricultural seasons, protect stored grain from spoilage and pests, and manage labor for planting, tending, and harvesting — a very different rhythm of life from the more immediate, day-to-day resource gathering typical of mobile foraging.
A herder managing goats, sheep, or cattle: Evidence for changing herd composition and animal age-at-death patterns suggests decisions about which animals to slaughter, which to keep for breeding or milk, and how to manage grazing land, reflecting a distinct set of daily and seasonal concerns from those of a primarily cultivating household, even though many households likely combined both cultivation and herding.
A craftsperson specializing in beadwork or pottery: The increasing skill and standardization visible in later Mehrgarh craft objects suggest individuals who devoted substantial time and expertise to particular crafts, potentially exchanging their finished goods for food or other necessities produced by other households, an early form of the economic specialization that would become far more elaborate in the Mature Harappan world.
A community member responsible for burying the dead: The care evident in Mehrgarh burials — placement, grave goods, and evidence of ritual practice — suggests that death was marked by deliberate community response, though the specific beliefs motivating these practices cannot be reconstructed with confidence from the material evidence alone.
Continuity and Change Over Time

From Camp to Village

What Changed

  • Stone-tool traditions changed and diversified over long timescales, from Acheulian bifaces through Middle Paleolithic flake technologies and later blade and microlithic traditions, with considerable chronological overlap among these traditions rather than a single linear succession
  • Subsistence shifted, in the northwestern borderlands, from mobile foraging toward cultivation of wheat and barley and herding of goats, sheep, and cattle, though this shift was gradual and partial rather than sudden and complete
  • Settlement shifted from mobile camps and repeatedly visited rock shelters toward permanent, rebuilt mudbrick villages
  • Craft production shifted from simple, largely self-sufficient household production toward increasingly specialized, skilled, and exchange-oriented craft traditions
  • Exchange networks expanded from local resource use toward connections spanning hundreds of kilometers, reaching Afghanistan, the Iranian plateau, and the Arabian Sea coast

What Stayed the Same

  • Communities throughout this period continued to depend directly on the resources of their immediate environment, whether through foraging, farming, or herding
  • Wild plant and animal resources continued to supplement diet even after the adoption of cultivation and herding
  • At later prehistoric sites where burials are preserved, such as Mehrgarh, communities sometimes treated the dead through deliberate and socially meaningful practices, though this cannot be assumed to hold for the much earlier Paleolithic record, where comparable burial evidence is largely absent
  • Regional environmental variation continued to produce distinct local adaptations rather than a single uniform pattern across the subcontinent

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both continuity and change prevents historians from treating the shift to farming as a total, instantaneous rupture with the past, while still appreciating the genuinely transformative long-term consequences of cultivation, herding, and sedentism for South Asian society.

Cause and Effect

Why Communities Became Sedentary

Long-term causes

Changing Pleistocene and early Holocene environments altered the distribution of plant and animal resources, while contact with, or proximity to, regions of early Southwest Asian domestication placed South Asia's northwestern borderlands within reach of new crops and herd animals.

Short-term causes

Local decisions by particular communities to invest labor in cultivating fields, managing herds, and constructing durable mudbrick architecture created growing practical incentives to remain near those investments rather than moving on.

Effect

Communities such as Mehrgarh developed permanent architecture, storage facilities, craft specialization, and expanding exchange networks, supporting larger and more materially complex populations than mobile foraging alone typically sustained.

Everyday Life and Social History

Imagining Daily Life without Written Records

Because no texts survive from this period, reconstructing everyday life at a site such as Mehrgarh requires historians to read material evidence with particular care. A household's day likely involved tending fields or herds according to the season, preparing food from a combination of cultivated grain, herd animals, and wild resources, maintaining and repairing mudbrick structures, and producing or trading for the tools, ornaments, and pottery that archaeologists later recover in such abundance.

Labor was almost certainly organized, at least in part, around household and kin groups, though the precise structure of authority, inheritance, and gender roles within these groups cannot be reconstructed with confidence from the available evidence. What can be said is that these were not simple, undifferentiated communities: the presence of specialized crafts, varying burial goods, and evidence of exchange with distant regions all indicate a social and economic life of real complexity, even in the absence of writing, cities, or the more familiar political institutions that occupy later chapters of this textbook. Approaching this evidence with both curiosity and appropriate caution — willing to reconstruct what the evidence supports, while resisting the temptation to fill gaps with unsupported speculation — is precisely the historical skill this textbook asks students to practice throughout.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced the human history of South Asia from the earliest securely attested hominin presence, well over a million years ago, through the Early Harappan cultural traditions immediately preceding the rise of Mature Harappan urban civilization around 2600 BCE. It has argued throughout that this long span of prehistory deserves serious historical attention in its own right, not merely as a rushed prelude to the Indus cities examined in Chapter 3.

The chapter began with the Paleolithic record, tracing hominin occupation of the subcontinent through changing stone-tool technologies — Acheulian, Middle Paleolithic, and Upper Paleolithic — documented at sites including Attirampakkam and the Soan valley, while emphasizing the genuine uncertainty surrounding much of this chronology. It then turned to the Mesolithic period, in which microlithic technology and intensified foraging strategies, documented at sites such as Bhimbetka, reflect growing specialization and resource intensification among still-mobile or semi-sedentary communities.

The chapter's central argument concerns the origins of food production. Rejecting the idea of a single, uniform "Agricultural Revolution," it has shown that domestication, cultivation, pastoralism, and sedentism developed as related but distinct processes, unfolding unevenly across South Asia's varied regions. In the northwestern borderlands, examined in greatest depth through the exceptionally well-documented site of Mehrgarh, farming and herd management developed from around the mid-fifth millennium BCE onward according to a 2025 radiocarbon reassessment that substantially revised older, longer excavator chronologies, combining crops and herd animals derived chiefly through diffusion from the wider Southwest Asian world of early domestication with what the evidence suggests was an independent, local process of cattle domestication.

The chapter examined Mehrgarh's occupation sequence in detail: its earliest aceramic farming and cemetery phase, marked by mudbrick architecture, cultivated barley and wheat, and managed goats and sheep; its later phases, beginning around the mid-fifth millennium BCE, marked by the appearance of pottery, expanding cattle herd management, increasingly sophisticated craft production including beadwork and early copper working, and growing evidence of long-distance exchange reaching Afghanistan and the Arabian Sea coast; and its eventual decline as the region's primary settlement, as population and activity shifted toward nearby sites such as Nausharo around, or shortly before, the beginning of the Mature Harappan period.

The chapter emphasized that Mehrgarh's story, however well documented, was only one regional trajectory among several unfolding across South Asia during this era, with farming and pastoral communities developing on different schedules, with different crops and animals, in the Gangetic plain, eastern India, and other regions whose fuller treatment belongs to later chapters. Within the northwestern borderlands and the wider Indus zone, however, this chapter traced a continuous, if gradual, process of growth: increasing settlement size, growing evidence of settlement hierarchy, possible small-scale local water management in some areas, and increasingly distinguishable regional cultural traditions.

The chapter examined early village social organization and everyday life with appropriate caution, using evidence from architecture, diet, skeletal remains, and burial practice to reconstruct household economy, health, and mortuary custom, while explicitly acknowledging the limits of what such evidence can establish about social organization, leadership, gender, and belief.

The chapter then turned to craft production and exchange, tracing the growth of pottery, bead-making, and early metallurgy, and the expanding networks of long-distance exchange that connected the greater Indus zone to Central Asia, the Iranian plateau, and the Arabian Sea coast well before the emergence of Mature Harappan cities.

Finally, the chapter examined the Early Harappan (Regionalization-era) period, developing at different times from the later fourth millennium BCE onward and generally understood in overview as spanning roughly 3300 to 2600 BCE, during which regional cultural traditions including the Ravi phase, the Kot Diji phase, Amri, and Hakra-associated cultures developed across the greater Indus and Ghaggar-Hakra zone, marked by growing settlement size, fortification at some sites, and increasingly standardized craft production — the immediate cultural and economic foundation from which Mature Harappan urban civilization, the subject of Chapter 3, would emerge.

Throughout, this chapter has emphasized a central methodological lesson that will recur across this textbook's treatment of every subsequent major transformation: dramatic historical changes are almost always the visible culmination of long, often uneven, and frequently regionally varied processes. The rise of Indus urban civilization, examined next, was not a sudden invention but the outcome of many centuries of cumulative development traced in this chapter.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Hominin
The broader group of upright-walking primates including modern humans and several extinct related species, whose presence in South Asia is documented chiefly through stone tools.
Paleolithic
The long period of human prehistory characterized by chipped-stone tool technology, generally divided into Lower, Middle, and Upper phases based on changing tool-manufacturing techniques.
Acheulian
An early, long-lasting stone-tool tradition characterized by carefully shaped hand-axes and cleavers, documented at South Asian sites including Attirampakkam and the Soan valley.
Mesolithic
The period, beginning around ten thousand years ago in South Asia, characterized by small, specialized microlithic stone tools and intensified foraging, fishing, and hunting strategies.
Microlith
A small, carefully shaped stone tool, often hafted in combination with others onto a wood or bone shaft to create composite tools such as arrows or sickles.
Domestication
A long-term genetic and morphological process by which a plant or animal population becomes distinguishable from its wild ancestors through sustained human management and selection.
Cultivation
The human practice of deliberately planting, tending, and harvesting plants, which may occur before full domestication is complete.
Pastoralism
An economy substantially organized around the herding of domesticated animals, which may be combined with cultivation or practiced independently.
Sedentism
The practice of remaining in one location over an extended period, typically evidenced archaeologically by durable architecture and accumulated occupation deposits.
Aceramic Neolithic
An early farming period, such as Mehrgarh's earliest phase, in which communities practiced cultivation and herding but had not yet adopted pottery.
Archaeobotany
The study of recovered plant remains, including seeds and chaff, used to reconstruct ancient diet, cultivation, and domestication.
Archaeozoology
The study of recovered animal remains, including bones, used to reconstruct ancient diet, herding, and domestication.
Radiocarbon dating
A dating method measuring the decay of a radioactive carbon isotope in organic material, widely used to date archaeological deposits containing charcoal, bone, or plant remains.
Craft specialization
The development of skilled, often standardized production of particular categories of goods by individuals or households, rather than each household producing every needed good itself.
Long-distance exchange
The movement of goods, and likely people, ideas, and techniques, across substantial distances, evidenced at Mehrgarh by materials such as marine shell and lapis lazuli originating far from the settlement itself.
Early Harappan
A set of related regional cultural traditions (also called the Regionalization era), including the Ravi phase, Kot Diji phase, Amri, and Hakra-associated cultures, that developed across the greater Indus zone at different times from the later fourth millennium BCE onward, immediately preceding Mature Harappan urbanism.
Mature Harappan
The urban phase of the Indus Civilization, beginning around 2600 BCE and examined in Chapter 3, characterized by large planned cities, standardized weights and measures, and extensive craft production and trade.
Kachi Plain
The plain in Balochistan, present-day Pakistan, where the Bolan River emerges from the mountains, home to the early farming settlement of Mehrgarh.
Ghaggar-Hakra System
A largely dry historical river system in the eastern portion of the greater Indus cultural zone, associated with Early Harappan and later Mature Harappan settlement.
Fertile Crescent
The broader region of Southwest Asia, including Mesopotamia and neighboring areas, where some of the world's earliest plant and animal domestication took place, closely connected to developments in South Asia's northwestern borderlands.
Settlement hierarchy
Differences in size and, plausibly, function or status among neighboring settlements within a region, used by archaeologists as possible evidence of growing social or economic complexity.
Material culture
The physical objects — tools, pottery, ornaments, architecture — produced and used by a society, serving as the primary evidence for reconstructing societies that left no writing.
Faunal assemblage
The collected animal remains recovered from an archaeological excavation, analyzed to reconstruct diet, herding, and domestication.
Floral assemblage
The collected plant remains recovered from an archaeological excavation, analyzed to reconstruct diet, cultivation, and domestication.
Mehrgarh
The early farming settlement on the Kachi plain in Balochistan; its aceramic Period I is now dated, per a 2025 radiocarbon reassessment, broadly to c. 5250–4650 cal BCE, substantially later and shorter than older excavator estimates, providing the best-documented sequence for the origins of farming in South Asia.
Attirampakkam
Archaeological site in Tamil Nadu providing some of the most securely dated early Acheulian evidence in South Asia.
Bhimbetka
Complex of rock shelters in central India with a long occupation sequence from the Paleolithic through the Mesolithic, notable for stone tools and rock art.
Kot Diji
Site in Sindh giving its name to an Early Harappan cultural tradition marked by growing settlement size and fortification.
Amri
Site in Sindh associated with a distinct Early Harappan regional cultural tradition.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has asked students to take seriously a period of history that leaves no written trace and that unfolds across timescales difficult to hold in mind alongside the centuries and decades that occupy most of this textbook. That difficulty is itself instructive. Learning to read a mudbrick foundation, a scatter of carbonized barley seeds, or the changing shape of a goat's leg bone as historical evidence, with the same seriousness historians bring to an inscription or a chronicle, is a skill this textbook will continue to draw on whenever later chapters confront gaps, silences, or contested evidence in the historical record.

The developments traced in this chapter — the long adaptation of Paleolithic and Mesolithic foragers to a changing environment, the gradual and regionally uneven adoption of cultivation, herding, and permanent settlement, the growth of craft specialization and long-distance exchange, and the emergence of increasingly complex Early Harappan regional traditions — did not by themselves constitute a civilization in the sense historians usually mean by that word. But they created every precondition that civilization would require: a reliable agricultural surplus, durable architecture, specialized craft production, exchange networks capable of moving goods and ideas across long distances, and communities large and stable enough to sustain increasingly complex forms of social organization.

Students who understand that the rise of Indus civilization did not spring from nothing, that South Asia's transition to farming was plural and regionally varied rather than singular and uniform, and that even ordinary-seeming villages such as Mehrgarh were embedded in surprisingly wide networks of contact and exchange, are well prepared to encounter the great cities of Chapter 3 not as a sudden miracle but as the achievement of many generations of farmers, herders, and craftspeople whose names have not survived but whose work is written, patiently and permanently, into the archaeological record.

Chapter 3 now turns to that achievement directly: the emergence, around 2600 BCE, of the Mature Harappan urban transformation, and the great planned cities — Harappa, Mohenjo-daro, and Dholavira — that arose from the long foundation this chapter has traced.
