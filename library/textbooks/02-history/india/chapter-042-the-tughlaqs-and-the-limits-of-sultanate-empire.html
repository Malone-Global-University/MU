The Tughlaqs and the Limits of Sultanate Empire

Indian History: South Asia from the Indus Civilization to the Present

Chapter Opening

In the later 1330s, a visitor arriving at the court of the Delhi sultan could have learned a great deal about the ambitions of a medieval state simply by studying its coins. Alongside the familiar gold and silver pieces, the royal mint had briefly issued copper and brass tokens bearing inscriptions that commanded obedience to the sultan and was asking subjects to accept them as the equivalent of silver. Within a few years the experiment was over, the treasury was exchanging precious metal for the discredited pieces, and the government that had issued them was struggling with revolts in Bengal, the far south, and the Deccan. The coin is a small object, but it points toward the central problem of this chapter: how a state that had acquired extraordinary reach in the early fourteenth century lost the ability to turn that reach into durable authority.

Chapter 41 followed the Khalji dynasty from the revolution of 1290 to its collapse in 1320, describing Alauddin Khalji's market regulations, revenue reforms, defense against the Mongols, and campaigns into the Deccan. This chapter begins at the point where that story ends. In 1320, after the brief seizure of the throne by Khusrau Khan, a frontier governor named Ghazi Malik defeated the usurper and became Sultan Ghiyath al-Din Tughlaq. The dynasty he founded ruled in Delhi, in a steadily shrinking sense, until 1414. Its history can be divided naturally into three phases: the brief, sober reconstruction under Ghiyath al-Din (1320–1325); the long, restless, and ultimately disastrous reign of his son Muhammad bin Tughlaq (1325–1351); and the long reign of Muhammad's cousin Firuz Shah (1351–1388), who accepted a smaller sphere of authority and turned to canals, cities, and accommodation with powerful groups. A final phase, from 1388 to 1414, saw succession wars, regional secession, and the devastating invasion of Timur in 1398–1399.

The reign of Muhammad bin Tughlaq has long attracted the attention of historians and general readers alike, in part because the medieval sources describe him in contradictory terms. The chronicler Ziya al-Din Barani, who knew him personally, portrays a learned, generous, and fearsome ruler whose policies repeatedly went wrong. The poet Isami, writing in the Deccan under a dynasty that had broken away from Delhi, offers a hostile account. The Moroccan traveler Ibn Battuta, who served the sultan as a judge in the 1330s, admired his generosity while recording episodes of terrible violence. Modern historians have sometimes treated the sultan as an eccentric visionary or a failed idealist. This chapter takes a different approach. It asks what Muhammad was trying to accomplish, how his officials attempted to carry out his plans, why those attempts encountered resistance, and what the difficulties reveal about the practical limits of governing a vast realm from a single capital with the communications, revenue systems, and military organization available in the fourteenth century.

The chapter also resists two other simplifications. Firuz Shah is sometimes remembered as a benevolent reformer who undid the harm of his predecessor, and his reign did indeed produce impressive canals, cities, and charitable institutions. But the same reign extended hereditary claims to office and revenue, relied heavily on enslaved labor and soldiers, and left the military and fiscal machinery of the state in a condition that made the crisis after his death far more severe. Timur's invasion, in turn, is sometimes presented as the event that destroyed a healthy empire. In fact, by 1398 the Delhi sultanate had already lost most of its southern and eastern territories, its governors had established autonomous states, and its court was divided between rival sultans. Timur sacked a political structure that was already fragmenting.

The evidence for these events is abundant but unevenly distributed and often hostile or retrospective. Persian chronicles written at or near the court, such as Barani's Tarikh-i Firuz Shahi and the later work of the same name by Shams-i Siraj Afif, provide narrative frameworks but also reflect the moral and political commitments of elite authors. Arabic travel accounts and administrative writing from outside India supply important independent perspectives. Coins, inscriptions, and surviving buildings give material evidence that can sometimes confirm and sometimes complicate the chronicles; Firuz Shah's own memoir, the Futuhat-i Firuz Shahi, was in part inscribed on a mosque in the city he founded. Archaeology and historical geography help recover canal routes and settlement patterns that no chronicle describes fully. Throughout this chapter, students are asked to consider who wrote each source, for whom, and how its purposes shape what it reveals.

The chapter also looks outward. The Tughlaq state was part of a Persianate and Islamic world that stretched from Cairo to Samarkand, and its rulers cared about recognition from the Abbasid caliph in Cairo. It was threatened by the Chaghatai Mongols and, eventually, by Timur. It was a major consumer of horses from Central Asia and a destination for scholars, Sufis, soldiers, and merchants from Iran, Central Asia, and the Arab world. At the same time, the sultans governed a population overwhelmingly drawn from South Asian agrarian and urban communities, Hindu and Muslim, whose cooperation or resistance determined whether orders from Delhi could be carried out in the countryside. Understanding the Tughlaqs requires attention to both of these worlds.

By the end of the chapter, students should be able to explain why the Tughlaqs, who inherited the Khaljis' imperial ambitions and expanded the mechanisms of the state, nevertheless presided over the loss of most of the Deccan, the south, and the east, and why Delhi's authority had contracted to a small region around the capital by 1414. They should also understand that territorial reach, administrative ambition, and durable political authority were three different things. Chapter 43 picks up the story with the Sayyid dynasty founded by Khizr Khan and carries it through the Lodis to the end of the Delhi Sultanate in 1526, while Chapters 45, 46, and 47 examine the states that emerged from Delhi's retreat: Vijayanagara, the Bahmani Sultanate and its successors, and the regional sultanates of Bengal, Gujarat, and elsewhere.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how Ghazi Malik came to power in 1320 and how Ghiyath al-Din Tughlaq reconstructed royal authority after the Khalji collapse.
  2. Analyze the purposes and implementation of Muhammad bin Tughlaq's major policies, including the capital transfer, revenue demands in the Doab, agricultural projects, and the token currency.
  3. Evaluate the strengths and limitations of Barani, Isami, Ibn Battuta, Afif, and other sources for reconstructing the Tughlaq period, and explain how each author's position shaped his account.
  4. Distinguish between plans, preparations, and actual achievements in episodes such as the Khurasan project and the Qarachil expedition.
  5. Explain why regional powers in Bengal, Ma'bar, the Telugu country, Karnataka, and the Deccan broke away from Delhi's authority during Muhammad's reign.
  6. Compare the political strategies of Ghiyath al-Din, Muhammad bin Tughlaq, and Firuz Shah in revenue, military organization, and relations with elites.
  7. Assess Firuz Shah's canal system, urban foundations, charitable institutions, religious policy, and use of enslaved labor, distinguishing documented achievements from the claims of laudatory chronicles.
  8. Analyze how hereditary claims to office and revenue assignments affected military capacity and administrative control after Firuz Shah.
  9. Trace the succession crises of 1388–1394 and the emergence of autonomous successor states in Jaunpur, Gujarat, and Malwa.
  10. Interpret Timur's invasion of 1398–1399 using competing sources, separating ideological justification from military and economic realities.
  11. Explain why the fragmentation of Delhi's authority cannot be attributed to a single ruler, policy, or invasion.
  12. Connect the Tughlaq experience to the larger themes of Volume II concerning state formation, regional power, and the limits of imperial rule.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did the Delhi Sultanate reach extraordinary territorial and administrative ambitions under the Tughlaqs, yet lose its ability to govern much of the territory it claimed?
  2. How should historians evaluate a ruler such as Muhammad bin Tughlaq when the principal narrative sources are contradictory, retrospective, and shaped by the political purposes of their authors?
  3. What practical constraints of distance, communication, revenue, and military deployment limited the ability of a medieval state to govern a subcontinental realm?
  4. To what extent were the failures of Muhammad's experiments caused by flaws in their conception, and to what extent by implementation, environmental crisis, and political resistance?
  5. In what ways did Firuz Shah's response to his predecessor's difficulties differ from simple reversal, and what new problems did his approach create?
  6. How did the structure of the Sultanate's elite, including foreign-born nobles, slaves, hereditary office holders, and regional governors, shape political stability?
  7. Why did regional states emerge when and where they did, and what did their emergence mean for Delhi?
  8. How should historians separate Timur's ideological justifications for invading India from the material conditions that made the invasion possible and destructive?
  9. How did ordinary cultivators, artisans, and townspeople experience the policies of the Tughlaq sultans?

Historical Context

By the opening of the fourteenth century, the Delhi Sultanate had become the largest and best-organized Muslim-ruled state in South Asia. Its early rulers, whose rise Chapters 39 and 40 traced, had converted the conquests of the Ghurids into a durable territorial state based on military assignments (iqta), a Persianate court, and the revenue of the Indo-Gangetic plain. Alauddin Khalji, as Chapter 41 explained, had defeated repeated Mongol invasions, maintained a very large standing army through price controls and tightened revenue collection, and sent armies deep into the Deccan and the far south, where the kingdoms of Devagiri, Warangal, the Hoysalas, and the Pandyas were forced to pay tribute or submit. These campaigns created an enormous sphere of influence, but they were largely raids and tribute-extraction expeditions rather than permanent administrative annexations. Alauddin's death in 1316 and the unstable years that followed exposed how heavily his system depended on the personality of the ruler and the loyalty of a few commanders.

The geography that the Tughlaqs inherited was challenging. The core of the Sultanate lay in the Punjab, the upper Gangetic plain, and the Doab between the Ganga and the Yamuna, where reliable rainfall and river water supported relatively dense agricultural populations and the revenue demanded by a large army. Beyond this core lay regions with very different environments and political structures: the arid Rajasthan and Sindh frontiers; the delta country of Bengal, separated from Delhi by long distances and seasonal rivers; Gujarat with its rich ports; the Deccan plateau, with its black-soil cotton lands and strong regional dynasties; and the southern peninsula, where Tamil, Telugu, and Kannada-speaking communities had their own political traditions. Crossing these regions required weeks or months of travel, and the monsoon rendered large areas difficult to traverse for several months each year.

The political society of the Sultanate was also complex. The ruling elite included Turkic and Afghan military families, immigrants from Iran and Central Asia who arrived as soldiers, scholars, Sufis, and administrators, converts from local communities, and, at the lower levels of administration, Hindu officials, revenue collectors, and accountants. Rural society was controlled at the local level by hereditary intermediaries, known in the sources by various terms including khut, muqaddam, and chaudhuri, who collected revenue from cultivators and were essential to the sultanate's fiscal system. Chapter 44 examines these social structures in greater detail. For purposes of this chapter, the important point is that the sultan's authority depended upon cooperation, coercion, or compromise with local power-holders whose interests did not always match those of the court.

The external environment was equally important. The Mongol Ilkhanate in Iran, the Chaghatai khanate in Central Asia, and the Mongol-descended and Mongol-influenced rulers who followed them remained a threat to the northwestern frontier throughout the fourteenth century. The Mamluk sultanate in Cairo, which sheltered the Abbasid caliph after the Mongol destruction of Baghdad in 1258, was a source of religious legitimacy that Muhammad bin Tughlaq sought to cultivate. The Indian Ocean trade that connected Gujarat, Malabar, and the Coromandel coast to Arabia, Iran, and Southeast Asia, discussed in Chapter 49, brought wealth to coastal regions that Delhi found difficult to control. And a long climatic and monetary background, including a regional shortage of silver in the fourteenth century, shaped the economic conditions in which Tughlaq monetary and fiscal policy was made.

This chapter is also a study in sources. Almost everything students will read here depends on a small number of authors whose perspectives were shaped by their own positions. Barani wrote after Muhammad's death, under Firuz Shah, as a disappointed former courtier with strong views on how Muslim rulers ought to govern. Isami wrote under rivals of Delhi. Ibn Battuta wrote decades after his experiences, from memory and with the assistance of an editor, and with a traveler's interest in marvels. Afif wrote at the end of the century, after the dynasty's decline, with a nostalgic picture of Firuz Shah's reign. Students should read each description of a Tughlaq policy as a claim to be evaluated, comparing narrative accounts with coins, inscriptions, buildings, and the observed behavior of the state.

Section 42.1

From Khalji Collapse to Tughlaq Rule, 1320

The Khalji dynasty did not decline gradually. After Alauddin Khalji's death in 1316, a succession of palace struggles consumed the court, and by 1320 the last Khalji sultan, Qutb al-Din Mubarak Shah, had been murdered by his favorite, Khusrau Khan, a convert from a community of Gujarat origin who had commanded forces in the Deccan and Malabar campaigns. Khusrau Khan took the throne in Delhi and ruled for only a few months. Barani, writing decades later, condemns his reign as a disaster for Islam, claiming that his Hindu supporters desecrated the mosque and its copies of the Qur'an. Modern historians treat these accusations cautiously, since Barani's account is shaped by his conviction that an elite of Turkic and long-established Muslim families should rule, and since the evidence for religious desecration is thin and hostile in origin. What is clear is that Khusrau Khan's rule was unpopular with many of the old Khalji nobles and with commanders on the frontier who regarded him as an upstart who had murdered a legitimate sultan.

The man who exploited this discontent was Ghazi Malik, governor of Dipalpur in the Punjab. Barani describes his father as a Turkic slave who had served under Balban and his mother as a Jat woman, a background that placed him within the military society of the northwestern frontier rather than the older court aristocracy of Delhi. For years Ghazi Malik had commanded the defenses against Mongol raids, and the troops under his command were seasoned. He gathered support from other governors, including his son Fakhr al-Din Jauna, later known as Ulugh Khan, and marched on Delhi. In the late summer of 1320 his forces defeated Khusrau Khan's army near the capital, and Khusrau was captured and executed. According to the chronicles, Ghazi Malik declined the throne at first, asking whether any survivor of the Khalji family remained who could take it; finding none, the nobles proclaimed him sultan under the title Ghiyath al-Din Tughlaq.

The story of the reluctant sultan deserves caution. It is a literary pattern that appears in many accounts of usurpations, and it served the interests of those who wished to portray the new dynasty as legitimate rescuers of the state. A more reliable measure of the new ruler's position was the political inheritance he faced. Alauddin Khalji had left the Sultanate with a very large army, a revenue system that had pushed the demands upon rural society to high levels, and tributary relations stretching to the far south. Yet the years of civil conflict since 1316 had loosened control over distant provinces, emptied the treasury, and disorganized the Delhi nobility. Several governors in the Deccan had stopped sending tribute. Ghiyath al-Din's first task was to restore the authority of the center without reproducing the extreme measures of the Khaljis.

The transition of 1320 is also a reminder that the Delhi Sultanate was not governed through an automatic hereditary succession. Legitimacy rested on military success, the recognition of the nobility and religious leaders, the khutba and coinage in the sultan's name, and the sultan's ability to provide security and revenue. The Tughlaqs, like the Khaljis before them, had to demonstrate these capacities repeatedly. This helps explain both the intense attention that Tughlaq rulers paid to ceremony, buildings, and recognition from the Abbasid caliph, and the persistent vulnerability of the dynasty to coups and revolts by its own officers. A history of the Tughlaqs is therefore, in large part, a history of the problem of loyalty: how a ruler persuaded generals, governors, scholars, and local intermediaries that their interests lay in obeying Delhi.

Section 42.2

Ghiyath al-Din Tughlaq and the Reconstruction of Royal Authority

Ghiyath al-Din's reign lasted only about five years, but it established the political framework within which his successors operated. His military background gave him credibility with the troops. He had spent years fighting Mongols on the frontier, and he knew from experience how thin the line between security and invasion could be. On taking power he strengthened the defenses of the northwest, posting trusted commanders and garrisons along the route into the Punjab, and he appears to have secured relative quiet from the Chaghatai Mongols during his reign. This frontier security was a precondition for any attempt to look southward or eastward, and it was never entirely taken for granted by his successors.

Relations with the nobility required careful handling. Ghiyath al-Din rewarded the nobles who had supported his seizure of power, restored the properties and positions of officers whom Khusrau Khan had displaced, and attempted to restore confidence among the old Khalji nobility. Barani, who admired him, claims that the sultan was careful and moderate in his dealings with officials and refused to inflict the harshest punishments that had characterized earlier reigns. This portrait of mildness should be set beside the evidence that he dealt severely with those who threatened his rule, but it reflects a real contrast with the atmosphere of fear under Alauddin and the disorder after him.

His revenue policy sought to relieve the pressure on cultivators without sacrificing the income needed by the state. According to Barani, Ghiyath al-Din issued instructions that revenue assessments should be increased only gradually and by small amounts, not by sudden demands that exhausted peasants and local headmen, and he aimed to encourage cultivation and the return of land to production after the disruptions of the preceding years. He also sought to rein in the extortion of revenue officials and to restore the privileges of local intermediaries whom Alauddin's reforms had weakened. Historians debate how far these measures represented a deliberate reversal of Alauddin's system rather than a practical adjustment. The evidence suggests an effort to rebuild fiscal capacity through moderate and predictable collection, a strategy that stands in notable contrast to the more ambitious and coercive approaches that followed.

The new dynasty also needed a visible center. Ghiyath al-Din ordered the construction of Tughlaqabad, a heavily fortified city a few miles east of the earlier Delhi settlements, on a rocky ridge. Its massive sloping stone walls, bastions, and citadel, built from local quartzite, are among the most distinctive surviving monuments of the Sultanate and express both the military character of the dynasty and the experience of the frontier commander who commissioned them. Construction took place during the early 1320s. The city was not occupied for long; later tradition ties its abandonment to water shortage and to a legendary curse attributed to the Sufi master Nizam al-Din Auliya, who is said to have remarked that the sultan would not reach Delhi. This story, recorded in later hagiographical literature, cannot be confirmed from contemporary sources. It is better read as evidence of the tensions that existed between sultans and independent Sufi centers than as a historical explanation for the city's abandonment.

Taken together, these measures amounted to a program of consolidation. Ghiyath al-Din restored the security of the frontier, rebuilt the sultan's relations with the nobility, moderated revenue demands, and created a monumental seat of power. He also recognized that the authority of the sultan in the Deccan had weakened and that tribute could no longer be assumed. This led directly to the campaigns described in the next section.

Section 42.3

Warangal, Bengal, and the Last Campaigns of Ghiyath al-Din

The first major campaign of Ghiyath al-Din's reign was directed at Warangal, the capital of the Kakatiya king Prataparudra, whose state had paid tribute to Alauddin Khalji's armies but had withheld it during the disorders of the preceding years. In 1321 the sultan sent his son and designated heir, Ulugh Khan, with a large force. The army besieged Warangal, but the expedition failed. According to Barani and others, rumors that the sultan had died circulated in the camp, nobles quarreled, and Ulugh Khan's authority collapsed, leading to the flight of his army. Some officers apparently contemplated changing allegiance. The episode is instructive: even before the reign of Muhammad bin Tughlaq, the army's cohesion in a distant campaign depended heavily on confidence in the central authority and on the stability of the succession.

Ghiyath al-Din responded by disciplining the commanders who had panicked and sending Ulugh Khan back in 1323 with reinforcements. This second expedition succeeded. After a siege, Prataparudra surrendered. He was taken to Delhi, and the Kakatiya kingdom was brought under direct Delhi control, with Warangal renamed Sultanpur. Whether the Kakatiya king died on the way, by suicide or otherwise, is variously reported. The conquest was significant because it marked a step from tribute-taking raids to administration of the Telugu country by officers of the Sultanate. It also began a process whose consequences would unfold rapidly under Muhammad bin Tughlaq: the incorporation of territories far from the center, requiring governors, garrisons, and revenue officials, and thereby creating many opportunities for local resistance and rebel ambition. Regional memory of Kakatiya rule did not vanish, and by the 1330s Telugu commanders, among them the Musunuri Nayakas, were leading effective campaigns to expel the Delhi garrison.

In 1324 Ghiyath al-Din himself marched east to intervene in Bengal, where civil disputes among the sons of the late ruler of Lakhnauti, Shams al-Din Firuz Shah, had weakened the regional sultanate. Delhi's forces entered the region, installed a client ruler, Nasir al-Din, and took one of the rival claimants, Bahadur Shah, back to Delhi as a prisoner. The campaign extended the Sultanate's influence in the east, but it did not create a stable administrative presence. Bengal's distance from Delhi, its network of rivers, and its autonomous tradition made long-term control extremely difficult, and the region would soon slip away, as Section 42.13 explains.

Ghiyath al-Din died in early 1325 on his return from Bengal, when a wooden pavilion constructed for his reception at Afghanpur, near Tughlaqabad, collapsed on him. The cause of the collapse is a classic example of a historical problem involving sources. Barani reports the accident without suggesting that it was deliberate. Ibn Battuta, who visited India several years later and heard the story from informants in Delhi, reports that the building had been designed to collapse and that Ulugh Khan arranged the death of his father. Other later writers repeated or elaborated these suspicions. The account in Ibn Battuta is secondhand, was recorded long afterward, and reflects a view in some court circles that Muhammad was capable of patricide. Contemporary evidence for a deliberate murder is weak, and accidents of this kind happened. Historians generally regard the question as unresolved, while noting that the persistence of the accusation shaped how many contemporaries viewed the new sultan from the beginning of his reign. A responsible account separates the established facts, the collapse and Ghiyath al-Din's death, from the later accusation, which cannot be proven.

Section 42.4

Muhammad bin Tughlaq: Accession, Kingship, and the Problem of the Sources

Muhammad bin Tughlaq took the throne in 1325, a few days after his father's death, and he ruled for twenty-six years. He was already experienced, having commanded armies at Warangal. Contemporaries and later historians agree that he was unusually educated: he was said to be skilled in Persian and Arabic composition, logic, philosophy, medicine, mathematics, and astronomy, a patron and debater among scholars, and an elegant calligrapher. These accounts are generally taken to reflect real intellectual interests, though they are also stock praises of a cultured ruler. His reign is notable for his readiness to experiment, his promotion of officials from various origins, his determination to secure the recognition of the caliph in Cairo, and his severity toward those he saw as disloyal.

The sources on this reign need a careful introduction because they diverge so sharply. Ziya al-Din Barani (c. 1285–after 1357) spent seventeen years at Muhammad's court as a companion of the sultan, then fell from favor and, according to his own account, was nearly ruined. His Tarikh-i Firuz Shahi, completed around 1357 and dedicated to Firuz Shah, offers the most detailed narrative of the reign. But Barani was a moralist with a particular theory of kingship, believing that a ruler should uphold the rank of the highest-born Muslim families, enforce Islamic law against non-Muslims, and keep the "base-born" from office. He is therefore critical of Muhammad's promotion of low-born favorites and of his tolerance toward non-Muslim practices. His chronology is also frustrating; he arranges events by theme and sometimes blurs dates.

Isami, a poet-historian from the Deccan, wrote his verse chronicle Futuh al-Salatin in 1350 at Daulatabad, under the new Bahmani dynasty that had just broken away from Delhi. His family had been forced to relocate from Delhi to the Deccan in the sultan's capital transfer, and his book is hostile to Muhammad. It is a valuable source on the south and on the transfer, but its purposes are political. Ibn Battuta, the Moroccan jurist and traveler, arrived in Sindh in 1333, reached Delhi in 1334, and was appointed a qadi (judge) there. He remained in the sultan's service until 1341, when he was sent as an envoy toward China. His Rihla, dictated in Morocco in the 1350s to the writer Ibn Juzayy, offers firsthand descriptions of the court, postal system, famine, executions, and gifts, but its detail depends on memory and was shaped by Ibn Battuta's own interests as an Arab scholar and by the literary conventions of travel writing. Other sources include the Arabic geographical work of al-'Umari, compiled in Damascus and Cairo from the reports of travelers returning from Delhi, and the coins and inscriptions of the reign.

The result is that Muhammad bin Tughlaq has come down to us in at least three portraits: the brilliant but impatient and sometimes cruel ruler of Barani; the oppressor of the Deccan in Isami; and the generous, learned, and terrifying patron of Ibn Battuta. Each contains elements of truth and each reflects its author's position. Early modern and colonial scholarship often emphasized the contradictions in his character, sometimes using terms such as "mad" or "mixture of opposites." This chapter does not treat character as a historical explanation. It examines instead the policies, the institutions through which they were carried out, and the circumstances in which they succeeded or failed.

Historical Vocabulary
Sikka and Khutba

In Islamic political practice, two public acts conventionally marked sovereignty: the khutba, the sermon delivered at Friday congregational prayer, in which the ruler's name was mentioned and prayers offered for him, and the sikka, the right to strike coins in one's own name. A ruler who omitted another's name from the khutba or struck coins in his own name was declaring independence. Delhi sultans also sought recognition by the caliph, who after 1258 resided in Cairo, and from time to time placed the caliph's name on coins or in the khutba.

These terms matter in this chapter because rebel governors who broke away from Delhi proclaimed their independence by issuing coins and reading the khutba in their own names, and because Muhammad bin Tughlaq's relations with the caliphate and Timur's imposition of the khutba after 1398 show how the language of sovereignty operated. They are also a reminder that legitimacy was publicly performed.

Section 42.5

The Geography and Military Demands of an Expanding Sultanate

By the early years of Muhammad's reign the Sultanate's claimed territory was larger than at any previous time. Barani, and the Arabic writer al-'Umari, who gathered information from travelers who had visited Delhi, depict a realm that reached from the Indus and Punjab through the Gangetic plain to Bengal in the east and, in the south, far into the peninsula. Al-'Umari's work lists a large number of provinces and describes extensive revenue and military resources, though such figures should be understood as the claims of informants and chroniclers, not as audited statistics. The practical reality was that the Sultanate was governed in different ways in different places. In the Doab and around Delhi, officials collected revenue directly or through assignments (iqta) held by military officers. In frontier or recently conquered areas, the sultan's authority rested on garrisons, fortified towns, and local rulers who accepted tribute and subordination.

Distance imposed severe constraints. A message from Delhi to Daulatabad, or to Madurai, took weeks. Ibn Battuta describes a postal system, with foot-runners stationed at short intervals along the roads and mounted couriers for urgent communications, which allowed news to travel considerably faster than ordinary travelers. The system gave the sultan impressive information about distant events, and he used it to hear of revolts and to send orders. But communication was not the same as control. Armies had to be paid, fed, and supplied with horses, many of which were imported from Central Asia and Iran. A single campaign to the south required months of marching, and the monsoon could halt operations entirely. Revenue collected in the Deccan was often spent locally, whereas revenue from the Doab had to support the central army.

Military deployment posed a persistent dilemma. The northwestern frontier required a standing force to counter the Chaghatai Mongols, whose invasion in the 1320s, led by Tarmashirin, reached the neighborhood of Delhi before it was repelled. At the same time, administering the Deccan and the south required garrisons and mobile columns. A ruler who stripped the north to secure the south risked a Mongol attack; one who relied on tribute from distant rulers risked rebellion. Muhammad's governors included military men on assignment, many of them foreign-born or of non-hereditary status whose loyalty depended on the sultan's favor. This made them reliable only so long as the center could pay and reward them, and each new region added to the burden.

The ambitions of the Tughlaq state should therefore be understood in relation to this geography. The sultan's major projects, including the transfer of the capital, the intensified revenue assessment of the Doab, the token currency, the Khurasan preparations, and the Himalayan expedition, can be seen as attempts to resolve problems of distance, military cost, and revenue scarcity. Whether the solutions were well chosen is a matter of debate, but the problems were real. The map study below invites students to look at the spatial dimensions of those problems.

Section 42.6

Delhi and Daulatabad: The Capital Transfer

Among the best known of Muhammad bin Tughlaq's decisions was the transfer of the capital from Delhi to Daulatabad, the new name given to Devagiri, the former capital of the Yadava rulers of the northern Deccan. The sultan's reasoning is not recorded in his own words, but the logic can be reconstructed from the sources and from geography. Daulatabad stood in a central position in the peninsula, at a strategic crossroads and in an area that had been brought under Delhi's rule in the preceding decades. A court established there could in principle direct the new southern provinces more effectively while still remaining within reach of the north. Some historians note that the move also placed the court farther from the Mongol frontier, though a capital in the Deccan would have been more exposed to the north-west only in the sense of distance from the supply of horses. The sultan may also have sought to curb the influence of old Delhi elites, including powerful ulama and nobles, by relocating the machinery of government.

The transfer took place in stages in the late 1320s, and the chronology is uncertain: Barani is vague, and modern scholars place the main move somewhere between about 1327 and 1329, with the return to Delhi beginning in the following decade. The sources do not support the popular belief that the entire population of Delhi was moved across a thousand-mile road. What the evidence does show is that the sultan ordered the relocation of the elite and of those on whom the functioning of government depended: nobles and their households, officials, scholars, Sufi leaders, soldiers, and some artisans and merchants. Isami, who experienced this migration as a child, describes the hardship and mortality of the journey, the grief of people torn from their homes, and the resentment that it created. Ibn Battuta, who arrived after the main move, claims that the sultan ordered the people out of Delhi and that a search found only a lame man and a blind man left. This anecdote is a vivid exaggeration, perhaps repeated in good faith, but it cannot be accepted literally; Ibn Battuta himself lived in a functioning Delhi in the 1330s.

Daulatabad's fortress was substantial and the city was equipped with roads, wells, and buildings. A Sufi presence developed there, and the migration helped spread Persianate court culture and Muslim scholarly and mystical communities into the Deccan, an effect that would shape the later Bahmani court (Chapter 46). That was not the intention of the order, but it was one of its long-term outcomes. For the affected communities the costs were severe. Families were divided, property was abandoned or sold, and the journey in summer heat was dangerous. Contemporary accounts say that many died, and that the sultan paid compensation or provided assistance, though the extent of this is unclear. The movement also deprived Delhi of administrative and commercial vitality for a time.

The experiment was abandoned within a few years. The reasons given include difficulties in supplying the new capital, the distance from the grain-producing north, the persistence of problems on the northern frontier, and the fact that Delhi remained the symbolic and economic center of the realm. The sultan ordered the return, and many of those who had moved were permitted or required to go back, though some remained and became part of Deccan society. The affair illustrates a recurring pattern in Tughlaq administration: a bold plan with a coherent strategic rationale, executed with insufficient attention to its human and logistical costs, and reversed when its burdens became obvious. It also exposes how heavily the Sultanate's administration relied on a relatively small elite whose cooperation was indispensable.

Section 42.7

Revenue Demands, Famine, and Rebellion in the Doab

The Doab was the most productive and revenue-rich region of the Sultanate, and it was the setting of one of the sharpest crises of Muhammad's reign. According to Barani, the sultan ordered a large increase in the assessment of revenue in the Doab, raising it, in the author's words, by ten or twenty times, and imposed additional cesses (abwab) that had not previously been customary. The numbers should not be accepted literally; Barani's rhetoric is hyperbolic, and he does not give assessments that can be checked. But the essential claim, that the government sharply increased its demands and that the peasantry resisted, is credible and is supported indirectly by other evidence of distress in the region.

The reasons for the increase are generally explained by the government's fiscal needs. The sultan had very large expenses: the army, the costs of governing and campaigning in the south, the expense of moving the capital, the lavish gifts and salaries that Ibn Battuta describes, and the preparations for ambitious expeditions. The Doab was the natural source of additional revenue. The measure also fit the Sultanate's long-term struggle against rural intermediaries who retained a share of the produce and who sometimes withheld revenue or resisted officials. Alauddin Khalji had attempted to curb them, and the Tughlaq administration continued to press for closer assessment.

The timing proved disastrous. Drought and famine struck the northern plains in the 1330s, and Ibn Battuta, arriving in Delhi in 1334, describes severe scarcity and high prices. Cultivators who could not pay fled, abandoned their fields, or in some cases took to the jungle and joined armed resistance. Officials who tried to collect the demanded sums used force, and Barani describes brutal punishments, the burning of crops, and the hunting of fugitives. Revolts by village headmen and cultivators broke out in the Doab, and the sultan's response was to send troops to put them down. The destruction reduced the productive capacity of the very region on which his revenue depended, leaving the state poorer.

The chronology of these events is disputed. Barani presents the Doab taxation, the capital transfer, and the token currency as a sequence, but some historians argue that they overlapped, or that the fiscal and agrarian crisis intensified later in the 1330s with the famine. What can be said is that the famine and the fiscal demand came together and reinforced each other. The government did attempt famine relief, distributing grain from state stocks in Delhi and offering aid, according to Ibn Battuta and Barani, but this was inadequate to the scale of the crisis and was concentrated in the capital. The episode highlights an important structural feature of the Sultanate: it depended on rural intermediaries and cultivators who could not be compelled indefinitely, and the fiscal system had little elasticity in a climatic emergency.

Section 42.8

Agricultural Recovery and the Diwan-i Kohi

The government responded to the agrarian crisis not only with relief and repression but also with an effort to rebuild cultivation. Around the late 1330s, Muhammad established a new department of agriculture, the diwan-i amir-i kohi (commonly shortened to diwan-i kohi), headed by an officer with the title amir-i kohi. Its purpose was to bring more land under the plough, to encourage better cultivation, and to increase production of the staple and commercial crops that the state needed. This was an unusual initiative. Few premodern South Asian governments created a department whose explicit task was agricultural expansion, and it shows that the sultan and his advisers understood the connection between agricultural production and political power.

According to Barani, the scheme was to take a block of land, often described as about sixty square miles, and to organize cultivation there on a rotation of crops, with the government providing capital, seed, oxen, and advances (sondhar or taqavi loans) to cultivators, and with officials supervising the work. The plan was to move from cultivation of wheat to other crops and to improve the produce. Officers were appointed to oversee the process, and large sums were advanced. Barani, however, reports that the scheme was a failure: the officials were not competent or honest, much of the money was misappropriated, the land was of poor quality or inadequately chosen, and cultivators did not benefit. After about three years the project was abandoned, with a substantial loss of public money.

Historians must be careful in assessing this evidence. Barani is the main source, and his judgment is hostile. We do not know how much land was actually brought into cultivation, whether the government's loans were recovered, or whether some local successes were overshadowed by the larger failure. The difference between government intention and demonstrable results is important here. The scheme reveals the sultan's willingness to try institutional solutions and to invest in production; it also shows the limits of a bureaucracy that was expected to carry out ambitious orders without adequate experienced personnel, local knowledge, or reliable accounting. Moreover, the project was launched during or after a famine, when cultivators were scarce, and in a political climate where officials were more likely to punish failure than to report it honestly.

The diwan-i kohi is also worth remembering because it contrasts with the approach of Firuz Shah, who a generation later pursued agricultural expansion through irrigation works, canals, and settlement, as Section 42.18 describes. Both rulers understood that revenue depended on cultivation. The difference lay in method: Muhammad relied on direct state management of production, whereas Firuz relied on infrastructure and the cooperation of landholders. The later approach produced longer-lasting and better documented results.

Section 42.9

Token Currency: Monetary Innovation and the Problem of Trust

Muhammad bin Tughlaq's issue of token coinage is one of the best-known episodes in medieval Indian history. Around the early 1330s, the government introduced copper and brass coins and ordered that they be accepted at the value of the silver tanka. This was a form of what economists call token or fiduciary money: coins whose face value exceeded the value of the metal they contained, accepted because of the authority of the issuer. The experiment is now usually dated, on the basis of numismatic evidence, to about 1329–1332, though earlier scholarship placed it in different years, and the chronicles do not give precise dates.

Historical Vocabulary
Tanka, Jital, and Fulus

The standard silver coin of the Delhi Sultanate was the tanka, weighing roughly 11 grams in the Tughlaq period, with gold coins (often called dinars in the sources) issued for large transactions. The jital was a small billon or copper coin used in everyday exchange, with a fixed number to the tanka. Fulus (singular fals) is an Arabic-derived term for copper coins. A token currency is money that circulates above the market value of its metal because the state commands its acceptance and promises, or is trusted, to redeem it. These terms matter here because Muhammad's experiment sought to give base-metal coins the purchasing power of silver.

Why did the sultan do this? Barani suggests that the treasury had been exhausted by expenses and by the cost of the sultan's generosity and military preparations. Other explanations point to a wider regional shortage of silver: in the fourteenth century, supplies of silver from Central Asian mines appear to have declined, creating pressure on monetary systems across a large area. The Mongol-ruled states of Iran and China experimented with paper money, and Muhammad may have been aware of these precedents, though this is conjecture; the Tughlaq tokens were of metal, not paper. The sultan may have hoped that a flexible currency would allow the state to finance its campaigns and to increase the money supply when silver was scarce. Whether the measure was conceptually unsound or only badly executed is a major question taken up later in the chapter's Historical Debate.

The failure is described by the sources in terms of trust. Token coins depend on the confidence of users that they will be accepted by others and redeemed by the state. Barani and Ibn Battuta both report that the tokens were easy to counterfeit: the coins were relatively simple in design, and, according to Barani, many people, especially in the countryside, began to produce their own. Barani's charge that Hindu households turned their houses into mints reflects his religious prejudice, but forgery was plausible as a general phenomenon. Merchants refused to accept the coins in trade, foreign traders avoided them, and prices rose. Landholders paid revenue in the tokens, while the state could not easily distinguish authentic pieces from forgeries. After a few years the sultan withdrew the experiment and agreed to exchange the tokens for gold and silver coins. According to Barani, such large quantities of tokens were brought to the treasury that they piled up in heaps, and the state suffered heavy losses, though the figures cannot be confirmed.

Numismatic evidence supplements the chronicles. Surviving copper and brass pieces from the reign bear inscriptions in Arabic and Persian that stress obedience to the sultan; one type, for example, links obedience to the ruler with obedience to God. Such phrases show an explicit attempt to build trust through the language of religious and political authority. The survival of these coins in modern collections demonstrates that the issue was actually made and was substantial. Their very survival, however, also underlines a limit of the evidence: it does not by itself reveal how many were produced, how widely they circulated, or how much they were discounted in the market.

Section 42.10

The Khurasan Project and the Ambitions of Foreign Policy

Barani reports that Muhammad bin Tughlaq planned to send an army to conquer Khurasan, the region of eastern Iran and western Afghanistan, and that he recruited a very large force for this purpose, giving the figure of 370,000 men, paying them a year's salary in advance, and keeping them in service for about a year before disbanding them. Modern historians treat the number with great skepticism: it is far larger than any army the Sultanate is otherwise known to have fielded, and it is typical of the exaggerated figures in medieval chronicles. The substantial point is that a large mobilization occurred and was later abandoned, at considerable expense.

The reasons for the plan can only be inferred. In the 1330s the Mongol successor states in Iran and Central Asia were unstable. The Ilkhanate disintegrated after 1335, and the Chaghatai khanate was divided. This created, for a ruler in Delhi, both an opportunity and a risk: the opportunity to expand the northwestern frontier and to seize the strategic regions that had long supplied the Sultanate with horses and soldiers, and the risk that unrest in the north-west would spill over into the Punjab. Some historians have suggested that the project aimed at a defensive forward policy, securing the passes of the Hindu Kush against future invaders, rather than a fantasy of conquering Iran. Others emphasize the sultan's ideological ambitions as a Muslim ruler seeking recognition as a major power in the Islamic world, an ambition also visible in his dealings with the caliphate.

It is essential to distinguish preparations from conquest. There is no evidence that any army marched to Khurasan, fought there, or conquered territory. The sources describe recruitment, payment, and eventual dismissal. The causes for abandonment are generally given as the changing political situation in the region, the absence of the expected opportunity, and, above all, the financial burden. The recruited troops had been paid but not employed, and the cost contributed to the treasury's difficulties and, indirectly, to the token currency crisis. The episode was a significant fiscal and political setback. It also illustrates a theme of the chapter: that planning and mobilization at the scale of the Sultanate could be launched by a decision of the sultan, but the recurrent expense of maintaining armies imposed a discipline that even an ambitious ruler could not avoid.

Section 42.11

The Qarachil Expedition and the Himalayan Frontier

The Qarachil expedition, which took place in about 1337–1338, was another project that ended in military disaster. According to Barani, the sultan sent an army, which he describes as 100,000 cavalry, under an officer named Khusrau Malik into the mountainous region of Qarachil in the Himalayan foothills. The goal, according to the chronicler, was to conquer the region and then to move on toward China, or to secure a mountain frontier and secure access to resources; the sources do not agree. The army advanced into the hills, and, once the rains came, supply lines broke down, the troops were attacked by local forces, and disease and hunger followed. Very few men, according to Barani, returned alive.

Several elements of this account are uncertain. The geographical identity of Qarachil is disputed: scholars have proposed locations in the Kumaon and Garhwal hills, in the region of Kullu and Kangra, or elsewhere in the western Himalaya. The precise objectives of the expedition are also unclear. It should not be described as an invasion of China, though some later writers did so; the evidence suggests, at most, an attempt to extend control over a hill region and to open or secure routes. The numbers of troops and casualties come from a source known for exaggeration. What can be said with confidence is that a large force was lost in difficult mountain terrain, that Muhammad's government suffered heavy military and financial costs, and that the failure encouraged contemporaries to see the sultan's reign as marked by overreach.

The expedition belongs to a pattern in which the Sultanate's armies, effective on open plains where cavalry could move quickly, struggled in mountain, forest, or marsh country where logistics, local knowledge, and defensive terrain mattered more. The same difficulties affected campaigns in Assam, in the hills of Orissa, and in parts of the peninsula. It was easier to demand tribute from the rulers of such regions than to hold them. The Himalayan frontier remained, for the Sultanate, an area of limited and indirect influence, a reminder that its military model had geographical limits.

Section 42.12

Court Government, Military Command, and Political Coercion

Muhammad bin Tughlaq governed through a court that combined older Delhi families with newcomers of many origins. The sultan wished to build a loyal administration and recruited widely, drawing on immigrants from Iran, Central Asia, and the Arab world, as well as Indian Muslims and non-Muslims. Foreign-born officers (ghariban) are prominent in the sources, and Ibn Battuta himself was one of them. He also elevated officials of humble origin, which Barani found unforgivable: the historian lists a vintner, a barber, a cook, and gardeners who were given high office, presenting this as evidence of the sultan's disregard for birth and worth. To a modern reader, such appointments may suggest an attempt to build an administration loyal to the ruler rather than to the old aristocracy, but the effect was to antagonize established nobles and ulama who saw their status threatened.

The sultan's search for legitimacy included relations with the Abbasid caliphate in Cairo. In the early 1340s, he received an investiture diploma (mansur) and robes from the caliph, and thereafter issued coins and read the khutba in the caliph's name. This enhanced his standing among the ulama but did not resolve his difficulties with them; he is described as insistent on royal control over legal and religious appointments, and some jurists were punished for resistance. The judicial system included qadis in towns, and the sultan personally heard cases and punished officials who acted unjustly, sometimes with extreme severity. Barani and Ibn Battuta both describe cruel punishments: executions, mutilations, and public humiliations of nobles and scholars who were accused of conspiracy, and Ibn Battuta himself was placed under arrest for a time after his association with a Sufi who had incurred the sultan's anger.

The coercive style of the reign was part of a system. A ruler with limited institutional means to ensure obedience over a vast territory relied on fear, on the personal oversight of his officers, and on the rapid circulation of information through the postal and spy network. The system rewarded those who succeeded and punished those who failed, but it also encouraged concealment, evasion, and, finally, rebellion by officers who feared punishment. Many of the revolts of the 1330s and 1340s were launched by governors and commanders who had reason to fear that failure or suspicion would lead to execution. The sultan's dependence on a small circle of trusted officials also meant that, when they were lost or alienated, his government lacked deep reserves.

He describes the postal service, with foot-runners and horsemen carrying messages across long distances, and he describes the famine conditions in and around Delhi during his stay. His account of the sultan's temper includes stories of mass punishments that left deep impressions on him.

Analysis: Ibn Battuta is the most important eyewitness to the court, and he offers independent confirmation of features also noted by Barani. But he wrote years later, probably relying on notes and memory, and the book was shaped by his collaborator's literary style. He was a foreign scholar dependent on the sultan, with little knowledge of local languages, and his sympathies lay with the learned Muslim elite. He says relatively little about ordinary cultivators or non-Muslim communities, and some of the stories he repeats, including those about the capital transfer and Ghiyath al-Din's death, are secondhand.

Section 42.13

Regional Secession: Bengal, Ma'bar, Vijayanagara, and the Bahmani Sultanate

The central political event of Muhammad's reign was the loss of the southern and eastern provinces. This section is concerned with why Delhi lost control; the later histories of the successor states belong to Chapters 45, 46, and 47. Several factors operated together. Provincial governors were often military commanders with their own troops, revenue assignments, and local alliances, and distance from Delhi lessened the ability of the sultan to supervise or replace them. The sultan's habit of severe punishment made governors reluctant to report failure or to appear in the capital. The famine and fiscal crisis in the north reduced the money available to pay armies and reward loyalty. And in the south, a revival of regional political communities, whose rulers had been defeated or humiliated by Delhi's armies, created opportunities for resistance.

The earliest major secession was in Ma'bar, the Pandya country on the Coromandel coast, where Jalal al-Din Ahsan Shah, the governor installed by Delhi, proclaimed his independence around 1334–1335 and founded the Madurai Sultanate. Muhammad marched south to deal with the rebellion but was stopped at Warangal when an epidemic, reportedly cholera, spread among his troops, and he returned to Daulatabad without restoring his authority. In the Telugu country, leaders of the Kakatiya successors, including Musunuri Kapaya Nayaka, mobilized resistance and took control of Warangal by about 1336, ending direct Delhi rule there. In the region of Kampili and the Tungabhadra, the Delhi government had suppressed a revolt in about 1327–1328; tradition holds that the brothers Harihara and Bukka, who had served the Kampili ruler, were taken to Delhi and later sent back to govern, and that they founded the city of Vijayanagara in the 1330s. This tradition comes from later sources and is debated, but the new state emerged in the region at about this time as a Hindu-ruled kingdom that would become the dominant power in the south (Chapter 45).

In the east, Bengal became independent in stages. In 1338, Fakhr al-Din Mubarak Shah proclaimed himself ruler at Sonargaon in eastern Bengal, and in Lakhnauti another governor soon asserted autonomy. Delhi's military response was limited by distance, the seasonal flooding of the delta, and the sultan's other difficulties. By the 1340s, Bengal was effectively independent under local sultans (Chapter 47). In the Deccan, the revolts of the "amiran-i sada," military officers in charge of centurions' contingents, culminated in 1347 when the commander Hasan, known as Zafar Khan, was proclaimed sultan at Daulatabad under the title Ala al-Din Bahman Shah, and soon moved his capital to Gulbarga, founding the Bahmani Sultanate (Chapter 46).

The pattern was not simply one of "Hindu" or "Muslim" breakaways: Delhi lost control to Muslim governors in Ma'bar and Bengal and to a new Hindu-ruled kingdom in the south, and also to former Delhi officers in the Deccan. The common factor was a loss of the center's capacity to compel obedience at a distance. The following table summarizes the main secessions, to be read as an overview rather than a full account.

Section 42.14

Gujarat, Sindh, and the Final Years of Muhammad bin Tughlaq

In the final years of his reign the sultan was occupied with revolts in Gujarat and the lower Indus. Gujarat, with its rich ports and merchant communities, was a vital province, and rebellions there, as well as unrest in Malwa and the Deccan, drew the sultan away from Delhi. One rebel, Taghi, a former slave-officer, held out in Gujarat in the late 1340s. The sultan marched in person to suppress the rebellion in Gujarat in about 1345–1347 and, according to the sources, grew increasingly suspicious of his officers. After repeated pursuits across Gujarat and Kathiawar, Taghi fled to Sindh, where local rulers sheltered him. The sultan followed him into the Indus country in 1350–1351.

Near Thatta, on the banks of the Indus, Muhammad fell ill and died on 20 March 1351. His death in the field brought an end to a reign during which the Sultanate had reached its greatest extent and then lost much of what it had gained. Barani's assessment, often retold, is that the sultan was released from his people and the people from their sultan. This verdict reflects the weariness of those who served him, but historians should treat it as an expression of Barani's moral judgment. The reign left behind exhaustion: a treasury drained by campaigns and failed experiments, an army whose officers had been alienated, and a realm reduced to the north and parts of the central regions.

Whether this outcome was inevitable or the result of identifiable decisions is a subject of long-running debate. The Historical Debate below invites students to examine the question.

Historical Debate

Were Muhammad bin Tughlaq's Policies Impractical in Conception, or Undermined Chiefly by Implementation and Political Constraints?

The question: The capital transfer, the Doab revenue demands, the token currency, and the large military projects all failed or were reversed. Were these policies fundamentally flawed from the beginning, or were they reasonable responses to real problems that failed because of poor execution, bad timing, famine, and the sultan's political circumstances?

Interpretation A — Flawed in conception: Barani, and many later writers, saw the policies as the product of an impatient ruler who disregarded practical constraints and the advice of experienced counselors. On this reading, a capital cannot easily be moved across a thousand miles, a token currency cannot circulate without strong institutions to prevent counterfeiting, and an army of hundreds of thousands cannot be maintained for an expedition without clear aims. The pattern of repeated reversal is taken as evidence of unrealistic planning.
Interpretation B — Reasonable aims undermined by circumstances and execution: Other historians, beginning with biographers of the sultan in the twentieth century such as Agha Mahdi Husain, and including scholars who emphasize the political and military context, have argued that the underlying ideas had rationales: a more central capital, token money in a time of silver scarcity, closer fiscal control over the richest region, and a forward defensive policy on the northwest frontier. On this view the policies failed because of the famine and epidemics of the 1330s, the poor quality and dishonesty of some officials, hostile elites, and the sultan's severity, which discouraged honest reporting. Scholars such as Peter Jackson have also emphasized the pressures of the Mongol threat and the dependency of the Delhi state on a narrow elite.

Evidence: Both interpretations rely on the same limited sources. The chronicles, especially Barani, provide the narrative; coins and inscriptions provide material evidence; comparative evidence from other contemporary states provides context. The difficulty is that the principal narrative source was written by a critic with his own theory of kingship, and the documentary records that would reveal how policies were actually implemented are lost.

Why it matters: If the policies were inherently flawed, the explanation for the Sultanate's decline lies largely in the ruler's decisions; if they were reasonable but undermined by circumstances, the explanation shifts toward structural features of the state: distance, limited bureaucratic capacity, dependence on elite cooperation, and the climate. Many historians now accept a mixed view in which both elements played a role, while stressing that "irrationality" is not a useful historical explanation.

Section 42.15

The Accession of Firuz Shah Tughlaq and a New Political Direction

Muhammad bin Tughlaq left no obvious heir, and his death in the camp near Thatta threw the army into a crisis. The Sultanate's forces, harassed by Sindhi opponents and short of supplies, were in danger of dissolution. The nobles turned to Firuz, a cousin of Muhammad and a son of the sultan's paternal uncle, who was in the camp. Firuz was in his forties, an experienced administrator who had been in the sultan's service, and he was the son of a Hindu Bhatti princess from Dipalpur, a fact that connected the dynasty with local ruling families of the Punjab. According to the sources, he was reluctant, but he accepted the nobles' request and was proclaimed sultan, in March 1351.

His authority was challenged almost at once. The wazir of the late sultan, Khwaja-i Jahan, who had remained in Delhi, put forward a boy whom he claimed to be Muhammad's son and proclaimed him sultan. This created the possibility of a civil war. Firuz led the army to Delhi, and the leading nobles and religious figures judged that the boy's claim was not credible; the wazir submitted and was later killed, according to some accounts on the way to exile. The episode shows the importance of the army's allegiance and of the judgment of influential nobles and ulama in determining the succession.

Firuz Shah's goals differed sharply from those of his predecessor. Instead of seeking to expand or to control every province directly, he sought stability. He reconciled with nobles and religious leaders who had suffered under Muhammad, reversed some of Muhammad's harsh measures, and tried to secure legitimacy through the caliphate: he obtained a fresh investiture from the Abbasid caliph in Cairo and placed the caliph's name on his coins and in the khutba. His principal wazir for most of his reign was Khan-i Jahan Maqbul, an experienced official of Telugu origin who had converted to Islam, and whose long tenure provided continuity. The nobles' support was essential, and Firuz secured it through generous treatment and security of position. The costs of this strategy, particularly for the army and the revenue system, become visible in Section 42.22.

Section 42.16

Military Retrenchment, Bengal, and Sindh

Firuz Shah did lead armies, but his campaigns reflect a choice to avoid the overextension of the previous reign. In Bengal, the two independent sultanates had been united by Shams al-Din Ilyas Shah, who ruled from Lakhnauti and Pandua. Firuz led an expedition in 1353–1354, besieged the Bengal ruler at his fortress of Ekdala, and withdrew after a negotiated settlement, claiming a victory. A second campaign in 1359–1360, against Ilyas's successor Sikandar Shah, also ended without lasting reconquest, after a siege of Ekdala, and with a treaty. The sultan accepted that Bengal could not be held from Delhi, and later relations were conducted on terms resembling those between independent states, with exchanges of envoys and gifts. The chroniclers present these as successes, but the outcome was the recognition of Bengal's independence.

In the northwest, Firuz campaigned in Nagarkot (Kangra) in 1361 and, in the early 1360s, in Sindh against the ruler of Thatta, the Jam, an expedition that suffered from supply problems, disease, and bad guiding and nearly ended in disaster before the sultan negotiated a submission. A campaign in Orissa (Jajnagar) around 1360 achieved plunder and tribute but not annexation. The military outcome of these expeditions shows a ruler who accepted limits on what Delhi could achieve. Firuz also left the Deccan and the south alone: he did not attempt to recover the Bahmani territory or Ma'bar, and he treated the Bahmanis' independence as a fact.

The fiscal burden of these wars, and of an army maintained by assignments and arrears, constrained further ambition. His government was able to keep order in a diminished territory, and for much of his reign the north enjoyed relative peace. But the price was the end of the effort to unify the peninsula under Delhi, and the sultan's passivity left his regional rivals free to consolidate. The decision to accommodate a smaller sphere of authority was deliberate and, in the short term, wise; it also meant that the military institutions of the Sultanate were not tested and renewed.

Section 42.17

Revenue Policy, Provincial Administration, and Fiscal Accommodation

Firuz Shah's revenue policy marked a significant contrast with the previous reign. According to Afif and to the sultan's own memoir, the Futuhat-i Firuz Shahi, he abolished a large number of taxes not sanctioned by Islamic law, retaining only the four that jurists recognized: land tax (kharaj), the poll tax on non-Muslims (jizya), the alms tax (zakat), and the share of war booty (khums). The figure of twenty-odd abolished cesses appears in the sources and may be accurate in spirit, though the practical effect of their abolition is unknown. He also remitted or reduced arrears, and he cancelled the agricultural advances (taqavi) that Muhammad had given and that cultivators had not repaid. These measures eased the burden on cultivators and on local holders.

A major administrative effort was a survey of revenue, undertaken in the 1350s by the officer Khwaja Husam al-Din Junaid, and said to have taken about six years. It produced an estimate of the total revenue of the empire, which Afif gives in a very large sum in tankas; this figure is useful as an indication of the existence of an assessment rather than as a precise calculation. The government's revenue was then fixed on a more stable basis, and Firuz Shah sought to keep assessments moderate. He also increased the salaries of officials and provided for the remission of dues, with the aim of reducing oppression by officials.

The result was an accommodation: the sultan reduced the state's demands on rural society and its intermediaries and tied the nobility to him through generous pay and security. The strategy worked in the sense that the north remained relatively calm. But it reduced the state's capacity to mobilize resources for emergencies and tied it to a stable income that was only sufficient for a limited program. Provincial governors retained substantial autonomy, and the sultan trusted Khan-i Jahan Maqbul to manage the administration. In later years, as the wazir aged and his son sought to succeed, politics at court became more complicated. Section 42.18

Irrigation, Canals, and Agricultural Development

Firuz Shah's most durable legacy was in irrigation. Afif describes a system of canals that brought water from the rivers of the northwest to the dry lands of what is now Haryana and adjacent regions, supporting new cultivation and settlements. The sultan's own memoir also refers to these works. The canals took water from the Sutlej, the Yamuna, and the Ghaggar, among others, and carried it to areas around Hisar, Firuzabad (Delhi), and other new towns. Afif lists several major canals; the historical reconstruction by Irfan Habib and Faiz Habib (2017) has used textual descriptions, maps, and geographical evidence to trace the probable alignments of these channels and to show how a later system of canals followed some of the same routes. Together these studies indicate that the canal program was real, substantial, and linked to settlement.

The works required large amounts of labor, which was drawn from the state's dependents and from conscripted or hired workers. They also required ongoing maintenance and administration of water distribution. According to Afif, the sultan imposed a water charge (haqq-i shirb) amounting to a tenth of the produce of irrigated land, and he consulted jurists about its legitimacy, which they accepted. This created a new form of revenue linked to infrastructure and meant that the canals financed themselves in part. The sultan thus found a way to expand the revenue base without raising rates on existing agriculture.

The effects were significant. New fields were opened, villages were settled, and towns such as Hisar-i Firuza (modern Hisar) and Fatehabad were founded or expanded, supplying grain and revenue to the court at Firuzabad. Afif claims that the canals brought prosperity and made the regions well populated and productive. These claims should be treated with caution; Afif was a laudatory writer, and the evidence for the long-term fate of the canals, which suffered from silting and neglect after Firuz's death and were later restored under Mughal and British administrations, is mixed. But the program stands as evidence of a different model of state action from that of Muhammad: investment in public infrastructure aimed at increasing the productivity of the land and the stability of revenue.

Section 42.19

New Cities, Monumental Architecture, and the Tughlaq Landscape

Firuz Shah was a prolific builder. Afif credits him with founding several cities, among them Firuzabad on the Yamuna north of earlier Delhi (founded in 1354), Hisar-i Firuza, Fatehabad, Firuzpur, and Jaunpur, the last named, in some accounts, for Muhammad bin Tughlaq (Jauna Khan). The royal palace at Firuzabad, known today as Firoz Shah Kotla, included a large mosque and an audience hall, parts of which survive in ruins. The city expressed the sultan's wish for a new center, as Tughlaqabad had expressed Ghiyath al-Din's, and its location on the river facilitated access to water and to the canals. The building style of the reign, with its plain, battered walls, rubble masonry covered with plaster, and restrained decoration, contrasts with the grander monuments of the Khaljis and, later, the Mughals, and reflects the dynasty's financial constraints and tastes. It also made use of local materials and builders, and the sultan relied on Hindu architects and craftsmen.

Firuz also devoted attention to the repair and maintenance of earlier monuments. According to his memoir, he restored the Qutb Minar after damage from lightning, repaired the reservoir of Iltutmish, and renovated other buildings. This conservation was an expression of dynastic continuity and religious piety. His most distinctive act of display was the transport of two ancient pillars of the Mauryan emperor Ashoka (Chapter 16), one from Topra in Ambala district and another from Meerut, to Delhi. Afif describes the elaborate engineering used to move the pillars, wrapped in silk cotton and carried on carts and boats. Firuz erected one at Firuzabad on top of a pavilion and the other near his hunting lodge. The inscriptions on the pillars were in Brahmi script that no one in the fourteenth century could read, and Afif says that scholars tried and failed to decipher them. The sultan seems to have valued the pillars as extraordinary antiquities and as marks of royal prestige rather than as records of Ashoka's edicts.

The construction program had several political meanings. It demonstrated the sultan's capacity to command labor and resources. It reinforced a dynastic claim to continuity with earlier rulers. It provided employment and demand for craftsmen, quarrymen, and merchants. And it helped create new market towns around the capital, in line with the canal and settlement policy. At the same time, the building projects drew on the same stock of forced and dependent labor discussed in Section 42.21, and they did not protect the capital from the disasters of the 1390s. The Tughlaq landscape of Delhi, a succession of walled cities from Tughlaqabad to Jahanpanah (founded by Muhammad bin Tughlaq) to Firuzabad, is a physical record of the dynasty's changing strategies.

Section 42.20

Religious Policy, the Ulama, and the Boundaries of Toleration

Firuz Shah presented himself as a pious ruler who upheld the shari'a, and religious justification pervades his memoir and Afif's account. He consulted the ulama about taxation and policy, funded mosques and madrasas, among them a college at Hauz Khas in Delhi where he was later buried, and sought blessings from Sufi saints, visiting the shrines of the Chishti order. He took action against those he regarded as heretical, such as certain Ismaili or messianic groups and what he described as unorthodox practices at the shrines of saints. In this he differed from Muhammad, who had been more independent of the ulama and more willing to dispute with them.

His policy toward non-Muslims is a matter of historical interpretation. The sources report that Firuz imposed the jizya on Brahmins, who had previously been exempt, and that he opposed the construction of new temples or the revival of rites that he considered illegitimate. The memoir records the execution of a Brahmin at Kohana in the Delhi region who, according to the sultan, led Muslims astray into idolatry. At the same time, evidence shows that the sultan did not systematically suppress Hindu worship, and that non-Muslims continued to serve in administration and as local intermediaries, in the army, and in revenue collection. The memoir's record of his religious acts is a self-presentation designed to display piety to an audience of ulama and fellow rulers, and it tends to emphasize actions that would be seen as meritorious, while the chroniclers' accounts of royal toleration or intolerance may reflect their own preferences.

A historian must therefore distinguish between royal pronouncements, selective acts of enforcement, and the broader practice of governance. The sultan's religious measures aligned him with the ulama and strengthened his legitimacy among Muslim elites, which was important after the contentious reign of Muhammad. They also created a sharper formal distinction between Muslim and non-Muslim subjects than had existed under some earlier rulers. But the Sultanate continued to rule a population that was overwhelmingly non-Muslim, through local intermediaries and officials of many faiths, and the day-to-day relations of communities were shaped as much by local custom, economic dependence, and shared institutions as by royal policy. Chapter 44 examines these social and religious relationships in the longer perspective of Sultanate rule.

Section 42.21

Slavery, State Workshops, Welfare, and Everyday Life

The reign of Firuz Shah saw an extensive use of enslaved and dependent people in the service of the state. Afif reports that the sultan collected a large number of slaves, acquired through war captives, gifts from governors, and purchase, and organized them into the household and state establishments. His figure for the total, about 180,000, appears in the chronicle together with other large numbers, including thousands of slaves who guarded the palace and thousands who were trained as craftsmen. Such numbers are claims requiring evaluation; they may reflect real scale, but they also reflect the author's desire to magnify the sultan's resources, and they cannot be confirmed from other sources. What is clear is that slave soldiers, servants, and artisans formed an important part of the state under Firuz Shah, and that the sultan used governors' obligations to supply slaves as part of the system of tribute and service.

Historical Vocabulary
Bandagan (Slaves of the Sultan)

Bandagan (singular banda) is a Persian term for "servants" or "slaves," used in the sources for people who were in the household and service of the ruler or of a noble. Under Firuz Shah, the term bandagan-i Firuz-Shahi designated the sultan's enslaved servants, including soldiers, attendants, and artisans. Slavery in the medieval Islamic world covered a wide range of conditions: some enslaved people rose to high office and military command, as the early Delhi "slave" sultans had, while others performed menial labor. The term should not be equated with later plantation slavery, though it implied legal subordination, lack of freedom, and the possibility of sale. It matters in this chapter because the reliance on slaves was part of Firuz's attempt to create an administration loyal to him and independent of the nobility, and because it created social hierarchies and political risks that surfaced after his death.

The sultan also established workshops (karkhanas), described by Afif as numbering about thirty-six, which produced goods for the royal household, the court, and the army, including textiles, weapons, saddles, and luxury items. These were staffed by skilled artisans, many of them enslaved or dependent, and supervised by state officials. In addition to producing goods, the workshops served as training institutions for slaves. They also reflect the state's role as a major consumer and organizer of craft production, a theme examined further in Chapter 44.

Welfare institutions complete the picture. Afif and the sultan's memoir describe a government office for charity (diwan-i khairat) that provided support for the marriages of poor Muslim girls, a bureau that sought to find employment for the jobless, hospitals (dar al-shifa) with physicians and medicines, and endowments for the poor. These institutions were in line with the sultan's image as a pious and benevolent ruler and with Islamic conceptions of royal charity. They also bound recipients to the court. Since the evidence comes largely from admiring sources, it is hard to know how many people benefited, but the institutions are consistent with the sultan's priorities and the resources of his reign.

Everyday Life and Social History

Living under Tughlaq Rule

For most people, the great events of the Tughlaq period reached them in the form of revenue demands, rumors of armies, the arrival of officials, or a change in the markets. A cultivator in the Doab in the 1330s experienced the dynasty as a tax collector whose demands might exceed the harvest, and as a recruiter or a rival in the struggle over scarce grain during famine. A cultivator in the canal zone of Haryana under Firuz Shah might experience the state as a provider of water and a new settlement opportunity, but also as a collector of a water charge and a source of conscripted labor. Artisans in the royal workshops had steady work and supplies but little freedom; those in the towns depended on the presence of the court and its demand for goods.

Women appear in the sources mostly as members of royal and noble households or as recipients of charity, such as the marriage grants of Firuz Shah. The queen mother, Bibi Naila, who was of Bhatti origin, and women of the court were important political figures. The experiences of rural women, enslaved women, and women artisans are rarely recorded. The picture that emerges is one of a society organized around hierarchies of status, religion, and dependence, in which the actions of rulers mattered but the daily life of most people was determined by local conditions of land, water, and community.

Section 42.22

Hereditary Offices, Military Obligations, and the Limits of Restoration

A weakness that became more visible in Firuz Shah's later years was the growing importance of hereditary claims to office and to revenue assignments. In earlier periods, rulers had tried to maintain the principle that assignments (iqta) were temporary grants in return for military service, subject to transfer or recall. Afif's account, and other evidence, suggest that under Firuz Shah the practice shifted toward allowing sons to inherit their fathers' positions, assignments, and military rank, even in cases of unfitness. Afif reports an anecdote in which a soldier too old to serve, whose son was sent in his place, was given an exemption, and another in which a clerk, accepting a bribe in gold, passed an unfit horse for military review. The sultan, according to the story, paid the bribe himself so that the soldier could be retained. Whatever the literal truth of the stories, they illustrate a pattern: personal kindness and a desire to avoid hardship came at the cost of the military and administrative discipline of the state.

The consequences were serious. The army became less efficient, as places were filled by hereditary right rather than competence. Revenue assignments became quasi-permanent possessions of families, reducing the sultan's ability to reward new talent and to redeploy resources. The nobles' growing security made them less dependent on the sultan and more likely to act in their own interest when the dynasty faltered. The reliance on slave troops, intended to provide an independent force, created its own problem, since the slaves became a faction with a stake in court politics. The administrative system continued to function so long as the wazir and the sultan maintained authority, but these relationships were personal and fragile.

Firuz Shah's long reign thus achieved a partial restoration: it restored order in the north, rebuilt agriculture and towns, and improved relations with elites and the ulama. But it did so by reducing the demands of the state, accepting the permanent loss of the peninsula and the east, and relaxing the controls that had kept the military and revenue systems responsive. In his old age, a struggle erupted among his sons and ministers; the wazir's son was dismissed, and the sultan's son Muhammad Khan ruled for a time as co-regent before the slaves and nobles fought for control. Firuz died in September 1388, at about eighty years of age, leaving a realm that depended on the authority of a single aged ruler.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From Muhammad bin Tughlaq to Firuz Shah

What Changed

  • The goal of the state shifted from expansion and direct control of a subcontinental realm to the stabilization of a reduced territory centered on the north
  • Revenue policy moved from heavy assessment and state-managed production to lower demands, canal-based expansion, and fiscal accommodation
  • The ruling style changed from personal intervention and severe punishment to patronage, religious legitimation, and consultation with ulama
  • Military organization shifted toward limited campaigns, slave troops, and growing hereditary claims

What Stayed the Same

  • Dependence on the revenue of the Doab and the northern plain
  • The need for recognition from the Abbasid caliph and the ulama
  • Reliance on provincial governors and military assignees who could not be fully supervised
  • The central role of the sultan's personal authority in holding the system together

Who experienced change most directly: Cultivators in the Doab and the canal zones, nobles whose positions became more secure, and the enslaved servants and artisans of the royal household. Who experienced greater continuity: Communities in the southern and eastern regions that were already independent, and local intermediaries whose position depended on cooperation with whichever authority collected the revenue.

Why the pattern matters: Firuz Shah's reign was not a simple reversal of Muhammad's. It changed the means by which the state sought stability, and in doing so created new weaknesses.

Division IV — Fragmentation, Timur, and the End of the Tughlaq DynastySections 42.23–42.26
Section 42.23

The Succession Crises after Firuz Shah, 1388–1394

After Firuz Shah's death in 1388 the dynasty entered a period of rapid and violent succession. His grandson Ghiyath al-Din Tughlaq Shah II took the throne but was killed in 1389 in a conspiracy of nobles and slaves. Abu Bakr Shah, another grandson, succeeded, but within months a rival claimant, Firuz's son Nasir al-Din Muhammad Shah, gathered support at Samana in the Punjab and marched on Delhi. In the struggle, the slave-officers of the late sultan, courtiers, and Hindu and Afghan commanders took sides, and the city was the scene of fighting. Nasir al-Din Muhammad took Delhi in 1390 and ruled until his death in 1394. His son Humayun Khan, who took the title Sikandar Shah, reigned only a few weeks before dying, and a younger son, Nasir al-Din Mahmud, was placed on the throne.

These events produced a divided capital. By the mid-1390s two competing courts existed in the Delhi region: one around Mahmud at Jahanpanah (old Delhi) and another around a rival claimant, Nusrat Khan, a grandson of Firuz Shah, who held Firuzabad. The two sides fought for years, and the nobles shifted their support according to their interests. Real power passed increasingly to ambitious officers, such as Mallu Iqbal Khan, who controlled the person of the sultan. The succession crises show the weakness of a system that depended on the authority of a long-reigning ruler, on a nobility whose offices had become hereditary and whose loyalties were personal, and on slave factions with their own agendas. They also drained the treasury and made the capital an arena of civil war.

The provincial governors reacted with calculation. Some stayed nominally loyal while ceasing to send revenue, while others took advantage of the confusion to establish their own power. The deterioration of central authority did not begin with the death of Firuz Shah; it was the outcome of the structural weaknesses already discussed. But the succession wars removed what remained of the center's capacity to compel obedience.

Section 42.24

The Fragmentation of Delhi's Authority

By the 1390s most of the large provinces outside the Doab and the Punjab were either independent or nominally dependent. The south and the Deccan had been lost in the 1330s and 1340s. Bengal was independent. Now the process spread to provinces that had remained under Delhi's control. In the east, a high official of Firuz Shah's court, Malik Sarwar, who bore the title Khwaja-i Jahan, was sent in 1394 as governor of the region around Jaunpur on the Gomti. He consolidated his authority, extended it over neighboring areas, and founded what became the Sharqi (Eastern) sultanate of Jaunpur, a state with its own cultural and political life. In Gujarat, Zafar Khan, who had been appointed governor around 1391, built up a strong base and in 1407 formally took the royal title as Muzaffar Shah, establishing the Gujarat Sultanate. In Malwa, the governor Dilawar Khan Ghuri ruled increasingly independently from the early 1400s and assumed royal title by about 1406. In Khandesh, an officer's family had established an autonomous principality in the 1380s.

These developments are examined in their own right in Chapter 47, and here the point concerns Delhi. The provinces that left were among the wealthiest, with productive agriculture, ports, and trade. Their loss meant that the Delhi sultan controlled a small area around the capital, parts of the Doab, and portions of the Punjab, with a revenue base far smaller than that required to support the court, the army, and the nobility. Many local leaders, including Rajput chiefs and rural intermediaries in the Doab, took advantage of the weakness to withhold revenue. The slow contraction of Delhi's authority was a more gradual process than a single collapse, and it is best seen as the culmination of centrifugal tendencies that began under Muhammad.

Cause and Effect

The Fragmentation of Delhi's Authority

Long-term causes

Vast distances and limited communications; dependence on provincial governors with their own troops and revenue; a thin administrative bureaucracy; reliance on cooperation from local intermediaries; and the revival of regional political communities in the south, east, and west.

Short-term causes

Fiscal strain and famine in the 1330s; military overreach and failures (Khurasan, Qarachil, the southern campaigns); the sultan's coercive methods, which encouraged officers to rebel; the growth of hereditary claims under Firuz Shah; and the succession wars after 1388.

Event or transformation

Between the 1330s and 1410s, Ma'bar, Telangana, Karnataka, the Deccan, Bengal, Jaunpur, Gujarat, and Malwa passed out of Delhi's control, and the Sultanate contracted to the region around the capital.

Immediate effects

Loss of revenue and manpower, divided courts in Delhi, and the vulnerability of the capital to external attack, which was realized in Timur's invasion.

Long-term consequences

A multipolar political order across South Asia, with new regional states (Chapters 45 to 47) that developed distinct courts, languages, and cultures, and a reduced Delhi Sultanate under the Sayyids and Lodis (Chapter 43).

Historical complexity

No single ruler, policy, or invasion caused the outcome. The fragmentation resulted from interacting structural conditions, particular decisions, environmental crises, and the choices of regional leaders who saw advantage in independence.
Section 42.25

Timur's Invasion and the Sack of Delhi, 1398–1399

Timur (Temür, called Tamerlane in European sources) had built an empire from Samarkand across Central Asia, Iran, and parts of the Middle East by the 1390s. He claimed legitimacy as a ruler within the Chinggisid tradition by installing puppet khans and by marrying into the line, and he presented himself as a champion of Islam. His campaigns relied on mobile cavalry, siege techniques, and calculated terror. The sources for his Indian campaign include Persian histories written for the Timurid court, notably the Zafarnama of Sharaf al-Din Ali Yazdi, and memoirs attributed to Timur whose authenticity is debated, together with accounts by outside writers such as Ibn Arabshah, who was hostile. All were written by authors who praised or condemned him, and the Timurid sources present his campaign as a holy war against idolaters and against the lax Muslim rulers of Delhi.

Timur's own justification emphasized that the sultans of Delhi tolerated their Hindu subjects and did not conduct holy war with sufficient zeal. This ideological claim should be separated from the material and military realities. India offered a rich target for plunder, a weak and divided regime, and the opportunity to gain wealth, captives, and skilled craftsmen. The decision to invade, in 1398, was influenced by reports of Delhi's disorder. Timur's grandson Pir Muhammad had already advanced into Multan, besieged it for months, and taken it in 1398, and Timur crossed the Indus in September of that year.

His army marched through the Punjab, sacking towns, enslaving populations, and killing those who resisted. At Tulamba and other places the army massacred or enslaved thousands. Before the battle for Delhi, according to Timur's chroniclers, he ordered the execution of a very large number of prisoners (the figure of 100,000 is given in the sources and is probably exaggerated), because they could not be guarded during the battle. On 17 December 1398 the forces of Sultan Mahmud and Mallu Iqbal Khan, including war elephants, were defeated near Delhi, and the sultan and his minister fled. Timur entered the city, where, according to the sources, disturbances broke out when his troops sought to seize the inhabitants and their goods. Plunder, massacre, and enslavement followed over several days. Sources disagree on the scale and responsibility, with Timurid writers attributing the destruction to the resistance and the unruly behavior of the city's inhabitants, while others describe a deliberate sack. Skilled craftsmen and others were taken as captives to Samarkand, where they were employed in the construction of buildings. Timur remained in the Delhi region only about two weeks before returning north in early 1399, plundering Meerut and other places on the way.

The material consequences were severe: the death or enslavement of many inhabitants, the loss of wealth and craft skill, the disruption of agriculture and trade in the surrounding countryside, and a famine and epidemic reported in the years after. Timur left behind no administration, only a governor in the Punjab, Khizr Khan, whom he appointed in Multan and Dipalpur. The invasion did not destroy a healthy, centralized state; it struck a divided and exhausted one, accelerating a process of collapse already underway. Its long-term significance lies in how it ended the realistic prospect of Tughlaq restoration and left Delhi's rulers with little prestige or means.

Section 42.26

Delhi after Timur and the End of Tughlaq Rule, 1399–1414

After Timur's departure, Delhi was a ruined and nearly empty city. Mallu Iqbal Khan returned and took control, and Sultan Mahmud, who had fled to Gujarat and Malwa, was eventually brought back to the capital in 1401, a puppet ruler dependent on Mallu. The court controlled a small territory, with Delhi's authority hardly extending beyond the Doab and the nearby region. Rebel officers and Rajput chiefs ignored its demands. In this period the sultanate of Jaunpur, Gujarat, and Malwa consolidated their independence, as Section 42.24 described.

Mallu Iqbal Khan was killed in battle against Khizr Khan in 1405, after which the authority of the sultan became even weaker. Mahmud died in 1412 (some sources give 1413), the last of the Tughlaq line, without an heir. The nobles chose Daulat Khan Lodi, an experienced officer, as ruler. Khizr Khan, who held the Punjab as Timur's governor and had built up forces, marched on Delhi and, in 1414, captured the city. He did not take the title of sultan but ruled in the name of Timur's successors, a pragmatic recognition of Timurid overlordship. His accession began the Sayyid dynasty, whose history belongs to Chapter 43.

The end of the Tughlaqs was therefore not the result of a single catastrophe but of a long series of political losses and failures. The dynasty that had begun in 1320 with the restoration of order ended in 1414 with a succession of weak rulers dependent on soldier-ministers, a small and impoverished realm, and a region of new states that no longer looked to Delhi for authority.

Section 42.27

Review and Historical Synthesis

The three principal Tughlaq rulers confronted related problems: how to secure the frontier against Mongol and Central Asian threats, how to finance an army and court from the revenue of agrarian society, how to govern distant provinces through officers who could not be perfectly supervised, and how to win the loyalty of nobles, ulama, and local intermediaries. Each ruler answered these problems differently. Ghiyath al-Din chose reconstruction and moderation, relying on frontier security, a trusted body of commanders, and measured revenue demands. Muhammad bin Tughlaq chose ambition and experiment: he sought to bring more territory under direct control, to raise more revenue, to create new institutions, and to project power beyond the frontiers, using coercion to obtain results. Firuz Shah chose accommodation: he accepted a reduced realm, lightened the burden on society, invested in infrastructure, and tied elites to the throne through patronage and hereditary security.

Their experience demonstrates that territorial reach, administrative ambition, and durable political authority were not the same thing. Under Muhammad, the Sultanate's claimed territory was at its greatest, yet its capacity to hold that territory was at its weakest, because armies, revenue, and communications could not sustain such extent. Administrative experiments in agriculture, money, and revenue were creative but outstripped the capacity of the officials who had to implement them, in an environment hit by famine and epidemic. Firuz Shah's policies produced a more stable and prosperous core but weakened the military and fiscal discipline of the state. The dynasty's problems were compounded by succession struggles, since the legitimacy of rulers rested on personal authority and the support of factions. When Timur arrived in 1398, he found a polity divided against itself.

Four themes recur across the chapter. The first is geography: distance, rivers, monsoon, and terrain shaped what a medieval state could do. The second is evidence: the narrative sources are partial, and historians must read them alongside coins, inscriptions, monuments, and geographical evidence. The third is the social base of power: the cooperation of local intermediaries, cultivators, artisans, and slaves was essential, and their experiences varied greatly. The fourth is the role of regional agency: the new states of the south, east, and west were not simply parts that fell away, but political communities with their own aims, whose emergence reshaped South Asia. This multipolar world is the subject of the chapters that follow, with Chapter 43 tracing the reduced Sultanate in Delhi under the Sayyids and Lodis, and Chapters 45 to 47 examining the regional states.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced the history of the Tughlaq dynasty from the accession of Ghiyath al-Din in 1320 to the seizure of Delhi by Khizr Khan in 1414. Its central question was why a state that attained extraordinary territorial and administrative ambitions lost the ability to govern much of the territory it claimed. The answer proposed here is that territorial reach, administrative ambition, and durable political authority were distinct, and that the Tughlaqs repeatedly discovered the gap between them.

Ghiyath al-Din, a frontier commander who defeated Khusrau Khan, reconstructed royal authority through frontier defense, moderate revenue policy, and the building of Tughlaqabad. His campaigns against Warangal and in Bengal extended Delhi's reach, and his death in the collapse of a pavilion in 1325 produced an accusation of murder that the evidence does not support firmly.

Muhammad bin Tughlaq inherited a realm of vast extent and pursued bold policies: the transfer of the capital toward Daulatabad, heavy revenue assessment in the Doab, the diwan-i kohi, token coinage, and expeditions to Khurasan and Qarachil. The sources for the reign, especially Barani, Isami, and Ibn Battuta, offer competing portraits and must be read in light of their authors' positions. The policies had defensible rationales but failed amid famine, administrative limitation, and hostility from elites, and the sultan's coercive methods encouraged revolt.

The loss of Ma'bar, Telangana, Bengal, and the Deccan, and the emergence of Vijayanagara and the Bahmani Sultanate, marked the end of Delhi's imperial reach in the south and east. This chapter treated these events as the loss of Delhi's authority, leaving the successor states to later chapters.

Firuz Shah responded by accepting a reduced realm, easing the burden on society, and investing in canals, cities, and charitable institutions. His work was real and sometimes impressive, supported by his memoir, Afif, and geographical evidence. But he also relied on enslaved and dependent labor, drew sharper religious distinctions in policy, and allowed hereditary claims to office and assignments to erode military discipline.

After his death in 1388, succession crises, divided courts, and the rise of powerful officers destroyed what remained of central authority. Jaunpur, Gujarat, and Malwa emerged as independent sultanates. Timur's invasion of 1398 to 1399 delivered a devastating blow to Delhi, but it struck a divided state and did not by itself cause the Sultanate's fragmentation.

Throughout the chapter, geography, evidence, social history, and regional agency were treated as essential to understanding the dynasty. The Tughlaq experience illuminates the practical limits of governing a large and diverse realm in the fourteenth century and prepares the way for the multipolar political order described in the following chapters.

The chapter also emphasized the importance of avoiding simplifications: Muhammad bin Tughlaq was not simply irrational; Firuz Shah was not simply a benevolent reformer; and Timur did not destroy a healthy empire. Each of these judgments, when examined through the evidence, becomes more complex and more historically useful.

Chapter Reflection

The Tughlaq chapter in South Asian history is often remembered through striking episodes: a capital moved, a coin devalued, an army lost in the mountains, a city sacked. This chapter has asked students to look behind those episodes to the structures that made them possible and, often, impossible to sustain. The central lesson is that power over territory is not the same as authority within it, and that the gap between the two is filled by revenue systems, communications, military organization, and the cooperation of people at every level of society.

The chapter also shows how the Delhi Sultanate's contraction opened space for new political communities. The states that emerged in the Deccan, the south, Bengal, Gujarat, and Malwa were not merely remnants of a broken empire but centers of new courts, languages, architecture, and religious life. Chapters 45 through 47 take up their histories, and Chapter 49 shows how the Indian Ocean connected them to a wider world. The history of South Asia from the fourteenth century onward is therefore a history of multiple centers rather than one.

Finally, the chapter reminds students of the importance of reading sources critically. Barani's moral judgments, Isami's hostility, Ibn Battuta's wonder, and Afif's nostalgia each helped create images of rulers that have lasted for centuries. Learning to read such sources alongside coins, canals, buildings, and maps is a skill that will be needed again in the chapters on the Mughals and the colonial period, where the evidence is richer but the interpretive challenges are no less demanding.
