Akbar's Government, Religion, and Imperial Vision

Indian History: South Asia from the Indus Civilization to the Present

Chapter Opening

In 1575, at the new imperial city of Fatehpur Sikri, Akbar ordered the construction of a hall for religious discussion, the Ibadat Khana. The same court that debated the proper limits of religious authority was also, in those very years, measuring fields, appointing collectors of revenue, and drawing up the administrative arrangements that would soon organize the empire into provinces. The two enterprises were not separate. An empire that stretched across the plains of North India and beyond needed both reliable income and a persuasive account of why one ruler should command the loyalty of so many different peoples.

Chapter 51 traced how Akbar acquired and consolidated territorial and military power: his accession in 1556, the defeat of rivals, the incorporation of Rajput lords, the conquest of Gujarat and Bengal, and the formation of the mansabdari and jagirdari arrangements that tied a diverse elite to the throne. This chapter asks a different question. Once an empire had been won, how was it governed, how was it financed, and how did its ruler justify himself to subjects who spoke different languages, farmed under different conditions, and worshipped in different ways?

The chapter's central argument is that Akbar's reign saw the gradual, uneven assembly of a system of government resting on four connected supports: a revenue administration that sought predictable income from agriculture; a hierarchy of provincial and local officials that carried imperial orders downward and revenue upward; a set of religious policies and debates through which the emperor negotiated his relationship with the empire's many communities; and a theory of kingship, elaborated most fully by Abu'l-Fazl, that presented the emperor as a source of justice and order standing above sectarian divisions. None of these supports was invented in a single moment, and none worked as smoothly in practice as it was described in the court's own writings.

Each of the four also had a history before Akbar. Revenue assessment drew on the experiments of the Delhi Sultans and especially on Sher Shah Sur, discussed in Chapter 50. Provincial government built on Sultanate and Sur practice. Mughal ideas of just rule drew on Persian ethical and Sufi traditions as well as Indic ones. For that reason the chapter avoids portraying Akbar as a solitary inventor. Historians have emphasized that reform was gradual, collaborative, and often reversed or modified, and that many of its most famous features are known mainly from sources written by the court's own officials and apologists.

Those sources require careful handling. The most important are the Akbarnama and the A'in-i Akbari of Abu'l-Fazl, a court insider and ideologue; the Muntakhab al-Tawarikh of Abd al-Qadir Bada'uni, a scholar who participated in some of the events he describes and later wrote a bitter and secretly circulated critique; the accounts of the Jesuit visitors who reached the court from 1580; Persian prefaces and translations; farmans and other documents; and, for the village, a far thinner record. The chapter repeatedly asks who wrote each source, for whom, and what it could not see.

The religious history of the reign requires particular care because it has been read for centuries through the lens of later controversies. Some writers have celebrated Akbar as a prototype of modern tolerance; others have accused him of apostasy or of attempting to found a new religion; others have treated his policies as purely calculated statecraft. This chapter evaluates his policies as historical developments shaped by imperial circumstances, personal interests, and competing intellectual positions among Muslim scholars, Sufis, Hindus, Jains, Christians, and others, and it distinguishes what the sources establish from what later polemic added.

The chapter is organized in five divisions of four numbered sections each. Division I examines revenue; Division II provincial and local administration, the court, and justice; Division III the religious landscape, reform, and the Ibadat Khana; Division IV the mahzar of 1579, sulh-i kul, Din-i Ilahi, and opposition; and Division V translation, intellectual exchange, and kingship. The fuller history of Mughal painting, architecture, and literary culture belongs to Chapter 56, and the broader economy and society of the empire to Chapter 55. Chapter 53 follows the dynasty after Akbar's death in 1605.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why the Mughal state depended on agricultural revenue and how Sultanate and Sur precedents shaped Akbar's fiscal system.
  2. Trace the development of land measurement and assessment under Akbar and assess the role of Raja Todar Mal within a collaborative reform process.
  3. Distinguish among the major methods of assessment and collection and explain the difference between assessed revenue and actual collection.
  4. Analyze how cultivators, zamindars, and local record-keepers experienced revenue policy, and identify the limits of imperial supervision.
  5. Describe the structure of provincial and local government, from the emperor and imperial ministers to the subah, sarkar, and pargana.
  6. Reconstruct the Mughal court as an administrative and political institution, including the influence of the royal household.
  7. Evaluate the abolition of the pilgrimage tax and jizya within the political and religious circumstances of the 1560s.
  8. Interpret the Ibadat Khana debates, the mahzar of 1579, and the concept of sulh-i kul without equating them with modern secularism.
  9. Assess competing scholarly interpretations of Din-i Ilahi and of the criticism of Bada'uni and other opponents.
  10. Examine the translation program and the intellectual exchange that surrounded it, distinguishing documented exchange from claims of universal harmony.
  11. Analyze Abu'l-Fazl's model of imperial kingship and distinguish ideal from documented practice.

Historical Context

Akbar inherited an uncertain position. His father Humayun had recovered Delhi and Agra in 1555 but died within a year, leaving a thirteen-year-old successor, a regency under Bairam Khan, and rivals including the Surs, the Afghan lords of the east, and Hemu, whom the Mughals defeated at the Second Battle of Panipat in 1556 (Chapter 51). The empire that emerged over the following decades covered the Indo-Gangetic plain, Malwa, Gujarat, Bengal, Kabul, Kashmir, and Sind, and by the end of the reign it had reached into the Deccan.

That empire was heavily agrarian. The great alluvial plains of the Indus and Ganges systems, watered by rivers, wells, and the monsoon, produced wheat, rice, millet, pulses, oilseeds, sugarcane, cotton, and indigo in varied combinations, and their harvests supplied by far the largest portion of state income. Environmental variation mattered: rainfall differed across the Punjab, Awadh, Bengal, Gujarat, and Malwa; some regions relied on wells and canals, others on rain; and harvests could fail for reasons the state could not control. Revenue policy therefore always depended on local conditions.

The empire's elite was equally varied. Central Asian, Persian, and Indian-born Muslim nobles, Afghan lords, and, increasingly, Rajput and other Hindu chiefs served the throne as mansabdars. The population beyond the court included Sunni and Shia Muslims, Sufi communities, Hindu Brahmins, merchants, warriors, and cultivators, Jain merchant networks, Sikh communities in the Punjab, and, on the coasts, Portuguese traders. The court language was Persian, but most subjects spoke languages such as Hindavi, Punjabi, Bengali, Gujarati, and many others. Governing such a population required translation in the broadest sense, among languages, legal traditions, and religious claims.

Historians depend on an unusually rich but heavily court-centered documentary record for this period. Persian chronicles, administrative manuals, farmans, and later copies of documents survive in quantity, yet they were written chiefly by and for imperial officials. Peasants, women outside the court, and most non-Persian-literate subjects appear mainly as objects of description. Students should keep in mind throughout the chapter that the Mughal government looks more orderly in its own texts than it probably was on the ground.

Revenue Reform and the Agrarian Foundations of Imperial Government

How the imperial government financed itself, and why administrative rationalization did not eliminate regional variation or peasant vulnerability.

Section 52.1

The Fiscal Foundations of Akbar's Empire

A large early modern empire could not survive on conquest alone. Armies had to be paid, officials maintained, courts supplied, roads and forts kept up, and the throne's prestige sustained through display and patronage. In Mughal India the overwhelming source of these resources was the land. Cultivators paid a share of the harvest, assessed in grain or, increasingly, in cash, and the state's ability to collect that share determined how large an army it could field and how many nobles it could attract. Revenue was therefore not a technical matter separate from politics. It was the material basis of the empire.

Akbar's government inherited several earlier traditions. The Delhi Sultans, as Chapters 40 through 43 showed, had experimented with measurement and assessment, most ambitiously under Alauddin Khalji, whose market and revenue regulations aimed to direct a larger share of rural wealth to the treasury. Sher Shah Sur, whose administration Chapter 50 examined, had carried out a survey of cultivated land, fixed a schedule of rates for different crops, and issued written documents to cultivators. The Mughals, who briefly lost north India to the Surs, found in this earlier system a usable model, and some of the officials who had served Sher Shah entered Akbar's service, bringing practical knowledge with them.

The fiscal pressures of Akbar's reign were substantial. Expansion across the plains and into Gujarat and Bengal brought new territory but also new obligations, because every newly conquered region required garrisons, officials, and arrangements with existing local rulers and revenue-holders. The mansabdari system, discussed in Chapter 51, assigned ranks that entailed salary and military obligation, and most nobles were paid by assignments of revenue (jagirs) rather than direct cash. This arrangement made the accurate estimation of expected revenue essential. If the empire's officials could not say what a given territory would yield, they could not assign it fairly to a noble, and nobles in turn would not know what resources they could count on.

For the same reason the state distinguished between lands assigned to nobles and lands reserved to supply the emperor directly. The latter, called khalisa, were administered by imperial officials and supplied the treasury. The proportion of land held as khalisa varied across the reign and became a source of tension, because a larger khalisa meant fewer assignments for nobles. Chapter 51 explained the political logic of that balance; here the point is that the entire structure presupposed that the state could measure, estimate, and collect.

Three problems shaped everything that followed. The first was information: how could a distant court know what a village produced? The second was intermediation: the court did not have enough salaried personnel to reach every village, and had to work through hereditary local figures whose own interests might diverge from the throne's. The third was variation: the Punjab, the Doab, Awadh, Bihar, Bengal, Gujarat, and Malwa differed in crops, rainfall, irrigation, and customary arrangements, so a single schedule could not fit all. Akbar's revenue reforms are best understood as a series of attempts to answer these problems, never completely.

Section 52.2

Todar Mal and the Development of Mughal Revenue Reform

Raja Todar Mal is the name most closely connected with Mughal revenue reform, and he deserves his reputation as a capable and persistent administrator. A Khatri official whose reported birthplace is disputed between Lahore and Laharpur in Awadh, he is traditionally said to have served under Sher Shah before entering Akbar's service, rising through military and financial responsibilities to become one of the empire's leading officials. He is associated with several assignments: a revenue settlement in Gujarat around 1573, work on the empire's financial administration in the 1570s, and a role in the settlement of the early 1580s, after which he served as a senior financial official. He died in 1589.

Yet the history of Mughal revenue reform should not be reduced to one minister. Several officials contributed, among them Muzaffar Khan Turbati, who held a leading financial post in the 1570s, and Shah Mansur, a later diwan. Reform also built on practices already in use, including Sur methods of measurement and rate-fixing. The court's chroniclers, and especially Abu'l-Fazl, had reasons to present reform as the achievement of a wise emperor and his trusted minister, and later tradition amplified Todar Mal's role. Modern historians, notably Irfan Habib in his study of the empire's agrarian system, have reconstructed a more gradual and experimental process.

The experiments of the 1570s illustrate this. In 1574–75, Akbar appointed officials called karoris, each charged with collecting roughly a crore (ten million) of dams, the copper coins in which accounts were kept, from a specified area of crown land. The karoris were to survey, assess, and collect, and they were supervised by clerks and treasurers. The experiment generated information about local conditions and productivity but also complaints of harshness, and it did not permanently resolve the problem of reliable assessment. Its significance lies partly in the data it produced and partly in what it revealed about the difficulty of supervising officials at a distance.

The most important result of the following years was the dahsala, or ten-year, settlement, reported in the sources for the early 1580s. Officials compiled records of the produce and prices of the major crops over a recent ten-year period, calculated an average, and used it to fix cash rates per unit of land for each crop in each locality. The aim was to base assessment on observed history rather than on annual estimation or negotiation, and to give cultivators a predictable demand. The system is closely associated with the measurement-based method called zabt. Scholars differ on how thoroughly and uniformly it was applied, but the general principle seems clear: a shift toward standardized, recorded, cash-denominated assessment.

Measurement itself was standardized. In the later 1580s the court introduced the Ilahi gaz, a uniform yard intended to replace the variable measures in use, together with a measuring rope or chain of bamboo joints, which was less liable to stretch and manipulation than a plain rope. This was part of a broader effort, visible also in the introduction of the Ilahi era in 1584, to give the empire common standards of time and space. As with other features of the reign, the ideal of uniformity was real, but it coexisted with persistent local custom.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Revenue Administration from the Sultanate to Akbar

What changed? Assessment became increasingly systematic and cash-based. Records of crop yields and prices were compiled over time; measurement was standardized; officials were charged with preparing written schedules of rates; and the proportion of land assessed through measurement expanded across the central regions of the empire.

What remained continuous? The land remained the principal source of income; hereditary local intermediaries remained essential; the state continued to claim a substantial share of the harvest in principle; and the gap between assessed and collected revenue persisted.

Who experienced change most directly? Cultivators in regions where measurement was introduced and officials charged with enforcing it. Who experienced greater continuity? Peasants in more remote, less closely supervised regions, where customary arrangements continued largely unchanged.

Why does the pattern matter? It cautions against treating Akbar's reforms as a sudden transformation. They were refinements of earlier practice, applied unevenly, and their durability depended on continuing supervision.

Section 52.3

Land Assessment, Revenue Collection, and Regional Variation

Mughal officials used several methods to determine how much a cultivator or village owed, and the choice among them depended on region, crop, and the state's capacity to supervise. The method most identified with Akbar's reforms, zabt, involved measuring the cultivated area and applying a fixed cash rate per unit of land for each crop. Officials classified land by the regularity of its cultivation: land cultivated every year, land left fallow briefly, land fallow for several years, and land uncultivated for a longer time, with assessment adjusted accordingly. Land was also graded by quality, and the rates were expressed as a cash sum per measure, derived from the average of produce and prices over the preceding period.

Zabt was most fully applied in the central plains, in provinces such as Lahore, Delhi, Agra, Allahabad, Awadh, Malwa, and parts of Gujarat and Multan, where the administration was best established. Elsewhere other methods prevailed. In batai, also known as crop-sharing, the harvest was divided between the cultivator and the state in agreed proportions, either by dividing the cut grain in the field or the threshed grain. Batai had the advantage of adjusting automatically to the size of the harvest, which protected cultivators in bad years, but it demanded constant supervision to prevent concealment and was costly to administer. In kankut, officials estimated the expected yield of standing crops and set the demand accordingly. Nasaq, as described by modern scholars, was an arrangement based on earlier practice or on a group assessment of a village, rather than field-by-field measurement.

The nominal share the state claimed was substantial. The A'in-i Akbari indicates that the demand was around one third of the estimated produce, though how consistently and in what form that share was actually taken is difficult to establish. Some historians argue that actual demands often came closer to half in many regions; others emphasize the variation. Here the distinction between jama, the assessed revenue recorded on paper, and hasil, the amount actually collected, is crucial (see the Historical Vocabulary box below). Court documents and chronicles describe the jama; the hasil depended on harvests, the integrity of officials, the power of local intermediaries, and the capacity of cultivators to pay.

Historical Vocabulary
Jama and Hasil

Jama was the revenue assessed or expected from a territory, recorded in registers and used to assign income to nobles. Hasil was the amount actually collected. Many of the empire's most quoted figures are jama figures, which describe the state's expectations rather than its receipts.

The distinction matters because nobles, who were assigned jagirs on the basis of jama, bore the consequences when hasil fell short. It also reminds students that a document describing assessment does not tell us what happened in the village.

Regional variation was extensive. In Gujarat and the plains of the north, coin-based assessment and an extensive monetized economy made cash demands feasible. In Bengal, with its riverine rice cultivation and shifting channels, imperial administration was thinner and local arrangements remained more influential, a pattern reinforced by the difficulties of conquest and the persistence of Afghan and zamindari power in the east. In the hill regions, border areas, and territories held through tribute arrangements with local rulers, the state often collected tribute rather than assessing individual cultivators. Even within the central regions, officials adjusted rates, granted remissions for drought or flood, and negotiated with local intermediaries.

Students should therefore think of Mughal revenue not as a uniform national tax but as a layered set of arrangements, with a standardized core in the most closely governed regions and diverse practice at the margins. The comprehensive economic history of production and exchange across the empire belongs to Chapter 55; the important point here is that the machinery of assessment determined how imperial authority touched ordinary lives.

Section 52.4

Peasants, Zamindars, and the Social Consequences of Revenue Policy

Between the imperial treasury and the field stood a chain of people. At the base were the cultivators, who differed greatly among themselves. Some, the resident cultivators of a village, held customary rights to land and often cultivated it across generations. Others were non-resident cultivators who came from neighboring villages to farm land on shorter terms. Within villages there were also wealthier and poorer households, artisans, laborers, and village servants, whose interests did not coincide with those of village leaders. Village headmen and accountants managed local collection and kept local records. Above them, in the pargana, stood hereditary officials such as the qanungo and the chaudhuri, and the more powerful category of the zamindar.

The term zamindar covers a wide range of people. It could refer to a village leader who held hereditary rights to a share of local revenue, to a local landholder with substantial rights over a group of villages, or to a regional chief with armed followers and a recognized territory. Many zamindars were rooted in their localities for generations, and some of the larger ones were former rulers or chiefs whose submission to the Mughals had left them with considerable local power. In return for assisting collection and supplying military contingents, they retained a share of the revenue and customary privileges. Their position was recognized by the state but not created by it, and the state could not easily remove them without risking rebellion.

Revenue policy therefore redistributed burdens in ways that policy documents do not show. A fixed cash assessment based on past prices could protect cultivators when prices rose but could burden them when prices fell or crops failed. The requirement to pay in cash pushed cultivators toward markets and, where they lacked money, toward moneylenders and grain merchants. The state sometimes advanced loans for seed and implements, called taqavi, and granted remissions in times of drought or flood, but the extent of relief depended on local officials and conditions. Cultivators could also respond by flight: where demands were unbearable, they could leave for areas where intermediaries offered better terms. Flight and local resistance, ranging from refusal to pay to armed uprising, were recurrent features of the period.

Zamindars occupied an ambivalent position. They were indispensable collectors and military supporters, yet they could also be rivals who withheld revenue or defied imperial officials. Mughal policy toward them combined recognition, incorporation (some became mansabdars), and punitive force when they rebelled. For cultivators, a zamindar might be a protector against outsiders or an additional burden on top of the state's demand. Which role predominated depended on the individual and the circumstances.

Multiple Perspectives

One Revenue System, Several Experiences

An imperial financial official: Standardized measurement and rate schedules offered a means of predicting income, assigning jagirs, and checking the honesty of subordinates, though the official depended on local informants for information.

Why the differences matter: These groups did not hold equal power. The system's costs fell most heavily on those with the least bargaining capacity, and the state's reliance on intermediaries limited its ability to correct abuses.

These tensions mark the limits of imperial supervision. The government had to rely on documents prepared by the very local officials whose conduct it hoped to monitor, and on inspectors whose reports it could not easily verify. Division II turns to the larger structure of provinces and officials through which the empire tried to bridge that distance.

Division II · Sections 52.5–52.8

Provincial Administration, the Imperial Court, and the Machinery of Government

How government functioned between the imperial center and the village, rather than simply a list of administrative titles.

Section 52.5

The Subah System and the Organization of Mughal Provinces

By about 1580 the empire had been organized into twelve provinces called subahs. Over the course of the reign the structure developed gradually, and the number grew as conquests continued: the later additions in the Deccan, such as Berar, Khandesh, and Ahmadnagar, brought the total to fifteen by the end of the reign. Earlier arrangements had been looser, with territories administered through military commanders, jagirdars, and officials of varying jurisdiction. The subah system gave the empire a regular provincial framework with defined officials and responsibilities.

The listing in the A'in-i Akbari includes Allahabad, Agra, Awadh, Ajmer, Ahmadabad (Gujarat), Bihar, Bengal, Delhi, Kabul, Lahore, Multan, and Malwa. This list is itself a historical document. It shows an empire centered on the plains of the north, stretching from Kabul in the northwest to Bengal in the east and Gujarat in the west, and it shows that modern borders do not map neatly onto Mughal divisions: Lahore and Multan together covered parts of what is now Pakistan and Indian Punjab, while Bengal extended across territory now divided between India and Bangladesh.

Each subah was headed by a governor, referred to in the sources by titles including sipah salar and later subahdar or nazim. He held responsibility for law and order, military command within the province, and the enforcement of imperial orders. Alongside him stood a provincial diwan, responsible for revenue and finance, who was appointed from the center and answered to the imperial diwan rather than to the governor. Other officers included the provincial bakhshi, who handled military pay and records; the provincial sadr, responsible for religious grants and charitable endowments; the qazi, a judge; and the kotwal, the chief police and market official of a city. News-writers (waqia-navis) reported to the court.

The deliberate division of responsibility was the core of the design. By separating military command from financial control, and by making the diwan and other officials answerable directly to the center, the emperor sought to prevent any provincial governor from combining the army and the treasury, a combination that could support rebellion. News reporting and the frequent transfer of officials served the same purpose. The structure shows the Mughals' awareness of the dangers of provincial autonomy, a danger that later became real in the eighteenth century, as Chapters 57 and 60 discuss.

In practice, the separation was imperfect. Governors were usually high-ranking mansabdars with their own followers and resources and could not easily be overruled. Diwans and governors quarreled over jurisdiction. Distance, monsoon-delayed communication, and the sheer difficulty of supervising officials far from court meant that provincial authority often exceeded the formal design. The Bengal and Bihar revolts of 1580–81, partly provoked by the application of new regulations on military assignments and branding, showed how quickly central reform could collide with local elite resistance. The subah system provided structure, but it was always negotiated.

Section 52.6

Sarkars, Parganas, District Officials, and Administrative Records

Below the subah, the empire was divided into sarkars, roughly districts, and the sarkars into parganas (also called mahals), the basic unit of revenue and local administration. The A'in-i Akbari lists these units systematically for each province, with information on their revenue, area, and the zamindar castes and military contingents associated with them. The pargana was the level at which the state's demands and the local community's customs most directly met.

At the sarkar level, a faujdar was responsible for maintaining order and assisting revenue collection with military force, while an amalguzar oversaw revenue. In the pargana a number of figures operated. The qanungo, a hereditary record-keeper, maintained information on local land, rights, revenue rates, and customs, and served as a repository of local knowledge that imperial officials needed. The chaudhuri was a local revenue headman. The shiqdar was an executive officer, and the qazi a judge. The patwari and village headman handled accounts at the village level. Many of these positions were hereditary and held by families who often belonged to literate communities, including Khatri, Kayastha, and Brahmin families, who adapted to Persian administrative language.

This reliance on local intermediaries explains how the Mughal administration worked with a relatively small salaried staff. Imperial officials, rotated frequently, brought orders and standards from the center, but they depended on local record-keepers for information about land and customs. The qanungo's registers, the village accountant's books, and the revenue rolls were the documentary skeleton of the state. They also gave local families a form of leverage: control over records meant control over information, and the ability to shape what imperial officials could know.

Documents shaped the relation between the government and its subjects. Cultivators received a written statement of the demand (patta) and gave a written undertaking in return (qabuliyat). Orders and decisions were issued as farmans, parwanas, and other documents, which bore seals and were copied and circulated. Records of assessment, collection, and land grants allowed disputes to be adjudicated by reference to earlier documents. The extensive Persian paperwork that survives, from the central chancery to the pargana, reveals a state that depended on writing to maintain authority.

A surviving administrative regulation makes the division of labor clearer. In the A'in-i Akbari, Abu'l-Fazl assigns the aml-guzar responsibility for the collection of revenue, the timing of demands, and the treatment of cultivators. The bitikchi, by contrast, is expected to draw together information from the qanungo, identify plots and cultivators, and maintain written accounts. The former must act on information that the latter compiles, while both depend on village-level intermediaries whose records may preserve local interests as well as facts. An imperial manual thus shows the ambition to make administration inspectable; it does not demonstrate that every pargana possessed a complete, accurate, or regularly updated archive.

Everyday Life and Social History

The Village and the Written State

For most villagers, the state was less a distant emperor than a handful of local figures: the headman who collected the share, the accountant who recorded it, the zamindar who offered protection or demanded dues, and, at harvest time, the official who measured or estimated the crop. Few cultivators could read Persian, the language of the registers, which meant that their understanding of what they owed depended on what intermediaries told them.

This gap between the written record and the lived reality created both opportunities and vulnerabilities. Families who could read and keep accounts gained influence; those who could not relied on them. Historians who work with Mughal documents must therefore remember that the records were created by and for those with literate access to power, and that the voices of cultivators survive mainly in petitions, complaints, and descriptions written by others.

Section 52.7

The Imperial Court, Household, and Political Decision-Making

The imperial court was not simply a ceremonial setting. It was the institution through which the emperor made decisions, distributed patronage, received petitions, and displayed authority. The central ministers included the wakil, whose powers Akbar reduced after the experience with Bairam Khan's regency, the imperial diwan responsible for finance, the mir bakhshi who oversaw the military establishment and the ranks of mansabdars, the sadr al-sudur responsible for religious endowments, and the mir saman, who managed the imperial household and workshops. The emperor's secretaries, scribes, and personal attendants stood between these officials and the throne.

Daily routines gave court politics a rhythm. Public audiences, in which the emperor appeared before the assembled nobility, alternated with more private meetings with advisers. The practice of appearing at a balcony window (jharokha) to be seen by the people and to receive petitions linked the emperor to ordinary subjects and became an element of the court's ideology of accessible justice. Court ritual, including forms of salutation and the observance of festivals such as the Persian New Year (Nauruz), expressed rank and loyalty. Access to the emperor, and proximity to him, were valuable political resources.

The royal household was a political actor in its own right. Women of the imperial family held property, supported dependents, and patronized building, learning, and religious institutions. Hamida Banu Begum, Akbar's mother, and Gulbadan Begum, his aunt, who wrote an account of Humayun's reign, held respected positions. In the early years of the reign, Maham Anga, Akbar's former nurse, and her son Adham Khan exercised considerable influence until 1562, when Akbar asserted his own authority. Mughal women in the 1570s and 1580s also undertook the pilgrimage to Mecca, a journey of significance in the court's religious life. Because household women could advise, intercede, and support or oppose officials, they were involved in decisions that official chronicles, written by men, often record only indirectly.

Fatehpur Sikri, begun about 1571 near the village where the Chishti shaikh Salim lived, served as the principal imperial residence until the mid-1580s. It combined palaces, audience halls, a mosque, residences for nobles, and the Ibadat Khana, and it served as the capital where the empire's most important administrative and religious deliberations took place. Akbar left in 1585 to deal with northwestern affairs and spent much of the following period at Lahore, returning to Agra only in 1598. The movement of the court mattered because the court traveled: the imperial camp, with its officials, treasury, and retinue, was itself a mobile center of government, and decisions were often made on the march. Chapter 56 will treat the architecture and artistic culture of the court in fuller detail; here Fatehpur Sikri matters as an administrative and political space.

Political decision-making involved competition among factions of nobles, favorites, ministers, and household figures. Patronage determined careers: access to rank, jagirs, and office flowed through the emperor and his advisers, and rivalries among nobles and between provincial and central officials shaped outcomes. Akbar's skill lay in balancing these factions, preventing any single group from dominating, and maintaining his own freedom of action. Nonetheless, the personalized character of authority meant that the system depended heavily on the emperor's own judgment and energy, a vulnerability that Chapter 53 will explore.

Section 52.8

Justice, Administrative Supervision, and the Limits of Centralization

The Mughal government regarded justice as a central duty of the ruler, and the apparatus of justice combined several overlapping systems. Islamic law, as interpreted by qazis and jurists, governed disputes among Muslims and shaped broader legal practice, but many matters, including local property disputes, caste and community questions, and revenue conflicts, were handled by local custom, community councils, and revenue officials. Hindu communities often resolved disputes through their own councils, and imperial courts could take account of local custom. The emperor, as the highest source of justice, heard appeals and petitions, and the court's emphasis on accessibility reinforced his image as a just ruler.

Religious and judicial offices were closely connected. The sadr supervised grants of land and cash, known as suyurghal or madad-i ma'ash, made to scholars, Sufis, religious families, and others, and often appointed local judicial officials. The chief sadr of the early reign, Shaikh Abd al-Nabi, together with Makhdum al-Mulk, a leading jurist, exercised extensive influence over religious appointments and grants. Akbar grew dissatisfied with the authority and alleged misconduct of this religious establishment in the 1570s. He reorganized the supervision of grants, appointing provincial sadrs and reviewing the holdings of grantees, and by the early 1580s both men had fallen from favor. This episode, discussed again in Section 52.13, shows how disputes over financial grants and religious authority were inseparable.

The government also tried to supervise its own officials. News-writers sent reports to the court; inspectors and special commissioners investigated complaints; officials were rotated to prevent them from building local power bases; and the emperor received petitions directly. Written orders specified duties and penalties. The court's insistence on accounting and records, and the periodic review of assignments, reflected a conscious effort at accountability.

The limits were considerable. Communication was slow, and officials had incentives to conceal information. Local elites could resist or evade. The state did not possess a professional police or a large civil bureaucracy, so enforcement ultimately rested on the same mansabdars and local intermediaries whose conduct required monitoring. The gap between imperial instruction and local enforcement was a structural feature of the system rather than an exception. Historians have debated how far to describe the Mughal state as "centralized." It was centralized in its ideology, its documentation, and its formal hierarchy, yet dependent in practice on negotiated relations with powerful intermediaries. The system worked best when the emperor was active, the elite was loyal, and local conditions were stable, and it proved most fragile when those conditions failed, a theme Chapters 54 and 57 take up. Division III turns from administration to the religious world that this government had to govern.

Religious Communities, Imperial Reform, and the Ibadat Khana

Akbar's religious policies as historical developments shaped by imperial circumstances, personal interests, and competing intellectual positions.

Section 52.9

The Religious Landscape of Akbar's Empire

The empire Akbar governed was religiously diverse, and none of its communities was internally uniform. Muslims, a minority of the population overall though dominant in the ruling elite, included Sunnis of the Hanafi legal school, Shias (some of them Persian migrants who held high office at court), Sufis of several orders, and communities with distinctive local practices. Among Sufis the Chishti order was prominent in north India, the Suhrawardi in Sind and the Punjab, and the Naqshbandi, represented by later visitors from Central Asia, increasingly influential among those who favored stricter observance. Muslim scholars (ulama) disagreed among themselves over law, over the role of the ruler, and over the relation between legal orthodoxy and mystical practice. Mahdawi teachings, which looked for a renewer of the faith, also circulated and drew suspicion from many jurists.

Hindu religious life was equally varied. Brahmin scholars preserved Sanskrit learning and ritual traditions; devotional movements centered on Vishnu, Krishna, Rama, and Shiva, as Chapter 48 traced, attracted followers across castes; temple towns such as Mathura, Vrindavan, Banaras, and Puri were centers of pilgrimage and patronage; and countless village and regional cults shaped local life. Brahmins, Rajput warrior lineages, merchant communities, cultivators, and others differed in status and in relation to the state. Jain communities, strongly represented among merchants and bankers in Gujarat and Rajasthan, maintained monastic orders and scholarly networks. Sikh communities in the Punjab were developing around the teachings of Guru Nanak and his successors, a story Chapter 48 introduced and later chapters continue; in Akbar's reign the Gurus maintained cordial relations with the court, though the Sikh Panth was still taking institutional shape.

Beyond these, the empire included Zoroastrians, especially Parsis in Gujarat; Christians, including the Portuguese on the western coast; and many communities that did not fit neat categories, among them regional cults and the practitioners of religious traditions on the margins of orthodoxy. Religious identity was often fluid, and individuals and communities moved between devotional and legal frameworks in ways that modern census-style categories obscure. Chapter 74's discussion of nineteenth-century religious identities will show how much later definitions differed.

Political power interacted with religion in several ways. The court patronized Muslim institutions through grants, appointed qazis and sadrs, and supported pilgrimage; it also supported Hindu temples and Brahmin scholars through grants and alliances, as in its relations with Rajput rulers, discussed in Chapter 51. Jain leaders received imperial recognition and, as Section 52.18 shows, concessions. Religious institutions in turn supplied legitimacy, literacy, and local social networks. A ruler who wished to govern widely could not ignore any of them, but neither could he satisfy them all, and policy choices carried political costs.

For all these reasons, historians avoid describing the empire in terms of a simple Hindu-Muslim binary. Muslim nobles included Persians, Central Asians, Afghans, and Indian-born Muslims with different interests; Hindu elites included Rajputs, Khatris, Kayasthas, and Brahmins with different relationships to the court; religious specialists of every community disputed with each other. The conflicts and accommodations of the reign were more complicated than a contest between two blocs.

Section 52.10

The Pilgrimage Tax, Jizya, and Religious Policy Reform

Two measures from the early reign are central to evaluations of Akbar's religious policy: the abolition of a tax on pilgrims and the abolition of the jizya. Court sources report that in 1563 Akbar ended the pilgrimage tax, a levy on Hindu pilgrims visiting sacred sites, and that in 1564 he abolished the jizya, a tax levied in Islamic law on non-Muslims who were not required to perform military service in return for protection. Abu'l-Fazl presents these acts as expressions of the emperor's justice and impartiality. They were also taken in a period when Akbar was still consolidating power, had recently asserted independence from his regents, and was building alliances with Rajput lords, as Chapter 51 explained.

Political considerations are plausible alongside ethical ones. A tax that distinguished subjects by religion could strain relations with the Hindu elites on whom the regime depended, and its abolition, together with other measures such as the end of the practice of enslaving the families of war captives, signaled that the emperor was willing to treat non-Muslim subjects as part of the political community. The measures also had a financial dimension, since the jizya and pilgrim taxes were sources of revenue and the state had to compensate for their loss, and the reaction of orthodox Muslim scholars, many of whom regarded the jizya as obligatory in law, was a factor in later tensions.

Scholars have raised several questions about these measures. One concerns dating and sources: the chronicle of Abu'l-Fazl places the jizya's abolition in 1564, while Bada'uni's critical history gives different chronological indications for some related measures, and modern historians have debated the precise sequence. A second concerns enforcement: it is difficult to know how uniformly the abolition was implemented across the empire, and the evidence does not permit a confident reconstruction of local compliance in every province, even though the legal and symbolic significance of the abolition was considerable. A third concerns subsequent policy: later rulers took different approaches, and Aurangzeb's reimposition of the jizya in 1679, discussed in Chapter 54, shows that the question remained open. Students should therefore treat the abolition as a significant act whose scope and later history remain subjects of scholarly discussion.

The two repeals should also be distinguished from the broader treatment of land revenue. The pilgrimage levy applied to particular acts of travel and worship; the jizya marked a juridical classification of non-Muslim subjects. Neither was identical with the agricultural assessment that continued to support the empire. Their abolition could therefore alter the public symbolism of the state while leaving the great fiscal machinery of village and provincial collection intact. This helps explain why historians must test an imperial proclamation in more than one way: by its stated principle, by subsequent orders and grants, and, wherever evidence survives, by the experience of the communities it affected. The sources are far richer on the first two questions than on consistent enforcement in distant localities.

Cause and Effect

Why the Early Religious Tax Reforms Occurred

Long-term causes

Centuries of experience under the Delhi Sultans, in which rulers governed predominantly non-Muslim populations; the Persianate ethical tradition emphasizing just rule; and the Sufi and devotional currents of the period.

Short-term causes

Akbar's need to build alliances with Rajput and other Hindu elites; his assertion of personal authority after the regency; and a developing ideal of the ruler as impartial protector.

Immediate effects

Reduced fiscal and symbolic distinctions between subjects by religion; support among Hindu elites; and opposition among some jurists who considered the jizya legally obligatory.

Long-term consequences

The reforms became part of a long debate about the Mughal state's relation to religious communities, recalled in both praise and criticism, notably when later policy changed.

Historical complexity: The reforms cannot be reduced to political calculation or to principle alone. They reflected both, along with fiscal and diplomatic considerations, and their implementation was uneven.

Section 52.11

Akbar's Personal Piety, Sufi Connections, and Intellectual Curiosity

Akbar's religious life was not static, and it would be a mistake to read his later experiments back into his early reign. In his first decades he behaved as a pious Muslim ruler by the conventions of his dynasty and court. He built mosques, observed the rituals of the faith, and cultivated relations with Sufi shaikhs and ulama. Court sources describe growing spiritual restlessness as the reign proceeded, but the direction of his development was not inevitable, and some of its features were shaped by events as much as by settled intention.

The Chishti Sufi tradition was central. The shrine of Mu'in al-Din Chishti at Ajmer, the most famous Chishti center in India, became an important pilgrimage destination for Akbar. Court sources report that he first visited in 1562, on the road from a hunting expedition, and that over the following years he returned many times, in one case, according to tradition, making part of the journey on foot. The pilgrimages had personal and political significance. They expressed devotion, but they also associated the emperor with a respected Sufi lineage that had popular following among Muslims and Hindus alike, and they placed him in relation to a spiritual authority that was independent of the ulama. The visits also coincided with the conquest of Rajasthan and the alliance with Rajput lords, so the Ajmer pilgrimages formed part of the performance of imperial kingship.

Another Chishti figure, Shaikh Salim Chishti of Sikri, was linked in tradition to the birth of Akbar's son Salim, the future Jahangir, in 1569. After his birth, Akbar developed the settlement at Sikri into the new capital, Fatehpur Sikri, in the 1570s. The conferral of honor on the shaikh and his family, and the siting of the capital beside his dwelling, fused spiritual patronage with imperial planning.

Akbar's intellectual curiosity also extended well beyond the Chishtis. He listened to scholars of different traditions, was interested in mystical experiences and in the nature of kingship and knowledge, and was influenced by Persian philosophers and ethical writers who treated the ruler's task as the harmonization of different elements in society. His circle included courtiers such as Faizi and Abu'l-Fazl, whose own intellectual background combined Sufi and philosophical learning. Accounts by Abu'l-Fazl and Bada'uni describe a striking episode during a royal hunt in 1578, but interpret its meaning differently. The emperor abruptly halted the hunt and ordered trapped animals released; Abu'l-Fazl framed the event as an extraordinary spiritual experience. Its inner meaning cannot be independently recovered, and it should not be treated as a demonstrable moment of conversion or final doctrinal rupture.

The result was a combination of devotion and inquiry that resists simple labels. Akbar was neither a skeptic who abandoned Islam nor a Sufi who withdrew from politics. His religious interests informed his political choices, and his political position shaped what he could say and do about religion. Understanding the Ibadat Khana debates requires keeping both in view.

Section 52.12

The Ibadat Khana and the Religious Debates at Fatehpur Sikri

In 1575 Akbar established the Ibadat Khana, the "House of Worship," at Fatehpur Sikri. Its initial function, as reported in the sources, was to host gatherings on Thursday evenings at which Muslim scholars, Sufis, and nobles discussed religious questions in the emperor's presence. The setting was a formal assembly with ranked seating, and the early sessions were confined to Muslim participants. Their purpose, as Abu'l-Fazl presents it, was the search for truth; for Bada'uni, writing with disapproval, the debates rapidly degenerated.

The early debates revealed deep divisions within the Muslim establishment. Scholars quarreled over questions of law, precedence, and the legitimacy of competing interpretations, and, according to Bada'uni, accused each other of error and even unbelief. The disputes involved real stakes: control of religious offices, the distribution of grants, and the authority to define orthodoxy. The sadr Abd al-Nabi and the jurist Makhdum al-Mulk were among the leading figures in these conflicts, and their disagreements with each other and with other scholars weakened the religious establishment's claim to speak with a single voice.

Around 1578 the assemblies were widened to include participants from other traditions. Over the following years the court received Hindu scholars, Jain monks, Zoroastrians, and, from 1580, Jesuit priests from Goa, who participated in discussions with Muslim scholars and with the emperor. Sufi shaikhs and philosophers also attended. The topics included theology, the nature of revelation, the status of prophets, and the claims of different faiths. Some sessions were formal debates; others were conversations, and the sources do not always distinguish them.

The importance of the Ibadat Khana lies less in any doctrinal result than in what the debates did to the question of authority. If learned Muslims could not agree among themselves, and if the emperor was willing to listen to non-Muslims, then the claim of any single group of jurists to define truth for the empire was weakened. This mattered politically, because the ulama's control of religious grants and legal interpretation made them a potential rival to the emperor, and because Akbar wished to establish himself as an arbiter above partisan disputes. The debates thus fed directly into the mahzar of 1579 and the broader question of religious authority treated in Section 52.13.

Historians approach the Ibadat Khana cautiously. The most detailed accounts come from Abu'l-Fazl, who celebrated the emperor's openness, and Bada'uni, who deplored it, and both wrote with ideological aims. The Jesuit reports, written for European audiences with missionary expectations, tended to overestimate the emperor's interest in Christianity. What can be said with reasonable confidence is that the court became, in the late 1570s and 1580s, an unusually open forum for religious exchange, that this openness had political purposes as well as intellectual ones, and that it provoked strong opposition. Whether the debates represented sincere inquiry, calculated politics, or both is a matter on which sources can support multiple readings.

The central interpretive division of the chapter: Akbar's approach evaluated neither as modern religious tolerance nor as a project to create a compulsory religion.

Section 52.13

The Mahzar of 1579 and the Question of Religious Authority

In 1579, a document known as the mahzar (a formal attestation or declaration) was drawn up at court and signed by leading religious scholars. It was drafted with the involvement of Shaikh Mubarak, the learned father of Faizi and Abu'l-Fazl, and was signed by the principal ulama, including, reportedly with reluctance, Makhdum al-Mulk and Abd al-Nabi. In the same year Akbar is reported to have delivered the Friday sermon at the mosque of Fatehpur Sikri, a sermon composed by Faizi that praised God in terms that invited varied interpretations, and his act of leading prayer himself drew notice.

The content of the mahzar concerned the interpretation of Islamic law. Its essential claim, as reported, was that when learned jurists disagreed on a legal question, the just ruler (imam-i adil), whose rank before God was higher than that of a mujtahid, an independent interpreter of law, might choose among the conflicting opinions, provided his decision was consistent with the Quran and served the welfare of the people. It also stated that the emperor's decree, if consistent with these conditions, was binding. Such a claim drew on existing juristic concepts and on the argument that a legitimate ruler's judgment could settle disputes.

It is misleading to describe the mahzar simply as a declaration of Akbar's infallibility, as hostile accounts did. The text, as the sources preserve it, placed conditions on the ruler's authority: his decision had to accord with scripture and the public interest, and it applied to disputed legal questions rather than to the whole of religion. It did not, on its face, claim that the emperor was above revelation. What the document did was to assert that the ruler had a role in adjudicating among the ulama, a role that previous rulers had also claimed in practice but rarely in such formal terms. Critics, including Bada'uni, regarded it as an unacceptable subordination of religious learning to royal will, and the fact that senior ulama signed under pressure strengthened that impression.

The political context explains the timing. After years of dispute among the ulama, the sadr's influence, and the fiscal and administrative friction over religious grants discussed in Section 52.8, the mahzar enabled the emperor to claim authority to resolve deadlocks and to limit the power of a religious establishment he considered quarrelsome. Makhdum al-Mulk and Abd al-Nabi were sent away on pilgrimage and lost their former authority. Makhdum al-Mulk died in 1582; Abd al-Nabi died around 1584 after returning to India, although accounts of his last years differ. Revolts in the east in 1580–81 featured rebel leaders who challenged the emperor's religious standing as well as his administrative reforms, showing how combustible the question had become. The mahzar thus stands at the junction of administrative reform, intellectual debate, and political rivalry.

Section 52.14

Sulh-i Kul: Universal Peace and the Ethics of Imperial Rule

Sulh-i kul, usually translated "universal peace" or "peace with all," is the principle most often associated with Akbar's vision of rule. The expression was developed above all by Abu'l-Fazl, who used it to describe an ideal in which the ruler stood impartially above the quarrels of rival communities and protected all subjects. In his writings it appears as a principle of governance, a moral outlook, and a description of the emperor's character.

Historical Vocabulary
Sulh-i Kul

Sulh-i kul means "peace with all" or "universal peace." In the writings of Abu'l-Fazl it names the ruler's duty to maintain order and protect all subjects, regardless of belief, by refusing to let one community's claims override the rest.

It is often compared to modern secularism or religious equality, but those comparisons are misleading. Sulh-i kul did not separate religion from the state, did not establish legal equality among communities, and did not abolish hierarchy. It was an ethic of imperial impartiality grounded in Persian ethical and Sufi traditions, and it coexisted with a court whose elite remained overwhelmingly Muslim and in which Persian was the language of power.

The concept drew on several intellectual sources. Persian ethical literature, which presented the ideal ruler as a balancer of the different elements of society, supplied one. The Sufi idea that all religions point toward a single divine reality, associated with the tradition stemming from Ibn al-'Arabi, supplied another. Indian traditions of coexistence among sects, including the experience of Hindus and Muslims in local devotional communities, were a background. Combined with the practical experience of governing a plural population, these produced a language for describing the emperor as a ruler for all.

Its political function was clear. A ruler whose power rested on a Muslim military elite but whose subjects were mostly non-Muslim needed a framework of legitimacy that did not depend on the claims of any single group. Sulh-i kul gave the court such a framework. It supported the alliance with Rajput lords, justified the abolition of religious taxes, authorized the patronage of non-Muslim scholars, and presented the emperor as the guarantor of order. It also provided a rhetorical weapon against rivals: a court faction that insisted on legal orthodoxy could be portrayed as a source of disorder.

The limits of implementation should be noted. The imperial elite remained overwhelmingly Muslim, with Hindu nobles, mainly Rajputs, as a significant but minority element. Persian remained the court language, and the legal framework retained Islamic features. Caste and gender hierarchies persisted. Some measures associated with the ideal, such as restrictions on the slaughter of animals at certain times, reflected the influence of particular communities, notably the Jains, rather than a neutral principle. And the principle was not applied to all conflicts equally: the court was willing to repress those it regarded as threats, including some religious movements. Sulh-i kul was therefore a political ethic with real effects, not a legal guarantee of equality.

An important interpretive problem is how to connect the language of universal peace with ordinary administrative practice. Abu'l-Fazl wrote about a ruler whose impartiality was a virtue of kingship; the sources do not provide a comprehensive law that guaranteed equal treatment to every individual regardless of community. Accommodation often took concrete, uneven forms: the confirmation of a local institution's privileges, the patronage of learned men from different traditions, or the protection of a pilgrim route. A regional chief could welcome such arrangements while still resisting taxation or military commands. Recent historians accordingly examine sulh-i kul as an evolving solution to the problem of imperial coexistence, not as an ahistorical claim that competing interests and religious disagreements had disappeared.

Section 52.15

Din-i Ilahi, Tawhid-i Ilahi, and the Problem of Interpretation

Around 1582 Akbar began to accept a small circle of disciples into a form of imperial discipleship. The sources use several terms. Abu'l-Fazl refers to the "divine monotheism" (tawhid-i ilahi) and to the disciples' pledge of devotion; the later term Din-i Ilahi, "divine religion" or "divine faith," appears in some sources, notably Bada'uni, and became standard in later writing. The difference in terminology is itself important, because the term "religion" implies something that the earliest sources did not necessarily claim.

What the sources describe is a form of initiation modeled in part on the relationship between a Sufi master and his disciples. A candidate presented himself to the emperor, offered his turban as a sign of submission, and received in return a symbol, sometimes described as a turban ornament or a seal-ring engraved with a short invocation. The disciple promised devotion to the emperor and willingness to sacrifice property, life, honor, and religion in his service, a formula that Abu'l-Fazl describes in the A'in-i Akbari. The expected ethical conduct included the avoidance of certain forms of killing, restraint in sensual indulgence, and generosity. The circle was small. The sources name relatively few disciples, and most were prominent courtiers. Only one Hindu noble, Birbal, is commonly named, and the disciples were not drawn from the mass of the population.

Historical Debate

Was Din-i Ilahi a New Religion?

The question: Did Akbar found a new religion, a courtly spiritual fellowship, or a distinctive form of imperial discipleship?

Interpretation A — A new syncretic religion: Influential earlier accounts, including Vincent Smith's biography of the early twentieth century, described Din-i Ilahi as an eclectic new faith created by Akbar from elements of several religions, and some hostile traditional writers presented it as apostasy. On this view, the emperor attempted to replace existing religions with his own synthesis.
Interpretation B — A limited discipleship connected to kingship: Many modern historians, among them Stephen P. Blake, Athar Ali, and others, reject the idea of a new religion. They point out that the sources describe a small circle of disciples, no established doctrine, scripture, clergy, or program of conversion, and no attempt to impose the practice on subjects. The institution is better understood as a Sufi-inspired bond of loyalty linking selected courtiers to the emperor, closely related to the ethic of sulh-i kul and to the ideology of the emperor as spiritual guide.

Evidence: The debate turns on how to read terms and silences in the sources. Abu'l-Fazl's use of "tawhid" and descriptions of discipleship, Bada'uni's hostile use of "religion," the small number of named disciples, the absence of doctrinal texts, and the lack of evidence of conversions of ordinary subjects all bear on it.

Why it matters: If Din-i Ilahi was a new religion, Akbar's reign appears as a radical ideological break; if it was a discipleship circle, it appears as an extension of Mughal courtly and Sufi practice. The second reading is better supported by the surviving evidence, though it does not remove the significance of the experiment.

Student task: Identify two features of the sources that support the interpretation of a limited discipleship and one feature that might support the claim of a new religion. What additional evidence would help resolve the question?

Even within the limited-discipleship reading, important questions remain. The initiation, with its pledge to the emperor, could place devotion to the ruler above or alongside the disciple's inherited religion, which is why critics reacted so strongly. The relationship also had political uses: it created a circle of personally loyal nobles bound to the emperor by a spiritual tie, in addition to the ties of rank and patronage. Whether disciples regarded it primarily as a spiritual commitment or a mark of favor is a question the sources cannot fully answer. The institution remained marginal in practice and did not survive Akbar's death as an organized community.

The scale and composition of the group are decisive evidence against the idea of a mass conversion campaign. The A'in-i Akbari presents initiation under rules for chosen disciples, using a vocabulary comparable to Sufi relationships between a master and his followers. Of the small group of high-ranking adherents identifiable there, most were Muslims; Birbal is the Hindu courtier most often mentioned. This does not make the institution religiously insignificant. It suggests instead that the ruler drew on forms of spiritual authority to reinforce an exceptionally personal relationship with selected servants of the crown. The language of devotion, including a reported pledge to surrender religion as well as life and property, remains controversial: historians must distinguish the ritual language of loyalty from proof of an individual disciple's subsequent beliefs or everyday religious practice.

Section 52.16

Religious Opposition, Bada'uni, and the Limits of Akbar's Vision

Akbar's religious policy attracted opposition from several directions. Conservative ulama objected to the mahzar, the Ibadat Khana's open debates, the court's engagement with non-Muslims, and what they regarded as the emperor's growing independence from legal norms. Some nobles disliked the changes in administrative practice that accompanied them, and the revolts of 1580–81 included religious accusations against the emperor, though their causes were chiefly political and administrative. Later Naqshbandi writers, such as Shaikh Ahmad Sirhindi in the early seventeenth century, looked back on the reign as a period of religious decline and called for a return to stricter observance, a theme that later chapters take up.

The most important critical voice is Abd al-Qadir Bada'uni. A scholar and translator at court who took part in some of the Ibadat Khana debates and in the translation projects discussed below, he wrote a history, the Muntakhab al-Tawarikh, completed in the mid-1590s and kept secret until after Akbar's death. It gives a vivid and bitter account of what he saw as the emperor's departure from Islam, of the ulama's quarrels, and of the influence of Abu'l-Fazl and his family. Because it is one of the few sustained critical sources from within the court, it is invaluable.

Section 52.17

The Translation Bureau and the Persianization of Sanskrit Knowledge

One of the court's most distinctive undertakings was a program of translating works from Sanskrit and other languages into Persian, the language of the empire's administration and elite culture. The program was associated with the Maktab Khana, a translation bureau established at Fatehpur Sikri in the mid-1570s, and with the broader library and scriptorium that supported it. Its significance lies in the effort to make the learning and narratives of Indic traditions accessible to the Persian-reading elite, and in the interaction between scholars of different communities that the work required.

The best-known project was the Persian translation of the Mahabharata, titled the Razmnama, "Book of War," undertaken from about 1582 and completed in the mid-1580s. Other translations included the Ramayana, rendered by Bada'uni and others later in the 1580s; the Sanskrit chronicle of Kashmir, the Rajatarangini; the Harivamsa; the story collection Singhasan Battisi; a work on mathematics, the Lilavati, translated by Faizi; and a version of the Atharvaveda, an effort reportedly complicated by difficulties of interpretation. The court also commissioned or supported translations from Arabic and Persian into other forms and the preparation of new Persian versions of older Persian texts. Some of these were illustrated, and the illustrated Razmnama is among the most important products of Mughal manuscript painting, a subject Chapter 56 takes up.

The translation process was collaborative. Brahmin scholars, who knew Sanskrit, explained the text orally, sometimes in a vernacular, and Persian-literate translators, among them Muslim scholars such as Naqib Khan, Bada'uni, and Sultan Haji Thaneswari, rendered it into Persian, while others polished the prose and verse. Because the translators worked from explanation rather than from a direct reading of the Sanskrit, the Persian versions often condensed, interpreted, and adapted the original, and they reflect the choices of the translators as well as the content of the source. This intermediation also meant that translation was a form of exchange, bringing together scholars who might not otherwise have worked together.

Section 52.18

Intellectual Exchange among Brahmins, Jains, Jesuits, Sufis, and Court Scholars

The translation bureau was one site of intellectual exchange among many. Akbar's court attracted scholars, poets, musicians, and holy men from across the empire and beyond, and the sources describe conversations among Brahmins, Sufis, Jain monks, Zoroastrians, Christians, and Muslim scholars. These interactions should be understood as part of the court's politics of patronage and prestige as well as of intellectual curiosity.

Brahmin scholars participated as informants and translators. Their involvement shows that the court was not solely a Muslim space. Yet the sources rarely give their voices directly, and what they gained or sacrificed in this service is poorly documented.

Jain engagement was substantial. Jain merchants and bankers were influential in Gujarat and Rajasthan, and Jain monastic leaders, notably Hiravijaya Suri of the Tapa Gaccha, were invited to the court, where he arrived in the early 1580s. Jain sources, among them biographies and letters preserved in the tradition, report that the emperor was impressed by Jain ascetic discipline and issued orders restricting animal slaughter on certain days and protecting Jain pilgrimage sites. Such concessions illustrate the influence a well-organized community could exert on court policy, and they also show that sulh-i kul did not mean the same treatment for all communities, since the concessions reflected the efforts and standing of particular groups. Jain accounts, like others, have their own interests in presenting the emperor's sympathy.

The Jesuits arrived in 1580 on an invitation sent to Goa after Akbar heard of Christian learning from Portuguese contacts. The first mission, led by Rudolf Acquaviva with Antonio Monserrate and Francisco Henriques, remained until 1583, and later missions followed in the 1590s. The priests discussed theology with the emperor and scholars, presented Christian books and images, and hoped for his conversion. They did not obtain it. Akbar listened, asked questions, and took an interest in European art and technology, but his interest was that of a ruler who collected learned discourse, not of a prospective convert. The Jesuit accounts, written to inspire support in Europe, tended to read his courtesy as an openness to conversion, and their hopes should be read with caution. Chapter 49 introduced the Portuguese presence at Goa from which these missions departed.

Sufi shaikhs, philosophers, and court scholars also took part, and the intellectual life of the court included Persian poets, historians, and musicians. Taken together, the evidence supports an interpretation of the court as a place of real intellectual exchange. It does not support the more exuberant claim that the court was a realm of universal harmony. Participants had interests and rivalries; some scholars were excluded or marginalized; the exchange occurred within a hierarchy, and the sources most often reflect the viewpoints of the court's own writers. The court's openness was real but conditional, and it was shaped by the emperor's authority and by the competition for his attention.

Section 52.19

Abu'l-Fazl and Akbar's Model of Imperial Kingship

The most developed statement of Akbar's model of kingship comes from Abu'l-Fazl. Born in 1551 to the learned Shaikh Mubarak, he entered the court in the 1570s, became one of the emperor's closest advisers, and was assigned a mansab in the imperial service. He wrote the Akbarnama, a chronicle of the reign and of the dynasty, and the A'in-i Akbari, a compendium describing the empire's institutions, geography, and culture. He also wrote prefaces and letters, and he was widely regarded as the architect of the court's ideology. In 1602, while returning to court, he was killed by Bir Singh Bundela, an act that tradition links to the heir Salim, the future Jahangir; the circumstances belong to Chapter 53.

Historical Vocabulary
Farr-i Izadi and Insan-i Kamil

Farr-i izadi, the "divine light" or divine glory, is an idea from Iranian tradition describing a radiance that God bestows on a legitimate king. Insan-i kamil, the "perfect man," comes from Sufi thought and describes a human who fully reflects divine attributes.

Abu'l-Fazl combined these ideas to present Akbar as a ruler whose authority derived from divine light and whose character made him a guide for his subjects. The terms are essential to understanding court ideology, but they describe a claim made on the emperor's behalf rather than a belief shared by all subjects.

Several features of the model stand out. First, sovereignty derives from God: the ruler receives a light that sets him apart and obliges him to protect his subjects. Second, justice is the ruler's primary duty, and it includes impartiality toward all communities, the principle of sulh-i kul. Third, the ruler is a moral and spiritual guide as well as a political and military leader, a role reflected in the disciple-master relationship described in Section 52.15. Fourth, order depends on a hierarchy of officials who carry out the ruler's will, so moral kingship is joined to a centralized administration. The A'in-i Akbari shows these ideas in a concrete form by placing the imperial household, army, and officials, together with the revenue and the empire's regions, within a single ordered picture centered on the emperor.

Abu'l-Fazl opens the A'in-i Akbari with a particularly revealing contrast between genuine kingship and mere possession of royal power. An ordinary conqueror may command treasure, troops, and officials, but these alone do not prove the moral quality of his rule. The true king, according to this argument, receives farr-i izadi, a divinely communicated radiance, and should exhibit paternal solicitude for his subjects, freedom from sectarian strife, and readiness to hear the grievances of both powerful and weak. The passage is more than flattery: it supplies a standard by which imperial authority could be praised or criticized. Yet its author was a leading beneficiary of the regime he described. Historians must therefore read his vision alongside the coercive demands of revenue collection, the penalties imposed on opponents, and the unequal access of different groups to court patronage. Its historical importance lies in the political ideal it articulated, not in proof that the empire invariably embodied that ideal.

Historical Debate

Abu'l-Fazl's Kingship: Governing Ideology or Court Image?

The question: How closely did Abu'l-Fazl's theory of kingship correspond to how the Mughal state actually operated?

Interpretation A — A coherent and functioning ideology: Some historians argue that the ideas of divinely sanctioned kingship, justice, and sulh-i kul formed a real basis of Mughal governance. On this reading, they gave the empire a language of legitimacy that suited its plural population, and they shaped policies such as the abolition of religious taxes, the recruitment of Hindu nobles, and the patronage of translation.
Interpretation B — A court image that exceeded practice: Other historians emphasize the gap between the ideology and the realities of rule, including the dependence on local intermediaries, the burdens of revenue, the persistence of hierarchy, and the strength of opposition. They stress that the theory was written by a court insider with an interest in glorification and that it reflected the aspirations of a narrow elite rather than the experience of most subjects. Scholars who study the Mughal state as a combination of personal and bureaucratic authority, such as Stephen Blake, and those who stress its dependence on local power, such as Irfan Habib in his analysis of the agrarian system, offer different emphases.

Evidence: The question depends on comparing the ideological texts with administrative documents, revenue records, petitions, and accounts by critics and foreign visitors.

Why it matters: If the ideology was effective, it explains the stability of the empire; if it was largely rhetorical, stability must be explained by other factors such as the military, the mansabdari system, and the cooperation of local elites. The best-supported view probably combines the two: the ideology was a real instrument of legitimation, and its reach was limited by the structures of the state.

Section 52.20

Review and Historical Synthesis

The preceding sections have followed four strands of Akbar's government: revenue, administration, religion, and kingship. Taken together, they show how a conquest state sought to become a durable political order, and where its limits lay.

The revenue system provided the material basis. Building on Sultanate and Sur precedents and developing through experiments in the 1570s and the dahsala settlement of the early 1580s, it sought to replace uncertain estimation with measurement, records, and fixed cash rates, and it integrated the land revenue with the assignment of jagirs to nobles, as Chapter 51 described. It worked best in the central plains, where administration was thickest, and elsewhere gave way to crop-sharing, estimation, and tribute. Its social costs fell on cultivators, whose bargaining power was weakest, and its reliance on zamindars, qanungos, and village officials limited the state's ability to prevent abuse.

The administrative structure of subahs, sarkars, and parganas, with its deliberate division of authority among governors, diwans, bakhshis, sadrs, and qazis, was an effort to project imperial control while preventing the concentration of local power. The court, with its ministers, household, and rituals, was the place where decisions were made and patronage distributed. Supervision through news-writers, rotation, and inspections existed, but communication delays and the dependence on powerful intermediaries meant that centralization was always partial.

The religious developments were closely tied to these administrative concerns. The abolition of religious taxes in the 1560s, the Ibadat Khana debates, the mahzar of 1579, and the development of sulh-i kul were responses to a political problem: how to govern a plural society with a ruling elite that was largely Muslim and a religious establishment that claimed authority over law and grants. The answers were improvisations shaped by Akbar's own spiritual interests, by the court's intellectual circle, and by the pressure of political rivals. They were neither a modern commitment to equality nor an attempt to impose a new religion. Din-i Ilahi was a limited discipleship, not a mass religion.

The translation program and the court's wider intellectual exchange gave these ideas a cultural form, and Abu'l-Fazl's model of kingship, with its themes of divine light, justice, and impartiality, united them in a theory of legitimate rule. The achievement was considerable: the court produced a language of sovereignty that suited a diverse empire, and it supported institutions that helped the empire survive for generations. The limits were equally significant. The ideology was the voice of a court elite and exceeded what the administration could deliver; opposition from conservative ulama and from rival nobles was real; and the system depended heavily on the emperor's own attention.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how Akbar's expanding military empire was turned into a system of government, and how that system was justified. Its central argument is that revenue administration, provincial and local government, religious policy, intellectual patronage, and a theory of kingship developed together and supported one another, but that each fell short of the uniformity and harmony that the court's own writers described.

Division I traced the fiscal foundations. The state depended on agricultural revenue to pay armies and officials, and the mansabdari and jagirdari arrangements of Chapter 51 made predictable assessment essential. Building on Sultanate and Sur precedents, officials developed measurement, rate schedules, and the ten-year dahsala settlement. Raja Todar Mal was prominent in this work, but reform was gradual and collaborative, involving the karori experiment, the Gujarat measurement, and the work of several officials.

Assessment methods varied by region. Zabt prevailed in the central plains, while batai, kankut, and nasaq were used elsewhere, and in frontier and tributary territories the state collected tribute. The difference between jama, the assessed revenue, and hasil, the amount actually collected, shows why official figures describe expectations more than outcomes. Cultivators, zamindars, qanungos, and jagirdars experienced the system very differently, and the reliance on local intermediaries limited the state's ability to protect peasants from abuse.

Division II examined government between the center and the village. The empire was organized into subahs, with governors, diwans, bakhshis, sadrs, and qazis whose responsibilities were deliberately divided, and below them sarkars and parganas administered by faujdars, amalguzars, qanungos, and village officials. The court, with its ministers, household women, rituals, and mobile camp, was the place of decision-making. Justice combined Islamic law, local custom, and imperial petitions. Supervision through news-writers, rotation, and inspection coexisted with slow communication and the dependence on local power, so centralization was real in ideology and partial in practice.

Division III turned to religion. The empire's population was diverse and internally divided, and the court interacted with Muslims, Hindus, Jains, Sikhs, Zoroastrians, and Christians in ways that cannot be reduced to a Hindu-Muslim binary. The abolition of the pilgrimage tax and the jizya in the 1560s reflected both political calculation and an emerging ethic of impartial rule, though questions of dating and enforcement remain. Akbar's piety, shaped by the Chishti tradition and the Ajmer pilgrimages, developed over time, and the Ibadat Khana, founded in 1575, became a forum in which the quarrels of the ulama weakened their claim to religious authority.

Division IV addressed the chapter's central interpretive problems. The mahzar of 1579 asserted the just ruler's role in choosing among conflicting legal opinions, within conditions, and should not be described simply as a claim to infallibility. Sulh-i kul, developed by Abu'l-Fazl, was an ethic of imperial impartiality drawing on Persian ethical and Sufi sources; it was not secularism or equality. Din-i Ilahi, or Tawhid-i Ilahi, was a small circle of imperial discipleship, best understood as a Sufi-inspired bond of loyalty rather than a new religion. Opposition from Bada'uni and others was real, but his work is polemical and must be read critically.

Division V examined translation, exchange, and kingship. The translation bureau produced Persian versions of the Mahabharata, the Ramayana, and other works through collaboration between Brahmin informants and Persian-literate translators. The court's intellectual exchange with Jains, Jesuits, Sufis, and scholars was real but conditional, shaped by patronage and rivalry. Abu'l-Fazl's model of kingship, resting on divine light, justice, and impartiality, supplied the court's ideology, and debate continues over how closely it matched practice.

The sources require constant care. The Akbarnama and A'in-i Akbari are indispensable but written by a court insider; the Muntakhab al-Tawarikh is invaluable but hostile; Jesuit and Jain accounts reflect their own aims; and the voices of cultivators and most ordinary subjects survive largely through others. Historians balance these sources against one another and against administrative documents.

Overall, the chapter argues that Akbar's governing system was an impressive and consequential synthesis of administrative rationalization, negotiated rule, and moral ideology. Its achievements allowed the empire to endure, and its limits, including dependence on intermediaries, the burdens on cultivators, and reliance on the emperor's personal engagement, shaped the problems that later chapters examine.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Jama
The revenue assessed on paper for a territory, used to assign income to nobles; distinct from what was actually collected.
Hasil
The revenue actually collected, which depended on harvests, local power, and the honesty of officials.
Zabt
A method of assessment based on measuring cultivated land and applying fixed cash rates per crop and land category.
Dahsala
The ten-year settlement, based on averages of produce and prices over a recent decade, used to fix cash rates.
Batai
A crop-sharing method in which the harvest was divided between cultivator and state.
Kankut
Assessment based on an official's estimate of the standing crop.
Nasaq
An assessment based on earlier practice or on a collective village estimate rather than on field measurement.
Karori
An official appointed in the 1570s to collect about a crore of dams from a defined area of crown land.
Khalisa
Land whose revenue went directly to the imperial treasury rather than being assigned to nobles.
Zamindar
A hereditary holder of local rights to a share of revenue, ranging from village leaders to regional chiefs with armed followers.
Qanungo
A hereditary pargana record-keeper who held local knowledge of land, rights, and revenue rates.
Subah
A Mughal province, headed by a governor and with separate financial, military, and judicial officials.
Sarkar
An administrative district within a subah, containing several parganas.
Pargana
The basic unit of local revenue and administration, also called a mahal.
Diwan
The official responsible for revenue and finance, appointed from the center and independent of the governor in the provinces.
Sadr
The official who supervised religious and charitable grants and often religious appointments.
Qazi
A judge who applied Islamic law and handled many local disputes.
Jizya
A tax on non-Muslim subjects in Islamic law; abolished under Akbar according to court sources and reimposed under Aurangzeb.
Ibadat Khana
The "House of Worship" founded at Fatehpur Sikri in 1575 as a forum for religious discussion.
Mahzar
The 1579 declaration, signed by leading scholars, asserting the just ruler's role in deciding disputed points of law.
Sulh-i kul
"Universal peace"; an ethic of impartial rule toward all communities, not equivalent to modern secularism.
Tawhid-i Ilahi / Din-i Ilahi
The imperial discipleship centered on Akbar, described as divine monotheism or divine faith, involving a small circle of courtiers.
Maktab Khana
The translation bureau at Fatehpur Sikri that supported Persian translations of Sanskrit and other works.
Razmnama
The Persian translation of the Mahabharata prepared at Akbar's court in the 1580s.
Farr-i izadi
The "divine light" that, in Iranian tradition and Abu'l-Fazl's theory, legitimizes a king.
Insan-i kamil
The "perfect man" of Sufi thought, applied by court writers to the ideal ruler.
Ilahi era
A dating system introduced in 1584 that counted years from Akbar's accession.

Chapter Reflection

Chapter 52 matters because it explains how a state that began with conquest learned to govern. The Mughal Empire's endurance over generations did not rest on armies alone but on a framework of revenue, administration, and legitimacy that Akbar and his officials assembled from Sultanate, Sur, Persian, Sufi, and Indic materials. Looking at that framework shows both how much was achieved and how much depended on negotiation with local power.

The chapter also reveals how closely religion and government were intertwined. The disputes at Fatehpur Sikri were about law, grants, and authority as much as about doctrine. Seeing the reign in this way guards against two common errors: treating Akbar as a modern liberal avant la lettre, and treating his policies as nothing but calculation. The evidence supports a more complicated picture in which belief, interest, and circumstance combined.

Within the six-volume series, the chapter connects the conquests of Chapter 51 with the dynastic and cultural histories of Chapters 53 through 56, and it anticipates the eighteenth-century problems of Chapter 57. The tension between a centralized ideal and a negotiated reality, and between an inclusive ideology and a Muslim-dominated elite, recurs in the later Mughal story and in the successor states that inherited its forms.

Finally, the chapter shows why historians must read sources critically. The Akbarnama and the Muntakhab al-Tawarikh describe the same reign in contrasting ways, and neither can be accepted at face value. Learning to hold such sources in tension, and to ask whose voices are missing from both, is among the most important skills the study of South Asian history can teach.
