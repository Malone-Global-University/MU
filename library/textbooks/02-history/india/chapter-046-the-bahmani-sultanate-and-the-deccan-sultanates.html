The Bahmani Sultanate and the Deccan Sultanates

Indian History: South Asia from the Indus Civilization to the Present

Chapter Opening

The fortress of Gulbarga rises from a dry plain in northern Karnataka, its massive walls and moat encircling a citadel, a ruined palace, and a congregational mosque whose entire courtyard is roofed over by arches. Near it stands the shrine of a Sufi who came south from Delhi in 1398, and nearby are the tombs of kings. This was the first capital of a sultanate that emerged in 1347 from a rebellion against the sultan of Delhi, and that, for nearly a century and a half, governed much of the Deccan plateau.

This chapter studies the Bahmani Sultanate and the five successor states that grew out of it: Bijapur, Ahmadnagar, Golconda, Berar, and Bidar. It asks how a province became a kingdom, how a kingdom governed a varied and often difficult territory, why it broke apart, and why Bijapur and Golconda, among its successors, endured until the Mughal conquests of 1686 and 1687. The story does not fit a simple narrative of rise and fall. It includes experiments in centralization, the careers of immigrant ministers and African-origin generals, a multilingual literary culture, and some of the most striking architecture of medieval South Asia.

The chapter also corrects a common distortion. The Deccan sultanates are often presented only as the opponents of Vijayanagara, the Hindu kingdom examined in Chapter 45, as if the history of the plateau were a contest of two religious blocs. The evidence shows something more complicated. Hindu and Muslim soldiers fought on both sides, sultans allied with Vijayanagara against fellow sultans, Brahmins and Marathas served as officials in the sultanates, and rivalries among the sultans were as important as their conflicts with the south.

Evidence for the period is rich but uneven. Persian chronicles, composed at courts and sometimes long after the events they describe, give narrative structure. Inscriptions in several languages, coins, collected letters, travelers' accounts, and surviving buildings, paintings, and manuscripts allow historians to check and extend the chronicles. Each kind of source carries a point of view, and the chapter will repeatedly distinguish contemporary evidence from later legend.

The chapter is organized in six divisions: the formation and early expansion of the Bahmani state; its government, society, and fragmentation; the five successor sultanates; the wars, institutions, and commerce that tied them together; their religious and cultural life; and the seventeenth-century transformation that ended their independence. Three interpretive themes run through all: the problem of state formation and centralization, the meaning and limits of the Deccani-Afaqi distinction, and the need to evaluate rather than assume a Deccani cultural synthesis.

The chapter connects with the larger six-volume narrative. It follows the Delhi Sultanate chapters (Chapters 39 to 44) that explain the northern power from which the Bahmanis broke away, it runs parallel to Chapter 45 on Vijayanagara and Chapter 47 on the other regional sultanates, and it looks ahead to the Mughal conquests of Chapters 51 to 54 and the Maratha rise in Chapter 58.

Historical Context

By the early fourteenth century, the Delhi Sultanate had extended its reach over the Deccan through the campaigns of the Khaljis and Tughlaqs (Chapters 41 and 42). The conquest of Devagiri and Warangal brought the plateau into the sultanate's orbit, and Muhammad bin Tughlaq's experiment of making Daulatabad a second capital transferred officials and soldiers southward. The arrangement was costly and unstable. Distance from Delhi, the burden of revenue demands, and the ambitions of military officers made the Deccan a fragile possession.

The Deccan itself was a region of rich agricultural districts, black cotton soils, great rivers, and strong fortresses, with an established network of local officials and chiefs who had served the Yadavas and Kakatiyas and were ready to serve new rulers. Its coasts, the Konkan in the west and the Coromandel in the east, linked it to Indian Ocean trade, and its position between the north and the far south made it a corridor of movement for armies, merchants, scholars, and holy men.

Two other developments formed the context. In the south, Vijayanagara had emerged as a major kingdom in the mid-fourteenth century and quickly became the Deccan rulers' principal rival. And across the Arabian Sea and Persian Gulf, the Persianate world of Iran, Iraq, and Central Asia supplied the Deccan courts with scholars, soldiers, merchants, and cultural models. The chapter follows the interaction of these regional and international forces.

Section 46.1

The Deccan before the Bahmani Sultanate: Geography, Political Conditions, and Historical Sources

The Deccan is the broad plateau that fills the peninsula south of the Vindhya and Satpura ranges and north of the Tungabhadra, bounded on the west by the Western Ghats and sloping eastward toward the Bay of Bengal. Its great rivers, the Godavari, the Bhima, the Krishna, and the Tungabhadra, rise in or near the Ghats and flow east, dividing the plateau into river basins and interfluves whose names recur throughout this chapter: the Raichur doab between the Krishna and the Tungabhadra, the Telangana uplands of the Godavari and Krishna, the Marathwada and Berar plains of the north, and the Konkan strip below the Ghats. Much of the interior is covered by dark regur soil, well suited to cotton and dryland grains, while the better-watered river valleys supported rice and sugarcane. Rainfall is moderate and variable, so that tanks, wells, and small channels mattered greatly to agrarian life.

Politically, the Deccan was not an empty frontier awaiting conquest. Before the fourteenth century its principal powers were the Yadavas of Devagiri and the Kakatiyas of Warangal, with the Hoysalas to the south, all of whose royal and temple institutions are discussed in earlier chapters. These kingdoms had built networks of fortresses, granted revenue rights to chiefs, temples, and Brahmins, and relied on local village officials who recorded and collected the agricultural surplus. When Delhi's armies under the Khaljis and then the Tughlaqs (Chapters 41 and 42) overthrew these kingdoms in the early fourteenth century, they inherited rather than abolished much of this local machinery. Muhammad bin Tughlaq's decision around 1327 to make Daulatabad, formerly Devagiri, a second capital brought Delhi's elites, soldiers, and scholars into the plateau in large numbers.

Our knowledge of what followed depends on a distinctive body of evidence. The Futuh al-Salatin of Isami, a Persian verse chronicle completed in 1350, was written at the court of the first Bahmani ruler and is therefore close to the events it describes, though it is partisan. The Burhan-i Ma'asir, composed in Ahmadnagar in the 1590s, and the history of Firishta, completed at Bijapur in the early seventeenth century, narrate the dynasty from later vantage points and sometimes preserve legends. To these chronicles historians add inscriptions in Arabic, Persian, Kannada, Telugu, and Marathi, coins, collected letters, European travel accounts, and surviving architecture. Students should treat the chronicles as interpretations with their own audiences and purposes, not as neutral records.

Section 46.2

The Rebellion of 1347 and the Foundation of Bahmani Rule

The immediate origin of the Bahmani Sultanate lay in the overstretched government of Muhammad bin Tughlaq (r. 1325-1351). His distant campaigns, heavy revenue demands, and administrative experiments strained relations with provincial officers. Among the most exposed were the amiran-i sada, the "commanders of a hundred," a body of military officers who held charge of districts in Gujarat and the Deccan and who were responsible for collecting revenue and maintaining troops. When the sultan's officials pressed them harshly, and when rebellions erupted elsewhere in his realm, a number of these officers rose in the Deccan in 1345, seizing Daulatabad's surroundings.

The rebels first raised an officer of Afghan origin, Isma'il Mukh, to the throne as Nasir al-Din Isma'il Shah. Isma'il was an older man who lacked the military ability to hold off the sultan's returning army, and the rebellion quickly turned to a more capable leader. Hasan, an officer known by the title Zafar Khan, had defeated a Tughlaq force and was acclaimed in August 1347 as Sultan Alauddin Hasan Bahman Shah. Isma'il stepped aside. The new ruler took Gulbarga as his capital, and by late 1347 the Deccan had a Muslim sultanate independent of Delhi. Muhammad bin Tughlaq, occupied with revolt in Gujarat and Sindh, died in 1351 without recovering the plateau.

The origins of the founder himself are overlaid by legend. A tradition recorded by Firishta describes Hasan as a humble servant of a Brahmin astrologer named Gangu, who foretold his greatness, and says that the dynasty's name, Bahmani, derived from "Brahman." Modern historians generally doubt this etymology and regard it as a later tale that explained a name actually derived from Bahman, a legendary Iranian king, whose lineage the founder claimed. This is an example of a recurring task in the chapter: separating contemporary evidence, such as Isami's account and the coins, from the stories later chroniclers told to give a dynasty an auspicious beginning.

The rebellion of 1347 should not be read as the product of a single act of defiance. It grew from the real difficulty of governing the plateau from Delhi, from the aspirations of a military elite that had acquired local resources and followings, and from the willingness of regional notables, including Hindu chiefs and village officers, to deal with whichever authority could protect their rights.

Primary Source Window

Isami's Futuh al-Salatin and Firishta's Chronicle

Source types: Persian verse chronicle (Isami, completed 1350) and Persian prose history (Firishta, early seventeenth century) · Places: Bahmani court at Gulbarga; Adil Shahi court at Bijapur

Context: Abd al-Malik Isami composed the Futuh al-Salatin, a long poem on the history of Muslim rulers in India, at the court of Alauddin Bahman Shah, dedicating it to the new sultan. Muhammad Qasim Firishta, an Iranian-born historian in Bijapur, wrote his history of Muslim India, including a section on the Bahmanis, some two and a half centuries after the foundation of the dynasty.

Paraphrase of the evidence (not a quotation): Isami presents the rebellion against Muhammad bin Tughlaq as justified by the sultan's oppressive policies and portrays Hasan as a worthy ruler chosen by his peers. Firishta's account adds the story of Hasan's service to the Brahmin Gangu and the derivation of the dynasty's name, which does not appear in the earlier source.

Analysis: Isami offers near-contemporary testimony and shows how the founding generation justified its break with Delhi. But he wrote for his patron and was hostile to Muhammad bin Tughlaq. Firishta had access to earlier works now lost, which makes him indispensable, yet he wrote at a time and place when dynasties sought prestigious genealogies, and some of his anecdotes carry later meanings. Historians therefore prefer Isami and datable inscriptions and coins for events of the 1340s, and use Firishta cautiously for details found only in him.

Questions for students:

  1. Why is a chronicle written in 1350 more valuable than one written in the 1600s for the founding of the sultanate, and in what ways is it also limited?
  2. What purposes might Firishta's story of Gangu have served?
  3. How can historians use coins and inscriptions to test chronicle claims?
  4. Whose point of view is missing from both accounts?
Section 46.3

Gulbarga, the First Capital, and the Organization of the New State

Gulbarga, which the founder renamed Ahsanabad, lay in the dry northern Karnataka plain on the road between the Maharashtra uplands and the Krishna valley. It was not a great city before 1347, and its choice reflected strategy. Gulbarga's position allowed access to the Bhima basin and to routes toward Telangana, the Raichur doab, and the western coast, and it lay far enough from Daulatabad to be defended against a Delhi counterattack. The site was fortified with a massive circuit of walls and moats, within which the early rulers built a citadel, palaces, and a congregational mosque.

Royal authority in the new state drew on the models its founders knew. The sultan held the military and fiscal levers of government, appointed commanders and governors, and presided over a court that used Persian for administration, as the Delhi sultans had done. Titles and offices recalled Delhi: a chief minister (wakil-i sultanat or vazir), a minister for finance, a commander of the army, and an office responsible for the royal household. Early appointments generally went to officers who had joined the rebellion, and rewards took the form of assignments of revenue from districts in return for maintaining soldiers, an arrangement that resembled the iqta system described in Chapter 40.

Legitimacy was a continual concern. A new dynasty born of rebellion needed to show that it had both power and righteousness. The Bahmani rulers did so through public displays of Islamic piety, patronage of scholars and Sufis, construction of mosques, and, later, through diplomatic recognition from the Abbasid caliph in Cairo, whose investiture of Muhammad Shah I is reported in the chronicles. They also cultivated connections with regional society. The early court employed Brahmin and other local officials, particularly in the keeping of village and district accounts, and tolerated local religious practice while using the language of Islamic kingship.

The Gulbarga congregational mosque, built in the later fourteenth century, illustrates the founding generation's style and its resources. Its unusual plan, in which the entire courtyard is covered by arches and small domes, has been interpreted as an adaptation to the plateau's climate and to Iranian and Delhi traditions. Whatever its sources, it asserted the permanence of the new rule on the landscape. Students should remember that Gulbarga's early rulers governed a small territory around their capital, and that the sultanate's fuller institutions developed in later reigns.

Section 46.4

The Early Bahmani Sultans and Territorial Expansion

Alauddin Bahman Shah (r. 1347-1358) spent his reign establishing control over the territory between the upper Krishna and the Godavari. His son Muhammad Shah I (r. 1358-1375) is generally regarded as the true organizer of the state. He expanded its holdings, confirmed its structure of provinces, and fought a long contest with the Kakatiya successor chiefs of Telangana and with Vijayanagara, the new Hindu kingdom founded on the Tungabhadra in the 1330s (Chapter 45). The wars of his reign introduced two themes that would define the sultanate's foreign relations: the competition for the Raichur doab, whose revenues and fortresses commanded the crossing between the two kingdoms, and the struggle for Telangana, whose rulers held a rich agricultural region and the fort of Warangal.

The chronicles describe an agreement of 1363 or so, following the war with Kapaya Nayaka of Warangal, in which the Telangana ruler is said to have presented the Bahmani sultan with a famous turquoise throne and ceded territory including the fort of Golconda. Historians use this tradition with caution, but Golconda's later importance gives the story weight. In the same period, Muhammad Shah I's artillery, if the early references to cannon and firearms are accurate, may have made the Bahmanis among the first South Asian powers to use gunpowder weapons, though the evidence for the date is debated and the chronicle claims may be anachronistic.

The decades after Muhammad Shah I were unstable. His son Mujahid was killed in 1378 by a cousin, who in turn was killed, and Muhammad Shah II (r. 1378-1397) restored order. His reign was comparatively peaceful and noted for support of learning, the consolidation of provincial administration, and a settlement with Vijayanagara. Yet the succession disputes revealed a pattern. Royal power rested on the loyalty of military commanders who controlled large followings, and a ruler who failed to command their allegiance, or who died leaving young heirs, risked factional struggle.

Territorially, by the end of the fourteenth century the Bahmani Sultanate controlled a belt across the northern and central Deccan, from the Berar border to the Krishna and from the Konkan hills toward the edge of Telangana. It remained a regional kingdom, not yet an empire, but it had defined the frontiers that its successors would contest.

Section 46.5

Firuz Shah Bahmani: Warfare, Diplomacy, and Courtly Ambitions

Firuz Shah (r. 1397-1422) is one of the most interesting Bahmani sultans, in part because his court was unusually well documented and in part because the chronicles present him as a scholar-king. Firishta reports that he had learning in the religious and natural sciences, spoke many languages, and kept in his service astronomers, physicians, and poets. He founded a new city, Firuzabad, on the Bhima near Gulbarga, and encouraged immigration of scholars from Iran and Iraq. Whatever the details, his reign shows that the Bahmani court by 1400 was a center of Persianate learning drawing on networks that reached across the Arabian Sea.

His military career was equally ambitious. Firuz Shah fought repeated campaigns against Vijayanagara for the Raichur doab, and against the rulers of Telangana, Kherla in the north, and the chiefs of the Konkan. The war of 1406, narrated with embellishment by Firishta, involved a dispute about a Vijayanagara marriage and a Bahmani siege that was lifted by a negotiated settlement in which Deva Raya I of Vijayanagara gave his daughter in marriage to the sultan along with a large indemnity. Whether the details are exact or not, the report illustrates the reality that Bahmani and Vijayanagara rulers regularly combined war with diplomatic marriages and tribute.

Firuz Shah's ambitions were checked in 1417, when Deva Raya's forces defeated a Bahmani army and cost the sultan territory and prestige. The setback intensified an existing problem, the sultan's reliance on influential nobles, and strengthened rivals within the court, including the Sufi Gesu Daraz, who had come to Gulbarga in 1398 and whose spiritual authority offered an alternative focus of loyalty. The sultan's relations with the Sufi were reportedly strained, and the chronicles tell of tension over the succession, with the Sufi favoring the sultan's brother Ahmad.

In 1422 Firuz Shah abdicated or was forced from power by his brother, and he died soon afterward. His reign shows both the potential and the limits of Bahmani kingship: a ruler could mobilize learning, soldiers, and revenue on an impressive scale, but remained dependent on powerful commanders and on shifting alliances with neighbors.

Section 46.6

Ahmad Shah Wali and the Transfer of the Capital to Bidar

Ahmad Shah I Wali (r. 1422-1436) took the throne with the support of Gesu Daraz and his followers, and he is remembered in the Bahmani tradition as Wali, "the saint." His reign marked an important turn. He campaigned vigorously against the Telangana rulers, conquering Warangal around 1425 and bringing the Kakatiya heartland under Bahmani control. He also warred with Malwa, Gujarat, and the Konkan chiefs, while avenging the defeat of 1417 against Vijayanagara. Territorial expansion in the northeast and northwest changed the sultanate's strategic center of gravity.

That change underlay the transfer of the capital to Bidar. Bidar lay on a cooler, higher plateau closer to the center of the enlarged kingdom, on the road to Telangana and to the north, and its laterite fortress was strategically strong. The shift is usually dated to about 1425, though some historians place the completion of the move later in the 1420s or early 1430s. Gulbarga was not abandoned, since the tombs of the earlier sultans and the shrine of Gesu Daraz kept it important, but the court, administration, and building program moved to Bidar.

Bidar was reconstructed as a royal city. The fort, rebuilt in the following decades, enclosed palaces, audience halls, mosques, and water systems that brought water from springs into the citadel through underground channels, a notable achievement of engineering in a dry region. The royal tombs at Ashtur, east of the city, for Ahmad Shah and his successors, display painted interiors and show the dynasty's presentation of sacred kingship. Ahmad Shah's association with Sufis, especially the Gulbarga shrine and the Chishti-Gesu Daraz circle, gave the dynasty a religious dimension: the saint-king, blessed by living holy men, and his tomb a place of veneration.

Ahmad Shah's reign thus illustrates three processes: territorial expansion, the physical creation of a new capital, and the creation of a sacred aura around rulership. Each strengthened the sultanate in the short run. Together they also increased its demands on the provinces and the nobles who governed them.

Division II · §§ 46.7-46.12Bahmani Government, Society, and Political Fragmentation
Section 46.7

Deccanis, Afaqis, and the Composition of the Ruling Elite

The Bahmani nobility is usually described by historians in terms of two groups, the Deccanis (dakhnis) and the Afaqis (afaqis, or gharibs, "foreigners"). The Deccanis were Muslims rooted in the Deccan, including descendants of earlier migrants from the north who had settled and intermarried with local society, converts, and many men of mixed background. The Afaqis were newcomers who arrived by sea and land from Iran, Iraq, Central Asia, and Arabia during the fifteenth century, often educated, mercantile, and Persian-speaking, and often Shi'i or inclined to Iranian scholarly traditions.

The distinction mattered because it overlapped with competition for office. Afaqis often brought skills in administration and scholarship and were favored by sultans who sought loyal officials independent of local factions. Deccanis tended to hold influence through local connections, military followings, and long residence. Rivalry between the two groups is prominent in the chronicles, especially in accounts of the politics of Mahmud Gawan's ministry.

Historians nevertheless warn against reading these labels as fixed ethnic identities. The groups were internally diverse. Among Afaqis were Persians, Turks, Arabs, and others, with differences of region and sect, while the Deccanis included Sunni, Sufi-influenced, and other communities, as well as men of Maratha and Telugu background who entered the elite through service and conversion. African-origin soldiers and officials, often called Habshis, held important positions as well, many of them first brought to the Deccan as enslaved military recruits. Alliances shifted along lines of kinship, patronage, local interest, and sect, and individuals sometimes changed sides.

The roles of Brahmins, Marathas, and other regional communities also deserve emphasis. Hindu officials served as accountants, revenue officers, and diplomats, and landed families such as the deshmukhs and deshpandes retained their positions. The elite of the sultanate was therefore not simply a Muslim ruling class standing apart from a Hindu population. It was a layered political society with several overlapping hierarchies, in which religion, ancestry, language, wealth, military command, and loyalty to the sultan each counted, and in which the meaning of each category changed from reign to reign.

Historical Vocabulary
Deccani and Afaqi

Deccani (dakhni) designated Muslims rooted in the Deccan, including descendants of earlier northern migrants, converts, and men of mixed background. Afaqi (also gharib, "stranger" or "foreigner") designated recent arrivals from Iran, Iraq, Central Asia, and Arabia. The terms became politically charged in the fifteenth-century Bahmani court, where they marked competing networks seeking offices and revenue assignments.

Modern equivalents such as "natives versus foreigners" or "Sunni versus Shi'i" are misleading. Both groups were internally diverse, many Deccanis were Persian-speaking, many Afaqis were Sunni, and individuals changed affiliation. Historians use the terms to describe changing factions, not fixed ethnic or sectarian communities.

Section 46.8

Provincial Administration, Revenue, and Local Political Networks

The Bahmani sultans governed through provinces called tarafs. Early in the dynasty there were four, centered on Gulbarga, Daulatabad, Berar, and Bidar, each under a governor (tarafdar) who held both civil and military power. As the sultanate expanded, new tarafs were added, and Mahmud Gawan later increased their number to eight, as discussed in Section 46.11. Each governor was responsible for revenue collection, the maintenance of troops, and the administration of justice, subject to the sultan's authority and the dispatch of royal inspectors.

Land revenue was the principal source of income. The state claimed a share of the crop, typically assessed with the help of local officials, and collected it in grain or cash. In many districts the sultanate relied on hereditary local intermediaries, the village headmen (patils), the village accountants (kulkarnis), and district officers (deshmukhs and deshpandes), who knew local production and could mobilize cultivators. Brahmin families filled many accounting roles, often with continuity from the earlier Yadava and Kakatiya governments. The Bahmani state thus ruled through existing institutions, adding new offices above them.

Revenue was distributed in two ways. Crown or khalisa lands paid directly to the royal treasury and supported the court, while assignments to nobles supported their troops. A noble might hold a province or a district and keep a fixed number of horsemen in return. The system gave governors strong incentives to build local power, since revenue, soldiers, and local officers were all under their control. Strong governors could protect the frontier and support the dynasty, but they could also resist the center.

Local chiefs mattered too. In Telangana, in the Konkan, and in forested or hilly districts, rulers dealt with nayakas, reddis, and other chiefs who paid tribute or served as military allies without being fully absorbed. The sultans negotiated with them through gifts, marriage, honors, and the threat of force. Historians therefore describe Bahmani authority as variable across space, firmer in the plains near the capitals and more negotiated in remote districts.

Land revenue was not the sultanate's only income, and the other sources help explain why governors and sultans fought over particular places. Ports and passes yielded customs duties and tolls, markets and craft quarters paid levies, subordinate chiefs paid tribute, and successful campaigns brought indemnities and booty. A district that combined agricultural revenue with a trade route, such as the Konkan approaches to the Ghats or the Telangana road to the coast, was worth far more than its acreage suggested. This is one reason why the control of forts and tolls, not only of fields, figures so heavily in the chronicles.

The evidence for how revenue was actually assessed is thin for the Bahmani period. The chronicles describe officials and offices but rarely the mechanics of assessment, and few contemporary revenue documents survive. Historians infer practice from later Adil Shahi and Nizam Shahi records, from inscriptions that record grants and local rights, and from analogies with the Delhi Sultanate. This means that statements about Bahmani revenue, including those in this section, describe broad patterns and probabilities rather than documented procedures district by district. Students should also remember that officials who wrote about administration were usually writing from the court's point of view, and that the cultivators who bore the demand appear in the sources mainly as a category to be taxed.

Section 46.9

Bahmani Warfare, Fortresses, and the Eastern and Southern Frontiers

Warfare was the Bahmani sultans' most visible activity, and the chronicles are filled with campaigns. Understanding it requires attention to geography. The Raichur doab, a fertile, well-watered land between the Krishna and Tungabhadra, was valued for its agricultural wealth, and its fortresses of Raichur and Mudgal commanded the routes between the plateau's two great powers. Whoever held the doab could threaten the other's heartland. The result was a contest that continued, with interruptions, from the fourteenth century until the sixteenth, and one that must be seen from both sides, as Chapter 45 shows the Vijayanagara perspective.

To the east, Warangal and the Telangana uplands were the other major prize. The region possessed large tanks, productive rice lands, and powerful local chiefs, and it controlled access to the Andhra coast and its ports. Bahmani campaigns there gradually moved the frontier eastward, and by the mid-fifteenth century the sultanate reached toward the eastern coast and the Orissa border. To the west, the Konkan and the ports of Dabhol and Chaul offered maritime commerce and horse imports, and Bahmani control of the coast brought the sultanate into contact with Indian Ocean trade.

Fortresses held the system together. Bahmani engineers and masons built or enlarged citadels with multiple walls, bastions, moats, and gates designed to resist sieges and, increasingly, artillery. Control of forts was a matter of constant political concern, since a governor who held a fort and its garrison held a province. For this reason royal appointments of fort commanders (qiladars) and attempts to separate them from provincial governors figured in later reforms.

Campaigns were seasonal, timed to the monsoon and harvest, and sustained by grain from the countryside, bullocks, and large baggage trains. Soldiers included cavalry, drawn from local and immigrant elites, infantry from many communities, and later specialists in artillery. For the people of border districts, campaigns brought requisition, destruction of crops, and displacement, as the sources occasionally reveal, and the recurring wars of the doab and Telangana imposed lasting burdens on cultivators.

Section 46.10

Mahmud Gawan: Minister, Diplomat, and Imperial Strategist

Mahmud Gawan (c. 1411-1481) is the best-known Bahmani statesman, and his career shows the sultanate's links to the wider Persianate world. Born in Gilan, in northern Iran, into a family of officials, he engaged in trade and study before arriving at Bidar in 1453. He entered the service of Sultan Alauddin Ahmad II and rose under his successors, receiving the title Malik al-Tujjar, "chief of merchants," and, from the 1460s, the senior position of minister (vakil-i sultanat and later vazir) during the reign of the young Muhammad Shah III (r. 1463-1482), whose regency he effectively directed.

Gawan's strength lay in combining skills. He was a scholar who corresponded with learned figures abroad, including the poet Jami, and whose letters survive in collections such as the Riyaz al-Insha. He was also an energetic military commander who led campaigns to the west coast, notably taking Goa in 1472 from Vijayanagara-aligned authorities; Dabhol was already part of the Bahmani maritime sphere. He also campaigned to the east against Orissa and Kondavidu, and in Telangana. These campaigns extended the sultanate to both coasts, giving it control of the horse trade and access to the Bay of Bengal.

His diplomacy reflected the sultanate's international connections. Bahmani embassies reached Iran, Egypt, and Gujarat, and Gawan exchanged letters with rulers of Gilan, Gujarat, Malwa, and the Timurid world. The Mahmud Gawan Madrasa at Bidar, completed in 1472, is a physical expression of these ties. Its Iranian-style plan, with a courtyard, iwans, and a library, and its glazed tile decoration, suggests the Persian educational model transplanted to the plateau. The madrasa held a large collection of manuscripts and taught religious and secular sciences.

Gawan's own life illustrates how a newcomer could rise within the Bahmani court by service and talent, but also why he had enemies. As an Afaqi, he was resented by Deccani nobles, and his large wealth, administrative initiatives, and influence over the young sultan made him a target. Students should treat the laudatory portraits in later chronicles, many written by writers sympathetic to Persian immigrants, with the same caution as hostile portraits by his enemies.

Section 46.11

Mahmud Gawan's Administrative Reforms and the Limits of Centralization

The reforms associated with Mahmud Gawan responded to a practical problem. The four great provinces had grown powerful, and their governors held authority over revenue, soldiers, and forts. Gawan's solution, adopted in the 1470s, was to divide the kingdom into eight tarafs, reducing the size of each. He also reorganized royal control of resources, setting aside some districts in each province as khalisa lands that paid directly to the sultan, which gave the crown revenue independent of the governors.

Further measures aimed to reduce the governors' independence. He is said to have arranged that only one fort in each province would remain under the governor, with the others held by commandants appointed and paid by the sultan, so that provincial officers could not turn the entire district into a personal base. Revenue and military obligations were also standardized, with salary scales and numbers of troops fixed by rank, and a campaign to measure land and fix assessment was undertaken, though the extent of the survey is uncertain. These changes sought to make the nobility more dependent on royal salaries and supervision.

The reforms' effect needs careful evaluation. In the short run, they strengthened royal authority and may have improved revenue. But they also threatened the interests of powerful nobles, especially Deccanis, who had held large assignments and who now faced reduced territory, close supervision, and greater competition from Afaqis. By cutting provinces down, Gawan made governors less able to rebel individually, but he also made enemies who could combine against him at court.

Historians therefore avoid presenting Gawan's reforms as a successful modernization cut short. They were practical institutional adjustments in a political system where revenue, soldiers, and loyalty were tied together, and their success depended on the sultan's ability to enforce them. When the sultan's support wavered, as it did in 1481, the measures stood exposed. The case shows both the logic of centralization and its dangers in a state governed through negotiated relationships with powerful men.

The difficulty of evaluating Gawan's measures is partly one of evidence. The fullest descriptions come from Firishta, writing more than a century later, and from chronicles that were shaped by the factional politics that followed his death. His own letters and the Riyaz al-Insha show his concerns and self-presentation but do not amount to an administrative record. Historians therefore agree on the broad outline, the creation of eight tarafs, the separation of some forts and lands from the governors' control, and the fixing of ranks and salaries, while disagreeing about how thoroughly these arrangements were enforced and for how long.

The reforms also had a fiscal logic that is easy to miss. By setting aside khalisa lands and paying nobles according to rank and the troops they maintained, Gawan sought to make the cost of the army visible and accountable, so that a noble could no longer retain large revenue claims while presenting fewer soldiers than he was paid to keep. Such checks were an old problem for assignment-based states, and the Delhi sultans had faced the same issue. Their enforcement, however, required honest inspection, regular musters, and a sultan willing to discipline powerful men, conditions that could not be sustained under a young ruler and a hostile court. The lesson of the episode is therefore institutional as well as personal: a reform that depended on one minister's authority was only as durable as his position at court.

Section 46.12

The Execution of Mahmud Gawan and the Disintegration of Bahmani Authority

The final act of Gawan's career unfolded in the spring of 1481. After a campaign in the southwest, a group of Deccani nobles forged a letter, according to the chronicles, purporting to show Gawan in treasonous correspondence with the ruler of Orissa. They presented it to the sultan, Muhammad Shah III, who was intoxicated or in a rage, and who ordered the minister's execution. Gawan was killed on 5 April 1481 (conventionally dated), reportedly at the age of about seventy. The chronicles report that the sultan soon regretted the act and died the next year, though the story has the shape of a moral tale and its details are uncertain.

The execution mattered less as a personal tragedy than as a political turning point. It removed the one minister who had held the factions together and discredited the reform program. The Deccani nobles who took power did not restore the old order, but neither could they govern the state effectively. The sultans who followed, beginning with Mahmud Shah (r. 1482-1518), came to the throne young and were dominated by court factions. Real power passed to ministers such as Qasim Barid, who from the 1490s held Bidar, and to provincial governors who acted ever more independently.

Provincial ambitions accelerated the process. Yusuf Adil Khan in Bijapur, Malik Ahmad Nizam al-Mulk in Junnar and Ahmadnagar, Fathullah Imad al-Mulk in Berar, and Quli Qutb al-Mulk in Telangana were all governors with strong local followings and, by the 1490s, large armies. The sultan in Bidar could no longer enforce his orders. Rebellion, succession struggles, and attacks on the capital, including outbreaks of factional violence between Deccani and Afaqi groups in the capital, drove the process further.

By the early sixteenth century the Bahmani dynasty continued as a title but not as a government. A series of sultans reigned until about 1527 under the control of Bidar's regent Barid, and the last of them, Kalimullah Shah, fled to Bijapur and then to Ahmadnagar, where he died. The sultanate that had begun in the rebellion of 1347 ended with no formal abolition, as its provinces became separate states.

Historical Debate

Was Bahmani Fragmentation Primarily Administrative or Factional?

The question: Why did a large and wealthy sultanate break into five states within about fifty years of its peak?

Interpretation A: administrative. The Bahmani state governed through large provinces whose governors controlled revenue, troops, and forts. Over time, the structure of provincial government gave governors the means to act independently, whoever sat on the throne. On this view, fragmentation was built into the system, and Gawan's reforms were a recognition of the problem.
Interpretation B: factional. The decisive causes were the conflict between Deccani and Afaqi nobles, the weakness of boy-sultans, and the killing of Gawan, which destroyed the one balancing figure. On this view, the state might have continued had politics at the center remained stable, and the governors rebelled because the court had become a battleground.

Evidence: Both interpretations use the chronicles, which emphasize court factions, as well as evidence of governors' control of revenue and forts. The chronicles' emphasis on factional drama may reflect their authors' interests, while structural arguments rely on inference from the system's design.

Why it matters: The answer shapes how we see the successor states, as products of long-term structural change or of political accident, and how we compare the Bahmani case with other medieval states governed through assignments, including the Delhi Sultanate. Most historians now see the two explanations as complementary: structure created the opportunity, and faction supplied the occasion.

Division III · §§ 46.13-46.21The Five Successor Sultanates
Section 46.13

From Bahmani Provinces to Five Independent Sultanates

The Bahmani Sultanate did not divide on a single day. Its five successors emerged between about 1490 and 1528, in a staggered sequence that reflected the different positions of their founders. Bijapur, Ahmadnagar, and Berar became effectively independent around 1490, when their governors ceased to obey Bidar and began to rule in their own names. Bidar itself passed into the hands of Qasim Barid, a minister who controlled the sultans and, from the early sixteenth century, ruled as their guardian. Golconda's founder, Quli Qutb al-Mulk, governed Telangana from the 1490s and declared independence only around 1518, after the sultanate's authority had visibly vanished.

Historians distinguish practical autonomy from formal independence. A governor might withhold revenue, command his own army, and appoint his own officials while still reciting the Bahmani sultan's name in the Friday sermon (khutba) and issuing coins in his name. Formal independence came when the ruler took royal titles, replaced the sultan's name in the khutba, and struck coins in his own name, and the dates of these steps often differ by years from the political reality. For this reason, the dates given in textbooks, including this one, are conventional and should be read as approximate markers of a longer process.

Why did the five states not emerge at once? Part of the answer lies in the geography of the old tarafs, each with a defensible core, local revenue, and a loyal following. Part lies in personal circumstance: governors such as Yusuf Adil Shah and Malik Ahmad had large armies and were far from the capital, while Quli Qutb al-Mulk faced local chiefs who made him reliant on Bidar for a time. Part lies in the rivalry between Deccani and Afaqi nobles, which made the governors' loyalty to a faction-ridden center unattractive.

The five states shared institutions and elites inherited from the Bahmanis, but they differed in size, religious orientation, and ruling families. Understanding them separately, as the following sections do, is essential to avoid treating the "Deccan Sultanates" as a single interchangeable bloc.

Section 46.14

Bijapur: Yusuf Adil Shah and the Foundation of Adil Shahi Rule

Yusuf Adil Shah (r. c. 1490-1510), the founder of the Adil Shahi dynasty of Bijapur, had risen under Mahmud Gawan and governed the Bijapur taraf from the 1480s. Firishta and later Bijapur tradition describe him as a son of the Ottoman sultan Murad II who escaped an attempted murder, was sold as a slave, and eventually reached the Deccan. Modern historians regard this as a dynastic legend, since the dates do not fit and the story served to give the dynasty a prestigious lineage. What is secure is that Yusuf was an Afaqi of probable Turkic or Iranian background, who entered Bahmani service as a slave-soldier and rose by ability.

As governor Yusuf acquired a strong army and the allegiance of many officers, and after about 1490 he governed independently. His territory centered on Bijapur, in the arid plateau south of the Bhima, and extended toward the Krishna and the Konkan. He asserted his independence cautiously, however, and the traditional date for his adoption of a Shi'i khutba, 1502, indicates both a political break with the Sunni Bahmani court and an alignment with the new Safavid dynasty of Iran, which came to power in that year. The Shi'i orientation created tensions with other nobles, and the sultan's successors alternated between Sunni and Shi'i observance depending on their circumstances.

Yusuf's reign was dominated by relations with neighbors. He fought with the Barid at Bidar, with Vijayanagara over the doab, and with the Portuguese, who captured Goa from the Adil Shahi governor in 1510, the year Yusuf died. The loss of Goa was a serious blow to Bijapur's maritime commerce, since the port had been a principal point of entry for horses and luxury goods, and it opened a long and varied relationship with the Portuguese, discussed further in Chapter 49.

The development of Bijapur as a capital began in this reign with fortification and the construction of palaces and a congregational mosque. But the city's monumental growth came in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, as Section 46.29 describes. Yusuf's achievement was political: he created a state where a collapsing province had been, and he left a dynasty that would survive until 1686.

Yusuf Adil Shah's early career also illustrates how the successor states recruited their elites. His court drew on Persian, Turkic, and Deccani officers, and the Shi'i orientation he adopted attracted Iranian scholars and soldiers who looked to the Safavids. The same policy alienated Sunni nobles and ordinary Deccani soldiers, and the dynasty's later vacillation between Shi'i and Sunni observance can be read as a series of attempts to rebalance the elite. Religious policy, in other words, was a tool of coalition-building as much as an expression of belief.

The Portuguese episode of 1510 showed how quickly Bijapur's position could be threatened from the sea. The Portuguese seized Goa that year, were expelled by Adil Shahi forces within months, and returned in strength in the autumn, after which they held the island permanently. For Bijapur the loss meant not only a harbor but a customs revenue and the best landing for horses. Afterwards the sultanate relied on other ports and on negotiated access to Goa itself, arrangements that Chapter 49 treats from the Portuguese side. The episode also shows why the early Adil Shahi rulers could not concentrate solely on their land frontiers.

Section 46.15

The Adil Shahi Sultanate: Expansion, Kingship, and Political Consolidation

The Adil Shahi dynasty lasted nearly two hundred years, and its history is best understood in phases. Under Yusuf's successors, Isma'il Adil Shah (r. 1510-1534) and Mallu Adil Shah, the dynasty faced instability, including a brief takeover by an Afaqi faction and a restoration. Isma'il fought Vijayanagara and the Barid and Ahmadnagar sultans, and he is credited with a diplomatic marriage into a Maratha family. Ibrahim Adil Shah I (r. 1534-1558) turned to a Sunni orientation, replaced Persian officials with Deccanis, and used Marathi in revenue records, a change of lasting significance for the state's relations with the countryside.

Ali Adil Shah I (r. 1558-1580) was the dynasty's major sixteenth-century ruler. Returning to a Shi'i court, he built an alliance with Vijayanagara's regent Rama Raya in the 1550s against Ahmadnagar, then joined the sultanate coalition that destroyed Rama Raya at Talikota in 1565 (Section 46.23). He then extended Bijapur's control into the Raichur doab and parts of the Krishna-Tungabhadra country, and he strengthened the capital with new fortifications, an aqueduct system, and the Jami Masjid. His marriage to Chand Bibi, daughter of the Nizam Shahi dynasty of Ahmadnagar, tied the two kingdoms together, though not enduringly.

Ibrahim Adil Shah II (r. 1580-1627) came to the throne as a child, and the regency passed through factional hands before he took power around 1590. His reign saw the consolidation of Bijapur as a major regional power, with campaigns against Ahmadnagar, the annexation of Bidar in 1619, and a diplomatic posture that balanced the growing Mughal presence. He pursued a policy of religious inclusiveness at court, patronizing Sufis, Hindu scholars, and musicians, and he is remembered for his musical interests, discussed in Section 46.32.

The Adil Shahi sultans ruled with the aid of a flexible elite of Deccanis, Afaqis, Habshis, Marathas, and Brahmins. Their success depended on balancing these groups and on the revenues of a large and varied territory. The political development of Bijapur in the seventeenth century, under Muhammad Adil Shah and his successors, is treated in Section 46.34.

The reign of Isma'il Adil Shah and the brief reign of Mallu Adil Shah show how fragile the new dynasty remained in its second generation. Court factions rose and fell, and the shifting religious policy of successive rulers reflected struggles among Deccani, Afaqi, and Habshi nobles. The sultanate recovered stability under Ibrahim Adil Shah I and Ali Adil Shah I, who strengthened the army, expanded the cultivated and taxed area, and invested in the capital's defenses and water supply. Ali Adil Shah I also began the Jami Masjid of Bijapur, one of the largest buildings of his reign.

The minority of Ibrahim Adil Shah II is a useful example of how the sultanates were governed in a crisis. When he succeeded as a boy, power passed through a series of regents drawn from different factions, among them the Habshi noble Dilawar Khan and, according to the chronicles, the dowager queen Chand Bibi, whose later fame at Ahmadnagar began in this setting. The regents' rivalry and their appeals to different groups of soldiers and officials show that the sultan's person was less important to governance than the balance of forces around him. When Ibrahim took personal control around 1590, he reduced the influence of the Habshi and Persian factions and relied more on a mixed court of Deccani, Maratha, and Brahmin officials, a shift that fits his reputation for broad patronage.

Section 46.16

Ahmadnagar and the Foundation of the Nizam Shahi Dynasty

The Nizam Shahi dynasty of Ahmadnagar was founded by Malik Ahmad, the son of Nizam al-Mulk Bahri, a high Bahmani minister who was killed in the factional struggles of 1486. Malik Ahmad, governor of Junnar, inherited his father's title and following. In June 1490 he defeated a Bahmani army sent against him and, according to tradition, built a camp on the site that became Ahmadnagar, founded in 1494 on the Sina river. The city's name honored the founder, and from about 1490 he governed independently.

The Nizam Shahi political base was the northern Deccan, the territory of the old Daulatabad province and the western plateau. Malik Ahmad's army contained many Brahmins and Marathas, a result of the region's population and of the dynasty's origins. His father had been a Hindu convert, the son of a Brahmin accountant according to tradition, a circumstance that gave the family connections with local administrative classes. This background partly explains the prominent place of Brahmin officials and Maratha chiefs in the Ahmadnagar government.

The dynasty's early history was dominated by campaigns to seize forts. Malik Ahmad took Daulatabad, the former Yadava capital, along with neighboring hill forts, and he fought to hold Berar and the Konkan. His successors, Burhan Nizam Shah I (r. c. 1509–1553) and Husain Nizam Shah I (r. c. 1553–1565), continued the competition with Bijapur for Sholapur and the Bhima valley, with Berar for control of the northern frontier, and with Gujarat for the western coast. Burhan declared the Shi'i faith as the state religion in 1537, a decision that tied the dynasty to Iranian religious networks and introduced new scholars to the court.

Ahmadnagar was the first of the Deccan sultanates to feel the Mughal advance, because it lay closest to Khandesh and Gujarat. Its location, wealth, and strong fortresses made it both a target and a buffer. The city of Ahmadnagar itself developed as a fortified capital with palaces, gardens, and an elaborate water system, though much has been lost. Daulatabad, Ahmadnagar, and later Junnar and Khirki remained key sites of the state's military and administrative life.

Religious change at Ahmadnagar was tied to the arrival of Iranian scholars. Burhan Nizam Shah I's proclamation of Shi'ism in 1537 followed the influence of learned figures such as Shah Tahir, an Iranian scholar and diplomat of Ismaili background who became a leading religious adviser at the court, according to the chronicles. The state religion did not translate into the exclusion of other communities: Brahmins held high revenue offices, Maratha families provided cavalry, and the dynasty's relations with the Hindu elite of the Maharashtra uplands were close. The tension between a Persianate and Shi'i court culture and a largely Marathi-speaking, Hindu and Sunni countryside is a recurrent theme of Nizam Shahi history.

Ahmadnagar's position between Khandesh, Gujarat, Berar, and Bijapur meant it fought on several fronts at once. Under Murtaza Nizam Shah I (r. 1565-1588) it conquered Berar and fought campaigns against Bijapur and Golconda, and under his successors faction fights between Deccani and foreign nobles and between Sunni and Shi'i groups weakened the state precisely when Mughal pressure was beginning. The best contemporary history of the dynasty, the Burhan-i Ma'asir, was written within it, which gives it value and also explains why its picture of Berar and of Ahmadnagar's neighbors is not neutral.

Section 46.17

Ahmadnagar under Pressure: Chand Bibi, Malik Ambar, and Political Survival

By the 1590s, Ahmadnagar faced the combined pressures of Mughal expansion and internal disorder. Succession disputes following the death of Burhan Nizam Shah II in 1595 split the nobility, and the Mughal prince Murad, Akbar's son, besieged the city. The defense was led by Chand Bibi, a daughter of Husain Nizam Shah I and the widow of Ali Adil Shah of Bijapur, who had returned to Ahmadnagar and who acted for an infant claimant, her young relative. She organized the garrison, supervised repairs to the fortifications, and in 1596 negotiated a treaty that ceded Berar to the Mughals. She was later killed by her own officers during the final crisis of 1599–1600, amid accusations that she was negotiating with the Mughals, and the fort fell to Akbar's forces in 1600.

The fall of the fort did not end the Nizam Shahi state. Malik Ambar (c. 1548-1626), an Ethiopian-born former slave who had risen in the Ahmadnagar army, organized resistance and installed a Nizam Shahi prince on the throne. Taken from the Horn of Africa, sold in Arabian slave markets, and bought by an Ahmadnagar minister who was himself a Habshi, Malik Ambar made himself the effective ruler as regent and chief minister. He relocated the capital to Khirki, near Daulatabad, which later became Aurangabad.

Malik Ambar is remembered for his military organization and for his revenue arrangements. He recruited Maratha horsemen who fought by raiding and harassing larger Mughal armies, adopting tactics later associated with Shivaji's forces, and he cooperated with Bijapur and Golconda, though not always steadily. In the countryside he introduced or consolidated a system of crop-based land assessment and reduced the role of revenue-assignment holders, at least according to later tradition; the details are debated, and his reputation as a revenue reformer rests partly on later sources. The Maratha chief Shahaji Bhonsle, the father of Shivaji, served under Malik Ambar and later others.

Malik Ambar's career illustrates the role of African-origin elites in the Deccan. Habshis were not incidental outsiders but men of power, commanders and administrators whose careers show the Deccan's connections to the Indian Ocean slave trade and to routes of mobility. After his death in 1626, Ahmadnagar's fortunes declined, and Shah Jahan's armies extinguished the dynasty in 1636.

The long contest with the Mughals that followed Malik Ambar's rise produced one of the most striking episodes in Deccan history. Malik Ambar organized an army that combined Habshi and Deccani cavalry with Maratha light horse and, at times, allied forces from Bijapur and Golconda. In 1624, at the Battle of Bhatvadi, Malik Ambar's forces defeated an opposing coalition of Mughal and Bijapuri troops, temporarily relieving pressure on Ahmadnagar. The coalition shows why alliances should not be inferred from shared religion or from later cooperation against the Mughals. Malik Ambar also founded and developed Khirki, with markets, water works, and mosques, as a new capital less exposed than Ahmadnagar itself.

After his death, the dynasty could not sustain itself. His son Fath Khan, who succeeded to the ministry, quarreled with the Nizam Shahi ruler and ultimately surrendered Daulatabad to the Mughals in 1633, an act the chronicles treat as a betrayal. Shah Jahan's forces took the last Nizam Shahi ruler into custody, and the attempt of Shahaji Bhonsle to maintain a Nizam Shahi claimant in the following years, with some Bijapuri help, ended in 1636. The failure shows that Malik Ambar's achievement had depended on a particular combination of talent and circumstance, not on institutions that could outlast him.

Section 46.18

Golconda and the Foundation of Qutb Shahi Power

The origins of the Qutb Shahi dynasty of Golconda lie in the career of Quli Qutb al-Mulk, a Turkman noble from Hamadan in Iran who migrated to the Deccan as a young man in the 1470s and entered Bahmani service. He served under Mahmud Gawan and later became governor of the Telangana taraf, sent to the eastern Deccan around 1496, with his seat at the fortress of Golconda. In the confusion following the Bahmani collapse he acquired control of surrounding districts, and around 1518 he formally declared independence, taking the title Sultan Quli Qutb Shah.

His state was built on the Telugu-speaking lands of the Krishna and Godavari basins, a region with an established agricultural economy, a population of Telugu-speaking cultivators and chiefs, and an established tradition of fort-based rule from the Kakatiya period. Sultan Quli expanded eastward, capturing forts such as Kondapalli and Kondavidu from the Gajapati rulers of Orissa and from local chiefs, and he fought with Vijayanagara over the east coast. He also made Golconda a strong citadel, reinforcing its granite walls on a rocky hill, and began the construction of mosques and other buildings.

Relations with Vijayanagara were complicated. Qutb Shahi forces fought Vijayanagara armies, but Qutb Shahi rulers were also, at times, allies and tributary partners. Ibrahim Qutb Shah (r. 1550-1580), who had spent years in exile in Vijayanagara before becoming sultan, was personally familiar with its court, and he is credited with beginning Telugu literary patronage in the sultanate. He joined the 1565 coalition against Rama Raya, and after the victory his armies took Vijayanagara forts in the eastern territories.

The Qutb Shahi dynasty was Shi'i from an early date and maintained diplomatic and religious ties with Safavid Iran. This orientation, along with the many Persian migrants who entered its service, gave the court a distinctive cultural character, though it also continued to rely on Telugu Brahmins and local chiefs for administration. Sultan Quli was assassinated in 1543 by his own son, a reminder that dynastic politics in the sultanate could be violent, and the following half-century saw rapid turnover before consolidation under Ibrahim and his successors.

The Qutb Shahi state was built on an unusually mixed society. In its early decades the court included Persian-speaking immigrants, Deccani Muslims, and Telugu-speaking chiefs and Brahmins. The Niyogi Brahmin families, who had long served as accountants and administrators in the Telugu country, became prominent in the revenue offices, and Telugu inscriptions of the period record grants and rights alongside Persian ones. This pattern distinguishes Golconda from Bijapur and Ahmadnagar, where Marathi and Kannada administrative traditions were more important.

Dynastic violence marked the sultanate's early years. After Sultan Quli's assassination in 1543, his son Jamshid Qutb Shah took the throne and ruled harshly until his death in 1550, and Ibrahim Qutb Shah returned from Vijayanagara to take power, bringing with him connections to the southern court and a wider Telugu patronage. His reign (1550-1580) saw the consolidation of the eastern coast, as the Qutb Shahis extended their authority toward the Krishna delta and the Andhra uplands, areas rich in rice, cotton, and ports. These conquests gave the dynasty resources that its neighbors lacked and explain why Golconda could fund a large court and military without the exhausting border wars that drained Ahmadnagar.

Section 46.19

The Qutb Shahis: Hyderabad, Kingship, and Regional Expansion

Muhammad Quli Qutb Shah (r. 1580-1612) is remembered as the founder of Hyderabad and as one of the great patrons of Deccani culture. Succeeding his father Ibrahim as a boy, he governed at first with the help of advisers and then personally. His reign was relatively peaceful, protected by a policy of balance with Bijapur and Ahmadnagar and by the strength of the Golconda economy.

Golconda's growth had made its citadel too small and its water supply inadequate for the expanding court and the commercial population. In 1591, Muhammad Quli founded a new city on the south bank of the Musi river, about eight kilometers from the fort, and named it Hyderabad (or Bhagnagar, as some sources report in connection with a local name). The city's plan centered on the Charminar, a monumental gateway with four minarets built in 1591 at the intersection of four main avenues. The new city included palaces, a congregational mosque, markets, residential neighborhoods for different social groups, and gardens, and its founding can be understood as a large-scale effort at urban planning that also expressed dynastic ambition.

The later Qutb Shahi sultans, Muhammad Qutb Shah (r. 1612-1626), Abdullah Qutb Shah (r. 1626-1672), and Abul Hasan Tana Shah (r. 1672-1687), governed a state that became more connected to international trade and also more exposed to Mughal pressure. Abdullah accepted Mughal suzerainty in 1636, agreeing to pay tribute and recite the emperor's name in the khutba. Under Abul Hasan, real power rested with two Telugu Brahmin ministers, Madanna and Akkanna, whose policies, including conciliation of the Marathas under Shivaji, brought them into conflict with Deccani and Afaqi nobles and with the Mughals.

The eastern Deccan territories were economically important beyond their agricultural revenue. They contained the diamond mines near Kollur and the textile-producing districts of the Coromandel coast, including the port of Masulipatnam, a major point of contact with Indian Ocean merchants and with Dutch and English companies. These resources are examined in Section 46.26.

The founding of Hyderabad is traditionally linked with the planning of the minister Mir Mumin Astarabadi, an Iranian immigrant, who is credited in some accounts with the design of the city's grid. Whatever the details, the new city reflected a deliberate program: a main axis from the Charminar to the Musi river, markets and public buildings clustered around it, and residential quarters assigned to different groups. The Mecca Masjid, begun under Muhammad Qutb Shah in the 1610s (sources differ between 1614 and 1617), was a very large building completed decades later, after the Mughal conquest. The long construction shows how royal projects could outlast the dynasty that began them.

The Qutb Shahi court also reached across the Indian Ocean. Masulipatnam, the principal port, drew Iranian, Armenian, and Hindu merchants as well as Dutch and English traders, who established factories there in the early seventeenth century. The sultanate collected customs, sold diamonds, and bought horses and luxury goods. The political economy of Golconda therefore depended heavily on the maritime trade of the Coromandel coast, and this dependence is one reason that Mughal control of Masulipatnam and Bengal later threatened the kingdom.

Section 46.20

Berar and the Imad Shahi Dynasty

Berar, the region of the upper Purna and Wardha valleys, is a fertile cotton-growing tract and was one of the Bahmani sultanate's four original provinces. Its agricultural wealth and strategic position between the Deccan and the northern plains made it a persistent object of competition. Its governor, Fathullah Imad al-Mulk, was a Kannadiga convert, reportedly from a Hindu family of Vijayanagara's territory who was taken prisoner as a boy and entered Bahmani service, then rose to become governor of Berar. Around 1490 he asserted independence and founded the Imad Shahi dynasty, with the hill fortress of Gawilgarh as a major stronghold and Ellichpur as its principal capital.

The Imad Shahi state was one of the smaller successors, though Berar was an extensive and agriculturally productive province; its history is less well documented than that of the major sultanates, partly because its chronicles have not survived in comparable form. Fathullah (d. c. 1504) was followed by his son Ala al-Din Imad Shah (r. c. 1504-1530), who sought support from the Gujarat sultans and fought with Ahmadnagar. A succession involving the child ruler Burhan Imad Shah in 1562 left the state under the growing control of the minister Tufal Khan, who subsequently displaced the ruler or exercised power in his name; the chronology of this takeover is disputed.

Berar's rulers encountered difficulties that made survival unlikely. The state was exposed to Ahmadnagar on its southern frontier and to Gujarat and Khandesh on the north, and it lacked the territorial depth of the larger states. Following a campaign variously dated to 1572–1574, Murtaza Nizam Shah I of Ahmadnagar absorbed Berar; genealogical scholarship commonly places the final annexation in 1574, although other historical chronologies use 1572. Berar's absorption into Ahmadnagar was a sign of how rivalry among the sultanates could end in conquest, not just competition.

The Berar case also reveals the limits of the label "Muslim dynasty" as an explanation. Fathullah Imad al-Mulk's Hindu origins, the region's mixed population, and the local revenue officers' continuity across regimes all complicate a simple picture. After Berar was ceded to the Mughals in the 1596 treaty with Ahmadnagar, although enforcement remained contested, the region came increasingly under Mughal provincial rule, but it retained its distinct character, and its importance in the later cotton economy is a reminder of its longer agricultural history.

Because Berar's own chronicles did not survive in the way those of its neighbors did, historians rely heavily on the Burhan-i Ma'asir and Firishta, both written by authors attached to rival courts. This is a problem of method: a reader should treat their descriptions of the Imad Shahis as the view of competitors, especially their accounts of Tufal Khan's usurpation and of Ahmadnagar's justification for invasion. Inscriptions, coins, and the architecture of Ellichpur and the hill forts of Gawilgarh and Narnala provide a partial check, but a full reconstruction of the dynasty's internal politics is not possible.

Berar's economic importance is clearer. The black soils of the Purna and Wardha valleys supported cotton cultivation on a large scale, and cotton and cloth moved south and west along the roads to Ahmadnagar and Burhanpur and north toward Malwa. Control of this surplus was an obvious motive for Ahmadnagar's conquest, and later, for the Mughals' insistence on the cession of Berar in 1596. Berar was therefore a province whose wealth made it vulnerable, a pattern that helps explain its repeated transfer.

Section 46.21

Bidar and the Barid Shahi Dynasty

Bidar, the former Bahmani capital, passed after the decline of the sultans to the Barid Shahi dynasty. Qasim Barid, a Turkic or possibly Georgian slave who had been a minister under Mahmud Shah Bahmani, effectively controlled the court from the 1490s, holding the sultan as a puppet. After Qasim's death in 1504, his son Amir Barid continued as regent, controlling the last Bahmani rulers and ruling Bidar, in effect, from 1504. After the last Bahmani sultan withdrew from effective politics in the later 1520s, the Barids continued their de facto rule; formal royal titulature is most securely associated with Ali Barid Shah, who succeeded in 1542 and asserted sovereignty around 1542–1543. The dynasty's name derived from "Barid," a term associated with the postal service.

The distinction between effective control and formal sovereignty matters here. For several decades, the Barids exercised power while formally retaining the Bahmani dynasty, a pattern that preserved the legitimacy of the Bahmani line while delegating power. The effective disappearance of Bahmani government and the Barids' later assumption of their own royal style were distinct stages; Bidar remained a small state in the shadow of larger neighbors. Ali Barid Shah (r. 1542-1580) was the most prominent of the dynasty and a participant in the Talikota coalition.

The Barid Shahi dynasty's importance lies partly in its culture. Bidar, with its fort, madrasa, and tombs, was a center of learning, of Dakhni poetry, and of metalwork, and the Bidri ware that takes its name from the city, an alloy of zinc inlaid with silver, was refined here and became widely appreciated (Section 46.33). The Barid rulers also built tombs and mosques, though the political state was small and its resources limited.

The end of the state came in 1619, when Ibrahim Adil Shah II of Bijapur annexed Bidar, and the Barid ruler Amir Barid Shah III was taken to Bijapur. The fall of Bidar by Bijapur, and of Berar by Ahmadnagar, shows that the Deccan's smaller states survived only so long as rival powers tolerated them. The decline of the small powers left three major sultanates facing the Mughal Empire.

Bidar's history under the Barids is the story of a small state sustained by position and prestige. The city commanded roads linking Telangana, Bijapur, and the Godavari country, and it inherited the buildings, water system, and reputation of the former capital. The Barid rulers could not match the revenues of Bijapur or Golconda, and their security depended on skillful diplomacy and occasional alliances, such as Ali Barid's participation in the Talikota coalition. Bijapur's chroniclers, notably Firishta, tend to present the Barids as dependents or usurpers, and the lack of a substantial Barid Shahi chronicle means that historians must read these accounts critically.

The Barid period was also one of cultural continuity. Scholars and poets who had served the Bahmanis remained in Bidar, and the city's craft workshops continued to produce the metalwork that became famous as Bidri. The Barid tombs and the royal garden at Bidar show that the dynasty invested in the symbols of kingship even as its political independence narrowed. When Bijapur annexed the city in 1619, it absorbed a place whose cultural prestige far exceeded its political weight.

Section 46.22

Rivalries and Alliances among the Deccan Sultanates

The relations among the Deccan sultanates were fluid, driven by interest, kinship, and circumstance. Competition centered on border territories: Sholapur between Bijapur and Ahmadnagar, the Raichur doab between Bijapur and Vijayanagara, Bidar between several claimants, and Berar between Ahmadnagar and its northern neighbors. States fought for revenue, forts, and prestige, and also for control of trade routes to the coast.

Diplomacy used marriage, embassies, gifts, and shared Persianate court conventions. The marriage of Chand Bibi to Ali Adil Shah, and of other princesses among the dynasties, created kinship ties that were sometimes honored and sometimes ignored. Alliances shifted with remarkable speed: in the 1550s, Bijapur and Vijayanagara allied against Ahmadnagar; in 1565, Bijapur, Ahmadnagar, Golconda, and Bidar allied against Vijayanagara; and within a few years Bijapur and Ahmadnagar were again at war, while Golconda and Vijayanagara remnants cooperated.

Religion complicated but did not determine these alignments. Ahmadnagar's Shi'i dynasty fought Bijapur's Shi'i rulers, while Sunni and Shi'i sultans cooperated against others. Hindu rulers and officials participated at all levels. Rama Raya of Vijayanagara intervened repeatedly in Muslim states' disputes, supporting one against another and recruiting Muslim soldiers for his own army. The historian looking for a permanent division between "Muslim" and "Hindu" political blocs in the Deccan will not find it. Rulers made decisions on strategic, dynastic, and economic grounds, though religious language was sometimes used for legitimacy and mobilization.

The Deccan system resembled other multi-state systems in its balance-of-power logic. A state that grew too powerful, as Vijayanagara under Rama Raya appeared to do in the 1560s, provoked a coalition, and a state that was weakened became a target. This logic changed when a much larger power, the Mughal Empire, entered the region in the late sixteenth century, shifting the balance from competition among peers to survival before an imperial neighbor.

Section 46.23

The Sultanate Coalition and the Battle of Talikota in 1565

The coalition that fought at Talikota in January 1565 was the culmination of a long evolution. Rama Raya (Aliya Rama Raya), the regent of Vijayanagara, had dominated Deccan politics for decades, intervening in the sultanates' disputes and gaining territory, including parts of the Raichur doab. Contemporary and later sources report that his policy and his troops' conduct in the sultanates' territories, including destruction of mosques, provoked resentment, though Chapter 45 explains that these reports must be weighed against the political circumstances and the biases of the chroniclers. Alliances formed among the sultans, sealed by marriages, and the four rulers of Bijapur, Ahmadnagar, Golconda, and Bidar combined their forces.

The armies met near the Krishna at a place known in the sources as Talikota, though the battle was fought some miles away at Rakkasagi and Tangadagi. After a hard-fought engagement in which Rama Raya's army initially did well, the battle turned when Rama Raya was captured and killed, and the Vijayanagara army broke. The victors then sacked the capital, leaving a destruction from which the city never recovered.

For the sultanates, the consequences were uneven. Bijapur and Golconda took territories in the Raichur doab and beyond, and Ahmadnagar gained prestige but little land. The coalition itself dissolved quickly, as the allies resumed their rivalries, and the sultans did not occupy and administer Vijayanagara as a conquered empire. The Aravidu dynasty moved to Penukonda and later Chandragiri, continuing as a southern power.

The longer effect was to remove a major counterweight in the south, but not to produce Deccan unity. Within three decades, the Mughal threat would appear, and the sultans would find that the destruction of Vijayanagara had not secured their position. Historians therefore see Talikota less as the triumph of a Muslim confederacy than as a decisive episode in a continuing contest among rival states.

Section 46.24

Armies, Cavalry, Artillery, and Fortress Warfare

Military organization in the sultanates combined inherited and imported practices. Cavalry remained the elite arm, supplied by nobles who held revenue assignments and maintained horsemen in return. Horses were expensive, and most came by sea from Arabia, Iran, and the Gulf through the ports of Goa, Chaul, Dabhol, and Masulipatnam. Control of the horse trade, therefore, was a strategic matter, and the Portuguese in Goa used it as a lever in negotiations with Bijapur and Vijayanagara.

Infantry, drawn from many communities, was numerically large, and included matchlock men, archers, and pikemen, many from the Telugu, Kannada, and Maratha countryside. Artillery grew in importance in the sixteenth century. The sultanates cast large bronze and iron guns, and the cannon of Bijapur, such as the huge bronze piece known as Malik-i Maidan at Bijapur, cast at Ahmadnagar in the 1540s, show the scale of the technology. Specialist gunners included Ottoman, Iranian, and European personnel as well as locally trained men. At the battle of Talikota, the sultans' artillery was used to good effect, according to the sources.

Fortresses remained decisive. Golconda, Bidar, Daulatabad, Bijapur, Parenda, and many others had multiple wall circuits, bastions designed for guns, and elaborate gateways. A siege required engineering, mining, and heavy guns, and the campaigns of Murad and Akbar at Ahmadnagar, and later of Aurangzeb at Bidar, Bijapur, and Golconda, were sieges of months or years.

Armies depended on logistics: grain carried by banjara traders and pack bullocks, forage for horses, and water. Military labor, including sappers, camp followers, porters, and enslaved workers, was large and rarely recorded. For villagers on the line of march, a campaign meant requisition and risk, and for the commanders, it required careful scheduling around monsoon and harvest. Attention to these practical matters shows that war was an institution embedded in the economy, not just a series of battles.

Section 46.25

Revenue, Landholding, Provincial Government, and Local Elites

The administrative systems of the successor states grew from Bahmani models but diverged in important ways. All used provincial governors, military officers holding revenue assignments, and local hereditary officials. Yet the balance varied. Bijapur under Ibrahim Adil Shah I adopted Marathi for revenue accounts, making local accountants and village officials central to government, and its later rulers relied heavily on Maratha deshmukhs and Brahmin accountants. Ahmadnagar, with a high proportion of Brahmins and Marathas in its elite, developed a form of land measurement and assessment under Malik Ambar that attempted to base revenue on productive capacity. Golconda used Telugu as a language of administration alongside Persian, and its revenue was often collected through revenue farmers and local chiefs.

The role of local elites was crucial everywhere. Village headmen, accountants, district officers, and chiefs acted as intermediaries between the state and the cultivators, and their cooperation determined the effectiveness of revenue collection. In return for service, they received rights and perquisites, and their positions often passed to heirs. Sultanate rulers sometimes tried to restrict these intermediaries, as Mahmud Gawan had done, but usually accommodated them.

Maratha, Kannada, and Telugu political networks shaped the sultanates from below. Families such as the Bhonsles, Jadhavs, Ghorpades, and Mores served in the armies and administrations of Bijapur and Ahmadnagar, receiving rights over territory and playing the courts against each other. In Telangana, the Velama and Reddi chiefs, and in the Karnataka districts, the nayakas of the old Vijayanagara system, held positions that mixed autonomy with service. The sultanates were therefore not alien structures imposed on an unwilling land, but states built in negotiation with regional society.

Because evidence is uneven, with Bijapur's revenue records better preserved than those of Berar or Bidar, historians should avoid assuming that a practice documented in one state applied to all. Comparison across the kingdoms, as the feature below shows, reveals differences in priorities and capacities.

Evidence for comparison across the states comes from different kinds of documents. Bijapur's administrative practice can be seen in Marathi and Kannada records and in later Maratha papers that preserve Adil Shahi practice. Golconda's is documented in Telugu inscriptions and in European accounts, such as William Methwold's description of the kingdom written in the 1620s and Tavernier's later travels. Ahmadnagar's is preserved mostly in Persian chronicles and in the traditions attached to Malik Ambar. These differences in surviving evidence mean that comparisons between the states partly reflect what is known about each, not necessarily real differences in practice.

Within the countryside, the central relationship was between the state's demand and the capacity of local intermediaries to deliver it. Cultivators paid a share of the produce or its cash equivalent, village headmen and accountants verified and collected, district officers forwarded the amounts, and revenue assignees or the treasury received them. Each layer took its share. Disputes arose when a state raised its demand, when a harvest failed, or when an assignee tried to extract more than custom allowed, and the surviving records show appeals to officials and sometimes flight from villages. Rulers who wished to raise revenue therefore had to bargain with those who knew the land.

Section 46.26

Inland Trade, Maritime Commerce, Diamonds, and Textiles

The Deccan's wealth rested substantially on trade. Inland routes carried cotton, textiles, grain, and cattle across the plateau and linked the interior to the ports. To the west lay Goa, Chaul, Dabhol, and the Konkan ports, through which horses, copper, and luxury goods entered and cotton cloth and spices departed. To the east, Masulipatnam, Motupalli, and other Coromandel coast ports linked Golconda to Southeast Asia, Iran, and later the Dutch and English East India Companies. The Western Ghats passes were important commercial arteries, patrolled and taxed.

Textile manufacturing was a large part of the economy. Cotton was grown on the black soils and woven in many centers. Golconda's painted and printed cotton cloths, known later in Europe as chintz, and its fine muslins, were exported widely. Weavers, dyers, printers, and merchants formed a complex network, with advance payments and credit supporting production, an arrangement the European companies later used.

The diamonds of Golconda, mined at Kollur and other sites in the Krishna valley, became famous. The mines were controlled by the sultan, who leased or taxed them, and large stones were reserved for the crown. The diamond trade attracted merchants from Iran, Europe, and elsewhere, and European travelers such as Tavernier left descriptions. The wealth gave Golconda's rulers and nobles a source of income and gifts and made the kingdom an object of Mughal ambition.

Merchant communities included Muslim traders such as the Navayat and Bohras, Hindu Komatis, Chettis, and Banias, Armenians, Persians, and Europeans. Prominent merchants served as bankers and officials, and the great merchant-ministers such as Mir Jumla of Golconda combined commerce with high office. The sultanates' relations with the Portuguese were complex, ranging from conflict, as in the Ahmadnagar-Bijapur siege of Chaul and Goa in 1570-1571, to treaties on trade and horses. Their commercial networks thus connected the plateau to the Indian Ocean, and royal revenues rose and fell with trade.

Trade connected revenue, war, and production in a continuous cycle. Revenue collected in cash paid the cavalry commanders, who bought horses imported from Arabia and Iran through the ports; the ports earned customs on those imports and on the cloth and spices carried out; and the cash that returned to the countryside sustained weavers and traders. A state that lost a port, as Bijapur lost Goa, or a trade route, as Berar's neighbors blocked its roads, suffered in all of these ways at once.

Cloth production relied on credit. Merchants advanced money to weavers and dyers before the season, collected the finished goods, and sold them to exporters at the ports, where European companies and Asian traders bought them largely with silver. This bullion flow, together with diamond sales, is part of why Golconda appears so wealthy in contemporary accounts. Tavernier and other visitors described the diamond mines as large, crowded with workers, and closely supervised by the state, which collected fees and claimed the finest stones. These accounts are valuable but written by outsiders with commercial interests, and their numbers are better treated as impressions than as statistics.

Section 46.27

The Mughal Frontier and the Struggle for Deccan Independence

Akbar's interest in the Deccan began in the 1580s and 1590s, as the Mughal conquest of Gujarat and Khandesh brought Mughal armies to the northern edge of the plateau. Diplomatic missions to the sultans demanded recognition of Mughal overlordship. The sultans responded variably: Khandesh submitted, while Ahmadnagar, riven by factions, resisted. The siege of Ahmadnagar in 1595-1596, with Chand Bibi's defense, and the fall of the fort in 1600 brought the Mughals into the Deccan, but they did not hold the whole of the Nizam Shahi state, which Malik Ambar continued to defend.

For Bijapur and Golconda, the strategic problem was acute. They could cooperate with Ahmadnagar against the Mughals, but experience taught that Ahmadnagar's weakness meant Mughal pressure would reach them. They could also seek accommodation with the Mughals, paying tribute or sending embassies, in the hope of preserving autonomy. Both strategies were pursued at different times. Ibrahim Adil Shah II maintained cordial ties with Akbar and Jahangir, marrying a daughter into the Mughal family, while preserving his independence. Under Shah Jahan, the Mughal armies attacked Ahmadnagar again, extinguished its dynasty in 1636, and imposed treaties on Bijapur and Golconda that required tribute and recognition.

The Mughal frontier also changed regional politics. Mughal commanders and Deccan nobles moved between services, and Maratha chiefs, long serving the sultans, entered Mughal pay or acted independently. Shahaji Bhonsle, who served Ahmadnagar, Bijapur, and the Mughals in turn, shows the possibilities and perils of the position. His son Shivaji built an independent power in the 1640s and 1650s, taking forts from Bijapur, as Chapter 58 will describe.

The Mughal-Deccan relationship was thus not only conquest. It involved tribute, marriage, military service, and diplomacy, as well as war, and the sultans' strategic dilemma was how to maintain autonomy against an empire whose resources far exceeded their own. Chapter 53 examines Mughal Deccan policy in the reigns of Jahangir and Shah Jahan, and Chapter 54 the conquests of Aurangzeb.

Section 46.28

Gulbarga, Bidar, Bijapur, Ahmadnagar, Golconda, and Hyderabad as Urban Centers

The capitals of the Bahmani and successor states were the most visible expression of royal power, and each had a distinct urban character. Gulbarga, the first capital, was a fortified garrison town with a walled citadel, palaces, and a congregational mosque, surrounded by tombs and the shrine of Gesu Daraz. Bidar, built on a plateau edge, had a laterite fort with palaces and audience halls, a lower town, a madrasa, and an elaborate system of subterranean water channels (karez) that supplied the fort, a technology associated with Iran and Central Asia.

Bijapur grew into one of the largest cities of the sixteenth- and seventeenth-century Deccan. Surrounded by a long circuit of walls with gates and bastions, it contained an inner citadel, the Arkilla, with palaces and a Durbar hall, and a large urban area of residences, markets, and mosques. Water was a perennial problem in the dry plateau, and the Adil Shahis built aqueducts, tanks, and wells, some of them drawing on supplies several kilometers away. Visitors described crowded markets and diverse populations, and the city's size by the mid-seventeenth century reached into the hundreds of thousands, though estimates are uncertain.

Ahmadnagar was a fortified town on the Sina river with a round fort, gardens, and a system of canals, and Golconda, a granite fortress on a hill, had a lower town with a thriving commercial quarter. Hyderabad, planned in 1591, was laid out around the Charminar and the streets that radiated from it, with separate districts for nobles, merchants, and artisans, and with markets, caravanserais, and mosques. Contemporary European observers such as Tavernier praised the city's order and vitality.

Cities were also social worlds. Elite neighborhoods around the palaces housed nobles, officials, and soldiers, while artisans and merchants lived in specialized quarters named for their crafts. Religious institutions, including mosques, Sufi hospices, Hindu temples, and Jain shrines, were scattered through the urban areas, and gardens and suburban estates provided leisure. The relationship between capital and countryside was also close: cities drew grain, cotton, and labor from the surrounding region and distributed manufactured goods and royal patronage in return.

Cities in the Deccan were shaped by the need for water, defense, and display. Bijapur's walls enclosed a very large area, within which the citadel (Arkilla) with its palaces stood apart from the dense commercial quarters, mosques, and tombs. The city's water came from tanks, wells, and underground channels, some of which are still traceable. At Golconda, the granite fort and its lower town grew together, with royal pavilions inside the walls and the merchants and craftsmen of the market below. Hyderabad, by contrast, was laid out on a plain, with the Charminar at its center and with wide avenues, a feature praised by seventeenth-century travelers.

Gulbarga's story is one of decline relative to its successors. Once the Bahmani capital, it lost political importance when the court moved to Bidar, but its shrines and tombs maintained its significance as a religious center. This shows that cities served different functions that could diverge: a capital might be a political center in one reign and a pilgrimage site in another. Cities also depended on their hinterlands. Grain, cloth, fuel, and construction stone came from the surrounding region, and the movement of populations to and from the capitals in times of war, famine, or siege affected the countryside as well.

Section 46.29

Deccan Sultanate Architecture and Monumental Patronage

The architecture of the Deccan sultanates reveals the cultural and political ambitions of the dynasties. The Bahmani buildings at Gulbarga include the Jami Masjid, completed about 1367, whose covered courtyard and low arches form a distinctive composition, and the royal tombs, including the large tomb enclosure known as the Haft Gumbaz, "seven domes." These buildings drew on Tughlaq models from Delhi but developed regional features, including lower proportions, a fusion of dome and arch, and the use of local stone.

At Bidar, the Bahmani period saw the rebuilding of the fort, with its palaces, the Rangin Mahal with tile and mother-of-pearl decoration, and the great Mahmud Gawan Madrasa of 1472, with a tall minaret, an arched facade with tile decoration, and halls for teaching. The tombs of the Bahmani rulers at Ashtur, with their high domes and painted interiors, and the Barid Shahi tombs add further layers. The madrasa's design is closest to Iranian and Central Asian examples, an indication of Gawan's cultural orientation.

Bijapur's monuments are among the most famous in the Deccan. The Ibrahim Rauza, built by Ibrahim Adil Shah II in the early seventeenth century as a tomb for himself and his queen, with its slender minarets and richly carved stonework, is celebrated for its proportions. The Gol Gumbaz, the mausoleum of Muhammad Adil Shah (d. 1656), has one of the largest domed chambers in the world, with a dome supported on intersecting arches, and was completed about 1656. The Jami Masjid and other mosques display the dynasty's patronage, as do palaces such as the Gagan Mahal and Anand Mahal.

At Golconda and Hyderabad, the Qutb Shahi dynasty left the fort with its gates, palaces, and mosques, the Charminar, the Mecca Masjid begun in 1617, and a large necropolis of domed tombs near the fort, constructed for the sultans and their families. Ahmadnagar's buildings, including the Farah Bagh palace and the tomb of Salabat Khan, and surviving Nizam Shahi monuments in nearby towns, show another variant.

Dates, patrons, and functions differ, and students should avoid seeing a single "Deccani style." Some features recur, including plastered facades with stucco ornament, bulbous domes on lotus-petal bases, arcades, and fine stone carving, but each dynasty developed its own forms, and these buildings also incorporated Hindu craft traditions of masonry and ornament, as patrons relied on local builders.

Individual monuments show how patrons used architecture. The Gulbarga Jami Masjid, completed around 1367 under Muhammad Shah I, bears an inscription naming Rafi from Qazvin, whom architectural historians identify as its architect; the evidence thus rests on an inscription rather than merely on a later tradition. The Haft Gumbaz tombs of the early Bahmani rulers, the grand tombs at Ashtur, and the madrasa of Mahmud Gawan show a progression from relatively plain forms to richly tiled and painted interiors. Dates and attributions should always be taken from inscriptions where possible.

At Bijapur, the Ibrahim Rauza, associated with the architect Malik Sandal, combines a tomb and mosque on a shared platform, with a delicate stonework facade. The Gol Gumbaz, built for Muhammad Adil Shah, covers a vast square hall with a dome spanning roughly 44 meters, and its construction demanded advanced engineering with intersecting arches and pendentives. At Hyderabad, the Charminar and the Mecca Masjid are the most prominent Qutb Shahi works, while the great tomb complex near Golconda shows how royal burial grounds became sites of dynastic display. A student comparing these buildings should note that their builders were often Hindu and Muslim craftsmen working side by side, and that the decorative vocabulary combined lotus motifs and plaster work with calligraphy and Persian forms.

Section 46.30

Sufism, Islamic Scholarship, Religious Communities, and Court Patronage

Religious life in the sultanates was varied and institutionally complex. Sufi networks were prominent from the early period. The Chishti Sufi Gesu Daraz (Sayyid Muhammad Husayni, d. 1422), who migrated from Delhi to Gulbarga in 1398 with his disciples after the disruption of Timur's invasion (Chapter 42), became the leading spiritual figure of the Bahmani realm. A prolific author in Persian and Arabic on mystical and doctrinal topics, he is also associated by tradition with early vernacular verse, though attributions to him of Dakhni works are disputed. His shrine at Gulbarga remains a major center of pilgrimage for people of many communities. Sufi lodges, in Bijapur, Bidar, and elsewhere, served as places of learning, charity, and spiritual guidance, and rulers sought their blessing and legitimation.

Islamic scholarship was supported through madrasas, mosques, and court appointments. Qazis administered justice in the Hanafi and Shafi'i traditions, with Shafi'i practice common among Deccan and coastal communities. The arrival of Iranian scholars and the influence of Safavid Iran gave Twelver Shi'ism an institutional presence in the Adil Shahi, Nizam Shahi, and Qutb Shahi courts, where rulers sponsored Shi'i ceremonies such as the observance of Muharram, with the Qutb Shahi 'ashurkhanas, the buildings for mourning ceremonies, as the most famous. Sunni traditions remained strong, and the attitude of rulers shifted with circumstances.

Relations with Hindu and other communities varied. Hindu temples continued to function, receiving grants in some instances and facing destruction in wars. The sultans patronized Brahmin scholars and employed Hindu officials. Some Hindu religious figures and Sufis shared devotional ideas, as in the case of the poet-saints of Maharashtra, whose traditions are touched on in Chapter 48. Jain communities, strong in the Karnataka districts, and the Parsi, Jewish, and Christian merchants on the coast formed other parts of the religious landscape.

Evidence about religion comes from inscriptions, shrines, chronicles, and the writings of scholars, and it tends to reflect elite and institutional perspectives. The religious lives of ordinary villagers and artisans, who visited shrines of saints and venerated local deities, are harder to recover, though the persistence of shared pilgrimage sites suggests that the lines between communities were often porous.

Religious institutions were supported through grants, and the sultans used patronage to build relationships with different communities. Shrines of Sufis such as Gesu Daraz at Gulbarga received income from villages, and the dargahs hosted annual commemorations (urs) that attracted large crowds. In Bijapur, the Sufi Shah Burhan al-Din Janam, who lived in the late sixteenth century, wrote devotional poetry in Dakhni and taught a circle of disciples, illustrating the link between Sufi practice and vernacular literature that Chapter 48 will explore.

The Shi'i presence is visible in the Qutb Shahi 'ashurkhanas, the buildings where the mourning rituals of Muharram were observed, and in the processions held in Golconda and Hyderabad, which drew participants from various communities. Evidence for Hindu religious life is more scattered. Telugu and Kannada inscriptions record gifts to temples in territories under the sultans, and some Hindu communities took part in the festivals of the court. At the same time, war damaged shrines and institutions on all sides, and religious toleration in these states was shaped by political interest rather than any consistent doctrine.

Section 46.31

Persian, Dakhni, and the Vernacular Literary World

The literary world of the Deccan sultanates was multilingual. Persian was the language of administration, diplomacy, and high culture, and the sultanates produced notable Persian histories and poetry, including the Burhan-i Ma'asir, Firishta's chronicle, and the correspondence of Mahmud Gawan. Arabic remained the language of religious scholarship. Persian poets came from Iran and settled at the courts, where they were patronized, and the Deccan became known as a destination for ambitious writers.

Alongside Persian grew a vernacular literary language, now called Dakhni (Deccani), a form of Hindavi or early Urdu spoken in the Deccan. It combined an Indo-Aryan grammatical base with Persian and Arabic vocabulary and absorbed Marathi, Kannada, and Telugu elements. Early works include Fakhr al-Din Nizami's Kadam Rao Padam Rao (c. 1420s-1430s), a narrative poem composed in the Bahmani court, and later poems by Ashraf Biyabani, Ibn Nishati, Mulla Wajhi, and others. Under the Adil Shahis and Qutb Shahis, Dakhni became a courtly literary language, used in mystical poetry, narrative romances, and lyric verse. Its history is important to the later development of Urdu, though historians debate how to describe the relationship.

Marathi, Kannada, and Telugu traditions also flourished within the sultanates' territories. Marathi was used in revenue records in Bijapur and Ahmadnagar, and Marathi saint-poets such as Eknath lived under Nizam Shahi rule at Paithan. Telugu poets enjoyed patronage at Golconda, where Telugu was used in official documents, and Kannada literature continued in the Adil Shahi south. These traditions were not simply neighbors of the courtly Persian world but part of the same political society, and their writers and officials moved among languages as their professions required.

The multilingualism of the sultanates raises a methodological point. Different languages served different functions and audiences, and a writer composing in Persian addressed a different readership from one writing in Marathi. Historians must therefore read across languages to recover the full range of political and cultural life, and need to be wary of equating a language with a religious community, since Hindus wrote in Persian and Muslims wrote in Marathi and Telugu.

Persian poets and scholars were drawn to the Deccan by patronage. At Bijapur, Zuhuri, a poet who came from Iran, became associated with the court of Ibrahim Adil Shah II and wrote prose and verse praised by contemporaries. At Golconda, Mulla Wajhi composed the Dakhni allegorical work Sabras in 1635. The growth of Dakhni literature under these patrons means that rulers supported both a cosmopolitan Persian tradition and a more local vernacular one.

The relationship among these languages was practical. A scribe in a revenue office might write Persian for the central treasury, Marathi for village accounts, and Telugu for a local grant. A poet might write Persian ghazals for the court and Dakhni verse for a wider audience. For historians, this means that the languages of surviving documents reflect the audiences and functions of the texts. It also means that language alone cannot identify a writer's religion or origin.

Historical Vocabulary
Dakhni (Deccani)

Dakhni is the name given to the literary form of Hindavi spoken and written in the Deccan from roughly the fifteenth century. It combined a North Indian grammatical base with Persian and Arabic vocabulary and incorporated Marathi, Kannada, and Telugu words and idioms. It became a court language of poetry at Bijapur and Golconda.

Historians debate whether to call Dakhni an early stage of Urdu, a sister language, or a distinct literary language, since later labels (Hindi, Urdu) belong to periods when religious and political identities attached themselves to scripts and vocabulary in new ways. This textbook uses "Dakhni" for the sultanate-period language and avoids assigning it to a single religious community.

Section 46.32

Painting, Music, Poetry, and Courtly Cultural Production

Courtly cultural production in the Deccan reached remarkable heights. Deccani painting, which emerged in the sixteenth century, differs from Mughal painting in its colors, its treatment of landscape, and its use of Persian, Turkish, and Indian conventions. Ahmadnagar produced early portraits and illustrated manuscripts, such as the Tarif-i Husain Shahi, a poem on Husain Nizam Shah. Bijapur's painters produced distinctive portraits, with idealized figures in rich colors, and illustrations for musical modes (ragamala) and poetry. Golconda's workshops were noted for their textiles, miniatures, and metal objects, and painters in the Qutb Shahi courts developed a style marked by rich ornament.

Ibrahim Adil Shah II, a musician and poet, wrote a collection of songs in Dakhni, the Kitab-i Nauras (compiled around 1600, though its songs developed over time and no precise completion date can be fixed), which invokes the Hindu goddess Sarasvati and the god Ganesha along with Islamic themes and sets lyrics to Indian musical modes. He founded the city of Nauraspur near Bijapur, though little of it survives. The work is often cited as evidence of the court's cultural openness, but historians also see it as an expression of royal self-presentation, in which the sultan portrayed himself as a patron of all learning, an image that suited his political position.

Muhammad Quli Qutb Shah composed thousands of verses in Dakhni, Persian, and Telugu, which were collected after his death in a kulliyat, the first complete divan in Urdu or Dakhni. His poems treat love, nature, seasons, festivals such as Basant and Nauroz, and religious themes, and they reveal a worldly, cosmopolitan court. They also show Hindu and Persian motifs, mixed in a manner characteristic of the Qutb Shahi milieu.

Music was widely patronized. Musicians from the north and from the Deccan performed at the courts, and treatises on music and dance were composed. The court's cultural production, it should be recalled, was a form of political communication: patrons displayed wealth, learning, and legitimacy through paintings, poems, buildings, and festivals, and artists and poets sought patronage in a competitive environment.

Painting at the Deccani courts developed along regional lines. Bijapur's workshop produced portraits of Ibrahim Adil Shah II and his nobles, ragamala illustrations, and paintings linked to the Kitab-i Nauras, and its style shows lavish color and a lyrical treatment of figures and landscape. Ahmadnagar's illustrated Tarif-i Husain Shahi, from about 1565, is one of the earliest known Deccani manuscripts and shows an early meeting of Persian, Ottoman, and Indian forms. Golconda painters developed their own approach, and objects made at the courts, including textiles and metalwork, reveal similar patterns. Dating Deccani paintings is difficult, as many are undated and are attributed by style, and scholars continue to revise attributions.

Music was an important court activity. Ibrahim Adil Shah II is reputed to have been a skilled performer, and the Kitab-i Nauras was composed to be sung in particular ragas. Poetry competitions, festivals, and musical performances were occasions for rulers to display generosity and taste. The cultural life of the courts should be read alongside the political situation: the same sultans who patronized artists were fighting wars and managing rivals, and their cultural output was one of the ways they claimed legitimacy.

Section 46.33

Everyday Society, Artisans, Women, Labor, and Cultural Exchange

Ordinary life in the sultanates is harder to see, because court chronicles say little about it. Yet inscriptions, administrative documents, foreign travelers, and material remains permit a partial reconstruction. The great majority of people lived in villages, cultivating dry grains, millets, cotton, pulses, and, in irrigated areas, rice and sugarcane. Village life was organized around the patil, the kulkarni, and the village artisans and servants, who received a share of the harvest in return for services in an arrangement often described as the balutedari system in later Maharashtrian sources. Debts, droughts, and the demands of revenue officers shaped household economies.

Cities contained large artisan populations, including weavers, dyers, metalworkers, masons, and jewelers. Bidri ware, produced at Bidar and later at other centers, used a blackened zinc alloy inlaid with silver or brass in delicate patterns. Golconda diamond cutters, Ahmadnagar and Bijapur armorers, and the many weavers of cotton supplied both local markets and distant ones. Merchants and bankers financed trade, and market regulations and officials supervised weights and prices.

Dependency and servitude were part of this world. Enslaved people, including Africans brought through the Indian Ocean trade, served as soldiers, domestic servants, and laborers. Some, like Malik Ambar, rose to high positions, while most remained in humble roles. Peasants who fled oppressive conditions, bonded laborers, and the many camp followers of armies also lived at the edges of security.

Women's roles varied by status. Royal and noble women participated in politics, as Chand Bibi, who organized Ahmadnagar’s defense; Makhduma-i-Jahan, the Bahmani queen mother who participated in the regency for her son Nizam Shah from 1461 to 1463; and Bari (Badi) Sahiba, an influential Bijapur queen who helped secure Ali Adil Shah II’s succession, though his parentage and her exact relationship to him were disputed, show. Women also owned property, patronized buildings, and administered estates. In the countryside, women worked in the fields and households, and in the cities, in spinning, trade, and service, though evidence is thin. Cultural exchange occurred in daily life: foods, dress, festivals, and languages blended, and shared saints' shrines and local festivals drew participants across religious lines, even as differences of status and community persisted.

Evidence of women's lives is mostly limited to elite women. Besides Chand Bibi, who has already been discussed, the Bahmani queen mother Makhduma-i-Jahan acted as a regent for her young son Nizam Shah in 1461-1463, working with leading ministers, including Mahmud Gawan, during a crisis. In Bijapur, the queen mother known in sources as Badi Sahiba was an influential figure in the 1650s and 1660s, though the details of her relationship to the sultan are debated. Such women controlled revenue, patronized buildings, and took part in diplomacy, which shows that royal households were also political institutions.

The Konkan coast offers a reminder of the scale of African participation in the Deccan. Habshi (Siddi) communities held fortified positions on the coast and served rulers on land and sea, and the island fortress of Janjira, which they controlled, remained a significant naval base in later centuries. This presence, like that of Malik Ambar, illustrates how migration across the Indian Ocean, including forced migration through the slave trade, reshaped Deccan society.

Everyday Life and Social History

Village, Workshop, and Household in the Deccan

A cultivator on the black soil planted millet and cotton according to the rains, paid a share of the crop through the village headman and accountant, and relied on village artisans and servants for services. In a Bidar or Golconda workshop, a craftsperson might spend months inlaying silver into a zinc alloy or printing cloth for export, working under contracts with a merchant. In an elite household, women managed estates and servants, while enslaved attendants, some of them African, performed domestic and military service. Festivals and shrine visits drew participants from many communities. What the evidence cannot easily show is the thoughts and feelings of those whose lives were recorded only in revenue lists and passing remarks by travelers.

Section 46.34

Bijapur and Golconda in the Seventeenth Century

Bijapur and Golconda survived the extinction of Ahmadnagar in 1636 and remained significant powers for another half-century. Both accepted Mughal suzerainty under the treaties of that year, agreeing to pay tribute and acknowledge the emperor, but they remained internally independent. This arrangement gave them a measure of security while exposing them to later demands.

Bijapur under Muhammad Adil Shah (r. 1627–1656) was at its territorial height, with conquests in the Carnatic south of the Tungabhadra, where its commanders captured forts such as Vellore and Jinji and exacted tribute from regional chiefs; the states of Ikkeri, Mysore, and Madurai were not simply annexed wholesale. Bijapur also held important districts of the Konkan coast. The sultanate used Maratha and Kannada commanders to hold these lands. Muhammad's reign also saw the construction of the Gol Gumbaz and other buildings. Yet the Carnatic expansion strained the kingdom's resources. Shahaji Bhonsle served as an important commander in Bijapur's southern campaigns, while his son Shivaji began challenging Bijapuri control of forts and territory in the western Deccan from the late 1640s onward. The killing of the Bijapur general Afzal Khan by Shivaji in 1659 and the failure of repeated Bijapur campaigns showed the limits of royal control.

Golconda under Abdullah Qutb Shah (r. 1626-1672) expanded in the Carnatic, aided by its merchant-general Mir Jumla, who built a personal domain and a large army in the region before switching allegiance to the Mughals in 1655-1656. The diamond revenues and the trade of Masulipatnam sustained the kingdom's wealth and its ability to buy off or fight rivals. Abul Hasan Tana Shah (r. 1672-1687) governed through Madanna and Akkanna and maintained an uneasy relationship with Shivaji, who visited Hyderabad in 1677 and agreed on a joint campaign in the Carnatic.

The kingdoms survived because they had resources, because regional elites remained loyal, and because the Mughal court's attention was divided. But their weakness grew: Bijapur in the late seventeenth century was riven by factions, and its sultan Sikandar Adil Shah, who came to the throne as a child in 1672, was dominated by ministers. Golconda's ministers and nobles quarreled. The rise of Maratha power on the western flank complicated all calculations.

The treaties of 1636 shaped the political environment for the next half-century. Shah Jahan's settlement with Bijapur defined its boundaries, allocated parts of the former Nizam Shahi territory, and required tribute, and Golconda's agreement similarly recognized the Mughal emperor in its coinage and Friday sermon. These arrangements limited the sultans' independence but also protected them against attack for some years. In 1656 Aurangzeb, then Mughal viceroy, attacked Hyderabad and besieged Golconda; the settlement involved an indemnity and a dynastic marriage, and Shah Jahan ordered withdrawal. In a distinct 1657 campaign against Bijapur, Aurangzeb captured Bidar and Kalyani before further imperial orders interrupted his advance amid the developing Mughal succession crisis.

The reign of Ali Adil Shah II (1656-1672) is often described as a period of stability followed by decline, as Shivaji's power grew and as the sultan's ministers struggled to keep the army and treasury working. The assassination of Madanna and Akkanna in Hyderabad in 1686, as the Mughal armies approached, illustrates the factionalism that had emerged in Golconda. In both kingdoms, strong ministers with connections to local elites, and with different religious and regional backgrounds, were attacked by rival nobles, while the sultans were unable to unify their courts.

Section 46.35

The Mughal Conquests of Bijapur and Golconda, 1686-1687

The end of the independent sultanates came under Aurangzeb. As viceroy of the Deccan in the 1650s, he had attacked both kingdoms, besieging Golconda in 1656 and capturing Bidar and Kalyani in 1657, before withdrawing under Shah Jahan's orders as the succession crisis developed (Chapter 53). Returning to the Deccan as emperor in 1681, he turned first against the Marathas, and then, in the mid-1680s, against the sultanates, which he accused of aiding them and of failing in their obligations as tributaries.

The Mughal siege of Bijapur began in 1685 and ended in September 1686, when Sikandar Adil Shah surrendered after the city was weakened by famine and the loss of supplies. The sultan was taken into Mughal custody and the Adil Shahi dynasty ended. Aurangzeb then besieged Golconda in 1687. The fort resisted for some eight months, helped by its strong walls, until it fell in September 1687, reportedly through the betrayal of a gate by an officer. Abul Hasan Tana Shah was imprisoned at Daulatabad, and the Qutb Shahi dynasty ended.

The Mughals annexed the territories as provinces, with the Deccan subas organized under imperial governors, but they struggled to establish stable control. The countryside was disrupted by war and famine, nobles and local chiefs were not easily absorbed, and Maratha forces continued to operate. The end of the sultanates did not bring the Deccan into peace, and Chapter 54 examines the long Mughal campaigns, the policies of Aurangzeb, and the consequences of imperial overextension. For this chapter, the essential point is that independent rule in Bijapur and Golconda ended in 1686 and 1687, two centuries after the Bahmani Sultanate had split into its successor states.

The Mughal campaigns were slow and costly. At Bijapur, Aurangzeb's armies, led at first by Prince Azam and later by the emperor himself, invested the fortress, cut off supplies, and used the monsoon and the countryside's exhaustion to their advantage. Reports of famine and epidemic in the besieged region appear in both Mughal and Deccani sources, though the numbers of those affected are not known. The garrison's surrender in September 1686 was followed by the movement of the sultan to the Mughal camp and the incorporation of Bijapur's nobles into the imperial service, often with new ranks.

At Golconda, the fortress's size, the wealth of its treasury, and the strength of its artillery allowed a long defense. The siege ended in September 1687, and a tradition, preserved in Mughal sources, attributes the fall to the opening of a gate by a commander, Abdullah Khan Pani. Because Mughal and Deccani writers had reasons to blame or praise individuals, historians treat the story as attributed rather than established. Whatever the exact circumstances, the Mughal victory rested on the exhaustion of resources, the loss of internal unity, and the emperor's determination.

Section 46.36

The Deccan Sultanates' Political and Cultural Legacy

The sultanates' legacy survived their dynasties in several forms. Institutionally, the practice of governing through hereditary local officials, assigning revenue to military commanders, and relying on a mixed elite of Deccanis, Afaqis, Habshis, Marathas, and Brahmins continued under the Mughal governors and the Marathas. Many of the families who had served the sultans, such as the Bhonsles, Ghorpades, and Jadhavs, became prominent in Maratha service, and Maratha revenue practice, with its reliance on deshmukhs and kulkarnis, built on Deccan traditions. Malik Ambar's methods of land assessment and guerrilla warfare are often cited as antecedents of Maratha systems, though historians caution that continuity was uneven.

Linguistically, the sultanates fostered Dakhni, which became an important ancestor of later literary Urdu in the Deccan and contributed to the culture of the Hyderabad region, and they fostered Marathi, Telugu, and Kannada as languages of administration and literature. Architecturally, their monuments remain central to the built heritage of Karnataka, Maharashtra, and Telangana, and the city of Hyderabad, founded by the Qutb Shahis, would again become the capital of a major state in the eighteenth century.

The commercial legacy was equally important. The ports, trade routes, textile traditions, and diamond industry of the Deccan connected it to the Indian Ocean economy and shaped the arrival of European companies. The region's merchants, financiers, and craftsmen continued their work under successor regimes.

The relationship to later history can be traced forward. After 1687 the Deccan became a Mughal frontier, then the territory of the Marathas and, from 1724, the state of Hyderabad under Asaf Jah, a former Mughal governor (Chapter 60). The sultanates also left a legacy of historical memory, contested in modern times, in which they are variously remembered as centers of cultural synthesis, as rivals of Vijayanagara, or as victims of Mughal expansion. Historians therefore have to examine claims about the Deccan sultanates' past through the evidence rather than present-day assumptions.

Historical Debate

How Should Historians Interpret Deccani Political and Cultural Integration?

The question: Did the Deccan sultanates create a distinctive, integrated "Deccani" political and cultural society, or were they Persianate courts layered over a regional society that remained largely separate?

Interpretation A: integration. Multilingual courts, the use of regional languages in administration, the role of Brahmins, Marathas, and Telugu chiefs, shared festivals, Dakhni poetry, and architecture that combined imported and local techniques indicate a genuinely interwoven society.
Interpretation B: layered or limited integration. Persian remained the language of high power, immigrants held top posts, religious and social hierarchies persisted, wars damaged local communities, and the courts' cosmopolitanism may not have reached most villagers. The integration was real but partial and politically managed.

Evidence: Both views draw on chronicles, inscriptions, monuments, literary works, and administrative records, but they weigh different kinds of evidence differently: courtly sources favor integration, while sources about land, labor, and violence point to persistent divisions.

Why it matters: The question affects how the Deccan is remembered, as a model of coexistence or as a site of conflict, and it shows the risks of using "synthesis" as a label without examining who participated and on what terms.

Section 46.37

Review and Historical Synthesis

The history of the Bahmani and Deccan sultanates can be summarized as the creation, growth, and division of a regional state system. The Bahmani Sultanate arose in 1347 from the weakening of Delhi's grip on a distant province, and over the next century its rulers built a government that combined military assignments, provincial administration, local officials, and Persianate court culture. They expanded from Gulbarga to Bidar, fought for the Raichur doab and Telangana, and reached both coasts under Mahmud Gawan.

The sultanate's strengths were also its weaknesses. Its government depended on powerful governors and noble factions, and the division between Deccani and Afaqi elites, along with the pressures of war, made centralization difficult. Mahmud Gawan's reforms attempted to reduce the autonomy of governors, but his execution in 1481 exposed the limits of the project. The governors then established the five successor states, whose different founders, geographies, religious orientations, and social bases produced distinct polities: Bijapur, Ahmadnagar, Golconda, Berar, and Bidar.

The successor states competed, cooperated, and intermarried, forming a Deccan system in which shifting alliances, not permanent religious blocs, were the rule. They defeated Vijayanagara at Talikota in 1565, yet they could not then unite against the Mughals, who annexed Ahmadnagar's heartland by 1636 and absorbed Bijapur and Golconda in 1686 and 1687. Their survival until then reflected their wealth in agriculture, textiles, and diamonds, their connections to the Indian Ocean, and their ability to draw on regional elites.

Culturally, the sultanates produced distinctive achievements in architecture, painting, music, and literature in Persian, Dakhni, and the regional languages. The term "Deccani synthesis" captures real exchange, but it should be used carefully, since interaction coexisted with hierarchy, rivalry, and violence. The relationship between regional autonomy and long-distance networks, which brought horses, scholars, merchants, and ideas from Iran, Arabia, and Africa, runs through the entire story.

Continuity and Change Over Time

The Deccan from the Tughlaqs to the Mughals

What changed?

Political authority moved from Delhi to Gulbarga and Bidar, then to five states, then to the Mughals. Persian-speaking immigrants and Habshi soldiers entered the elite, Dakhni and regional languages entered court life, and gunpowder transformed warfare.

What remained continuous?

Village institutions, hereditary revenue officers, agricultural patterns, trade routes, and the competition for the Raichur doab and Telangana continued across dynasties.

Who experienced change most directly?

Nobles, soldiers, and urban elites felt political change most sharply, as did populations on campaign routes and in besieged cities.

Who experienced greater continuity?

Cultivators and village officials often experienced continuity in daily routines, though the burden of revenue and war could rise or fall.

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both change and continuity explains why the sultanates' institutions could be inherited by later regimes.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced the history of the Bahmani Sultanate and the five Deccan sultanates from the rebellion of 1347 to the Mughal conquests of 1686 and 1687. It has argued that these states were not merely opponents of Vijayanagara but major regional powers with their own institutions, elites, economies, and cultures.

The Bahmani Sultanate arose from the Tughlaq decline in the Deccan, when military officers rebelled and chose Hasan Bahman Shah as their ruler. Isami's contemporary poem and later chronicles such as Firishta's preserve the story, though later legends about the founder's origins must be distinguished from earlier evidence.

From Gulbarga, the early sultans built a state on revenue assignments, provincial governors, and local hereditary officials. They fought Vijayanagara and the Telangana chiefs for the Raichur doab and the eastern plains, and Ahmad Shah Wali moved the capital to Bidar around 1425 as the kingdom's center of gravity shifted.

The Bahmani elite was divided into Deccani and Afaqi factions, labels that described changing political networks rather than fixed ethnic communities. Brahmins, Marathas, Habshis, and others also took part in government and war, making the sultanate a layered political society.

Mahmud Gawan, an immigrant merchant-scholar who became chief minister, extended the kingdom to both coasts and reorganized the provinces into eight tarafs while creating more royal lands and royal control over forts. His reforms threatened powerful nobles, and his execution in 1481 exposed the limits of centralization and accelerated fragmentation.

Between about 1490 and 1528, governors and ministers created five successor states: Bijapur under the Adil Shahis, Ahmadnagar under the Nizam Shahis, Golconda under the Qutb Shahis, Berar under the Imad Shahis, and Bidar under the Barid Shahis. They differed in geography, religious orientation, social base, and longevity, and two of them were absorbed by neighbors.

The sultanates competed and cooperated through war, marriage, and diplomacy. Their coalition defeated Rama Raya of Vijayanagara at Talikota in 1565, but did not produce permanent unity. Armies combined cavalry, infantry, artillery, and fortresses, and the states' revenues rested on agriculture, cotton textiles, diamonds, and Indian Ocean trade.

The courts and cities produced distinctive architecture, painting, music, and literature in Persian, Dakhni, and the regional languages, and Sufi and scholarly networks shaped religious life. Evidence of cultural exchange is strong, but it coexisted with hierarchy, slavery, rivalry, and war, which is why the idea of a Deccani synthesis must be examined carefully.

Mughal expansion, beginning with Akbar's pressure on Ahmadnagar and continuing under Shah Jahan and Aurangzeb, ended the independence of the sultanates. Their institutions, languages, monuments, and elites survived into the Mughal, Maratha, and Hyderabad periods, which is why the Deccan sultanates form an essential link in the longer history of South Asia.

Chapter Reflection

The history of the Deccan sultanates shows that regional states, not only great empires, shaped medieval South Asia. The Bahmanis and their successors created institutions, courts, cities, and cultural traditions that outlasted their dynasties, and they governed a plateau that was always more than a frontier between north and south.

The chapter also shows how fragile centralization could be in states built on negotiated relationships. Mahmud Gawan's reforms and their failure reveal the difficulty of balancing royal authority against powerful commanders, and the staggered emergence of five successor states shows that political change was gradual and uneven.

Finally, the chapter invites careful thought about cultural interaction. The Deccan produced extraordinary poetry, painting, music, and architecture through exchanges among Persian, Arabic, Dakhni, Marathi, Telugu, and Kannada traditions. Yet the same society included slavery, war, and social inequality. Understanding both is necessary to avoid either romanticizing or dismissing the past.

In the six-volume sequence, this chapter links the Delhi Sultanate and Vijayanagara to the Mughal and Maratha worlds. Chapter 47 now turns to the regional sultanates of Bengal, Gujarat, Malwa, Jaunpur, and Kashmir, which show that the Deccan was one part of a wider multipolar South Asia on the eve of the Mughal age.
