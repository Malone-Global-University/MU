Mughal Culture, Art, and Intellectual Life

Indian History: South Asia from the Indus Civilization to the Present

Chapter Opening

In the 1580s, in a workshop attached to the Mughal court, a team of translators sat down with Sanskrit scholars to render the Mahabharata into Persian. The Persian-literate courtier who left the most candid account of the project, Abd al-Qadir Badauni, was a pious scholar who felt uneasy about it. The emperor who ordered it, Akbar, was a ruler who could not, by most accounts, read at all. The finished work was copied, illustrated by painters whose names and families came from Gujarat, Rajasthan, Kashmir, and Iran, and preserved in a royal library. A single enterprise thus brought together a Persian prose tradition, a Sanskrit epic, Brahman interpreters, Muslim translators, imperial patronage, and the hands of painters, calligraphers, and binders.

Chapter 55 examined how Mughal society produced wealth, organized labor, and sustained its institutions. This chapter turns to how that society produced knowledge, literature, buildings, images, music, and religious thought, and to who made them, who paid for them, who could see or hear them, and how they travelled beyond the imperial court. It is the cultural and intellectual synthesis of Part X, and it completes the Mughal section of the textbook before Chapter 57 turns to the eighteenth-century transformation.

The chapter's principal period is 1526 to 1707, but the cultural world it describes had deeper roots. Persianate court culture had already been transplanted to South Asia under the Delhi Sultanate (Chapter 44) and the regional sultanates (Chapters 46 and 47). Sanskrit scholarship, Jain manuscript traditions, devotional poetry in regional languages, temple-building, and the Sufi shrines discussed in Chapter 48 all flourished independently of any Mughal emperor and continued to do so. The Mughal court did not create these traditions. It acted as an unusually wealthy, well-organized, and ambitious patron and meeting-place, and its choices shaped what was written down, illustrated, translated, and built.

Two interpretive cautions run through the chapter. First, it is tempting to describe Mughal culture as a "fusion" of Persian and Indian elements, as though two finished cultures were poured into a single vessel. The evidence suggests something more varied. Some Mughal projects involved sustained collaboration across languages and religious communities. Others were selective borrowings, competitive responses, or expressions of imperial self-confidence. Still others, such as theological disputes and the persecution of individual scholars, show that contact could sharpen boundaries as well as soften them. Second, Mughal culture should not be reduced to the emperor. Emperors commissioned and judged, but artisans, scribes, regional authors, performers, scholars, religious teachers, merchants, and women of the imperial household and beyond it did much of the work and frequently set its direction.

The evidence is itself a cultural product that must be read critically. Court chronicles such as Abu'l Fazl's Akbarnama are masterpieces of prose and also instruments of imperial legitimacy. Illustrated manuscripts, albums, and buildings survive in remarkable numbers but disproportionately reflect elite taste, and the survival of an object depends partly on later collectors, museums, and wars. Memoirs by Babur, Jahangir, and Gulbadan Begum speak in individual voices but represent only a few people. Provincial authors, performers whose art was never written down, and the many artisans who rarely signed their work are far harder to recover. Where this chapter makes claims about attribution or authorship, it distinguishes securely documented cases from tradition and later legend.

The chapter's twenty numbered sections are arranged in five divisions. Division I examines the institutions that produced and legitimized knowledge: the Persian language, court history-writing, the book arts, and education and science. Division II studies painting, portraiture, architecture, and gardens as material expressions of power, belief, and aesthetic experiment. Division III turns to literature, translation, regional vernaculars, and music. Division IV addresses religious thought, devotion, and intellectual encounter across Sufi, Hindu, Sikh, Jain, and other communities, and questions the usefulness of terms such as syncretism. Division V widens the lens to women, regional courts, labor, social boundaries, and the survival of Mughal cultural forms after 1707.

Chapter 53 introduced Jahangir's and Shah Jahan's patronage and the Taj Mahal, and Chapter 52 introduced Akbar's religious policy and translation bureau. The present chapter does not repeat those political biographies. It asks instead what these works reveal about institutions, techniques, audiences, and the people who made them. Chapter 57 will pick up the political history of the post-1707 empire; here the final sections only show how cultural forms outlasted, and sometimes migrated away from, imperial power.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain the role of Persian as an administrative, literary, and scholarly language in Mughal South Asia and distinguish an imperial language from the languages spoken by the wider population.
  2. Analyze Mughal court histories and memoirs as sources, identifying authorship, patronage, method, legitimizing purposes, and limitations.
  3. Describe the organization of book production, including the work of scribes, calligraphers, illuminators, painters, binders, and librarians.
  4. Distinguish long-standing South Asian scholarly traditions from developments directly associated with Mughal patronage in education, astronomy, mathematics, medicine, and geography.
  5. Trace the development of Mughal painting from Humayun to Aurangzeb and explain the roles of Persian masters, Indian artists, collaborative workshops, and European images.
  6. Interpret Mughal architecture and gardens as expressions of imperial ideology, religious meaning, engineering knowledge, and regional adaptation.
  7. Evaluate Persian literary culture, Sanskrit–Persian translation, and vernacular literatures as distinct but interacting fields of cultural production.
  8. Assess the place of music and performance in court and regional life, separating well-attested practices from later legend.
  9. Compare Sufi, Hindu devotional, Sikh, Jain, and other religious communities and explain why interaction did not erase doctrinal and social boundaries.
  10. Evaluate the contributions of women as authors, patrons, and cultural participants, distinguishing documented authorship from later attribution.
  11. Assess the strengths and weaknesses of the terms "Persianate," "syncretism," and "composite culture" as descriptions of Mughal South Asia.
  12. Connect Mughal cultural production to the economic foundations traced in Chapter 55 and explain how cultural forms survived and migrated after 1707.

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Who produced Mughal cultural works, who paid for them, and who could actually see, hear, or read them?
  2. In what sense was Persian an "imperial" language, and what does that tell us about the limits of imperial culture?
  3. How should historians read court chronicles and imperial memoirs that were written to glorify the rulers they describe?
  4. How did the organization of workshops, with their collaborative labor and shared techniques, shape the character of Mughal painting and book production?
  5. What do Mughal buildings and gardens reveal about imperial ideals, religious meaning, engineering, and regional adaptation?

  8. What roles did women play in Mughal literary, religious, and artistic life, and how reliable is the evidence?
  9. Is "composite culture" an adequate description of Mughal South Asia?
  10. How did cultural forms associated with the Mughal court survive and change when imperial political power weakened after 1707?

Historical Context

The Mughal cultural world rested on several overlapping inheritances. The first was the Persianate tradition of the Iranian plateau and Central Asia, which Babur (Chapter 50) carried from the Timurid courts of Samarqand and Herat. Timurid Herat, with its poets, painters, calligraphers, and garden-makers, supplied models that Babur admired and described in his memoir. The Timurid inheritance came to India already hybrid, shaped by Turkic, Mongol, Persian, and Islamic elements, and it was reinforced by the Safavid court of Iran, where Humayun spent part of his exile in the 1540s and whence he recruited artists.

The second inheritance was the Indo-Islamic culture developed under the Delhi Sultanate and the regional sultanates (Chapters 44, 46, and 47). Persian had been used for chronicles, poetry, and administration in Delhi since the thirteenth century; Sufi orders had built a network of shrines; and regional courts in Bengal, Gujarat, Malwa, Jaunpur, Kashmir, and the Deccan had sponsored distinctive architecture, painting, and literature. The Mughals inherited these traditions both as a legacy and as competition, and they drew on the skills of craftsmen long established in these regions.

The third inheritance was the Sanskritic and vernacular culture of South Asia itself: a long scholarly tradition in grammar, logic, law, astronomy, mathematics, and medicine; the epics and Puranas; Jain and Buddhist manuscript traditions; temple architecture and temple-centered learning; and a rapidly growing literature in regional languages, much of it devotional (Chapter 48). By the sixteenth century these traditions were not static survivals. Rajput courts, the Vijayanagara successor states (Chapter 45), and devotional communities were commissioning poetry, buildings, and paintings of their own, often in dialogue with their Muslim neighbors.

The material basis of cultural production lay in the agrarian and commercial economy described in Chapter 55. Revenue extracted through the mansabdari and jagirdari systems (Chapter 51) funded the nobility who, along with the emperor, were the main patrons. Urban craft production supplied paper, pigments, gold leaf, stone, textiles, and instruments; merchants and bankers financed buildings and the movement of goods and artists. Cultural history in this period is therefore inseparable from economic and political history: a manuscript required paper-makers and gold-beaters, a garden required engineers and water, and a monument required quarries, masons, and thousands of laborers.

Historians depend on an unusually rich but uneven body of evidence. Court chronicles, memoirs, biographical dictionaries (tazkiras), and travel accounts by Europeans and Iranians provide narrative. Illustrated manuscripts, album pages, and surviving buildings provide visual and material evidence. Inscriptions and farmans record patronage and grants. But the same sources overrepresent the court, the elite, and men; evidence for the lives of most artisans, performers, and women is indirect, and the dating and attribution of many works remain debated. Readers should expect the chapter to flag these limits repeatedly.

Section 56.1

Persian as an Imperial Language and Cultural Medium

Persian was the language of Mughal government, diplomacy, high literature, and learned conversation, and this was the single most consequential linguistic fact of the empire's cultural life. Revenue records, imperial orders (farmans), legal documents, diplomatic correspondence, and nearly all official chronicles were written in Persian. The language had been used in North Indian courts since the Delhi Sultanate (Chapter 44), and under the Mughals it became more tightly bound to the machinery of rule. Historians usually trace a decisive step to the 1580s, when Akbar's revenue officials, including Raja Todar Mal, are reported to have required that accounts be kept in Persian. Badauni, writing critically, noted that Hindu clerks accordingly turned to learning Persian in large numbers.

The history of the language at court was not simple. Babur wrote his memoir in Chagatai Turkic, the literary language of the Timurid world, and Turkic remained a family and military language among some nobles for generations. Akbar had the Baburnama translated into Persian in 1589, a task completed by Abd al-Rahim Khan-i Khanan, which shows that even the dynasty's own founding text had to be rendered into the administrative language to be broadly read at court. Arabic remained the language of religious law, theology, and Qur'anic scholarship. Sanskrit continued as the language of Brahmanical learning, ritual, and a vigorous court poetry, including at Mughal courts: Jagannatha Panditaraja and Kavindracarya Sarasvati, for example, are associated with Shah Jahan's court. The everyday speech of most inhabitants was one of many regional languages and dialects, among them Punjabi, the Hindavi-Braj-Awadhi continuum of the north, Gujarati, Marathi, Bengali, Kashmiri, and Telugu.

It is therefore important to distinguish an imperial language from the languages spoken by the wider population. Persian was learned, not inherited, by the great majority of those who used it. Persian literacy was concentrated among a few social groups: Iranian and Central Asian migrants, Muslim scholars and administrators, and scribal communities such as Khatris, Kayasthas, and Brahmans who, from Akbar's time especially, entered the revenue and secretarial services in large numbers. Their mastery of Persian made them eligible for office and gave them access to a prestigious literary culture; the Brahman poet and secretary Chandar Bhan, who served Shah Jahan and wrote Persian prose and verse of high quality, is a notable example. The language thus functioned as a kind of professional and cultural passport that crossed religious lines even while it separated those with access from those without.

Multilingualism was an asset at court. Abd al-Rahim Khan-i Khanan, a senior noble and the translator of the Baburnama, is reported by contemporaries to have commanded Turkic, Persian, Arabic, and Hindi, to have patronized poets in each, and to have composed verse in Hindi himself. Many officials, poets, and scholars moved among two or three languages in different settings. Persian lexicography reflected this: dictionaries such as the Farhang-i Jahangiri, compiled in Jahangir's reign, and the Farhang-i Rashidi, completed in Sindh in 1653, systematized the language for Indian users and sometimes quarrelled with Iranian usage. Persian as written in South Asia developed its own idioms, and Iranian visitors sometimes criticized them, a tension that returns in §56.9 under the label "Indian style."

Persian's prestige also depended on movement. Iranian poets, physicians, theologians, painters, and merchants migrated to the Mughal court in large numbers, particularly in the late sixteenth and early seventeenth centuries, attracted by generous patronage and by Safavid political and religious pressures at home. They carried books, styles, and ideas and became part of the Mughal nobility. Persian was thus both an Indian language of administration and part of a wider Persianate world that stretched from Anatolia to Bengal. The adjective "Persianate," discussed in the vocabulary feature below, expresses this: it describes a cultural sphere shaped by Persian language and models without implying that all its participants were Persian in ethnicity, religion, or origin.

Historical Vocabulary
Persianate

Persianate describes cultures, institutions, and practices that draw on Persian language, literature, administrative models, and aesthetic ideals, without necessarily being Iranian in origin. Historians use it because a Mughal secretary of Khatri origin, an Afghan noble, a Turkic-speaking soldier, and an Iranian poet could all participate in a shared Persianate world of letters and court etiquette while differing in religion, ancestry, and mother tongue.

The term matters here because it cautions against two errors: treating Mughal Persian culture as a foreign import that was alien to South Asia, and treating it as simply "Muslim" culture. Persian literacy was held by Hindus as well as Muslims, and Persian literature was written about South Asian subjects. The term is also contested, as Debate 1 below shows.

Section 56.2

Court Histories, Memoirs, and Imperial Self-Representation

The Mughal court produced one of the richest bodies of historical writing in the early modern world. It also produced it deliberately: histories were commissioned, checked, and used to shape how the dynasty would be remembered. Understanding these texts requires asking who wrote them, for whom, from what materials, and with what purpose.

The Baburnama stands apart as a ruler's own memoir, composed in Chagatai Turkic in the 1520s and early 1530s from journals and recollection. It is notable for its frankness: Babur describes landscapes, plants, animals, towns, and people with an observer's curiosity, records his own mistakes and illnesses, and offers sharp comparisons between Central Asia and Hindustan. His account is also selective and polemical; he writes as a dynast arguing for his claim and often judges the people and customs of India by Timurid standards. The manuscript tradition is complicated, with gaps and later copies, and the illustrated versions produced under Akbar represent Akbar's era's reading of the text as much as Babur's own.

The great chronicle of Akbar's reign is Abu'l Fazl's Akbarnama, begun around 1589 and elaborated through the 1590s, together with its appendix, the A'in-i Akbari, which describes the imperial household, the army, the administration, the provinces, the revenue system, and the religious and learned communities of the empire. Abu'l Fazl worked from archives, official reports, and interviews, and he was closely involved with Akbar's own policies. The work is a literary and administrative monument and a sustained argument that Akbar was a divinely guided universal ruler. Abu'l Fazl's prose is elaborate and rhetorical, and it frames events in a theory of sovereignty drawing on Persian, Islamic, and Central Asian ideas of royal light (see the Source Window below). The A'in-i Akbari is simultaneously the best single source on the Mughal state and an idealized portrait of it. Historians test its numbers against other evidence, and they read it alongside Badauni's Muntakhab al-Tawarikh, a critical history written in secret that disapproved of Akbar's religious experiments.

Other memoir and chronicle traditions are important. Jahangir's own memoir, the Jahangirnama (also called Tuzuk-i Jahangiri), offers observations on administration, art, and natural history, and it reveals his own preoccupations; later in his reign his health and the demands of rule meant that parts were completed with the help of others, notably Mu'tamad Khan. Shah Jahan's reign produced official chronicles, among them the Padshahnama of Abd al-Hamid Lahori and later historians, which were sometimes accompanied by lavishly illustrated manuscripts. Under Aurangzeb, official chronicling was curtailed after the first decade of the reign, according to later writers, but historical writing did not stop; private historians such as Khafi Khan wrote detailed accounts after Aurangzeb's death. Alongside these were biographical dictionaries of poets and nobles, histories of provinces and cities, and family-history memoirs such as those of Jauhar Aftabchi and Bayazid Bayat, who served Humayun.

Court-centered testimony has built-in limits. Chroniclers described the emperor as the center of the world, minimized failures, and recorded rivals as rebels. They wrote about the nobility, campaigns, and ceremonies much more than about villagers, artisans, or merchants. They often reported imperial decisions as the result of the emperor's wisdom rather than negotiation among factions. For these reasons historians read official histories against inscriptions, revenue records, European and Persian travel accounts, regional chronicles in other languages, and critical or dissident histories like Badauni's. The aim is neither to dismiss the court chronicles, whose detail is often reliable on matters of fact, nor to accept them uncritically. The practice is to ask, for each claim, whether an independent source corroborates it and who benefited from its being told this way.

These histories were also an instrument of legitimacy in a more specific sense. A dynasty founded by a conqueror from Central Asia, ruling a majority population of different religions and traditions, needed a compelling account of why it should rule. Abu'l Fazl linked Akbar's ancestry to Timur and, through a Mongol genealogy, to a legendary light-impregnated ancestress, and he presented the emperor as a ruler above sectarian conflict. The Persian histories that emphasized the dynasty's Timurid lineage coexisted with Sanskrit and vernacular texts that addressed Mughal rulers in other idioms, a point the later sections develop.

Primary Source Window

Abu'l Fazl and Imperial Historiography: The Akbarnama

Source type: Official court chronicle in Persian · Author: Abu'l Fazl, commissioned by Akbar · Date: composed c. 1589–1602 · Original language: Persian

Context: Akbar commissioned the history in the late 1580s as part of a broader program of recording his reign and his regime's principles. Abu'l Fazl was one of his closest advisers and the brother of the poet Faizi. He was killed in August 1602 in an ambush by Bir Singh Bundela that Prince Salim, the future Jahangir, had instigated, as Jahangir later acknowledged in his memoir. The Akbarnama survives in numerous manuscripts, several of which are illustrated.

Editorial paraphrase (not a verbatim quotation): In passages tracing the emperor's lineage and his accession, Abu'l Fazl presents Akbar as the bearer of a divine radiance that passes through the dynasty, and he describes the emperor's qualities, including justice, a universal sympathy toward all communities, and a role as guide for the whole realm, as signs of that endowment.

Analysis: The passage shows how a court historian turned dynastic history into political theology. It gives historians direct evidence of the ideology Akbar's regime wanted to project. It does not, by itself, establish how contemporaries in villages, provincial courts, or rival noble houses understood the emperor, and it avoids discussing disputes that did not support the ideal. Abu'l Fazl's own position as a friend and servant of Akbar, and his disputes with orthodox scholars, also shaped what he emphasized.

Questions for students:

  1. What claims does the source make about the nature of Akbar's authority?
  2. Who wrote the text, and what was the author's relationship to the ruler?
  3. What can historians learn about imperial ideology from the source?
  4. What can they not establish without other evidence, such as Badauni's history, revenue records, or regional chronicles?
Section 56.3

Manuscripts, Calligraphy, Libraries, and the Book Arts

Printing did not become a significant Mughal technology. European Jesuits brought printed books to Akbar's court from 1580, and Portuguese presses operated in Goa, but the Mughal court and its readers continued to rely on manuscripts. This made the book a labor-intensive object and a visible symbol of wealth. A fine manuscript might involve a papermaker, a ruler of lines and margins, a calligrapher, an illuminator who decorated headings and borders, painters who prepared illustrations, a binder, and the librarian who supervised the whole process. The finished work might be read aloud at court, shown to guests, kept in a royal library, or presented as a gift.

The imperial library, or kitabkhana, housed the dynasty's collection and the associated workshops (see the vocabulary box below). Humayun carried his books with him in exile, and Akbar built a large library that, in later estimates, held tens of thousands of volumes; such figures come from sources with an interest in praising the emperor and should be taken as indicative of scale rather than exact. Books were catalogued, graded by value, and read to the emperor. Akbar is repeatedly described as illiterate yet intensely devoted to hearing texts read aloud, a combination that shaped the court's practices of oral recitation, translation, and discussion. Libraries were also kept by princes, nobles, and royal women, and by Sufi lodges, madrasas, Jain monasteries, and temples, so that the imperial collection was one node in a wider manuscript culture.

Calligraphy ranked among the most admired arts. The dominant script for Persian literary texts was nastaliq, a flowing, rhythmic hand developed in Iran, and Mughal calligraphers worked to Iranian standards while developing local schools. Akbar's court gave honorific titles to distinguished scribes; Muhammad Husayn Kashmiri, for example, was honored with a title meaning "golden pen." Jahangir similarly honored Abd al-Rahim "Anbarin Qalam" (the "amber pen"). Other scripts served other purposes: naskh for Qur'ans and religious texts, thuluth for monumental inscriptions on buildings, and Devanagari and other regional scripts for Sanskrit, Hindi, and vernacular texts, including in Mughal-sponsored Sanskrit manuscripts. Calligraphers also trained in the shared aesthetics of the book arts, and their work often appeared on the same page as painting and illumination.

Paper, ink, pigments, and gold were material foundations of the book arts and linked this culture to the economy discussed in Chapter 55. Fine paper was produced in several centers, with Sialkot and Kashmir among the best known, and was burnished to a smooth finish for calligraphy and painting. Pigments came from mineral sources, plant dyes, and imported materials, including lapis lazuli from Afghanistan for blues, and gold was beaten into leaf or ground into ink. Bookbinding, with its leather covers, lacquered painted covers, and gilded decoration, was a specialist craft. Most of the people who prepared these materials remain anonymous, though their labor and skill were indispensable.

The organization of book production blended individual authorship with collaboration. Abu'l Fazl reports that the workshops were supervised by officials who reviewed work regularly and recommended pay and rewards for the best artists and scribes. Some scribes and painters held salaried positions or even ranks in the nobility; others worked on commission. Translators and compilers, such as those who produced the Razmnama (§56.10), were part of the same system. Illustrated manuscripts frequently show that one painter composed a scene, another filled in faces, and another added landscape or color, and inscriptions on the page sometimes record these divisions of labor. The result is a type of cultural work that is neither purely individual nor purely anonymous, and which is best understood as the product of skilled teams.

Historical Vocabulary
Kitabkhana and Nigarkhana

A kitabkhana (literally "house of books") was the imperial library and the workshop of scribes, illuminators, binders, and related specialists attached to it. A nigarkhana (a "house of pictures") was the term sometimes used for the painting workshop. In practice the divisions were fluid, and contemporary sources do not always distinguish them.

The terms matter because they remind students that Mughal painting and book production were institutions with supervisors, salaries, standards, and collective output, not simply the private enthusiasm of an emperor. They also warn against assuming a single fixed "royal atelier": the number and location of workshops changed with the court's movements, with the interests of successive rulers, and with princes and nobles who maintained their own studios.

Section 56.4

Education, Scholarship, Science, and the Circulation of Knowledge

Education in Mughal South Asia was diverse, decentralized, and mostly private. Muslim children might begin with a maktab, learning to read the Qur'an and perhaps Persian texts, and advance to a madrasa for Arabic grammar, law, theology, logic, and related subjects. Hindu children might learn in village or household settings and, among Brahmans, in scholarly households and pathshalas teaching Sanskrit grammar, law, logic, philosophy, astronomy, and ritual. Merchant and scribal families trained children in accounting and commercial scripts. Jain monasteries and Sufi lodges taught their own communities. Women of elite families were often educated at home, as §56.17 describes. There was no centralized state school system; the state's role was mainly to grant land and stipends to scholars, teachers, and religious institutions, and to appoint officials with learned qualifications.

Within this scene, Mughal patronage affected curricula and prestige. Abu'l Fazl reports that Akbar had views on educational reform and recommended a curriculum that emphasized subjects of practical and rational knowledge, including ethics, arithmetic, agriculture, geometry, astronomy, medicine, logic, and history, alongside religious studies; for Hindu students, the A'in-i Akbari lists Sanskrit grammar, logic, Vedanta, and the Yoga tradition. How widely such prescriptions were followed is uncertain. The Farangi Mahall scholarly household in Lucknow acquired an institutional base under late Mughal patronage, but the curriculum later called the "Dars-i Nizami" should not be projected back into the seventeenth century. It is associated with Mulla Nizam al-Din (1677–1748), whose mature intellectual activity belongs chiefly to the eighteenth century; the selection of texts evolved further and became more standardized only in the nineteenth century. Its subsequent emphasis on rational sciences alongside transmitted disciplines illustrates the longer history of South Asian scholarly learning rather than a finished Aurangzeb-era curriculum.

Astronomy offers one of the clearest examples of continuity and Mughal-era change. Astronomers in South Asia had long used Sanskrit traditions of calculation and Islamic traditions descending from Greek and Arabic astronomy. Akbar's court introduced the Ilahi calendar (from 1584–85), a solar-based reckoning used in revenue accounting alongside the Hijri lunar calendar. Instrument-making flourished: families of astrolabe-makers in Lahore, such as that of Allahdad, produced fine brass instruments in the late sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, and craftsmen in Lahore and Kashmir developed a remarkable technique for casting seamless celestial globes by the lost-wax process, a technical feat associated especially with Ali Kashmiri ibn Luqman around 1589–90 and continued by later craftsmen. The observatories sponsored by Sawai Jai Singh II in the eighteenth century drew on older Sanskritic and Islamicate astronomical traditions; their construction belongs principally to the post-1707 period rather than the core chronology of this chapter.

In mathematics and medicine the court sponsored translations and compendia rather than radical new theories. Faizi's Persian version of Bhaskara II's Lilavati (1587) and a Persian translation of his algebraic Bijaganita made for Shah Jahan in 1634–35 by Ata'ullah Rushdi (also written Rashidi) made Sanskrit mathematical scholarship accessible to Persian readers. Medical culture combined Unani medicine, descended from Greek and Arabic sources, with Ayurvedic practice; physicians such as those who served Akbar's and Jahangir's courts were often Iranian or Indian, and Persian medical compendia and pharmacological works circulated widely, including the Tibb-i Aurangshahi (also known as Tibb-i Aurangzebi), a compendium of Indian medicine attributed to Darvish Muhammad and named for Aurangzeb; its precise date of composition is not securely established here. Geography and administrative geography received a major contribution in the A'in-i Akbari, whose province-by-province description of resources, castes, and revenue is a unique early modern gazetteer. Philosophy and logic circulated through Sufi, scholastic, and Sanskritic channels and are discussed with religious thought in Division IV.

Knowledge also circulated through networks that had nothing to do with the court: Brahman scholars moving among Benares, Nasik, and southern centers; Sufi teachers traveling with disciples; Jain monks and merchants carrying manuscripts across western India; craftsmen passing techniques within families; and merchants, pilgrims, and soldiers exchanging news. European contact added another channel. Jesuits at Akbar's court from 1580, and later Dutch, English, and other visitors, introduced European books, prints, instruments, and ideas, but the Mughal court's interest was selective. Historians distinguish carefully between what the evidence supports as continuity with earlier South Asian traditions, such as the persistence of Sanskrit mathematical and astronomical texts, and what is clearly associated with Mughal patronage, such as the Persian translation program and the Ilahi calendar.

Section 56.5

The Mughal Painting Atelier: Artists, Techniques, and Patronage

Mughal painting began as a transplant and became a distinct tradition within a few decades. Its origins lay in Humayun's exile, during which he encountered Safavid painting in Iran and persuaded two artists trained in that tradition, Mir Sayyid Ali and Khwaja Abd al-Samad, to join him, around 1549 in Kabul. After Humayun's death in 1556 these masters worked for Akbar, supervising a growing workshop. The Persian training provided techniques of line, composition, and book illustration; what happened next depended on the many artists who joined and the demands made of them.

The Hamzanama, a huge illustrated manuscript of the romance of Amir Hamza, was the first great collective project. Begun in the 1560s and taking roughly fifteen years according to later accounts, it comprised about 1,400 large paintings on cloth, bound as multiple volumes, with narrative text on the reverse. Only a portion survives, dispersed among museums. Its scale required a large team under Mir Sayyid Ali and later Abd al-Samad, and it trained a generation of painters, many from Hindu craft and scribal backgrounds in Gujarat, Rajasthan, and the Gangetic plain. Their compositions, vivid color, and treatment of space and architecture departed from Persian precedent, and the Hamzanama is often cited as the moment when a Mughal style emerged.

From the 1570s, Akbar's workshop illustrated a wide range of texts: histories such as the Akbarnama and Baburnama, Persian classics, Sanskrit epics in Persian translation, and collections of animal fables. Abu'l Fazl reports that over a hundred painters were then active, that they were supervised and rewarded according to their skill, and that Akbar himself reviewed their work weekly. He also reports a remark attributed to Akbar that painting offered a painter a distinctive path toward recognizing the Creator, since the artist, seeking to capture a living form, must realize that he cannot grant life. This reported statement is evidence of how the court justified figural art in a religious setting where some scholars were critical of images. Artists named in the sources include Basawan, noted for composition and psychological expression; Daswanth, whose promising career ended in madness and suicide in about 1584 according to Abu'l Fazl; and Miskin, Kesu Das, Lal, Manohar, and Farrukh Beg, the latter an artist who came from the Kabul region and joined the court in the 1580s.

Techniques were collaborative and standardized. An illustrated page began with a sketch of the composition, often by a master; other artists added portraits, landscape, or architecture; pigments, often built up in layers, were applied and burnished; and borders, text blocks, and illuminated headings were added by other hands. Manuscript colophons and marginal notes sometimes name the artists responsible for different parts of a painting, providing rare evidence for how work was divided. Pigments included mineral colors such as lapis lazuli and vermilion, organic dyes, and gold and silver, and brushes were famously fine. The workshop trained apprentices, often from families of artists, so that painting dynasties formed: Manohar, for example, was the son of Basawan.

Patronage extended beyond the emperor. Princes maintained their own ateliers, as Prince Salim did at Allahabad before he became Jahangir, and nobles and royal women commissioned manuscripts and albums. Under Jahangir the workshop moved toward portraiture and naturalistic observation, discussed in §56.6. Under Shah Jahan painting became more formal and refined, with idealized court scenes and precise portraits, and the Padshahnama manuscripts represent this courtly polish. Under Aurangzeb, imperial patronage of painting narrowed in later decades, according to most art historians, and some artists left for regional courts; yet painting did not disappear from the Mughal world, and portraits, historical manuscripts, and albums continued to be produced at the court and by nobles.

Section 56.6

Portraiture, Natural History, Albums, and Artistic Exchange

Portraiture became a defining Mughal genre. Persian painting had produced idealized courtly figures; Mughal artists increasingly studied individual faces. Akbar reportedly had portraits made of his nobles, collected in albums, and by Jahangir's reign portraiture had developed into a form of close observation, with profile faces carefully modeled, individualized features, and attention to costume and gesture. Portraits of rulers, nobles, Sufis, and ascetics survive in great numbers, and they served as records of status, as gifts, and as objects of connoisseurship. Painters such as Bichitr, who depicted Jahangir enthroned and preferring the company of a Sufi teacher to that of earthly kings, used portraiture for symbolic statement, drawing on allegory as much as on observation.

Natural-history painting is perhaps the most celebrated artistic experiment of Jahangir's era. Jahangir was curious about animals, plants, and unusual phenomena, and he commissioned artists to depict them with precision. Ustad Mansur, who received a title meaning "wonder of the age," painted birds, animals, and flowering plants of strikingly careful detail, including a famous depiction of a turkey cock, an exotic bird brought to the court via Goa in 1612, and a zebra presented in March 1621, whose surviving painting carries an imperial attribution to Mansur. Abu'l Hasan, who received the title "wonder of the time," was also celebrated for portraits and allegories. Jahangir's own memoir discusses these artists and the creatures they painted (see the Source Window), but the observational skill belonged to the painters. Natural-history studies suggest a culture of empirical curiosity, though one directed by the ruler's interests and with no equivalent institution for scientific publication.

Albums (muraqqa) assembled single paintings, portraits, and calligraphy, mounted on pages with decorated borders. They were collected objects in the way an art collection is today, and some were compiled by individuals as personal treasures. Jahangir and Shah Jahan commissioned imperial albums, and Prince Dara Shikoh's album, assembled in the 1630s–40s and given to his wife Nadira Banu, survives with portraits, floral studies, and calligraphy. Albums also allowed artists to experiment with border design and with the juxtaposition of Persian, Indian, and European elements, and they preserved earlier works for later collectors.

European art entered the Mughal world through Jesuit missions and later through diplomatic and commercial gifts. Jesuits brought religious paintings and prints, including religious images that Akbar's artists studied and copied, adopting devices such as the halo for imperial portraits, aerial perspective, and modeling of light and shadow. Sir Thomas Roe, the English ambassador to Jahangir's court from 1615, recorded that Mughal painters copied European pictures so skillfully that he found it difficult to tell copies from originals. The reception was selective: artists adopted techniques and motifs that suited existing aims, such as depth in landscape, but did not abandon established conventions for flat, decorative composition. European prints, notably engravings, also served as sources for compositions that were then transformed into Mughal contexts.

This section develops the artistic and intellectual significance of works introduced in Chapter 53, where Jahangir's political career was discussed. The emphasis here is on how artists worked and on what their paintings show: a culture in which individual observation, hereditary skill, imperial interest, and foreign stimuli combined. Historians caution against reading Mughal naturalism as a straightforward path toward "modern" science or art. The images served the ruler's wonder, prestige, and sense of mastery, and they were frequently combined with symbolic and allegorical elements.

Section 56.7

Mughal Architecture: Imperial Ideals, Religious Buildings, and Monumental Design

Mughal architecture drew on Timurid and Persian models, on the Indo-Islamic buildings of the Delhi and regional sultanates, and on the Hindu, Jain, and vernacular building traditions of North India. The characteristic features that emerged by the late sixteenth century include red sandstone and white marble; pointed arches and domes; chhatris (pavilion-like domed kiosks); jharokhas (projecting balconies from which the emperor appeared to his subjects); courtyards; and elaborate ornament in carving, inlay, tile, and painted plaster. Builders worked with mason families, engineers, and laborers who belonged to the established regional building trades, and many details came from local craft traditions, particularly in Gujarat and Rajasthan.

Humayun's Tomb in Delhi, begun in the 1560s under the patronage of his widow Haji Begum and designed with the Persian architect Mirak Mirza Ghiyas, introduced the garden tomb on a grand scale to South Asia. Set in a walled charbagh garden, built of red sandstone with white marble accents and topped by a double dome, it provided a model for later imperial tombs. Akbar's own architecture was more varied. The rebuilding of Agra Fort from 1565 in red sandstone established a fortified palace-city, and Fatehpur Sikri, begun in 1571 near the dwelling of the Chishti Sufi Shaykh Salim, blended Gujarati, Rajput, and Persian elements in an imperial city. The Jama Masjid, the tomb of Shaykh Salim Chishti, the Buland Darwaza, and the palaces and audience halls of Fatehpur Sikri illustrate his architectural program, though the city was abandoned within about fifteen years, probably owing to a combination of political, logistical, and water-supply factors that historians still debate.

Akbar's tomb at Sikandra, near Agra, completed under Jahangir, took an unusual stepped-pavilion form. Jahangir's reign saw the Itimad-ud-Daula's tomb in Agra, built in the 1620s by Nur Jahan for her father, notable as an early large-scale use of white marble with inlaid stone decoration (pietra dura). Under Shah Jahan, imperial architecture turned to refined marble buildings: the Taj Mahal, begun in 1632 after the death of Mumtaz Mahal and substantially complete in the early 1650s, combined a garden, a tomb, a mosque, and a gateway into a harmonious whole, with the principal architect often identified in tradition as Ustad Ahmad Lahori, though the evidence for individual attribution is incomplete and the project involved many designers and craftsmen. The Red Fort in Delhi, begun in 1639 as part of the new city of Shahjahanabad, and the Jama Masjid of Delhi (1650–56) show the same grand scale. Aurangzeb's patronage favored mosques and fortifications; the Badshahi Mosque in Lahore (1673) is the most famous, while the Bibi ka Maqbara in Aurangabad, built for his wife by his son Azam Shah, adapted the Taj's form on a more modest scale.

Architecture did not merely house the emperor; it communicated ideology. Audience halls, balconies, and gates staged the emperor's appearance, and tombs and gardens expressed ideas of paradise and dynastic continuity. Scholars such as Ebba Koch have argued that imperial buildings were designed for ceremonial and symbolic effect, including the sequence of public and private audiences. Religious buildings had their own logic: the Jama Masjids at Fatehpur Sikri and Delhi proclaimed the ruler's role as patron of the Muslim community, while Mughal nobles, especially Rajput allies and Hindu officials, also built temples, particularly in regions such as Mathura-Vrindavan and Varanasi, within the political framework described in Chapters 51 and 52. The destruction and desecration of some temples, discussed in Chapter 54, forms part of the same landscape and shows that architecture could be an instrument of conflict as well as accommodation.

Engineering knowledge underlay these achievements. Builders handled large domes and foundations near rivers, quarried and transported sandstone and marble (the white marble of the Taj came largely from Makrana in Rajasthan), and designed water systems for gardens, fountains, and baths. Labor was organized through a mixture of salaried specialists, contracted craftsmen, and a large workforce of laborers whose conditions the sources rarely describe, a fact that reminds readers that the grandeur of monuments rested on the economic and social order explored in Chapter 55.

Cause and Effect

The Formation of a Distinctive Mughal Visual Culture

Long-term causes

Timurid and Safavid artistic traditions; the Indo-Islamic and regional building and painting traditions of South Asia; a tradition of royal patronage in both Persianate and Indian courts; and a supply of skilled craftsmen in Gujarat, Rajasthan, Kashmir, and the Gangetic cities.

Short-term causes

Humayun's exile and recruitment of Persian masters; Akbar's large resources after his consolidation of power; his personal interest in books and building; and the political need to project imperial authority to varied audiences.

Immediate effects

Large collaborative projects, such as the Hamzanama and Fatehpur Sikri, trained a generation of artists and builders and produced a recognizable Mughal style combining Persian and Indian elements.

Long-term consequences

Mughal visual forms spread to Rajput, Deccan, and later regional courts, and shaped South Asian art and architecture well into the colonial and postcolonial periods.

Historical complexity: The Mughal style was not the result of a single decision by Akbar or any ruler. It depended on the choices of artists and builders, the accumulated skills of regional trades, the movement of people across borders, and the economic surplus that supported patronage. Different arts also developed at different speeds and for different purposes.

Section 56.8

Gardens, Water, Landscape, and the Cultural Meaning of Space

Gardens were among the most cherished Mughal creations, and Babur's descriptions show that the tradition was personal before it became imperial. He laid out gardens at Agra and elsewhere, including the Bagh-i Wafa in Kabul, and in his memoir he complains of Hindustan's lack of running water and of gardens, while describing his own efforts to supply them. His aim was to create a controlled paradise in a landscape he regarded as unfamiliar, and the garden remained an emblem of dynasty and civilization for his successors.

The classic Mughal garden was the charbagh, a quadrilateral divided by water channels or walkways into four parts, often with a central pavilion or tomb where the channels met (see the vocabulary box). Water was central: channels, tanks, fountains, and cascades turned a practical resource into a visual and sensory experience, with raised walkways, pavilions, and terraces used for rest, entertainment, and ceremony. Engineering knowledge was crucial. Gardens in plains settings relied on canals, wells, and sometimes on remarkable hydraulic works; the Shalimar Garden in Lahore (1641–42), for instance, was fed by a canal engineered during Shah Jahan's reign and designed in terraces with hundreds of fountains.

Gardens carried several layers of meaning. They evoked Qur'anic descriptions of paradise with flowing water and shade, and the tomb-in-garden form could convey a hope of salvation as well as dynastic permanence. They were also settings for state ceremonies, imperial hospitality, hunting, and leisure, and they provided a spatial display of imperial authority over nature, as geometry and water were ordered by human design. Scholars have noted that Mughal gardens also adapted to regional environments and traditions rather than reproducing a single Persian prototype, using local plants, materials, and sometimes earlier Indian garden and water-architecture traditions such as stepwells and pleasure pavilions.

Kashmir offers the clearest example of landscape transformation. After the Mughal annexation of Kashmir in 1586, emperors and nobles, especially Jahangir and Shah Jahan, built terraced gardens along Dal Lake and mountain streams, including the Shalimar Bagh, the Nishat Bagh (associated with Asaf Khan, Nur Jahan's brother), and the Chashma Shahi. Further springs, such as Verinag and Achabal, were developed with pavilions and water channels. The Kashmir gardens used the mountain slope to create natural cascades and views toward the lake, and the court spent summers there to escape the plains' heat. Further gardens were built in Lahore, Agra (where the riverfront gardens formed a chain along the Yamuna and set the stage for the Taj), Delhi, and Pinjore, among other places.

Gardens also tell historians about the environment and labor. The water that flowed through them was drawn from rivers, springs, and wells, and its diversion affected local users; the upkeep of gardens required gardeners and water-managers whose names rarely survive. The economic analysis of Chapter 55 provides the context: the surplus that financed gardens came from agriculture and trade. Mughal gardens in the end were both beloved retreats and expressions of the emperor's ability to command resources, in a pattern shared by many early modern courts.

Historical Vocabulary
Charbagh

The charbagh (Persian, "four gardens") is a garden plan divided into four sections by axial channels or paths, typically with water and a central pavilion or tomb. It has Persian and Central Asian roots and was adopted and elaborated by the Mughals as a model for palaces, pleasure gardens, and tombs.

The term matters because the charbagh joined aesthetics, religious imagery, and hydraulic engineering. Using it as a label for all Mughal gardens would be misleading, since many gardens departed from a strict four-part pattern to fit slopes, rivers, and local conditions.

Section 56.9

Persian Literature, Poetry, and Courtly Literary Culture

Persian poetry was the most prestigious literary form of the Mughal world, and its production was shaped by patronage, competition, and an exacting set of conventions. The Persian ghazal, qasida, and masnavi had long been cultivated in India, and from Akbar's reign Mughal courts and noble households became major centers for Persian poets, many of them immigrants from Iran and Central Asia. Akbar appointed Faizi as poet laureate in 1588, and Faizi's verse and prose, along with his role in translation and in the imperial library, made him a central figure; he also composed a Persian commentary on the Qur'an composed without letters bearing diacritical dots, a technical tour de force that reflects the period's delight in verbal artistry.

Poets such as Urfi Shirazi and Naziri Nishapuri, both from Iran, achieved fame in India in the late sixteenth and early seventeenth centuries, the former at Akbar's court and the latter in the circle of Abd al-Rahim Khan-i Khanan. Under Jahangir and Shah Jahan the poets Talib Amuli, Kalim Kashani, and Sa'ib Tabrizi were prominent, with Kalim holding the poet-laureate position under Shah Jahan, and the Kashmiri poet Ghani continuing the tradition in the mid-seventeenth century. Many such poets came for patronage that the Safavid court, by comparison, offered less generously, though they also valued the freedom of Indian courts and the opportunity for travel.

Persian literature in India was not only produced for emperors. Nobles, provincial governors, Sufi lodges, and merchants all patronized poets, and poetic gatherings (mushairas) and circles of literary friends were widespread. Biographical anthologies (tazkiras) recorded poets' lives and verses, and these are invaluable for tracing social networks. Hindu poets and scribes also composed Persian verse; Chandar Bhan Brahman, mentioned earlier, left a diwan and prose writings, and later poets such as Mirza Abd al-Qadir Bedil of Azimabad (Patna), writing in the second half of the seventeenth century, produced dense and philosophical Persian poetry that later became influential far beyond India, especially in Central Asia and Afghanistan. These cases show that Persian literary culture in South Asia was not confined to Muslims, to Iranians, or to the court.

Historians and literary critics have long debated how to characterize the Persian poetry of this age. The term "Indian style" (sabk-i Hindi) is commonly applied to the intricate, metaphor-laden verse associated with poets from Urfi to Bedil, and some nineteenth- and twentieth-century critics, particularly in Iran, regarded it as decadent or as a deviation from Iranian norms. Modern scholarship has questioned both the label and the judgment, noting that the poetry's complexity reflected a cosmopolitan Persianate culture that stretched across Iran, Central Asia, and South Asia, and that the "Indian" label obscures how many of its leading poets were Iranian. The debate is a good example of how modern national categories can distort earlier literary history.

The relationship between poetry and political power was close but not simple. Poets sought rewards and posts, composed panegyrics, and recorded imperial events in verse, and the court's patronage shaped their themes. Yet poets also wrote mystical verse, satire, love poetry, and reflective works that could comment obliquely on politics, and they frequently moved between patrons. The prestige of Persian poetry depended on its being read, recited, and admired by those who could judge it, a group that included many Hindu and Muslim scribes and officials; the circulation of poems in manuscript and orally shows that this literary world extended well beyond the emperor.

Section 56.10

Sanskrit–Persian Translation and Cross-Cultural Intellectual Exchange

Mughal-sponsored translation was among the most ambitious intellectual programs of the early modern world. Chapter 52 introduced Akbar's translation bureau as part of his religious and political policy; this section examines the projects themselves and what they reveal about knowledge and power. Under Akbar, scholars translated into Persian the Mahabharata (as the Razmnama, "Book of Wars"), the Ramayana, the Harivamsa, the Atharvaveda (by tradition), the Rajatarangini (the Sanskrit chronicle of Kashmir), the mathematical Lilavati, the tale collection Singhasan Battisi, and other works, while a corresponding set of translations from Arabic, Turkic, and Greek-influenced works into Persian also took place.

Translation was not a mechanical exercise. It required Sanskrit scholars (pandits) who could explain the text, Persian stylists who could render it, and officials who supervised and reviewed the result. For the Razmnama, Badauni, Naqib Khan, and Shaykh Sultan Thanesari are named among the Persian translators, working with Brahman scholars, and the preface is generally attributed to Abu'l Fazl. Badauni's account shows his reluctance, for he regarded the task with religious unease, and he describes the emperor's order and the involvement of Brahman informants. The resulting text was a Persian rendering that selected, summarized, and interpreted; it was not a literal transcript of the Sanskrit. Choices about names, religious terms, and cultural concepts inevitably shaped the meaning, as when Persian equivalents were found for Sanskrit concepts of dharma or divine beings.

The motives for translation were several. Akbar's court sought knowledge, and the epics were seen as repositories of history and ethics. The ruler also wished to show himself as a patron of all learned traditions and to cultivate Hindu elites, including Rajput nobles and Brahman officials, as partners of the regime. Translation also enabled the court to control and interpret knowledge by having texts made accessible to its own Persian-reading officials. Scholars such as Audrey Truschke have argued that Sanskrit intellectuals and the Mughal court engaged with each other selectively and on their own terms, rather than as part of a seamless fusion, and that many Sanskrit authors paid little attention to the Mughals; the translation projects are best understood as a significant, but bounded, episode of exchange.

Translation continued after Akbar. Jahangir's reign saw further Persian renderings of Sanskrit works, including the Yoga Vasistha, and Shah Jahan's reign saw additional translations in mathematics and literature. The most famous later enterprise was Dara Shikoh's. A Qadiri Sufi who studied with Mian Mir and Mulla Shah Badakhshi, Dara gathered Sanskrit scholars in the 1650s and, with their help, produced a Persian translation of fifty Upanishads, called the Sirr-i Akbar ("the Great Secret"), completed in 1657. In its preface he argued that the Upanishads were the concealed book mentioned in the Qur'an, a claim that showed his effort to place Indian scripture within an Islamic framework. This claim was controversial then and later, and we examine it in §56.16. Dara's translation of the Upanishads reached Europe in the eighteenth century, via a Latin version by Anquetil-Duperron, and later influenced European readers' view of Indian philosophy.

Translation also highlights the collaborative and unequal character of Mughal knowledge production. Brahman scholars who explained Sanskrit texts were often paid by the court but are less well documented than the Persian stylists who made the final text. The resulting manuscripts reveal the court's ability to gather expertise. They also show the limits of the enterprise: few Mughal officials read Sanskrit, translations circulated in Persian-literate circles, and the Mahabharata and Ramayana continued to live more widely in Sanskrit and vernacular performance than in Persian renderings.

Section 56.11

Braj, Awadhi, Hindavi, and Regional Literary Traditions

While Persian was the language of the court, vernacular languages produced a flourishing literature that engaged much larger audiences. In northern India, the central region of the Hindi-speaking belt, two literary dialects dominated: Awadhi, spoken in the region around Ayodhya and Lucknow, and Braj Bhasha, associated with the Mathura-Agra region. "Hindavi," a term used at the time for the vernacular speech of northern India, covered a wide continuum of speech forms and was used by Sufis, poets, and travelers alike. Historians caution against imposing modern language identities, such as the later separation of Hindi and Urdu, on this earlier fluid linguistic landscape.

Tulsidas (c. 1532–1623, by traditional dating) composed the Ramcharitmanas in Awadhi, beginning, according to his own testimony, in 1574 at Ayodhya. It retold the story of Rama as a devotional narrative and became one of the most widely read and performed texts in the Hindi-speaking world. He lived largely in Varanasi, and the work circulated orally and in manuscript among Brahmans, merchants, and ordinary devotees; its association with community performance, notably the Ramlila tradition, is celebrated in later practice, though the precise early history of those performances is difficult to establish. Surdas, a poet of Braj associated with the Vaishnava community of Vallabha and the Pushtimarg tradition, wrote devotional verse about Krishna that was sung in temple settings; the Sur Sagar attributed to him is a large compilation assembled over time, and the attribution of many individual poems to the historical Surdas is uncertain.

Courtly patronage also supported vernacular literature. Keshavdas, a poet at the court of Orchha, wrote the Rasikapriya (1591) on the theory of love and poetry in Braj, helping establish a courtly poetic tradition known as riti, which flourished in Rajput and other courts. Bihari, whose Satsai was composed at the Amber court of Mirza Raja Jai Singh in the mid-seventeenth century, wrote compact, polished couplets. Abd al-Rahim Khan-i Khanan himself composed Hindi verse, and Mughal nobles patronized Hindi poets; Akbar's court is said to have had Hindi poets, though the extent of imperial patronage of Hindi is less well documented than that of Persian. Sufi poets wrote romances in Awadhi, most famously Malik Muhammad Jayasi's Padmavat (1540), which used a love story to express Sufi ideas, showing vernacular literature as a shared literary space rather than a purely Hindu one.

Beyond the Hindi belt, regional literatures flourished. In Maharashtra, poet-saints such as Eknath and Tukaram composed Marathi devotional poetry (abhangs) addressed to Vithoba of Pandharpur. In Bengal, Chaitanya's Vaishnava movement, Bengali versions of the epics such as Krittivasa's Ramayana, the Chandimangal poems of Mukundaram Chakrabarti, and the work of Muslim poets such as Alaol at the Arakan court, who wrote a Bengali Padmavati in 1648, show a rich vernacular culture. In Assam, Sankaradeva and Madhavadeva built a devotional tradition in Assamese. In Punjab, the Sikh Gurus wrote in Punjabi and related dialects, and Sufi poets such as Shah Husayn of Lahore composed verse in Punjabi; in Sindh and Kashmir, similar developments occurred. In the Deccan, Dakhni, an early form of the vernacular used by Muslim courts, produced a literature of its own. In the south, Telugu and Tamil literature continued under the Nayaka states and other courts (Chapter 45).

Performance and oral transmission were essential. Devotional poems were sung in temples, homes, and at festivals; epic stories were told by professional reciters and performed in drama; Sufi poetry was sung at shrines (§56.13). Written texts often followed oral performance rather than preceding it, and manuscripts of the same poet can vary widely. Patronage was equally diverse, ranging from Rajput and regional kings to temple communities, merchant guilds, and Sufi lodges. These facts argue against equating literary history with courts: a large share of the era's most influential writing took place outside imperial patronage, and its audiences included people who never read Persian.

Historical Debate

Was Mughal Culture Principally Imperial and Persianate, or Did It Depend on Regional and Multilingual Traditions?

The question: Should Mughal cultural history be understood chiefly as the story of an imperial, Persianate court culture, or as the product of many regional and multilingual traditions of which the court was one participant?

Interpretation A — Persianate and imperial emphasis: Scholars such as Muzaffar Alam and Richard Eaton have emphasized that Persian formed a durable, wide-ranging cultural and political language in the Indian subcontinent, shaping administration, ethics, literature, and etiquette across religious lines. Rajeev Kinra's work on Chandar Bhan Brahman, for example, shows a Hindu secretary fully at home in Persianate ideals of refinement. On this view, treating Persian culture as "foreign" misunderstands how deeply it was integrated into South Asian life, and the imperial court gave it unparalleled resources.
Interpretation B — Regional and multilingual emphasis: Other scholars, including those working on vernacular literature such as Allison Busch on Braj Bhasha, and those who build on Sheldon Pollock's account of Sanskrit and vernacularization, stress that Sanskrit, Braj, Awadhi, Marathi, Bengali, and other traditions had their own dynamics, patrons, and audiences. Audrey Truschke's study of Sanskrit intellectuals at the Mughal court documents substantial collaboration with Persian-speaking elites, complicating any clean division between courtly and regional traditions; meanwhile, much South Asian literary activity also proceeded under patrons and institutions beyond the imperial court. On this view, a court-centered account exaggerates imperial influence and neglects regional creativity.

Evidence: Both positions draw on the same kinds of evidence: chronicles and tazkiras, manuscripts in several languages, patronage records, and the careers of individuals who moved among languages. They differ in emphasis and scale, not necessarily in the facts.

Why it matters: The question affects how historians describe the relationship between the court and the wider society, how they assess who made Mughal culture, and whether they treat Persianate culture as a layer imposed on South Asia or as one of its constituent traditions. Most current scholarship combines the perspectives, recognizing that Persianate culture was integrated into South Asia while regional traditions had substantial independence.

Section 56.12

Music, Dance, Performance, and Courtly Entertainment

Music was central to Mughal court life, and its history shows both how a regional tradition entered imperial service and how difficult it is to separate documented practice from legend. The classical tradition of northern India, later called Hindustani music, had roots in earlier Sanskrit treatises and in the Delhi sultans' and regional courts' patronage. Dhrupad, a vocal genre composed in Braj and performed in a slow, meditative style with the pakhawaj drum, flourished at courts such as Gwalior, where Raja Man Singh Tomar (r. 1486–1516) patronized it, and it became the dominant courtly form in Akbar's time.

Tansen, from the Gwalior region, is the most famous musician of the period. He first served the Baghela ruler of Rewa and entered Akbar's court around 1562, where he remained until his death in the 1580s. Abu'l Fazl names him in the A'in-i Akbari among the leading musicians, and he is credited with compositions in dhrupad and with some of the ragas attributed to him. Beyond that, the stories told about him, such as feats of lighting lamps by singing or rainmaking, are later legends. Tansen's conversion to Islam, his pupils and descendants, and his connections to the Sufi Muhammad Ghaus of Gwalior are also the subject of later tradition rather than firm documentation. Other celebrated musicians included Baz Bahadur, the ruler of Malwa, who became a musician at Akbar's court after his defeat, and Swami Haridas, the Vaishnava saint-musician of Vrindavan, who is closely associated by tradition with dhrupad and with Tansen's training, though the ties are not fully attested.

Court musicians were a mix of hereditary specialists and individual talents, drawn from many communities. The A'in-i Akbari lists dozens of leading musicians, including Hindus, Muslims, Iranians, and Central Asians, in an organization that placed them in the emperor's service, with the best receiving rewards. Instruments included the rudra vina, the rabab, the pakhawaj, and various flutes and drums; the tanpura provided the drone. Performances took place in audience halls, private chambers, gardens, and on journeys, and they accompanied imperial ceremonies, including the daily naubat (kettledrum ensemble) that announced the emperor's presence. In temples and Sufi shrines, music took other forms: Vaishnava kirtans in temples at Vrindavan, and the sama (devotional listening) at Chishti shrines, which involved sung poetry and sometimes controversy among religious scholars about its permissibility.

Women performers contributed in many ways. Female singers, dancers, and instrumentalists performed for the court and in households; some, in the later period, became celebrated, and women musicians and dancers formed part of the imperial entourage. Their artistry, however, is documented mainly through male observers' accounts, and the social status of performing women ranged widely, from respected court artists to lower-status entertainers. Historians avoid generalizing from a few names and caution against the retrospective glamorization or stigmatization of female performers in later literature.

Music also generated scholarship. Persian-language treatises on Indian music appeared from Akbar's reign through the seventeenth century, including the Ragdarpan, which translated the Sanskrit-based Man Kutuhal for Persian readers, and the Tuhfat al-Hind of Mirza Khan, compiled in the 1670s for a Mughal prince. These works show that music was theorized and discussed across linguistic boundaries. The report that Aurangzeb ended music at his court around 1668, found in later narratives such as those of Khafi Khan, is contested: it seems that he reduced court musical patronage, but musical production in the empire, including at courts of nobles and princes and in the treatises composed during his reign, did not cease. The episode illustrates how complex the evidence is for reconstructing patronage policy from literary anecdote.

Everyday Life and Social History

Cultural Work and Cultural Audiences Beyond the Court

For most inhabitants of the Mughal world, culture meant things heard, seen, and shared in community rather than read in Persian. A village audience might gather to listen to a reciter singing episodes from the Ramayana or Krishna's youth in a regional language; townspeople might attend the processions and festivals of their temples, mosques, or shrines; pilgrims at a Sufi shrine such as Ajmer heard devotional poetry sung in sama; and urban neighborhoods supported storytellers, singers, and craftsmen who sold goods such as textiles and painted images in the bazaar. Houses of merchants and Jain bankers sometimes kept collections of manuscripts and hosted scholars and monks.

Cultural work was embedded in livelihoods. Paper-makers, gold-beaters, dyers, weavers, masons, stone-carvers, gardeners, musicians, and storytellers earned their living in cultural production, many through hereditary occupations organized by caste and guild. Their social standing varied: master calligraphers and painters could receive titles and rank, while quarry workers and laborers on monuments appear in sources mainly as anonymous labor. An autobiography in Braj by the Jain merchant Banarsidas of Agra (1641), discussed in §56.15, reveals the daily life, anxieties, and spiritual inquiries of a trader and shows how a non-elite person could write about himself in the vernacular. Such sources are rare, which is why historians treat them with particular care: they are windows into lives that official chronicles rarely mention.

Section 56.13

Sufism: Orders, Shrines, Scholarship, and Devotional Networks

Sufism, the mystical and devotional dimension of Islam, was a major presence in Mughal society, but it was not a single movement. It comprised several orders (silsilas), each with its own lineage of teachers, practices, and relationship to political authority, and individual Sufis varied in learning, politics, and outlook. Chapter 48 introduced the early development of South Asian Sufism; this section examines its institutions and intellectual debates in the Mughal centuries.

The Chishti order, with its roots in the Delhi Sultanate period and its central shrine at Ajmer, the tomb of Mu'in al-Din Chishti, enjoyed particular prominence in Akbar's time. Akbar made repeated pilgrimages to Ajmer, and he associated the birth of his son Salim with the blessing of Shaykh Salim Chishti of Sikri, whose tomb at Fatehpur Sikri became a major shrine. The Chishtis emphasized poverty, hospitality, and devotional music, and their lodges (khanqahs) offered food and spiritual guidance to all comers. The Qadiri order grew in influence in the seventeenth century; Mian Mir of Lahore (d. 1635), a revered teacher with followers across religious lines, was the guide of Dara Shikoh and of Jahanara Begum. The Naqshbandi order, introduced in Delhi by Khwaja Baqi Billah (d. 1603), stressed strict adherence to Islamic law and silent remembrance, and became a leading channel for a more legalist Sufism. Other orders, such as the Shattaris, who were active in Gwalior and Mandu, and the Suhrawardis in Sindh and Punjab, also played roles.

Ahmad Sirhindi (1564–1624), a Naqshbandi, is among the most discussed figures in the period. He wrote letters (Maktubat) to nobles and teachers, arguing for a distinction between the experience of unity with God (wahdat al-wujud, "unity of being," the doctrine associated with Ibn al-Arabi and widely accepted in Indian Sufi circles) and what he considered a more cautious understanding, wahdat al-shuhud ("unity of witnessing"). He insisted on the primacy of the shari'a, criticized practices he considered un-Islamic, and had himself called the "renewer of the second millennium" by his followers. Jahangir briefly imprisoned him in the Gwalior fort around 1619–20 before releasing him, apparently over concerns about his claims to spiritual rank. Later admirers cast him as a reformer who resisted Akbar's religious experiments; modern historians stress that his actual influence in his own lifetime was modest and that the later image of Sirhindi as a leader of a reactionary reversal owes much to later movements that claimed him as a forebear.

Sufi shrines (dargahs) functioned as religious, social, and economic centers. They received endowments of land and cash from rulers and nobles, hosted pilgrims and festivals (urs), maintained kitchens, and acted as meeting places for people of many communities. Devotional poetry, qawwali-style singing, and storytelling drew crowds; local deities and saints sometimes shared sacred landscapes with Sufi shrines. Sufis also served as mediators, advisers, and, at times, critics of rulers. Not all Sufis aligned with the court: some avoided royal contact altogether, and others, like the Mahdawis, a movement connected with Sayyid Muhammad Jaunpuri, faced suppression for their millenarian claims in earlier decades.

Sufi scholarship included biography, poetry, commentary, and law. Dara Shikoh's Safinat al-Awliya and Sakinat al-Awliya, composed in the 1640s, recorded the lives of earlier saints and of Mian Mir and his circle. Jahanara Begum's biography of Mu'in al-Din Chishti (§56.17) belongs to the same literature. The Shi'i scholar Qazi Nurullah Shushtari, who served in the judiciary under Akbar and Jahangir and wrote on Shi'i history, was flogged to death in 1610, an event that shows that the court's religious politics could turn harsh when scholars crossed sectarian lines. The historical landscape was thus not only one of Sufi mysticism and accommodation but one of competing orders, schools of law, and sectarian pressures.

Section 56.14

Hindu Religious Developments: Bhakti, Temples, and Religious Scholarship

The Mughal centuries were a period of vigor and organization in Hindu devotional and scholarly life, not a time of passive survival under Muslim rule. As noted in earlier chapters, the term "Hinduism" is a later umbrella category, and the religious traditions of this period are best described by their own communities: Vaishnava, Shaiva, Shakta, Smarta, and others, each with institutions, lineages, texts, and practices. Their relationships to the Mughal state varied by region, period, and community.

Vaishnava devotion flourished in several forms. Vallabha's Pushtimarg, centered on Krishna worship in Braj and the western region, developed temple-based worship with elaborate ritual and aesthetic culture and drew support from merchant communities. The Gaudiya Vaishnava movement, following Chaitanya (d. 1534), established its center at Vrindavan through a group of scholar-theologians known as the Goswamis, who produced theological and devotional writing in Sanskrit and oversaw temple construction. Raja Man Singh of Amber, a senior Mughal noble, sponsored the large Govind Dev temple at Vrindavan in 1590, showing how Rajput nobles in imperial service could act as patrons of devotional communities with the emperor's permission. In Maharashtra, the Varkari tradition of Pandharpur and poet-saints such as Eknath and Tukaram built a devotional community open to many castes. Rama devotion, as in Tulsidas's work, spread widely in the north.

Shaiva and Shakta traditions also continued. Shaiva monastic orders, including naga ascetic groups that functioned as organized, sometimes armed, communities, were prominent in Varanasi and at pilgrimage sites. Shakta worship of the goddess flourished in Bengal and Assam, with Kamakhya remaining a major center and Bengali Tantric writing continuing. In Bengal, local rulers and landholders built distinctive terracotta temples, as at Bishnupur, in styles shaped by local building materials and traditions. In south India, temple-centered life continued under the Nayakas and other rulers, as described in Chapter 45.

Religious scholarship thrived in Sanskrit. Varanasi (Benares) became an especially important center for the study of grammar, philosophy, law, and ritual, with scholars such as Bhattoji Dikshita, whose grammatical work remained authoritative, and legal digest writers such as Kamalakara Bhatta and Nilakantha Bhatta. Kavindracarya Sarasvati, a Varanasi scholar, is recorded as having persuaded Shah Jahan to abolish a tax on pilgrims, a petition acknowledged in contemporary sources, which shows scholars acting as intermediaries between religious communities and the state. Madhusudana Sarasvati, an influential Advaita Vedanta philosopher, wrote works that remain part of the tradition; later traditions, which historians treat cautiously, credit him with organizing ascetics into armed groups to defend sannyasis against harassment. The philosophical and legal literature of the period shows that Sanskrit scholarship was not only preservation but active interpretation, responding to changing social and political circumstances.

Relations between Hindu religious institutions and the Mughal state were complex and varied by reign. Rulers issued grants to temples and Brahmans, and nobles built and patronized temples; at the same time, particular periods saw the destruction or desecration of temples, notably under Aurangzeb and in some earlier episodes (Chapter 54). Historians seek to explain these episodes through political and local context rather than through generalizations about a permanent religious conflict. Local rulers, including Rajputs, Bundelas, and Marathas, also built and defended temples, and temple communities negotiated their position with local authorities. Pilgrimage routes to Varanasi, Mathura, Puri, Nasik, and other centers created networks of travel, commerce, and information that crossed political boundaries.

Section 56.15

Sikh, Jain, and Other Religious-Intellectual Communities

Sikh, Jain, and other communities were not extensions of Hinduism or Islam. Each had its own scriptures, leaders, institutions, and relationships to the state, and the Mughal centuries were pivotal for several of them. Treating them only as parts of a binary landscape would erase significant histories.

For the Sikhs, the Mughal period shaped the transformation of a devotional community into a distinct panth with scripture and institutions. Guru Nanak's teachings (Chapter 48) were developed by successive Gurus. Guru Arjan, the fifth Guru, compiled the Adi Granth in 1603–04, gathering the hymns of the earlier Gurus together with compositions of other saints, including Kabir, Ravidas, Namdev, and the Sufi Shaykh Farid, and installing it at the Harmandir Sahib in Amritsar in 1604, with Bhai Gurdas serving as scribe. This anthology fixed a scriptural canon whose inclusiveness reflected the devotional world from which it emerged. Guru Arjan died in Mughal custody in 1606 after Jahangir's order; the Sikh tradition understands this as martyrdom, and Jahangir's memoir refers to the episode in political terms, connecting it to the Guru's alleged support of Prince Khusrau. His son Guru Hargobind established the Akal Takht and a more militarized posture. Guru Tegh Bahadur was executed in Delhi in 1675 under Aurangzeb, and Guru Gobind Singh founded the Khalsa in 1699. Sikh literary traditions also included the janamsakhis (life narratives of Guru Nanak) and the Varan of Bhai Gurdas; the later Dasam Granth, attributed in part to Guru Gobind Singh, has a complicated textual history and is debated within the community. The causes of conflict between Gurus and Mughal officials were political, fiscal, and religious, and varied over time; Chapter 54 and Chapter 59 discuss the political aspects.

Jain communities, spread across Gujarat, Rajasthan, and the Gangetic plain and strongly represented among merchants and bankers, maintained a rich scholarly and manuscript culture. Akbar invited Hiravijaya Suri, head of the Tapa Gaccha, in 1582; the monk arrived at Fatehpur Sikri in 1583. Jinachandra Suri of the Kharatara Gaccha was invited in 1591 and reached the court at Lahore in 1592. These monks engaged in discussion with the emperor and his circle, and farmans issued in the period, preserved in Jain collections, restricted animal slaughter on specified days, particularly during the Paryushana festival, and protected Jain pilgrimage sites such as Shatrunjaya. Historians treat these decrees as evidence of Jain influence and of Akbar's attention to the community; scholars also note that Jain monks represented their own sources to emphasize imperial favor. Jain manuscript production, including illustrated Kalpasutra copies and large temple libraries (bhandars) at Patan and Jaisalmer, continued throughout the period. Banarsidas of Agra, a Jain merchant, wrote the Ardhakathanak (1641), a verse autobiography in Braj often described as one of the earliest autobiographies in a modern Indian language, in which he describes commercial ventures, illness, family life, and his religious quests.

Other communities also shaped Mughal religious-intellectual life. Parsi (Zoroastrian) priests, notably Dastur Meherji Rana of Navsari, visited Akbar's court around 1578 and contributed to discussions in which the emperor showed interest in fire reverence and Zoroastrian ideas. Jesuit missionaries, arriving in 1580, engaged in debates, translated Christian texts, and hoped, unsuccessfully, for the emperor's conversion; Armenian and other Christian merchants resided in Agra and elsewhere. The Dadu panth, a devotional movement in Rajasthan associated with the poet Dadu Dayal (c. 1544–1603), the Kabirpanth, and the Roshaniyya movement of the Afghan frontier, whose leaders clashed with Mughal forces in the late sixteenth and early seventeenth centuries, add further variety. Each community had its own relationship to political power and to its neighbors.

Section 56.16

Religious Debate, Intellectual Exchange, and the Limits of Cultural Synthesis

Interreligious engagement was a defining feature of Mughal intellectual life, and it took many forms: formal debates at court, translation, philosophical comparison, shared devotional spaces, and everyday neighborly interaction. It was also bounded by persistent differences of doctrine, social status, political interest, and power. The task for historians is to describe both the exchange and the limits without reducing either to a slogan.

Akbar's court debates in the Ibadat Khana, the house of worship built at Fatehpur Sikri in 1575 (Chapter 52), brought together Sunni and Shi'i scholars, Sufis, Brahmans, Jain monks, Jesuits, and Parsi priests. They began as discussions among Muslim scholars and quickly widened. Accounts of them come from Abu'l Fazl, who stressed Akbar's search for truth, and from Badauni, who recorded his disapproval of the debates' effects on orthodoxy. Both authors were participants with agendas. Akbar's policy of sulh-i kul ("peace with all"), the abolition of the jizya, and his own religious experiments, discussed in Chapter 52, were connected to this environment. They also brought sharp opposition, and the Mahdawi, Shi'i, and Naqshbandi episodes noted above demonstrate that the same court contained both openness and repression.

Dara Shikoh's work is the most sustained philosophical effort at comparison. In the Majma' al-Bahrayn ("The Mingling of the Two Oceans"), written about 1655, he compared the technical vocabulary of Sufism and Indian philosophy, particularly Vedanta, and concluded, in the preface's account, that their differences were largely verbal. His translation of the Upanishads and his reading of the Qur'an led to the claim that the Upanishads were the concealed book alluded to in the Qur'an. Dara's views were rooted in a Sufi doctrine of the unity of being, and they remained a minority position even within Sufism. After his defeat by Aurangzeb in the war of succession (Chapter 53), he was executed in 1659, and the charges against him included apostasy. Historians such as Supriya Gandhi have emphasized that Dara's thought must be understood within its Sufi and Persianate context and not as a modern secular ecumenism, while recognizing the real intellectual ambition of his project.

The limits of terms such as "syncretism" and "tolerance" are evident. "Syncretism" implies a blending of religions into something new, but many Mughal-era exchanges involved translation, comparison, or shared forms without merging beliefs; devotees of Kabir, Dadu, or Nanak accepted insights from different traditions while building distinct communities. "Tolerance" implies a ruler's benevolent permission, and it can obscure the power imbalance involved in a state deciding which communities to protect or tax. Historians increasingly describe the period as one of "negotiated coexistence," in which communities competed for patronage, defended boundaries, borrowed forms, and sometimes fought, all within a changing political framework.

Interaction therefore did not erase doctrinal distinctions, social boundaries, or conflict. Brahmans maintained rules of ritual purity that governed interaction with non-Brahmans and non-Hindus; ulama maintained distinctions between Muslim law and other traditions; Sikhs and Jains developed explicit boundary-marking practices. Violence occurred, including the executions of Guru Arjan, Qazi Nurullah, and Guru Tegh Bahadur, the destruction of particular temples, and the sectarian and political struggles of the succession wars. Religious knowledge was also a political resource, which rulers used to bolster legitimacy, as in Akbar's court ideology and Aurangzeb's emphasis on legal compilation, such as the Fatawa-i Alamgiri, a major digest of Hanafi law produced under his patronage in the later seventeenth century.

Context: Dara was the eldest son of Shah Jahan, a student of Qadiri Sufism, and a patron of translators. He wrote the Majma' al-Bahrayn as a comparison between the technical vocabulary of Sufis and of Indian monotheists, as he called them, in the years before the war of succession.

Editorial paraphrase (not a verbatim quotation): In his preface, Dara explains that he sought to compare the doctrines of the two groups of truth-seekers and found, after conversation with Indian scholars, that the differences between them were chiefly matters of expression; the treatise then sets out corresponding terms and concepts in parallel.

Analysis: The treatise shows a serious attempt at comparative thought within a Sufi framework. It cannot be read as evidence that most Mughal subjects, or even most Muslim scholars, shared Dara's conclusion. It was written by a prince with political ambitions, and its arguments were used by his opponents to portray him as a heretic. The text does not represent the perspectives of the Hindu scholars with whom he conversed except as he reports them.

Section 56.17

Women as Writers, Religious Figures, and Cultural Patrons

Women of the Mughal household and wider society took part in cultural life as authors, patrons, readers, performers, and religious seekers, although the surviving evidence is uneven and heavily weighted toward the imperial family. Royal women controlled substantial resources, including landed assignments and cash allowances, and used them to commission buildings, books, gardens, and charitable foundations. Seclusion practices in elite households limited their public appearances but did not prevent them from exercising influence through letters, patronage, and household networks. The task is to recover their contributions without assuming that a few well-documented women represent all women.

Gulbadan Begum (c. 1523–1603), daughter of Babur and aunt of Akbar, wrote the Humayun-nama (also known as the Ahval-i Humayun Padshah), a memoir of her father's and half-brother's reigns, at Akbar's request in about 1587, to supply material for Abu'l Fazl's history. She wrote in plain Persian with a personal and domestic eye, describing weddings, feasts, family quarrels, journeys, and the hardships of Humayun's flight, as well as the movements of the women of the household. The surviving manuscript is incomplete and survives in a single copy now in the British Library. In 1575–76 she departed with a party of royal women on a pilgrimage to Mecca, an expedition lasting several years; surviving chronicles give differing dates for the beginning of the journey.

Nur Jahan (originally Mihr un-Nisa), the principal wife of Jahangir from 1611, was one of the most politically powerful women of the dynasty (see Chapter 53), and she was also an active patron. She commissioned the tomb of her father, Itimad-ud-Daula, at Agra (1622–28), built caravanserais, including the Nur Mahal Serai at Jalandhar, and laid out gardens. She is credited with fostering fashion in textiles and jewelry and is reported to have composed verse in Persian, though the surviving attributions are limited. Mumtaz Mahal, wife of Shah Jahan, is remembered chiefly through the monument built in her memory; her own cultural role is less documented.

Jahanara Begum (1614–1681), Shah Jahan's eldest daughter, became the most prominent woman patron of her generation after her mother's death in 1631. She had extensive financial resources and sponsored the Jami Masjid at Agra (completed 1648), a caravanserai and market area in Delhi's Chandni Chowk, and gardens. A disciple of the Qadiri teacher Mulla Shah Badakhshi, she wrote two Persian works that survive: the Munis al-Arwah (1640), a biography of Mu'in al-Din Chishti, and the Risala-yi Sahibiyya (1648), an account of her own teacher and her spiritual experience. Her religious writing is among the best evidence for a Mughal woman's intellectual and spiritual life, and she is a reminder that the dynasty's women were part of Sufi networks as seekers and authors.

Zeb-un-Nissa (1638–1702), Aurangzeb's eldest daughter, is celebrated in later tradition as a poet writing under the pen name Makhfi ("the hidden one"), as a patron of scholars and copyists, and as the owner of an important library. The attribution of the Diwan-i Makhfi to her is debated: the collection was compiled long after her death, and some scholars question how much of it is hers. Her patronage of scholars, in contrast, is better supported, including the production of works commissioned in her name. Roshanara Begum, a sister of Jahanara and Aurangzeb, patronized a garden near Delhi that retains her name. Salima Sultan Begum, wife of Bairam Khan and later of Akbar, is also credited with verses under the pen name Makhfi; the same name appears in later accounts of Zeb-un-Nissa and, in some tazkiras, Nur Jahan. As the pen name was not unique, it cannot by itself establish authorship of a particular poem.

Nonroyal women appear in the record more rarely and usually in the accounts of men. Female attendants served as scribes, readers, and administrators within the imperial household; women musicians and dancers performed at court and in towns; Sufi women led devotional circles and some were venerated as saints; and women in merchant, artisan, and peasant households contributed labor to textile production and agriculture, as discussed in Chapter 55. The poet Mirabai, the Rajput princess and devotee of Krishna, is a major figure in the devotional literature of the earlier sixteenth century, though the historical details of her life are uncertain and her poems circulated in many versions. Historians therefore combine direct evidence, such as Gulbadan's and Jahanara's texts, with inferences from patronage records, inscriptions, and the accounts of travelers, remaining cautious about later attributions.

Section 56.18

Regional Courts, Artistic Networks, and Cultural Exchange Beyond the Imperial Center

Mughal culture is often described from the imperial center outward, but the traffic ran in several directions. Regional courts adopted, adapted, and resisted Mughal forms; artists moved between imperial and regional patrons; and independent states developed their own cultural identities in competition with Delhi and Agra. Understanding this network requires looking at specific regions and avoiding the assumption that regional art was simply a provincial imitation of the court.

The Rajput courts offer the clearest case of selective adaptation. Rulers of Amber, Bikaner, Bundi, Mewar, and Marwar entered into alliances with the Mughals from Akbar's time, serving as imperial officers and sometimes marrying daughters into the dynasty (Chapter 51). They adopted Mughal court dress, architectural forms such as jharokhas and gardens, and portraiture conventions, while maintaining distinct local religious practices, genealogical claims, and artistic traditions. Rajput painting developed in several schools, notably Mewar, Bundi, and Bikaner, that adapted Mughal naturalism alongside local color schemes, themes from the Ramayana and Krishna traditions, and poetic modes represented in ragamala and rasikapriya illustrations. Painting was also present at Kishangarh by the seventeenth century, but its distinctive and best-known mature school belongs especially to the eighteenth century. Artists moved between Mughal and Rajput workshops; some painters of the early Mughal atelier came from regions that later became Rajput centers, and in the later seventeenth century, as imperial painting patronage narrowed, some Mughal-trained artists found employment at Rajput and other courts.

The Deccan courts of Bijapur, Golconda, and Ahmadnagar (Chapter 46) maintained distinct cultural identities and drew on connections with Iran, the Ottoman world, and East Africa. Bijapur's architecture, with its massive domes such as the Gol Gumbaz (completed around 1656), and its painting and music, particularly under Ibrahim Adil Shah II (r. 1580–1627), developed in dialogue with, but not subordinate to, Mughal culture. Golconda, a center of diamond trade and Persianate culture, produced its own painting and a thriving Dakhni and Telugu literature. After the Mughal conquests of the 1680s, artists and scholars moved north, and Aurangabad and Hyderabad became cultural centers, a process that fed into the successor states of Chapter 60.

Bengal, absorbed by the Mughals in the late sixteenth and early seventeenth centuries, retained strong regional traditions: Bengali vernacular literature, terracotta temple architecture, and the Vaishnava and Shakta communities described earlier. The Mughal provincial capital at Dhaka (from 1610) developed a distinctive urban culture and a famous muslin industry, while Bengali poets, Hindu and Muslim, wrote in the vernacular under the patronage of local landholders and courts, including the Arakan court at Mrauk-U. Kashmir, absorbed in 1586, saw the imperial gardens mentioned above and an established tradition of Persian and Sanskrit learning, shawl-weaving, paper-making, and calligraphy; the Kashmiri poet Ghani and the scribal and artistic communities of the valley are part of that story. Gujarat, a commercial region with strong Jain and Muslim merchant communities, kept up a tradition of manuscript illustration and temple and mosque construction, and its craftsmen contributed to imperial building projects. In Punjab, Lahore combined Mughal court culture, Sufi shrines, Sikh institutions, and a vigorous Punjabi poetic tradition.

The patterns of exchange were multidirectional. Artists were recruited, captured, or invited; manuscripts were gifted, bought, and copied; poets traveled between courts; and merchants carried luxury goods and ideas along the routes mapped earlier. The relationships between imperial and nonimperial institutions were sometimes competitive and sometimes cooperative. An independent sultan or Rajput ruler could use Mughal-style architecture to claim parity with the empire, or could emphasize distinctive local styles to assert autonomy. These dynamics also show that cultural influence did not follow political borders neatly: the Mughals borrowed from the Deccan and Iran as much as they influenced them.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Cultural Production from Babur to the Early Eighteenth Century

What Changed

  • Persian became more tightly integrated into administration and drew in new groups, such as Hindu scribal communities
  • Court painting moved from Persian-trained beginnings to a distinct Mughal style, then to a more formal courtly manner, and finally to dispersed regional production
  • Architecture developed from red sandstone and mixed forms toward white-marble refinement and then to grand mosque-building and fortification
  • Vernacular literatures grew rapidly, and regional courts increasingly commissioned work of their own
  • Religious institutions, including the Sikh Panth, Pushtimarg, and Naqshbandi networks, developed sharper organizational forms

What Stayed the Same

  • Production depended on workshops, hereditary crafts, and patronage from rulers, nobles, and religious institutions
  • Manuscript culture remained dominant; printing was not adopted at court
  • Sanskrit scholarship, Jain manuscript traditions, devotional singing, and Sufi shrine culture continued with their own internal rhythms
  • Elite culture remained accessible mainly to those with literacy, wealth, and connections

Who experienced change most directly? Court artists, scribes, and nobles experienced the most visible shifts, as did communities whose position depended on patronage. Who experienced greater continuity? Village audiences, devotional communities, and many hereditary artisans experienced continuity in their practices, even as the political order above them changed. Why the pattern matters: It cautions against reading cultural history as a single arc of rise and decline, and it helps explain how cultural forms could outlast the empire.

Section 56.19

Cultural Interaction, Artistic Labor, Social Boundaries, and Mughal Legacies

The cultural achievements of the Mughal age were made by a large and varied workforce. Scribes and calligraphers, painters and illuminators, stone-carvers and masons, garden-builders, instrument-makers, musicians, scholars, translators, religious teachers, merchants, and patrons all contributed, and their relationships were shaped by status, wealth, caste, religion, gender, and political position. Understanding cultural production as labor connects this chapter to Chapter 55's analysis of artisans and merchants, and it prompts questions about who benefited and who was recognized.

Artistic labor was organized by hereditary craft families, workshop hierarchies, and patron-client relationships. Some artisans gained titles, salaries, and the right to sign their work; many others remained unnamed. Master painters and calligraphers moved among patrons, and artisans' guilds and caste associations governed training and standards. Women, including those who prepared textiles, embroidered, or performed, played roles that are less visible in records. Those who built monuments, such as quarrymen, stone-cutters, and laborers, worked under conditions that the sources seldom describe; the Taj Mahal, for example, required thousands of workers, but few of their names or circumstances are recorded. Interpreting Mughal culture requires acknowledging this uneven documentation.

Social boundaries shaped access to cultural resources. Persian literacy was concentrated among certain groups; Sanskrit learning was largely, though not exclusively, a Brahman preserve; Sufi lodges, temples, and monasteries had their own rules of entry; and women of different classes had different degrees of access. Caste and religious identities structured social interaction, as in Brahmanical rules governing contact with non-Brahmans and Muslims, and in the exclusion of lower-caste and Dalit communities from many religious and educational institutions. At the same time, devotional movements such as those of Kabir, Ravidas, and the Sikh Gurus, and Sufi shrines, offered spaces where such boundaries could be questioned or partly bridged, as the Adi Granth's inclusion of saints from many backgrounds shows. Mobility was real but limited: a Khatri or Kayastha could rise through Persian skills, but the social order itself was not abolished.

The description "composite culture" (in Urdu and Hindi, often rendered ganga-jamuni tahzib) has been widely used for Mughal and post-Mughal South Asia. It captures real phenomena: shared artistic practices, bilingual poets, joint participation in festivals and shrines, and collaborations like the Razmnama. But it has limits, as the debate below shows: the term can imply a harmony that obscures inequality, exclusion, and conflict, and it can suggest that two clearly defined blocs, Hindu and Muslim, merged, when the actual landscape included many more communities and many different degrees and forms of interaction.

Mughal cultural forms also survived and adapted after 1707, a theme developed in §56.20 and in Chapters 57 and 60. Persian remained an important administrative language in many successor states. Within the East India Company's expanding jurisdictions, its use in some judicial and revenue proceedings was progressively displaced, notably after legislation in 1837 permitted replacement of Persian in the Bengal regulations (Chapter 67); the process differed by office and region. Mughal-style architecture, gardens, painting, music, and etiquette were adopted by regional courts such as Lucknow, Hyderabad, and the Rajput states. Dhrupad and later khayal singing, calligraphy, and manuscript painting persisted; Urdu poetry, which began to take literary form in the early eighteenth century in Delhi and the Deccan, grew in part from the Hindavi and Dakhni traditions and Persian models discussed here, although its fuller history belongs to later chapters. This survival without political centralization is one of the most important legacies of the period.

Historical Debate

Does "Composite Culture" Adequately Describe Mughal South Asia?

The question: Is "composite culture" a useful way to describe the cultural history of Mughal South Asia, or does it obscure important divisions, inequalities, and regional differences?

Interpretation A — Exchange and shared practice: Twentieth-century historians such as Tara Chand and Mohammad Mujeeb emphasized the mutual influence of Islamic and Indian traditions in architecture, music, language, dress, food, and devotional life, and the idea of a composite culture became important in modern Indian historical writing and public discourse. Evidence includes bilingual poets, shared shrines, translations, courtly adoption of Indian forms, and Hindu participation in Persian letters. On this view, the Mughal centuries produced real and lasting cultural interpenetration.
Interpretation B — Distinct traditions, hierarchy, and variety: Other scholars argue that "composite culture" can flatten a more complex history. They point to doctrinal boundaries, competition for patronage, periods of religious persecution, social exclusion by caste and gender, and the many regional and linguistic traditions that did not fit a Hindu–Muslim binary. Studies of Sanskrit and the court, of Sikh and Jain communities, and of Dalit and Adivasi experience suggest a more differentiated picture. Some critics, from different political perspectives, also argue that the term has been used in modern politics as a slogan, whether to celebrate or reject it.

Evidence: Both views can cite the same sources: translation projects, court debates, architectural forms, devotional anthologies, and records of conflict and exclusion. The disagreement concerns how to weigh them and what framing best captures their combined significance.

Why it matters: The term affects how students understand both the Mughal period and its modern memory. A careful approach recognizes creative interaction, acknowledges that it was uneven and negotiated, and avoids using the past to support either a story of seamless harmony or one of permanent conflict.

Student task: Choose three examples from this chapter, one that supports the idea of composite culture, one that complicates it, and one that falls between, and write a short assessment of whether the term should be used, qualified, or replaced.

Section 56.20

Review and Historical Synthesis

This chapter has argued that Mughal culture was neither a simple fusion nor the property of a single court. It was the product of overlapping institutions and traditions: Persianate chancelleries and libraries, Sanskrit scholarly communities, vernacular poets and performers, Sufi and devotional networks, Jain and Sikh communities, hereditary workshops of craftsmen, and the imperial and noble patrons who funded many of the best-documented works. Their interaction produced distinctive achievements, such as the painting atelier, the Razmnama, the Taj Mahal, the Adi Granth, the Ramcharitmanas, and Dara Shikoh's comparative writings. It also produced boundaries, disputes, and silences.

Division I showed that Persian functioned as an imperial language that was learned rather than inherited, that court histories mixed information with legitimation, that manuscripts depended on large teams and on the paper, pigment, and gold economy, and that education and science continued older South Asian and Islamic traditions while receiving selective court patronage. Division II showed how painters, builders, and garden-makers adapted Persian and European models to South Asian materials and tastes, creating a recognizable visual culture that served imperial ideology, religious meaning, and individual curiosity. Division III examined translation, Persian poetry, vernacular literature, and music, and found that the most influential literary and musical traditions often grew outside the court even when the court patronized them. Division IV addressed religious communities in their own right and showed that dialogue and conflict coexisted. Division V restored women, regional courts, laborers, and social boundaries to the account.

The cultural achievements of the period depended on the social and economic foundations established in Chapter 55. The agrarian surplus extracted through the revenue system, the textile and commercial economy, the silver inflows, and the labor of artisans and builders paid for books, buildings, gardens, and performances. Cultural production, in turn, helped legitimize the system, through chronicles that justified imperial rule, buildings that staged it, and rituals that bound nobles and religious communities to the court. Recognizing this interdependence helps explain both the scale of Mughal achievements and their exclusivity.

The chapter also showed that cultural forms could survive changes in political power. When imperial authority weakened after 1707, artists, musicians, scholars, and craftsmen moved to the courts of successor states and regional powers, carrying techniques and repertoires with them. Persian continued as an administrative and literary language, Mughal architectural and garden forms were reproduced, and vernacular literatures and devotional institutions continued to develop. Chapter 57 will examine the political reconfiguration of the eighteenth century, the weakening of imperial authority, the rise of regional powers, and the interpretive debate about whether this was decline or transformation. Students should read that political narrative with the cultural continuities of this chapter in mind: the empire's reach contracted, but many of the institutions, skills, and ideas it had nurtured did not disappear.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the cultural and intellectual world of the Mughal Empire between 1526 and 1707 as the product of many participants, not only of emperors. Its central argument is that Mughal culture emerged from the interaction of Persianate court institutions, Sanskrit and Jain learning, vernacular literatures, Sufi and devotional networks, and the skills of hereditary craftsmen, supported by the agrarian and commercial economy analyzed in Chapter 55.

Division I established the institutions of knowledge. Persian served as the language of administration, diplomacy, and high literature, but it was learned by a limited population, including Iranian and Central Asian migrants and scribal communities such as Khatris, Kayasthas, and Brahmans. Court histories, from Babur's memoir to Abu'l Fazl's Akbarnama and the Jahangirnama, are indispensable sources and also instruments of legitimacy, to be read against critical voices such as Badauni and against non-court evidence. Manuscript production depended on teams of scribes, painters, illuminators, and binders and on materials such as paper, pigments, and gold, and education and science combined older South Asian and Islamic traditions with selective Mughal patronage, as in the Ilahi calendar, astrolabe and globe-making, and Persian translations of mathematical and medical works.

Division II traced the visual arts. Mughal painting began with Persian masters brought by Humayun, grew through large collaborative projects such as the Hamzanama, and diversified under Jahangir and Shah Jahan into portraiture, natural-history studies, and albums, with named artists such as Basawan, Abu'l Hasan, and Mansur. European prints and paintings were adopted selectively. Architecture combined Timurid, Persian, and Indian traditions in structures from Humayun's Tomb and Fatehpur Sikri to the Taj Mahal and the Badshahi Mosque, expressing imperial ideology and religious meaning, while charbagh gardens joined hydraulic engineering to ideas of paradise and sovereignty.

Division III examined literature and performance. Persian poetry attracted Iranian and Indian poets alike and was cultivated well beyond the emperor's circle. Sanskrit–Persian translation, notably the Razmnama and Dara Shikoh's Upanishad translation, was a significant but bounded form of exchange, and its interpretation remains debated. Vernacular literatures, from Tulsidas's Awadhi Ramcharitmanas to Braj court poetry, Marathi, Bengali, Assamese, and Punjabi traditions, reached far larger audiences than Persian, and music, especially dhrupad and the career of Tansen, illustrates both documented practice and later legend.

Division IV addressed religion. Sufi orders such as the Chishtis, Qadiris, and Naqshbandis differed in practice and outlook, and figures such as Ahmad Sirhindi and Mian Mir show the range of positions. Hindu devotional and scholarly communities, including the Pushtimarg, Gaudiya Vaishnavas, Varkaris, Shaivas, Shaktas, and the scholars of Varanasi, were active and organized. Sikhs compiled the Adi Granth in 1604, Jain monks engaged Akbar's court, and Parsis, Christians, and other groups added to the landscape. Court debates and Dara Shikoh's comparative project show real engagement, but terms such as syncretism and tolerance only partly fit, and doctrinal, social, and political boundaries persisted, sometimes violently.

Division V restored women, regions, and labor. Gulbadan Begum, Nur Jahan, Jahanara Begum, and, with caution about attribution, Zeb-un-Nissa show women's roles as authors and patrons, while evidence for nonroyal women is scarcer. Rajput, Deccan, Bengal, Kashmir, Gujarat, and Punjab developed their own cultural forms in dialogue with the empire. Artistic labor depended on hereditary families, workshops, and anonymous workers, and access to culture was structured by caste, religion, gender, and wealth.

Two debates framed the interpretation. The first asked whether Mughal culture was primarily imperial and Persianate or dependent on regional and multilingual traditions, and the best current answer combines both: Persianate culture was integrated into South Asia, while regional traditions had substantial independence. The second asked whether "composite culture" is adequate; it captures real exchange but can obscure hierarchy, exclusion, and the variety of communities beyond a Hindu–Muslim binary.

The evidence base is rich but skewed toward courts, elites, and men, and the chapter has repeatedly distinguished documented authorship and patronage from later legend, as in the stories about Tansen, the attribution of poetry to Zeb-un-Nissa, and the symbolic readings of monuments.

Finally, the chapter showed that cultural forms outlived imperial centralization. After 1707 artists, musicians, scholars, and craftsmen carried Mughal-derived styles and institutions into the courts of successor and regional states, a continuity that Chapter 57 and Chapter 60 build upon as they examine the political transformation of the eighteenth century.

Chapter Reflection

Mughal culture matters because it shows how a large early modern state interacted with the learning, art, and devotion of the societies it ruled. It created institutions of patronage on a remarkable scale, but its achievements depended on scribes, painters, masons, poets, performers, scholars, and religious teachers whose communities and traditions were older than the empire and would outlast it.

The chapter also reveals the importance of reading culture critically. Chronicles flatter, monuments stage power, and memories simplify. Careful historians ask who made a work, who paid, who could see it, and who is missing. This habit prepares students for the eighteenth century, where the interpretation of political change as decline or transformation depends in part on whether one measures the period by imperial power or by cultural and economic vitality.

Finally, the chapter shows why the six-volume sequence matters. Mughal Persianate institutions grew from the Sultanate world of Volume II; regional courts, Sikh and Maratha communities, and successor states would build on what the Mughals nurtured; and, in Volume IV, colonial scholars and rulers would encounter, classify, and often reinterpret the manuscripts, buildings, and languages described here. The cultural inheritance of the Mughal age is not a single story but a set of contested and continuing ones.

Chapter 57 now turns to the political history of the Mughal Empire after Aurangzeb, asking how imperial authority weakened and how regional powers and cultural centers took on new importance.
