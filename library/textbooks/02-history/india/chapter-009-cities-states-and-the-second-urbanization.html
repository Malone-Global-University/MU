Part III — The Second Urbanization and New Religious Traditions
9

Cities, States, and the Second Urbanization

Indian History: South Asia from the Indus Civilization to the Present

Chapter Opening

Somewhere along the middle Ganges, in the centuries before the Buddha's birth, a traveler descending from the drier uplands of the western Vedic heartland would have noticed something new pressing in on every side: denser fields of rice, more frequent villages, wider and busier roads, and, at certain points along the great river, clusters of houses and workshops behind earthen ramparts, many belonging to settlements that had expanded or acquired stronger fortifications over preceding generations. Cattle wealth, chanted genealogies, and small chiefly assemblies — the world Chapter 7 described — had not disappeared, but a different kind of society was visibly forming alongside it: a society of merchants weighing goods in marketplaces, of kings and assemblies levying dues on trade as well as on land, and of walled towns whose populations no longer knew one another as kin.

Chapter 7 traced how later Vedic communities moved from mobile pastoralism toward settled agriculture, from small tribal groupings toward territorial chiefdoms, and from simple lineage authority toward more elaborate kingship and Brahmanical ritual sanction. Chapter 8 then insisted that this northern, Gangetic trajectory was only one regional story among several unfolding across South Asia during the same centuries, and that historians must resist letting the Vedic textual record stand in for the whole subcontinent. This chapter returns to the northern and especially the middle Gangetic world that Chapter 7 left in transition, and asks a large question: how did that world of scattered janapadas, cattle wealth, and chiefly assemblies become, within a few centuries, a landscape of walled cities, coined money, professional merchants, and large territorial states contending for supremacy?

Historians and archaeologists commonly call this transformation the Second Urbanization of South Asia, a term that deliberately invokes, and just as deliberately distances itself from, the "first" urbanization examined in Chapters 3 and 4: the Mature Harappan cities of the Indus and Ghaggar-Hakra zone. The label is useful shorthand, not a claim of continuity. Centuries separated the decline of the Indus cities from the growth of Gangetic towns, the intervening geography was different, the script and administrative apparatus of the Indus world had left no known descendant in the Gangetic region, and nothing in the archaeological record suggests that Gangetic urban planners were consciously reviving a remembered Harappan model. What the two urbanizations share is a general historical pattern — dense settlement, craft specialization, long-distance exchange, and increasingly powerful political authority — not a direct institutional or demographic lineage.

This chapter's task is to explain that pattern without reducing it to a single cause. Older textbook accounts sometimes presented the Second Urbanization as the simple product of iron tools: iron axes cleared the dense jungle of the Gangetic doab, iron plowshares turned heavier soils, and the resulting agricultural surplus fed growing cities almost automatically. That story is too neat. Iron technology, examined already in Chapter 7 and revisited here, mattered, but so did rainfall and soil quality, rice cultivation and its distinctive demands on labor and water, growing population, the expansion of trade routes linking the Gangetic plain to the northwest and to peninsular India, the appearance of coined money, and the political ambitions of kings and assemblies eager to extract revenue from all of this activity. No single thread explains the whole cloth; this chapter follows several threads at once and shows how they became woven together.

This transformation also unfolded unevenly across the region it affected. Some settlements grew into substantial fortified towns by the sixth century BCE; others remained modest villages for generations longer. Some polities consolidated into larger territorial monarchies with increasingly concentrated royal authority; others retained older forms of assembly government in which power was shared, however unequally, among leading lineages. This chapter examines both patterns — the rise of powerful monarchies and the survival of oligarchic "republics" known to later tradition as gana-sanghas — without treating either as more "advanced" than the other or as the inevitable future of South Asian politics.

It is equally important, at the outset, to mark the boundaries of this chapter's story. The astonishing rise of Magadha from one janapada among many into the core of the later Mauryan Empire belongs to Chapter 10, which examines Bimbisara, Ajatashatru, the Nandas, and the specific strategic and administrative advantages that allowed one kingdom to outcompete its rivals. The religious and philosophical ferment this chapter's economic and political transformation helped to generate — the teachings of the Buddha, the teachings of Mahavira and the wider Shramana movements, and the philosophical developments recorded in the Upanishads — belong respectively to Chapters 11, 12, and 13. This chapter sets the social, economic, and political stage on which those later chapters' figures and movements will appear; it does not yet introduce them in detail.

By the chapter's end, students should understand the Second Urbanization not as a single event but as a convergence: agricultural intensification feeding population growth, population growth feeding denser settlement and expanding trade, trade encouraging coinage and craft specialization, and all of this activity feeding the ambitions and revenues of increasingly powerful states, whether governed by hereditary kings or by assemblies of leading families. That convergence, occurring across the middle Ganges valley and adjoining regions of northern South Asia during the first millennium BCE, created both the historical setting and the social tensions from which the religious and philosophical movements of the following chapters would emerge.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Define the term "Second Urbanization" and explain why it does not imply direct continuity with the Mature Harappan urban tradition.
  2. Explain the agrarian and demographic foundations of Gangetic urban growth, including the role, and the limits, of iron technology.
  3. Describe the growth of trade networks, merchant communities, and craft specialization associated with the Second Urbanization, while avoiding anachronistic assumptions about guild organization.
  4. Analyze the significance of early punch-marked coinage for exchange, taxation, and political authority, while recognizing the chronological uncertainty surrounding its origins.
  5. Trace the emergence of fortified towns and cities across the Ganges valley and distinguish settlement hierarchy among villages, towns, and cities.
  6. Explain the development from jana to janapada to mahajanapada, and evaluate the limitations of later textual lists of "sixteen mahajanapadas."
  7. Compare monarchical and assembly-based ("gana-sangha") forms of political organization among early historic states, using the term "republic" with appropriate caution.
  8. Assess the causes and consequences of interstate warfare and competition for resources, revenue, and strategic advantage among the janapadas and mahajanapadas.
  9. Connect economic and social transformation to the emergence of new religious and philosophical questioning, without yet detailing the specific movements examined in Chapters 11 through 13.
  10. Evaluate multiple, interacting causes for the Second Urbanization rather than attributing it to any single technological or political factor.
  11. Distinguish the geographic and chronological scope of this chapter's northern, Gangetic focus from the wider subcontinental diversity established in Chapter 8.
  12. Identify the specific historical questions reserved for Chapter 10's treatment of Magadha's rise to territorial empire.

Historical Context

Chapter 7 traced the later Vedic transformation: the eastward expansion of Indo-Aryan-speaking communities into the Gangetic basin, the adoption of increasingly intensive agriculture, the appearance of iron tools alongside continued and probably more important developments in land clearance and cultivation practice, and the gradual consolidation of small lineage-based janas into larger, more territorially defined janapadas under increasingly assertive kings supported by Brahmanical ritual authority. That chapter closed with northern South Asia poised between an older world of cattle wealth and lineage assemblies and a newer world of settled territorial kingship, without yet describing the full urban and commercial transformation that followed.

Chapter 8 then insisted that this northern, Vedic-derived narrative could not stand in for the history of the entire subcontinent during the same centuries. Central India, the Deccan, the far south, and Sri Lanka were following their own historical trajectories, some marked by megalithic burial traditions, others by early Tamil-speaking agrarian and pastoral societies, still others by patterns of coastal exchange only loosely connected to Gangetic developments. That chapter's caution remains in force here: this chapter's focus on the Ganges valley and adjoining regions of northern South Asia reflects the specific geography within which the Second Urbanization is most densely documented, not a claim that the rest of the subcontinent stood still.

The specific developments this chapter examines are anchored chiefly in the middle Gangetic region — the territory encompassing roughly modern eastern Uttar Pradesh and Bihar — together with adjoining parts of the upper Gangetic plain, though evidence for urban growth, coinage, and territorial state formation extends more broadly across northern South Asia during the same centuries, with regional variation in timing and intensity. Historians rely on a combination of archaeological evidence — excavated settlement mounds, fortification remains, pottery sequences (notably the widespread ceramic ware conventionally called Northern Black Polished Ware, whose distribution and chronology are used, with appropriate caution, as one rough chronological marker for this period), coin hoards, and craft debris — together with later Buddhist, Jain, and Brahmanical textual traditions that describe, from varying distances in time, the political and social landscape of this era. As Chapter 1 cautioned regarding the Vedic corpus generally, students should treat Buddhist and Jain canonical texts describing sixth-century-BCE political geography with the same care: these texts were compiled, edited, and transmitted over generations after the events and settings they describe, and they reflect the interests and perspectives of particular religious communities rather than functioning as neutral, contemporary political surveys.

This chapter's central methodological challenge, then, is to combine archaeological evidence, which is comparatively reliable for dating settlement growth and material culture but largely silent on political events and institutions, with textual evidence, which offers rich detail about kingdoms, assemblies, and rulers but requires careful handling given its later compilation and religious framing. Historians increasingly favor a cautious, converging-lines-of-evidence approach: where archaeology and textual tradition point toward similar conclusions — growing settlement size, expanding exchange, the emergence of larger territorial polities — those conclusions can be stated with reasonable confidence; where only textual tradition supplies detail, as with many specific political events and personal biographies, that detail is presented as tradition rather than established fact.

Section 9.1

The Return of Large Urban Centers

Between roughly the eighth and sixth centuries BCE, archaeologists begin to detect, across the middle Gangetic region and adjoining parts of northern South Asia, a marked increase in settlement size, the construction of substantial earthen and later brick or stone fortifications around some settlements, and growing evidence of craft specialization, storage, and exchange within these enlarged settlements. Historians and archaeologists commonly describe this development as South Asia's Second Urbanization, a term that intentionally recalls the Mature Harappan urban civilization examined in Chapters 3 and 4 while insisting on a crucial distinction: this was not a revival, continuation, or reawakening of Harappan urbanism, but an independent, later process occurring in a different region, under different economic and political conditions, without any known institutional, administrative, or linguistic link to the earlier Indus cities.

Several features justify the comparison even as they underline the difference. Like the Mature Harappan cities, the new Gangetic towns and cities depended on an agricultural surplus substantial enough to support populations not directly engaged in food production; like the Harappan world, they show increasing craft specialization, exchange networks extending well beyond the immediate settlement, and, eventually, a form of standardized value recognition, though coined money, examined in Section 9.4, represents a genuinely new development without a clear Harappan precedent. Unlike the Harappan cities, however, the Gangetic towns developed without the striking uniformity of weights, measures, brick proportions, and urban layout that so distinguishes Mature Harappan sites from one another; Gangetic urban centers instead show considerable local variation in size, fortification style, and material culture, reflecting a more politically fragmented landscape of competing janapadas rather than an integrated civilizational system.

The chronology of Gangetic urban growth remains a matter of ongoing archaeological refinement. Excavations at numerous sites across the middle and upper Gangetic plain document a gradual increase in settlement size beginning in the centuries after 1000 BCE, with more decisive evidence of fortification, denser occupation, and expanding craft production emerging particularly from around the eighth through sixth centuries BCE onward, though the pace and timing of this growth varied considerably from site to site. Settlement enlargement and fortification should not be dated by pottery style alone; rather, from roughly the later seventh century BCE onward, this era of growing settlements became increasingly associated, in the ceramic sequence widely used by archaeologists, with a distinctive, finely made pottery style conventionally called Northern Black Polished Ware. Its widespread distribution across northern South Asia during the middle centuries of the first millennium BCE has made it a useful, if imperfect, associated material marker for this era of urban and commercial growth; students should understand that pottery style alone cannot establish precise dates or prove uniform urbanization across every site where it appears, and that fortification and settlement enlargement began developing gradually before NBPW's distribution became widespread, but its broad distribution does help historians trace the general geographic and chronological extent of this period's expanding exchange networks.

It is essential that students resist two tempting oversimplifications at the outset of this chapter. The first is to imagine urbanization as a single, dateable event — a specific year in which Gangetic South Asia "became urban." The evidence instead shows a gradual, geographically uneven process unfolding across several centuries, with some settlements achieving substantial size and fortification considerably earlier than others. Village agriculture remained the dominant form of everyday life across most of the surrounding countryside even at the height of this period's city growth, though, as later sections show, expanding markets and states increasingly altered the wider economic and political environment in which villages operated. The second oversimplification is to treat this urbanization as evidence that South Asia as a whole was converging on a single urban future; as Chapter 8 established, other regions of the subcontinent followed markedly different trajectories during these same centuries, and even within northern South Asia, the intensity and timing of urban growth varied considerably from one janapada to another.

Section 9.2

Agricultural Surplus and Population Growth

No city, ancient or modern, can exist without an agricultural hinterland capable of feeding a population that does not itself grow food. The Second Urbanization's most basic precondition, therefore, was a sustained expansion of agricultural production across the middle Gangetic basin sufficient to support growing numbers of townspeople, craftspeople, merchants, soldiers, and administrators.

Several interacting developments contributed to this expansion. Chapter 7 already introduced the gradual eastward spread of settled agriculture into the wetter, more densely forested Gangetic basin, a region whose heavier soils and denser vegetation posed different challenges from the semi-arid Punjab plains where earlier Vedic communities had first settled. Iron tools — axes for clearing forest, and, increasingly, plowshares and other implements for working heavier alluvial soils — became more widely available across this period, and archaeological evidence documents growing quantities of iron artifacts at Gangetic sites from roughly the early first millennium BCE onward. It remains important, however, to repeat the caution Chapter 7 established: iron technology alone did not mechanically cause agricultural intensification or urban growth. Forest clearance could be, and often was, accomplished by fire and by tools of wood, stone, and bronze well before iron became widely available; population growth, changing land-tenure arrangements, political encouragement of settlement, and the increasing value of agricultural surplus to expanding states all worked alongside iron technology rather than being simply produced by it. Historians today generally treat iron as one significant contributing factor among several rather than as the primary engine of Gangetic urbanization.

Rice cultivation deserves particular attention in this chapter's account of agricultural change. The middle and lower Gangetic basin's higher rainfall and extensive floodplain made it considerably more suited to rice cultivation than the drier Punjab and upper Gangetic regions, where wheat and barley — long-established crops in South Asia that remained especially important in the northwest and upper Gangetic regions — continued to dominate. Rice cultivation in South Asia has a considerably deeper history than this period alone, and archaeobotanical evidence points toward continuity and regional diversification in rice use over many centuries rather than a sudden first-millennium-BCE adoption of an intensive new cultivation regime; historians should therefore speak cautiously of the increasing importance and intensification of rice cultivation in suitable Gangetic environments during this period, rather than asserting a specific cultivation method, such as deliberate transplanting, as an established and independently demonstrable engine of demographic growth, since particular cultivation techniques are considerably harder to demonstrate archaeologically than the presence of the crop itself. The archaeological and archaeobotanical evidence for the growing importance of rice cultivation across the middle Gangetic basin during this period, while still the subject of ongoing research, generally supports the view that rice contributed to the region's growing agricultural surplus and population density, working alongside, rather than separately from, the iron technology and expanding cultivated area already discussed.

Population growth, in turn, both resulted from and further encouraged this agricultural expansion. A larger agricultural surplus could support more people; a growing population required, and in some cases actively pursued, further land clearance and agricultural intensification to sustain itself. Archaeological settlement surveys across the middle Gangetic region document an increasing number and density of occupied sites across the centuries this chapter examines, consistent with genuine demographic growth, though historians should be cautious about deriving precise population figures from settlement counts alone, since factors such as incomplete excavation coverage, differential site preservation, and changing settlement patterns can all affect the archaeological record independently of actual population change.

This agricultural and demographic expansion did not occur in a political vacuum. As Chapter 7 explained, later Vedic kings and the Brahmanical ritual specialists who supported their authority had already begun asserting stronger claims over agricultural land, labor, and the surplus it produced. The expanding janapadas examined in Section 9.6 had every incentive to encourage further agricultural settlement within their territories, since a larger cultivated area and population meant greater potential revenue, manpower, and military capacity. Agricultural surplus, in other words, was not merely a precondition for urbanization; it was itself increasingly organized, taxed, and directed by expanding political authorities, a theme this chapter develops further in Sections 9.6 through 9.8.

Section 9.3

Trade and Merchant Communities

As agricultural surplus grew and settlements became denser, the movement of goods across the Gangetic landscape intensified correspondingly. Village-to-village exchange of the kind that had long characterized South Asian rural life, discussed in earlier chapters, was joined and increasingly overshadowed by longer-distance, more specialized forms of trade, connecting the Gangetic basin to the Indo-Gangetic divide and Punjab in the northwest, to the Deccan and peninsular India in the south, and, via the Ganges and its tributaries, to Bengal and the Bay of Bengal in the east.

Archaeological evidence for this expanding trade includes the wide distribution of certain distinctive pottery styles, including Northern Black Polished Ware, well beyond their immediate points of production, alongside finds of raw materials, semi-precious stones, and finished craft goods at considerable distances from their likely sources. Textual traditions compiled somewhat later, including early Buddhist and Jain literature, describe merchants (sometimes termed setthi or, for particularly wealthy and influential merchants, using titles suggesting high social and economic standing) traveling substantial distances with caravans of goods, and describe cities as sites of active marketplaces where craftspeople sold finished goods and where agricultural produce from the surrounding countryside was exchanged for manufactured items, imported goods, and, increasingly, coined money.

This chapter treats the growth of merchant communities and craft specialization as a genuine and significant development of this period, while resisting the temptation to describe it in the fully developed institutional terms sometimes projected backward from much later periods of South Asian economic history. Later textual and inscriptional evidence, from the Mauryan period onward and especially from the early centuries CE, documents elaborate merchant and craft guilds (often termed shreni), with internal governance structures, standardized practices, and even, in some later periods, a role in local political and financial life extending to acting as depositories or lenders. Evidence for such fully institutionalized guild structures during the sixth and fifth centuries BCE specifically is considerably thinner and more indirect than for later periods; some early textual references to organized craft or merchant groups may reflect genuine sixth- and fifth-century institutions in embryonic form, but others may reflect later compilers describing earlier periods through the lens of institutions more familiar from their own, later era. This textbook therefore presents the growth of specialized, professional merchant and craft activity during this period as well established, while treating claims about fully elaborated guild institutions specifically dated to the sixth or fifth century BCE with appropriate caution, reserving fuller discussion of developed guild institutions for the chapters examining the Mauryan and post-Mauryan economy.

Craft specialization itself, however, is well documented for this period through both archaeological and textual evidence. Excavated sites show evidence of specialized production areas for pottery, metalworking, bead-making, and other crafts, while textual traditions list numerous specific occupations — including smiths, potters, weavers, leatherworkers, carpenters, and many others — organized, at least in later textual memory, along increasingly specific occupational lines. This growing occupational specialization, discussed further in connection with social change in Section 9.9, represents one of the clearest social consequences of the economic transformation this chapter traces: a society in which most households had once produced most of what they needed was giving way, at least within and around the growing towns, to a society of specialized producers dependent on exchange for many of their basic needs.

Section 9.4

Coinage and Exchange

Among the most historically significant, and most chronologically uncertain, developments of this period is the appearance of punch-marked coins — small pieces of silver, and somewhat later copper, stamped with multiple distinct symbols (such as suns, geometric patterns, animals, and other marks whose precise meanings are often uncertain) applied individually with metal punches rather than cast or struck from a single die as in many later coinage traditions. Punch-marked coins constitute South Asia's earliest major coinage tradition. Whether this tradition developed largely independently within South Asia or was stimulated, in part, by contact with Achaemenid and Greek monetary traditions reaching the subcontinent's northwest remains a genuinely debated question among numismatists, and this textbook does not resolve it.

The exact date at which punch-marked coinage began circulating in northern South Asia remains genuinely and substantially debated among numismatists and historians. Some scholars, based on find-contexts, metallurgical analysis, and comparison with datable archaeological layers, argue for an origin as early as the sixth century BCE, broadly contemporary with the urban and political developments traced elsewhere in this chapter; other, more conservative reconstructions place the securely defensible beginnings of regional punch-marked series considerably later, toward the fifth or even fourth century BCE, with earlier exchange continuing to rely on weighed uncoined metal, barter, and other traditional forms of value transfer well into the period this chapter examines. This textbook presents the sixth through fourth centuries BCE as the range within which different scholarly reconstructions place the origins of different regional coin series, rather than endorsing any single date, and students should resist treating any one date as beyond dispute.

Whatever its precise origin date, coined money's broader historical significance for this chapter's argument is considerable, though it too should be stated with appropriate caution regarding causation. Coinage did not suddenly replace older forms of exchange; weighed metal, barter, and other traditional mechanisms almost certainly continued alongside coined money for a considerable period, and coins themselves likely circulated initially in more limited contexts — perhaps particularly urban, commercial, and administrative contexts — before becoming more broadly used across the wider economy. What coinage did provide, where and when it was adopted, was a more portable, more easily verified, and more readily divisible store of value than raw metal or agricultural produce, facilitating exchange across longer distances and among parties who did not necessarily know or trust one another personally, a significant advantage as trade networks expanded beyond the face-to-face relationships of village life.

Coinage also carries clear implications for the growth of political authority examined later in this chapter. The issuing of coinage, even in its earliest and most technologically simple form, generally required some degree of organizational capacity — access to silver or copper supplies, metalworking expertise, and, plausibly, some form of political or institutional sponsorship, though the precise relationship between early punch-marked coins and specific issuing authorities remains difficult to establish with confidence, since many early punch-marked coins do not bear inscriptions identifying a specific ruler or polity in the way many later South Asian coinages do. Coined money, once in circulation, could also facilitate monetized payments and revenue collection as state institutions developed, offering expanding states a potentially more flexible complement to earlier forms of tribute in kind, though the evidence for the earliest punch-marked horizon does not allow historians to demonstrate this fiscal relationship with confidence, since the specific issuing authority behind many early coin series cannot be securely identified. This chapter's later sections on state formation and warfare take up this connection further, though again without claiming that coinage single-handedly caused the growth of stronger territorial states examined in Sections 9.6 through 9.8.

Section 9.5

Towns and Cities of the Ganges Valley

The developments traced in the preceding three sections — agricultural surplus, expanding trade, and, eventually, coined money — converged most visibly in the growth of the region's towns and cities. Archaeological excavation across numerous sites in the middle and upper Gangetic basin documents a recognizable settlement hierarchy emerging during this period: small agricultural villages, considerably more numerous than any other settlement type, continued to dominate the countryside numerically; larger, sometimes fortified towns served as regional centers of craft production, exchange, and, often, political administration; and a smaller number of especially large, heavily fortified cities functioned as capitals of expanding janapadas and mahajanapadas, examined in Section 9.6.

Fortification is among the clearest archaeologically visible markers of this growing settlement hierarchy. Excavations at numerous sites across the middle Gangetic region have revealed substantial ramparts, initially constructed of packed earth and later, at some sites, reinforced or rebuilt in brick or stone, surrounding areas considerably larger than earlier village settlements, along with evidence of ditches, gateways, and other defensive features. The precise motivations behind this fortification building — defense against rival states, protection against river flooding, or the assertion of political and symbolic authority over a defined urban space, or, quite plausibly, some combination of all three — are not always clear from archaeological evidence alone, and historians should avoid assuming that every fortification necessarily reflects active warfare at the moment of its construction, even though warfare, examined in Section 9.8, was certainly a recurring feature of this period's politics.

Within these growing towns and cities, archaeological evidence documents increasingly specialized use of urban space: identifiable craft-production areas, evidence of storage facilities, and, at some sites, features interpreted as marketplaces or public gathering spaces. Roads and routes connecting these urban centers to one another and to their agricultural hinterlands, together with river transport along the Ganges and its major tributaries, formed the physical infrastructure supporting the trade networks examined in Section 9.3. The Ganges and its tributary rivers, in particular, offered a relatively efficient means of moving bulky goods, including agricultural produce and building materials, over considerable distances, and the growth of riverside settlements at strategic points along these waterways — including, as later chapters will examine, the eventual growth of Pataliputra at the confluence of the Ganges and Son rivers — reflects the strategic and economic value of controlling river-borne trade.

It is important to state clearly, at this point, a boundary this chapter deliberately maintains. Several of the specific cities that will loom large in Chapter 10's account of Magadha's rise — Rajagriha, an early Magadhan capital, and Pataliputra, which would later become one of the great cities of ancient South Asia — appear in this chapter only as examples of the broader urban pattern this section describes, not as the centerpieces of a specifically Magadhan political narrative. This chapter is concerned with the general phenomenon of Gangetic urban growth across many janapadas and mahajanapadas; the specific story of how one among them, Magadha, converted urban and economic strength into unmatched political and military power belongs to the next chapter.

Students should also resist assuming that every named settlement mentioned in later Buddhist, Jain, or Brahmanical textual tradition had already reached full urban status by a single, uniform date across this period. Archaeological evidence indicates that settlement growth was gradual and that different sites reached significant size, fortification, and craft specialization at different times across the several centuries this chapter examines; textual traditions compiled later sometimes describe a settled political and urban landscape more uniformly developed than the actual archaeological sequence, spread unevenly across several centuries, likely supports.

Section 9.6

Janapadas and Mahajanapadas

Chapter 7 introduced the transition from jana — a lineage-based tribal grouping identified chiefly by shared descent and kinship — to janapada, literally "the foothold of a jana" or "the territory occupied by a people," reflecting the increasing importance of settled territory, rather than lineage alone, as the basis of political identity and authority. This chapter picks up that development directly, tracing how, across the centuries examined here, some janapadas grew significantly larger, more populous, more economically developed, and more politically centralized than others, eventually being described in later textual tradition using the term mahajanapada — a "great" or particularly prominent janapada.

Later Buddhist and Jain textual traditions, compiled some generations after the period they describe, preserve lists enumerating a specific and often-cited number of sixteen mahajanapadas said to have existed across northern South Asia during this era, including such states as Magadha, Kosala, Vatsa, Avanti, Kashi, Anga, Vajji, and several others, extending in various textual versions from the northwest into the Deccan. This traditional list of sixteen has become a familiar feature of popular accounts of ancient Indian political geography, and students will encounter it repeatedly in secondary sources. This textbook uses the list cautiously rather than treating it as a precise, contemporary political map corresponding exactly to any single moment in the sixth or fifth century BCE. Several considerations support this caution: different textual traditions preserve somewhat different lists, not always agreeing on exactly which sixteen states are meant or on their precise boundaries; the texts recording these lists were compiled later than the period they claim to describe and reflect particular religious and literary purposes rather than systematic contemporary political surveys; and the actual political landscape of the sixth and fifth centuries BCE was almost certainly more fluid, with polities rising, declining, merging, and fragmenting over time, than a single fixed list of sixteen states can adequately represent.

What historians can state with greater confidence, combining archaeological and textual evidence, is the general pattern the "sixteen mahajanapadas" tradition reflects: across the sixth and fifth centuries BCE, the middle and upper Gangetic basin, together with adjoining regions extending toward the northwest and into parts of central India, was organized into a substantial number of competing territorial states of varying size, wealth, and political organization, ranging from smaller, more localized janapadas to larger, more powerful mahajanapadas capable of sustained warfare, extensive taxation, and significant urban development. This general pattern of political fragmentation into multiple competing territorial states, rather than the specific number sixteen or the precise identity of every listed state, is the historically important lesson of this section.

Among the mahajanapadas most frequently discussed in later tradition, several deserve brief introduction here, with fuller treatment reserved for later chapters where appropriate. Kosala, centered on the region around the city of Shravasti in the middle Gangetic basin, developed as a powerful monarchical state and appears repeatedly in Buddhist textual tradition as a major political power of the Buddha's lifetime. Magadha, situated further east and south of the Ganges in the region of modern southern Bihar, began this period as one significant janapada among several but would, as Chapter 10 examines in detail, eventually surpass its neighbors to become the core of a much larger territorial empire. Vatsa, centered on the city of Kaushambi, and Avanti, centered further west around the city of Ujjayini (Ujjain), represent additional significant monarchical states of this period, each appearing in later tradition as significant regional powers whose rulers are remembered, with varying degrees of historical reliability, in Buddhist and Jain literary sources.

Not every mahajanapada, however, developed as a centralized hereditary monarchy. Section 9.7 examines an important alternative political form, the gana-sangha, whose existence alongside the monarchical states just described demonstrates that the Second Urbanization's expanding wealth and territorial competition did not push every polity toward the same institutional outcome.

Section 9.7

Monarchies and Republics

Alongside the hereditary monarchical states discussed in Section 9.6, later Buddhist and other textual traditions, which provide the principal evidence for the internal organization of these polities, describe a second important form of political organization among the janapadas and mahajanapadas of this period: polities governed not by a single hereditary king but by an assembly of leading families or lineages, generally referred to in modern scholarship using the term gana-sangha (combining gana, meaning an assembly, group, or corporation, and sangha, meaning a community or association), and often described in popular accounts, somewhat loosely, as "republics."

Prominent examples cited in later tradition include the Vajjian confederacy, a grouping of several clans, most prominently the Licchavis, centered around the city of Vaishali in the middle Gangetic region, and the Shakya gana-sangha, located in the Himalayan foothills and notable in later Buddhist tradition as the community into which the Buddha was born, and the Malla gana-sanghas, associated with the cities of Kushinagar and Pava. These polities appear in Buddhist textual tradition in particular as governed through regular assemblies of adult male members of leading families, who met to debate and decide matters of common concern, elected or otherwise selected leading officials, and organized collective defense and administration. Later epigraphic evidence from subsequent centuries demonstrates that corporate, nonmonarchical political traditions persisted in parts of South Asia well beyond this period, which lends some general support to the plausibility of the gana-sangha tradition; however, such later evidence cannot simply be projected backward to confirm the detailed sixth- and fifth-century procedures described in Buddhist and Jain textual sources, and historians should treat those specific procedural details as textual tradition rather than independently corroborated fact.

The term "republic," while widely used in both older and more popular modern accounts of these polities, requires careful qualification, and this textbook uses it only with that qualification clearly stated. A gana-sangha of this period bore little resemblance to a modern representative democracy or even to a classical Mediterranean republic in the fullest institutional sense. Political participation within these assemblies was generally restricted to men belonging to a defined set of leading lineages or clans, often specifically warrior (kshatriya) lineages according to later tradition, rather than extending to the broader population of cultivators, artisans, laborers, or women living within the polity's territory. In this sense, a gana-sangha functioned as an oligarchy — rule by a relatively narrow elite — organized through collective assembly deliberation among that elite, rather than as a "republic" in the sense of broad-based popular sovereignty. This textbook therefore uses "gana-sangha" as the primary term for these polities and uses "republic" only in scare quotes or with explicit qualification, to avoid suggesting an anachronistic resemblance to later or distant political traditions that these ancient South Asian assemblies did not actually share.

The relative merits and historical trajectory of monarchical versus gana-sangha political organization should not be read, in either direction, as a simple morality tale of "progress" or "decline." Later historical developments, examined in Chapter 10, saw expanding monarchical states, especially Magadha, gradually absorb or defeat several neighboring gana-sanghas, including the Vajjian confederacy, a pattern sometimes read, in older historical writing, as demonstrating the inherent military or administrative superiority of centralized monarchy over assembly government. Modern historians generally treat this outcome with more caution, noting that centralized monarchies may have possessed certain military and fiscal advantages — more easily mobilized standing revenue, more centralized command in warfare, and fewer internal veto points requiring consensus before action — without concluding that assembly government was inherently weaker, less legitimate, or historically doomed as a form of political organization. Both monarchical and gana-sangha states, this chapter emphasizes, represented genuine, functioning political adaptations to the same underlying economic and social transformations traced in Sections 9.1 through 9.5, and their differing trajectories reflect specific historical circumstances at least as much as any inherent institutional superiority of one form over the other.

Section 9.8

Warfare and State Formation

The growing wealth, population, and territorial ambition of the janapadas and mahajanapadas examined in this chapter's preceding sections inevitably brought them into competition, and frequently into open warfare, with one another. Textual tradition, corroborated in general outline by archaeological evidence for fortification and, at some sites, evidence of destruction layers consistent with conflict, describes recurring warfare among neighboring states over territory, control of trade routes, access to fertile agricultural land, and, increasingly, control of river crossings and riverine transport along the Ganges and its tributaries.

Several interacting factors intensified this competition during the period this chapter examines. Larger populations, supported by the agricultural surplus discussed in Section 9.2, provided expanding states with greater manpower for both agricultural labor and military service. Growing revenue, drawn increasingly from agricultural shares, customs, tolls, and other levies within an increasingly monetized economy of the kind discussed in Section 9.4, gave rulers of larger and wealthier states the resources to maintain larger standing forces, construct and garrison fortifications, and sustain longer military campaigns than had been possible for the smaller, less centralized janas of earlier centuries. Control of strategic river crossings and trade routes offered direct economic advantages through tolls and taxation on merchant traffic, discussed in Section 9.3, giving states a clear incentive to extend and defend their territorial control over such routes.

Archaeological and later textual evidence together suggest that warfare during this period involved a combination of infantry, chariotry inherited in general form from earlier Vedic military tradition, and, increasingly through this period and more fully documented for slightly later periods, the use of war elephants, particularly by states with access to forested regions where elephants could be captured and trained; the precise scale and chronology of elephant warfare's adoption during the sixth and fifth centuries BCE specifically remains less securely documented than for the subsequent Mauryan period examined in later chapters, and this textbook presents it as an emerging, rather than fully established, feature of sixth- and fifth-century Gangetic warfare. Fortification, discussed already in Section 9.5, provided defensive capability against these expanding military forces, while also, as noted there, serving symbolic and administrative functions beyond purely military ones.

This chapter deliberately stops short of narrating the specific military and political history of Magadha's rise to supremacy over its neighbors, including its conflicts with Kosala, its conquest of the Vajjian confederacy, and its absorption or subordination of numerous other janapadas and mahajanapadas across the fifth and fourth centuries BCE. That specific narrative, together with detailed treatment of Magadhan rulers including Bimbisara and Ajatashatru, belongs to Chapter 10, which examines why Magadha in particular proved able to convert the general pattern of interstate warfare and state formation traced in this section into a uniquely successful trajectory toward territorial empire. This chapter's purpose is instead to establish the general pattern of competitive state formation across the wider mahajanapada landscape, a pattern within which Magadha's eventual success, examined next chapter, must be understood as one outcome among several possible outcomes rather than as an inevitable or predetermined result of the economic and political transformations traced here.

Section 9.9

Social Change and Religious Questioning

The economic and political transformations traced across this chapter — agricultural intensification, population growth, expanding trade, coined money, urban growth, and increasingly powerful territorial states — did not leave South Asian society or religious life unchanged. This concluding substantive section examines, in general terms, the social consequences of these transformations and the broader intellectual and religious atmosphere they helped to create, while deliberately stopping short of the detailed treatment of specific religious movements reserved for Chapters 11 through 13.

Growing craft specialization and urban commerce, discussed in Section 9.3, both reflected and intensified processes of social differentiation already underway in the later Vedic period examined in Chapter 7. The varna framework inherited from Vedic tradition, together with the increasingly specific occupational categories associated with urban craft production, combined to produce a more elaborately differentiated social landscape than the comparatively simple lineage-based societies of earlier centuries. Wealthy merchants, whose economic significance grew alongside the trade networks examined in Section 9.3, occupied an increasingly prominent, though not always straightforwardly high-status, position within this changing social order; merchant wealth did not automatically translate into the ritual status traditionally associated with Brahmanical hierarchy, creating potential tensions between economic power and inherited ritual status that some scholars connect to the receptiveness some urban and mercantile communities showed toward new religious movements offering alternative sources of status and meaning, discussed further below.

Urban life itself, distinct in important ways from the village- and lineage-centered social world of earlier centuries, brought together people of varied backgrounds, occupations, and, increasingly, geographic origins within a shared, densely populated space, weakening, to some degree, the close kin-based social bonds that had structured janas and early janapadas. Increased mobility, associated with expanding trade and, plausibly, with the greater ease of travel and resettlement coined money and developed trade routes afforded, further loosened the hold of purely local, kin-based social identity for at least some segments of the growing urban population.

Against this backdrop of economic transformation, growing social differentiation, and loosening traditional social bonds, the sixth and fifth centuries BCE witnessed a remarkable flourishing of religious and philosophical questioning across the Gangetic region. A wide range of wandering ascetics, teachers, and religious communities, collectively described using the term shramana (from a root meaning "one who exerts effort," referring generally to renunciant seekers who withdrew from settled household and ritual life in pursuit of spiritual liberation through their own effort, discipline, and insight; many shramana traditions operated outside, rejected, or challenged aspects of Brahmanical ritual authority, while the wider renunciatory environment of this period also interacted with, and influenced, developing Brahmanical traditions of the kind examined in Chapter 13), proposed varied and sometimes sharply competing answers to fundamental questions concerning the nature of the self, the workings of karma and rebirth, the possibility and nature of liberation, and the proper conduct of a meaningful life. Among these many shramana teachers and traditions, this period saw the emergence of the teachings later systematized as Buddhism, associated with the figure historical tradition remembers as the Buddha, and the teachings later systematized as Jainism, associated with the figure historical tradition remembers as Mahavira, together with numerous other traditions, some of which, including the Ajivikas, achieved considerable following in this period without surviving as continuously practiced traditions into the present.

Historians debate, and this textbook presents as a genuine open question rather than settled fact, exactly how directly the economic and social transformations traced in this chapter should be understood as "causing" this religious and philosophical ferment. A plausible general connection can be drawn: growing urban wealth provided the economic surplus and leisure that could support wandering ascetics and their followers; growing social mobility and the loosening of traditional kin-based identity may have made some urban and mercantile populations more receptive to teachers offering paths to status, meaning, or liberation not dependent on inherited ritual position; and the general atmosphere of change, competition, and instability associated with interstate warfare and rapid economic transformation may have encouraged broader questioning of inherited assumptions about the proper order of society and the cosmos. At the same time, historians caution against treating new religious movements as simple, mechanical products of economic change, since religious and philosophical innovation also draws on intellectual traditions, individual insight, and religious experience that cannot be reduced entirely to underlying economic or social causes. This chapter therefore presents the connection between the Second Urbanization and the shramana movements as a significant and well-supported contextual relationship, explored in far greater depth in Chapters 11 through 13, rather than as a fully sufficient causal explanation on its own.

Section 9.10

Review and Historical Synthesis

This chapter has traced the Second Urbanization of northern South Asia as a genuine, multicausal historical transformation rather than the product of any single technological, economic, or political factor. Several threads developed separately across the preceding nine sections converge here into a single interpretive foundation for Chapters 10 through 13.

The agrarian argument of Section 9.2 established the essential material foundation on which every other development in this chapter rests: expanding cultivation, growing use of iron tools alongside other, equally important changes in land clearance and cultivation practice, the growing importance and intensification of rice cultivation in suitable Gangetic environments, and consequent population growth together produced an agricultural surplus substantial enough to support growing numbers of people not directly engaged in food production. This chapter has repeatedly cautioned against reducing this development to iron technology alone, insisting instead on a genuinely multicausal agrarian transformation.

The commercial argument of Sections 9.3 and 9.4 traced how this growing surplus fed, and was in turn encouraged by, expanding trade networks, increasingly specialized craft production, and, eventually, the adoption of coined money, whose precise origin date remains a subject of continuing scholarly debate but whose broader significance for facilitating exchange across longer distances and among less personally familiar trading partners is well established. This chapter has cautioned against projecting fully developed later guild institutions backward onto the sixth and fifth centuries BCE specifically, while affirming that genuine craft and commercial specialization was a well-documented feature of this period.

The urban argument of Section 9.5 made this economic transformation spatially visible: growing settlement size, increasingly elaborate fortification, and a recognizable hierarchy running from village through town to city across the middle and upper Gangetic basin, with settlement growth proceeding gradually and unevenly across several centuries rather than as a single, uniform, dateable event.

The political argument of Sections 9.6 through 9.8 traced how this same convergence of population, wealth, trade, and urban growth fed the ambitions of increasingly powerful territorial states, developing from the janapadas of Chapter 7 into the larger mahajanapadas of this chapter, organized through varied political forms ranging from centralized hereditary monarchy to gana-sangha assembly government among leading lineages, and increasingly drawn into competitive, often violent, contests for territory, trade routes, and revenue. This chapter has cautioned against treating the traditional list of sixteen mahajanapadas as a precise contemporary map, against describing gana-sanghas as modern-style democratic republics, and against allowing Magadha's eventual triumph, examined fully in the next chapter, to be read backward into this chapter as though it were predetermined from the outset.

The social and intellectual argument of Section 9.9 completed this chapter's account by tracing how these economic, urban, and political transformations reshaped South Asian society — intensifying occupational specialization and social differentiation, loosening some traditional kin-based bonds within the new urban environment, and creating a social and intellectual atmosphere within which the shramana movements, including the traditions that would become Buddhism and Jainism, found fertile ground, without this chapter reducing those movements to simple products of economic change or anticipating the detailed treatment Chapters 11 through 13 will provide.

Taken together, these five interacting arguments answer this chapter's central question: cities, coined money, larger states, and new forms of religious questioning emerged together in first-millennium-BCE northern South Asia not because any one of them caused all the others, but because agricultural intensification, population growth, denser settlement, expanding exchange, craft specialization, monetization, and increasingly ambitious territorial politics reinforced one another in a genuine historical convergence. Chapter 10 now follows one particular thread of this convergence to its most consequential outcome, explaining why Magadha, among all the competing janapadas and mahajanapadas this chapter has surveyed, proved able to convert these shared regional transformations into unprecedented and eventually empire-building territorial power. Chapters 11 through 13 then follow a second thread, examining in full depth the specific teachings of the Buddha, of Mahavira and the wider shramana movements, and of the Upanishadic thinkers who, together, transformed South Asian religious and philosophical life amid the social world this chapter has described.

Material Source Window

The Narhan Hoard and Early Punch-Marked Coins

Evidence type: A hoard of roughly fifty silver pieces, including unmarked silver blanks and early punch-marked issues, discovered at the archaeological site of Narhan in the middle Gangetic region in 1985 · Region: The middle Gangetic basin · Recovered through: Surface exploration at the site rather than recovery from a sealed, stratified excavation layer; the pieces were reportedly found together within a small ceramic container, itself associated with red-ware and black-slipped pottery types characteristic of the wider period this chapter examines

Context: Punch-marked coins are typically small, irregularly shaped pieces of silver (and later copper) bearing distinct symbols stamped individually onto the metal's surface with separate punches, rather than struck all at once from a single prepared die, as became standard in many later coinage traditions, including later South Asian dynastic coinages. Because early punch-marked coins generally lack inscriptions naming a specific ruler, dynasty, or polity, numismatists must date and attribute them chiefly through find-context, metallurgical analysis, symbol comparison across coin series, and comparison with better-dated coinages from neighboring regions, rather than through direct textual identification. The Narhan hoard's importance to numismatists derives not from a controlled stratigraphic excavation context, which this surface find does not supply, but from the character of the assemblage itself: the presence together of unworked silver blanks, archaic local punch-marked forms, and coins resembling early Magadhan issues allows historians to reconstruct a plausible sequence of stages in the local development of coined money, from unmarked metal toward increasingly standardized punch-marked issues.

Description of the evidence: The hoard combines plain, unmarked silver blanks with early, sparsely marked local punch-marked pieces and coins comparable to early Magadhan types, all recovered together in a single small ceramic container associated with pottery types typical of this period's Gangetic sites.

Analysis: This kind of mixed assemblage — blanks alongside developed coins — offers historians a rare glimpse of what may be intermediate stages between weighed uncoined silver and fully punch-marked currency, supporting the general picture, discussed in Section 9.4, of monetization as a gradual process rather than a single sudden innovation. Because the hoard was recovered through surface exploration rather than a sealed excavation layer, however, its precise stratigraphic date cannot be established with the same confidence a controlled excavation would provide, and its absolute chronology remains, like that of punch-marked coinage generally, a matter of debate. Historians must therefore treat the Narhan hoard as valuable evidence for the character and sequence of early monetization while remaining appropriately cautious about assigning it a precise calendar date.

Historical Debate

What Caused the Second Urbanization?

The question: Which factor or factors should historians treat as most decisive in explaining the growth of cities and larger territorial states across northern South Asia during the first millennium BCE — agricultural and technological change, expanding trade and monetization, or political and military competition among states?

Interpretation A — Agrarian and technological primacy: Some historians emphasize agricultural intensification, including the spread of iron tools, expanded land clearance, and the growing importance of rice cultivation, as the fundamental precondition without which none of this chapter's other developments — trade, coinage, urban growth, or state formation — could have occurred. On this view, the agricultural surplus discussed in Section 9.2 is the base on which every other development in this chapter rests, and historians should look first to changes in agrarian technology, land use, and productivity when explaining the Second Urbanization.
Interpretation B — Commercial and political primacy: Other historians argue that agricultural surplus alone cannot explain why that surplus was converted into cities, coined money, and powerful states rather than simply supporting a larger but still dispersed rural population, as occurred in many agriculturally productive regions of the world without producing comparable urbanization. On this view, the decisive factors were the growth of long-distance trade networks, the political ambitions of rulers and assemblies eager to tax and control that trade and the agricultural surplus alike, and the competitive dynamic among states that rewarded those most able to concentrate population, wealth, and military capacity in fortified urban centers.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on the same body of archaeological evidence — settlement surveys, fortification remains, pottery sequences, and coin finds — together with later textual traditions describing political and economic life, but they weigh the causal importance of agrarian versus commercial and political factors differently.

Why it matters: This textbook resolves the debate by declining to name a single primary cause, treating the interpretations as complementary rather than competing: agricultural surplus was a necessary precondition, but its conversion into cities and states required the additional, interacting processes of trade, craft specialization, monetization, and political ambition that Sections 9.3 through 9.8 examine. Neither factor alone is sufficient; both together are necessary.

Student task: Using evidence from Sections 9.2 through 9.8, construct an argument for which interpretation you find more persuasive, or explain why you think the evidence supports treating both sets of factors as equally necessary and mutually reinforcing.

Historical Vocabulary
Second Urbanization

The Second Urbanization refers to the growth of cities and large fortified towns across northern South Asia, chiefly the middle and upper Gangetic basin, during roughly the eighth through fourth centuries BCE. The term deliberately invokes the "first" urbanization of the Mature Harappan civilization examined in Chapters 3 and 4, but historians use it to name a general historical pattern — dense settlement, craft specialization, and expanding exchange culminating in urban growth — rather than to claim any direct continuity, institutional inheritance, or conscious imitation connecting the two episodes. Centuries separated the Harappan decline from the Gangetic urban growth, the geography differed, and no evidence links Harappan administrative or written traditions to the later Gangetic world.

Students should use "Second Urbanization" as a precise historical label for this specific Gangetic phenomenon, not as a general synonym for "urbanization" applicable to any period of South Asian history, and should remain alert to the danger of assuming that because historians use a numbered label, the two urbanizations must share more in common than the general pattern the term is meant to capture.

Historical Vocabulary
Janapada and Mahajanapada

Janapada, introduced in Chapter 7, denotes a territorially defined polity, reflecting the increasing importance of settled land, rather than lineage alone, as the basis of political community during and after the later Vedic period. Mahajanapada, a term drawn from later Buddhist and Jain textual tradition, denotes a particularly large, populous, or powerful janapada. The traditional enumeration of "sixteen mahajanapadas," while a useful general indication of significant political fragmentation and competition across northern South Asia during the sixth and fifth centuries BCE, should not be treated as a precise, universally agreed, single-moment political map; different texts list somewhat different states, and the actual political landscape was almost certainly more fluid than any fixed list can represent.

Students encountering the "sixteen mahajanapadas" in other sources should treat the number itself as a traditional textual convention worth knowing, while focusing their historical understanding on the underlying pattern: a substantial number of competing territorial states of varying size and political organization, some monarchical and some organized as gana-sanghas, contending for territory, trade, and revenue across this period.

Multiple Perspectives

Living through a Changing Order: Perspectives on the Second Urbanization

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns historians have identified across archaeological and later textual evidence. They are not quotations from any single named historical individual.

From the perspective of a rice-cultivating household in the Gangetic floodplain: Growing demand for agricultural surplus, whether from expanding local markets or from the tax collectors of an increasingly assertive janapada, likely meant both new opportunities to exchange surplus grain for coined money or manufactured goods and new pressures to produce more than the household's own subsistence required, particularly as territorial states came to depend more heavily on agricultural revenue.
From the perspective of an urban merchant: Expanding trade networks and, eventually, coined money offered new opportunities for accumulating wealth and social influence not directly tied to inherited ritual status, even as that wealth did not always translate smoothly into the kind of status traditionally reserved for Brahmanical or kshatriya lineages, a tension that some scholars connect to merchant receptiveness toward new religious movements offering alternative paths to meaning and standing.
From the perspective of a member of a leading lineage within a gana-sangha assembly: Participation in collective assembly governance offered a share in political decision-making unavailable to ordinary cultivators and artisans, but also required navigating consensus and rivalry among fellow leading families, and, increasingly across this period, contending with the military and diplomatic pressure of larger, more centralized monarchical neighbors.
From the perspective of a wandering shramana renunciant: The growing wealth, social differentiation, and political competition of the towns and courts offered both potential patronage from wealthy merchants and rulers and a vivid setting in which questions of karma, rebirth, discipline, the nature of the self, and the possibility of liberation could be posed and debated before new audiences, even as different shramana teachers, including those whose traditions Chapters 11 and 12 examine, disagreed sharply among themselves about the proper answers to these shared questions.
Continuity and Change Over Time

From Janapada to Larger Territorial State

What Changed

  • Settlement patterns shifted from predominantly dispersed villages toward a recognizable hierarchy including fortified towns and cities
  • Exchange shifted from predominantly local, kin- and village-based transactions toward longer-distance trade increasingly mediated by specialized merchants and, eventually, coined money
  • Political organization shifted from smaller lineage-based janas and early janapadas toward larger, more centralized mahajanapadas, whether monarchical or gana-sangha in form
  • Social organization grew more differentiated, with expanding craft specialization and occupational distinction layered onto the varna framework inherited from Vedic tradition
  • Religious and philosophical life diversified with the emergence of shramana movements offering alternatives to established Brahmanical ritual authority

What Stayed the Same

  • Village-based agriculture remained the overwhelming numerical and economic base of society even at the height of urban growth
  • Brahmanical ritual authority and Vedic textual tradition continued to command significant social and religious weight alongside newer shramana movements
  • Kinship and lineage identity, while somewhat loosened within the new urban environment, continued to structure much of social and political life, including within gana-sangha assemblies restricted to leading lineages
  • Interstate warfare and competition for land and resources, already present among the janas and early janapadas of Chapter 7, continued and intensified rather than representing an entirely new phenomenon

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both change and continuity prevents historians from treating the Second Urbanization as a total rupture with the earlier Vedic world traced in Chapters 5 through 7, while still appreciating the genuinely transformative economic, political, and social consequences of the developments this chapter has traced.

Cause and Effect

The Convergence behind the Second Urbanization

Long-term causes

Gradual eastward agricultural expansion into the middle Gangetic basin across the later Vedic period, supported by increasing iron-tool availability, land clearance, and the growing importance and intensification of rice cultivation in suitable environments.

Short-term causes

Rapid population growth, intensifying trade and craft specialization, the adoption of coined money, and increasingly assertive political authorities eager to tax and organize this growing economic activity, all converging within a few centuries.

Effect

The growth of a recognizable settlement hierarchy including fortified towns and cities, the consolidation of janapadas into larger mahajanapadas under varied political forms, intensifying interstate warfare, and a changing social and religious atmosphere receptive to new philosophical and religious questioning.

This textbook's response

This chapter treats the Second Urbanization as a genuine historical convergence of agrarian, commercial, political, and social processes, setting the stage for Chapter 10's account of Magadhan empire-building and Chapters 11 through 13's account of Buddhism, Jainism, and Upanishadic philosophy.

Historical complexity: No single factor discussed in this chapter operated independently of the others; agricultural surplus enabled trade and urban growth, trade and urban growth generated revenue that funded state expansion, and state expansion in turn encouraged further agricultural settlement and taxation, in a mutually reinforcing cycle rather than a simple one-directional chain of causation.

Everyday Life and Social History

Daily Life amid Urban and Commercial Growth

For most people living across the middle Gangetic basin during this period, daily life almost certainly continued to center on agricultural labor within a village setting, largely unchanged in its basic rhythms by the urban and commercial developments this chapter has traced, even as those developments increasingly shaped the wider economic and political context within which village life took place — through taxation, through opportunities to sell surplus produce in nearby markets, and through the growing presence of state authority claiming a share of agricultural output.

For the smaller but growing population living within or near the new towns and cities, daily life took on a different character: craftspeople increasingly specialized in particular trades rather than producing a wide range of household goods themselves; merchants organized the movement of goods across considerable distances and managed relationships of credit and exchange with other merchants and with producers; and urban residents encountered, in the streets and marketplaces of the growing towns, a far wider range of occupations, backgrounds, and, increasingly, religious teachers and ideas than village life had typically offered. Later textual tradition, particularly early Buddhist literature, offers vivid, though not always historically precise, glimpses of this urban world — bustling marketplaces, wealthy merchant households, and wandering ascetics debating philosophical questions in public spaces — that historians read carefully as suggestive evidence of urban social texture even where specific details cannot be independently verified.

Women's experience of this changing order remains only partially recoverable from the available evidence. Later textual tradition suggests that women's formal political and ritual authority remained generally more limited than men's within both monarchical and gana-sangha political structures, and that women's economic roles varied by social status and occupation, with some evidence of women's participation in craft production and trade alongside continued emphasis, in Brahmanical textual tradition, on women's roles within household and family life. Historians should avoid assuming uniform experience across the wide range of social positions — from wealthy merchant households to cultivating families to the households of craftspeople and laborers — that this chapter's economic transformation encompassed.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced the Second Urbanization of northern South Asia, examining how agricultural intensification, population growth, expanding trade, coined money, urban growth, and increasingly powerful territorial states converged across the eighth through fourth centuries BCE to transform the middle and upper Gangetic basin. It has consistently emphasized that this transformation, whatever superficial parallels it bears to the much earlier Mature Harappan urbanization examined in Chapters 3 and 4, represents an independent historical development rather than a revival or continuation of that earlier urban tradition.

The chapter began by defining the Second Urbanization and situating it chronologically, using Northern Black Polished Ware pottery as one useful, if imperfect, archaeological marker for this period's expanding settlement and exchange networks. It then examined the agrarian foundations of this transformation, emphasizing that iron technology, while genuinely significant, worked alongside the growing importance of rice cultivation, land clearance, and population growth rather than serving as a single, sufficient cause of agricultural intensification.

The chapter traced the growth of trade and craft specialization, cautioning against projecting fully developed later guild institutions backward onto this specific period, and examined the emergence of punch-marked coinage, whose precise origin date remains genuinely debated among historians and numismatists but whose broader significance for facilitating longer-distance, less personally mediated exchange is well established.

The chapter then made this economic transformation spatially visible, tracing the growth of a settlement hierarchy running from village through town to city, marked archaeologically by increasing settlement size and elaborate fortification, while cautioning that this growth proceeded gradually and unevenly across several centuries rather than as a single, uniform event.

Turning to political history, the chapter traced the consolidation of janapadas into larger mahajanapadas, examining the traditional list of sixteen mahajanapadas with appropriate caution regarding its precision as a contemporary political map, and comparing monarchical states such as Kosala and Magadha with gana-sangha polities such as the Vajjian confederacy, the Shakyas, and the Mallas, using the term "republic" only with explicit qualification given the restricted, elite-lineage-based nature of gana-sangha political participation.

The chapter examined the recurring warfare among these competing states, tracing its connection to growing population, revenue, and control of strategic trade routes and river crossings, while deliberately stopping short of narrating Magadha's specific rise to supremacy, a story reserved for Chapter 10.

Finally, the chapter examined the social and religious consequences of this transformation: growing occupational specialization and social differentiation, the loosening of some traditional kin-based bonds within the new urban environment, and the flourishing of shramana religious and philosophical movements, including the traditions that would become Buddhism and Jainism, amid this changing social and economic order. The chapter presented the connection between economic transformation and religious questioning as a significant, well-supported contextual relationship rather than a simple, sufficient causal explanation, reserving detailed treatment of these movements for Chapters 11 through 13.

Throughout, this chapter has emphasized a central methodological lesson consistent with this textbook's treatment of every major transformation: the Second Urbanization emerged from the interaction of multiple, mutually reinforcing processes — agrarian, commercial, urban, political, and social — rather than from any single technological or political cause. This multicausal convergence, occurring specifically across the Gangetic north during the first millennium BCE rather than uniformly across the whole subcontinent, created both the historical setting and the underlying tensions from which Chapter 10's account of Magadhan empire and Chapters 11 through 13's account of new religious and philosophical movements will grow.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Second Urbanization
The growth of cities and large fortified towns across northern South Asia, chiefly the Gangetic basin, during roughly the eighth through fourth centuries BCE, understood as an independent historical development rather than a continuation of Mature Harappan urbanism.
Settlement hierarchy
A recognizable pattern of differently sized and functioning settlements — villages, towns, and cities — within a region, reflecting growing economic and political complexity.
Northern Black Polished Ware
A distinctive, finely finished pottery style widely distributed across northern South Asia during the middle centuries of the first millennium BCE, used cautiously as a chronological marker for this period's urban growth and exchange networks.
Janapada
A territorially defined polity, reflecting the increasing importance of settled land rather than lineage alone as the basis of political community, introduced in Chapter 7 and developed further in this chapter.
Mahajanapada
A term from later Buddhist and Jain tradition for a particularly large or powerful janapada; the traditional list of "sixteen mahajanapadas" should be used cautiously rather than as a precise contemporary political map.
Gana-sangha
A polity governed through assembly deliberation among a defined set of leading lineages or clans, rather than by a single hereditary monarch; often loosely termed a "republic," though political participation was generally restricted to an elite, making it more accurately described as an oligarchy organized through collective assembly.
Oligarchy
Rule by a relatively narrow elite, used in this chapter to qualify the term "republic" as applied to gana-sangha polities.
Punch-marked coin
South Asia's earliest major coinage tradition, typically of silver and later copper, bearing symbols individually stamped onto the metal; its origin date and the question of independent versus externally stimulated development both remain genuinely debated.
Monetization
The growing use of coined money in exchange, taxation, and other transactions, understood in this chapter as a gradual process occurring alongside, rather than immediately replacing, older forms of exchange such as barter and weighed metal.
Craft specialization
The development of skilled, often occupationally specific production of particular goods, intensifying during this period alongside urban growth and trade.
Guild (shreni)
An organized association of merchants or craftspeople, well documented for later periods of South Asian history; evidence for fully institutionalized guilds specifically dated to the sixth and fifth centuries BCE is thinner and should be treated cautiously.
Fortification
Defensive earthen, brick, or stone structures surrounding a settlement, among the clearest archaeological markers of growing settlement size and political organization during this period.
Surplus
Agricultural or other production beyond what is needed for a household's or community's own subsistence, essential for supporting non-food-producing urban populations.
Revenue
Income, typically drawn from taxation of agriculture, trade, or other economic activity, on which expanding states increasingly depended to fund administration, fortification, and warfare.
Rice cultivation
A long-established form of agriculture in South Asia, particularly well suited to the wetter middle and lower Gangetic basin, whose growing importance and intensification during this period contributed to the region's agricultural surplus and population growth, though specific cultivation techniques are difficult to establish archaeologically with precision.
Iron technology
The use of iron tools, including axes and plowshares, which contributed to but did not single-handedly cause the agricultural intensification and urban growth examined in this chapter.
Trade route
An established path, overland or riverine, along which goods, and often people and ideas, moved across considerable distances, connecting the Gangetic basin to neighboring regions.
Riverine transport
The movement of goods and people along rivers, particularly the Ganges and its tributaries, offering an efficient means of moving bulky goods and encouraging the growth of strategically located riverside settlements.
Shramana
A wandering ascetic or renunciant seeker pursuing spiritual liberation through personal effort and discipline, often in tension with Brahmanical ritual authority; the broader category within which the traditions later systematized as Buddhism and Jainism developed.
Renunciation
The deliberate withdrawal from settled household, family, and ritual life in pursuit of spiritual liberation, a practice associated with shramana traditions of this period.
Social differentiation
The process by which a society develops increasingly distinct and specialized social, occupational, and status categories, intensified in this period by growing craft specialization and urban commerce layered onto the inherited varna framework.
Kosala
A prominent monarchical mahajanapada of the middle Gangetic region, centered on Shravasti, discussed in Section 9.6.
Vatsa
A mahajanapada centered on the city of Kaushambi, discussed in Section 9.6.
Avanti
A mahajanapada centered on the city of Ujjayini (Ujjain), discussed in Section 9.6.
Vajjian confederacy
A prominent gana-sangha grouping of clans, most notably the Licchavis, centered around Vaishali, discussed in Section 9.7 as an example of assembly-based government.
Shakya gana-sangha
A gana-sangha polity in the Himalayan foothills, notable in later Buddhist tradition as the community into which the Buddha was born, discussed in Section 9.7.
Malla gana-sanghas
Gana-sangha polities associated with the cities of Kushinagar and Pava, discussed in Section 9.7.
Rajagriha
An early Magadhan capital and example of a fortified early historic city, mentioned in this chapter with its full political significance reserved for Chapter 10.

A settlement at the confluence of the Ganges and Son rivers that grew into a major city, mentioned here as an example of urban growth linked to river-borne trade, with its full history reserved for later chapters.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has asked students to hold several moving parts in mind at once: expanding fields and growing populations, busier roads and river routes, new coins passing from hand to hand, fortified towns rising along the Ganges, kings and assemblies alike reaching for greater revenue and authority, and, amid all of this change, wandering teachers proposing radically new answers to old questions about suffering, self, and liberation. No single thread in that list explains the others. Together, they explain a transformation that reshaped northern South Asia within a few centuries and set in motion historical processes this textbook will follow for many chapters to come.

The habits of mind this chapter has asked students to practice — resisting single-cause explanations, distinguishing archaeological evidence from later textual tradition, using loaded terms such as "republic" only with careful qualification, and respecting the boundary between what a chapter's assigned purpose requires and what belongs to a neighboring chapter — are not incidental to this chapter's subject. They are exactly the skills historians bring to any period where economic, political, and religious change unfold together, and they will recur throughout this textbook's treatment of every subsequent transformation, from the rise of the Mauryan Empire to the growth of colonial cities to the urbanization of contemporary South Asia.

Students who understand that cities, coined money, larger states, and new religious movements emerged together, through mutual reinforcement rather than simple one-directional causation, are well prepared for what follows. Chapter 10 now turns to a single, consequential thread within this chapter's wider story: the specific historical process by which Magadha, one competitive mahajanapada among many introduced here, converted the shared regional transformations of this chapter into the foundations of territorial empire. Chapters 11 through 13 then turn to a second thread, examining in full depth the lives, teachings, and historical significance of the Buddha, of Mahavira and the wider shramana movements, and of the Upanishadic thinkers whose questioning of inherited Brahmanical assumptions would permanently reshape South Asian religious and philosophical life.

The walled towns, coined silver, and competing assemblies and courts this chapter has described did not, by themselves, produce an empire or a new religion. But they created the dense, restless, economically dynamic world within which both would emerge — a world this textbook now follows in two directions at once, political and religious, each rooted in the same Gangetic transformation this chapter has traced.
