Part VIII — Islamic Frontiers and the Delhi Sultanate
38

Islam, Sindh, and the Early Muslim Presence

Indian History: South Asia from the Indus Civilization to the Present

Chapter Opening

Long before any Arab army reached the lower Indus, ships were already crossing the Arabian Sea between the Persian Gulf, the coast of Makran, the harbors of Sindh and Gujarat, the Konkan, Malabar, and Sri Lanka. They carried textiles, spices, aromatics, timber, metals, and horses, and they carried people: sailors, brokers, shipowners, and families who settled for a season or for a lifetime. When the faith of Islam took shape in Arabia in the seventh century, it entered a maritime world that was already old, and Muslim merchants moved along these same routes. This chapter asks how Islam became part of South Asian history, and its answer begins not with a single battle but with the sea.

Chapter 37 examined the agrarian, temple-centered, and commercial transformations of early medieval South Asia, including the ports and Indian Ocean exchange that connected its coasts to the wider world. This chapter opens Part VIII, which follows the establishment of Muslim communities and states in South Asia through the Delhi Sultanate. It is a bridge chapter. It must show that the Muslim presence in South Asia began by several routes at different times, and that these routes should not be collapsed into a single story of invasion.

Popular memory often compresses this history into a date: 711, the year in which an Arab army under Muhammad ibn Qasim captured the port of Debal in Sindh. The conquest of Sindh is a genuine and important event, and this chapter narrates it. But 711 is not "the date Islam arrived in India." Muslim merchants entered western Indian Ocean networks during the first Islamic century independently of the conquest of Sindh. Some traditions place their presence on Indian coasts before 711, but securely documented settled communities become clearer only in the eighth through tenth centuries. The Arab conquest of Sindh did not produce rapid or uniform conversion. Large Muslim communities in Sindh developed over generations. Elsewhere, on the Gujarat, Konkan, and Malabar coasts, Muslim communities took root where no Muslim army had established a state at all.

The chapter therefore keeps three processes distinct but connected: maritime contact and merchant settlement; political conquest in Sindh; and the gradual formation of Muslim communities and regional Muslim polities. Each has its own chronology, its own evidence, and its own geography. The merchant history rests on Arabic geographers, travel accounts, scattered inscriptions, and later community traditions. The conquest history depends on Arabic historians of the ninth and tenth centuries and on a Persian work of the thirteenth century, the Chachnama, whose reliability is a central problem of this chapter. The history of conversion and community formation must be assembled from fragments, because no source records it directly.

The chapter also resists several teleologies. Raja Dahir should not be read as the ruler of a modern Indian nation defending it against outsiders, and Muhammad ibn Qasim should be neither a civilizing hero nor a uniquely destructive conqueror. Sindh was not "Hindu" in a single sense, since its society included Brahmanical, Buddhist, mercantile, pastoral, and other communities. The Arab conquest of Sindh did not lead inevitably to the Ghaznavids, the Ghurids, or the Delhi Sultanate, which arose centuries later from different political systems and are the subject of Chapters 39 and 40.

Finally, the chapter treats Sindh as a place with its own history. It was a frontier province of the Umayyad and Abbasid worlds, and it remained connected to the commercial and cultural networks of South Asia. It became a site of exchange in languages, texts, ideas, and goods. Within a few generations of the conquest, its direct ties to the caliphs weakened and regional Muslim rulers emerged. By the end of the chapter, students should be able to explain why Muslim political rule in Sindh and Muslim merchant settlement on the western coast produced such different patterns of religious, social, and political change.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why the Muslim presence in South Asia began through multiple routes and why 711 should not be described as the date Islam arrived in India.
  2. Trace the continuity of Indian Ocean and Arabian Sea trade from the pre-Islamic period through the seventh and eighth centuries.
  3. Distinguish among the terms Arab, Muslim, and Islamic, and explain why these categories are not interchangeable.
  4. Describe the political, religious, and economic society of Sindh before the Arab conquest, including its ports, rivers, rulers, and religious institutions.
  5. Analyze the development of the Arab frontier in Makran and the causes of the Umayyad invasion of Sindh, including the limits of the piracy explanation.
  6. Narrate the principal stages of the conquest of Sindh from 711 to 715 without reducing it to heroic biography.
  7. Evaluate the Chachnama as a historical source, comparing it with the account of al-Baladhuri and other evidence.
  8. Assess how Arab rulers governed a largely non-Muslim population through taxation, negotiated surrender, and the retention of local institutions.
  9. Interpret conversion as a gradual, uneven, and multicausal process and distinguish political conquest from social and religious change.
  10. Trace Sindh's transition from caliphal province to regional Muslim polities centered on Mansura and Multan.
  11. Compare Muslim community formation in Sindh with that on the Gujarat, Konkan, and Malabar coasts.
  12. Evaluate the evidence for cultural, linguistic, and intellectual exchange in proportion to what the sources can support.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. In what ways did the early Muslim presence in South Asia develop through routes other than armed conquest?
  2. How did the rise of Islam change, and how did it fail to change, the older commercial world of the Arabian Sea?
  3. What was Sindh like as a society before 711, and why does that matter for understanding the conquest?
  4. Why did the Umayyad caliphate invade Sindh, and what do the explanations offered by the sources reveal about their authors?
  5. How can historians use a text such as the Chachnama responsibly when it was composed centuries after the events it describes?
  6. How did Arab rulers govern a society that was overwhelmingly non-Muslim, and what did they leave in place?
  7. Why did Muslim political rule in Sindh not produce rapid mass conversion?
  8. Why did Muslim communities flourish on western coasts where no Muslim state had been established?
  9. What does the history of early Sindh show about the difference between political conquest and religious or social change?

Historical Context

By the seventh century CE, South Asia was a region of many states and regional cultures rather than a single political system. Chapter 30 examined the end of Gupta supremacy and the reign of Harsha, and Chapters 31 through 36 traced the Chalukyas, Rashtrakutas, Pallavas, Cholas, Palas, Pratiharas, Rajput polities, and the Himalayan and island kingdoms. Chapter 37 analyzed the shared structural features of this early medieval order: expanding agrarian settlement, land grants, temple economies, brahmin and merchant communities, and ports connected to Indian Ocean commerce. The northwest and the lower Indus, where this chapter begins, were part of that world but also bordered the Iranian plateau and Central Asia, the region examined in Chapters 14 and 20 as a crossroads.

Beyond South Asia, the seventh century brought one of the largest political transformations of the premodern world. From the Arabian Peninsula, the community led by the Prophet Muhammad (died 632) expanded within a generation into Syria, Egypt, Iraq, and Iran, destroying the Sasanian Empire and challenging the Byzantine Empire. The Umayyad caliphate (661–750) governed this expanding territory from Damascus, and its eastern frontier regions were administered through the governors of Iraq. The Abbasid caliphate (750 onward) later shifted the center of the Islamic world to Baghdad. These events matter here because they brought the political power of an expanding empire to the western edge of South Asia.

The evidence for this chapter is uneven. For the maritime world, historians use Arabic and Persian geographies and travel accounts (including those of al-Masudi, Ibn Khurdadhbih, and the compilation known as Akhbar al-Sin wa'l-Hind), a limited number of inscriptions on stone and copper plate, coins, and archaeological excavation at Banbhore, the site often identified with Debal, on the Sindh coast. For the conquest, the principal sources are Arabic historians, especially al-Baladhuri's ninth-century Futuh al-Buldan and al-Tabari's chronicle, and the Persian Chachnama. No Sindhi narrative of the conquest survives from the eighth century, so students must remember whose voices are present and whose are absent. Chinese and Indian sources supply a partial picture of pre-conquest Sindh.

Students should also keep in mind the geographic and chronological limits of this chapter. Chapter 37 owns the full analysis of Indian Ocean commerce, and this chapter uses it only to explain Muslim settlement and community formation. Chapter 39 owns the Ghaznavids and Ghurids, and this chapter ends at the threshold of their arrival. The long interval between 711 and the Delhi Sultanate was filled with different political formations, and the chapter's task is to explain what happened within it.

Section 38.1

Islam Comes to South Asia by More Than One Route

The history of Islam in South Asia is often told as a single arrival, whether as a conquest that began in Sindh in 711, a later invasion from Central Asia, or a gradual commercial presence. Each description captures something real, but none is complete. Historians distinguish at least four kinds of historical actor who brought Islam into South Asia: merchants and sailors using the sea lanes; Arab armies and their commanders, who conquered Sindh; migrants and settlers, including soldiers, scholars, and families who stayed; and local people who converted. These groups overlapped, but they had different motives, moved at different times, and left different kinds of evidence.

The date 711 marks the capture of the port of Debal by an army sent by the Umayyad governor of Iraq, and it is important because it began the incorporation of Sindh into the expanding Islamic empire. It is not the beginning of Muslim presence in South Asia. Arabic sources report earlier maritime contacts, and Arab, Persian, and Indian seafarers had traded along the same coasts for centuries before Islam. It is also not the beginning of a mass Muslim population in the subcontinent, because most of Sindh's inhabitants in the eighth century were not Muslims and most would not become Muslims for generations.

This chapter therefore proceeds by following the three processes named in its opening: maritime contact and merchant settlement, political conquest in Sindh, and the gradual formation of Muslim communities and polities. The reader should keep in mind that these processes intersect. The conquest of Sindh took place in a region already connected to the Arabian Sea. Merchants on the coast sometimes interacted with Arab armies and governors. And conversion in Sindh drew upon both settlers and local people. But a student who merges them will mistake a complex history for a single event.

Historical Vocabulary
Arab, Muslim, and Islamic

These three terms are often used loosely, but they refer to different things. Arab is primarily an ethnic and linguistic designation, referring to Arabic-speaking peoples with tribal and regional identities, and in the early conquests it designates the dominant military and political elite. Muslim refers to a follower of Islam, whatever his or her language or origin: Persians, Berbers, Indians, and many others became Muslims. Islamic is an adjective for things associated with Islam as a religion, law, or civilization, such as an Islamic legal category or Islamic architecture.

In this chapter, the armies of 711 are properly called Arab (and Umayyad), while the merchants and later communities are Muslim. Many early Arab soldiers were Muslims, but the equation of Arab with Muslim obscures the many non-Arab Muslims, and the later converted populations of Sindh were Muslims without being Arabs. The term "Islamic" is used only for religion, law, and the wider civilization.

Section 38.2

Arabia and the Indian Ocean before Islam

Arabia's relationship to South Asia did not begin with Islam. The Arabian Peninsula sat at the junction of caravan routes linking the Mediterranean, the Red Sea, the Persian Gulf, and the Indian Ocean. South Arabia produced frankincense and myrrh, and the ports of the Gulf and the Red Sea handled goods from India and farther east. Chapter 25 traced the monsoon navigation and Roman-era commerce of the early historic period, and the Greek-language Periplus of the Erythraean Sea (first century CE) describes ports on the Indian coast and the commodities exchanged. By the sixth century, the Sasanian Empire controlled much of the Gulf, and Persian shipping was prominent in the western Indian Ocean.

Indian goods of value reached the western world: pepper and other spices, cotton textiles, teak and other timbers, fragrant woods, certain metals including iron and steel, and gems. Goods and animals also moved east, and horses from Arabia and Iran were significant imports for armies and elites in India in many periods. The monsoon winds structured the voyages, so that ships sailed to India with the southwest monsoon in summer and returned with the northeast winds in winter. A merchant therefore often stayed on the coast for months, which encouraged the establishment of foreign merchant quarters and lasting relationships with local rulers and communities.

Students should note two cautions. First, the pre-Islamic evidence for specifically Arab merchants in India is thinner than that for Persians and for the general category of western seafarers, and historians should not assume that Arabs dominated this trade before the seventh century. Second, Sindh and Makran were already part of these systems. The port of Debal on the Indus delta, whose identification with the excavated site of Banbhore is generally but not universally accepted, and the harbors of Gujarat were gateways between the sea and the interior.

Section 38.3

The Rise of Islam and the Continuity of Maritime Trade

In the early seventh century, the Prophet Muhammad led a community in the Hijaz that rapidly became the nucleus of a state. After his death in 632, his successors, the caliphs, led the armies that overthrew the Sasanian Empire and took Syria and Egypt from the Byzantines. For the western Indian Ocean, the consequences were considerable. The Persian Gulf, the Red Sea, and the Iranian plateau were brought under a single political authority, and Muslim rulers and merchants gained access to the maritime networks that had served earlier powers.

The conquests did not abolish the older commercial world. Merchants continued to sail from the Gulf and the Red Sea to India, and the trade in textiles, spices, and aromatics continued. The founding of new cities such as Basra in Iraq (in the 630s) created major markets and recruiting centers, and later the Abbasid foundation of Baghdad (762) created an even larger consumer market. The wealth of the caliphate stimulated demand for goods from the east. By the ninth century, Siraf and other Gulf ports flourished, and Arabic sources describe ships traveling to India and China.

Importantly, Muslim merchants were not agents of the caliphate. Many were independent traders, shipowners, and brokers, who operated according to commercial opportunity and who were often Persian, Arab, or from other communities. They learned from older seafarers, including Persian and Indian navigators, whose knowledge of the monsoon and coasts they inherited. The expansion of Islam thus meant that the same sea lanes were increasingly traveled by Muslims, a change in the religious identity of the traders more than in the structure of the trade. Chapter 37 treats the commercial system itself in greater detail.

Section 38.4

Muslim Merchants and Communities on the Western Coast

Along the coasts of Gujarat, the Konkan, and Malabar, Muslim merchants settled in port towns where they were hosted by local rulers. Arabic geographers of the ninth and tenth centuries describe coastal rulers who allowed Muslim traders to live under their own headmen and to follow their own religious practice, in return for the revenue and prestige that foreign commerce brought. Al-Masudi, who visited the western coast in 916–17, describes a sizeable Muslim community at the port he calls Saymur, usually identified with Chaul in the Konkan, and mentions the existence of a mosque there. Such reports are among the best contemporary evidence for the settled presence of Muslim communities, though their numbers and details should be read with caution.

Inscriptional evidence also reveals coastal contact. Rashtrakuta-era records from the Konkan and Gujarat refer to Tajikas, a term used in Indian sources for Arabs and, more broadly, for Muslims from the west, and Arabic accounts of the Rashtrakuta king, whom they call the Balhara, report that he was friendly to Muslim merchants. Such sources demonstrate that Muslims could live and trade in these polities without political conquest, a point developed further in Section 38.15.

Community formation occurred through familiar processes. Merchants married local women, built places of worship, and raised children who were at home in both worlds. Over time, these families formed communities with their own religious leaders, courts of arbitration, and burial grounds. Malabar offers a notable example. The Muslim community later called the Mappilas was shaped by Arab and other overseas merchants, by local converts, and by the interaction of both with Malabar's coastal society. Origin traditions, such as the story of a Chera ruler (Cheraman Perumal) who is said to have converted to Islam and to have encouraged the foundation of early mosques, are valuable evidence of how the community later understood itself. But they were written down centuries later, they differ in detail, and they are not contemporary documentation of seventh-century events. Historians must therefore treat the dates traditionally given for the first mosques as traditions rather than as established facts.

Everyday Life and Social History

How a Merchant Community Formed

A visiting merchant might first arrive with the monsoon and remain on the coast until the winds changed. Staying through the monsoon season required lodging, storage for cargo, access to brokers who knew local markets, and an arrangement with the local ruler or port authorities. A merchant who returned yearly gradually built relationships, and some settled permanently. Marriage into local families was a common means by which the newcomers acquired local kin networks, and children raised in these households learned local languages while retaining religious ties with the wider Muslim world.

These communities needed institutions: a place for prayer, a burial ground, someone to settle disputes, and a means of connecting with merchants elsewhere. The evidence for the details is thin and often indirect, and it concerns merchants far more than the sailors, laborers, and women whose work sustained the trade. Students should therefore treat the picture here as a reasoned reconstruction rather than a detailed record.

Section 38.5

Sindh before the Arab Conquest

Sindh in the early eighth century was a real society with a long history. Its core was the lower Indus plain, the same region that had hosted the Indus Civilization examined in Chapters 3 and 4, and it reached to the Arabian Sea at the Indus delta. The river was both an agricultural lifeline and a transport route, its floods and shifting channels shaping settlement and political control. Settlements and fortified towns were located along its banks, including Aror (Alor) in the north, Brahmanabad and Nerun farther south, and the port of Debal on the coast. Multan, upstream in the Punjab, was a major religious and commercial center associated with a celebrated sun temple.

The political history of Sindh before the conquest is known partly from the Chachnama, which narrates the rise of a family of Brahman origin: a minister named Chach who, in the tradition, took the throne from a ruling dynasty of the Rai family in the early seventh century, and whose family ruled until Dahir was defeated in 712. Because the Chachnama is a later work with its own agenda (see Section 38.9), historians use it cautiously. Chinese Buddhist travelers, including Xuanzang in the seventh century, left accounts of the Indus region that describe the presence of Buddhist monasteries and a ruler whose origins they described differently from the Chachnama. Such differences show how limited our knowledge of Sindh's dynastic history is.

Society was diverse. Brahmanical temples and Buddhist monasteries coexisted, and Buddhist institutions retained significant influence, especially in towns such as Nerun, where the sources describe a Buddhist official. Pastoral and riverine communities, including those the sources call Jats and Meds, are prominent in the narratives, the Meds in particular as seafarers and coastal raiders. Merchants and artisans lived in towns and ports. Students should resist labeling the whole population "Hindu" in the modern sense. The evidence shows a plural religious landscape, and the political loyalties of Sindh's communities were not defined by a single religious identity. Nor was Dahir a "national" ruler in any modern sense. He was a regional king whose authority over towns and chieftains was real but limited.

Sindh also sat between worlds. To the west lay Makran and Iran, and to the east and south lay Rajasthan and Gujarat, regions with their own rulers, discussed in Chapters 34 and 35. The sea connected its ports to Gulf and Indian Ocean ports. Sindh was thus a frontier region, and its political and commercial ties with both the Iranian and the Indian worlds explain why it became the first part of the subcontinent to be incorporated into the Islamic empire.

Section 38.6

Makran and the Making of an Arab Frontier

The conquest of Sindh was the culmination of a gradual eastward advance. After the fall of the Sasanian Empire in the 640s, Arab forces moved through southern Iran toward Makran, the arid coastal region between Kirman and the Indus plain. Al-Baladhuri and other Arabic sources report that Makran was reached in the 640s and early 650s, although they disagree on details, and that a caliph, according to one tradition, discouraged further advance into a land described as difficult and poor. Whatever the truth of that report, the sources show that raiding and exploring continued under later caliphs, including campaigns toward the frontier region on the western edge of Sindh.

Makran was costly to hold. Its terrain was harsh and its water scarce, its population scattered, and communication with Iraq and Fars long. Arab governors there faced repeated resistance and the sources record the loss of at least one governor to local forces around the turn of the eighth century. For Umayyad commanders the frontier posed a logistical problem and also a strategic opportunity: Makran offered a route to the richer lower Indus, and control of it would protect the coastal routes from raiding.

This background is essential to understanding the conquest. It was not a sudden bolt from Arabia but the extension of a frontier that had been developing for decades. Raids, garrisons, and the consolidation of Makran provided the staging ground, the experience, and the supply system on which the 711 expedition depended. It also meant that the Arab leaders knew something of Sindh's geography and politics.

Section 38.7

Why Did the Umayyads Invade Sindh?

The sources offer a famous explanation. According to al-Baladhuri, ships carrying people and gifts sent from Sri Lanka toward the governor al-Hajjaj were attacked by pirates near Debal, and al-Hajjaj demanded redress. The Chachnama gives a related but different version, in which Dahir declined responsibility for the pirates and the Arab governor therefore sent armies. The story was memorable, and later writers repeated it, but historians generally treat it as one narrative within a larger context rather than as a sufficient cause. A tale of captured women and a ruler's refusal gave the conquest a moral justification, and sources written for later Muslim audiences had reasons to supply one.

Other factors were probably more important. First, frontier security: Arab governors in Makran faced raids and resistance, and the coast and the Indus delta offered shelter to opponents. The Chachnama also reports that Dahir gave refuge to rebels against Umayyad authority, a claim that, if accurate, shows that Sindh was entangled in the politics of the caliphate. Second, imperial expansion: the Umayyad state was still expanding in the early eighth century, with armies simultaneously operating in Central Asia, North Africa, and Spain, and campaigns in Sindh fit within that wider pattern. Third, trade routes and wealth: control of Debal and the lower Indus offered access to the coastal and river commerce of the region and to the booty and revenue that successful campaigns produced. Fourth, personal and political ambition: al-Hajjaj was a powerful and ambitious governor who relied on kinsmen, including Muhammad ibn Qasim, and success in the east brought prestige and revenue.

The most responsible conclusion is that the invasion had several causes and that the sources emphasize those that suited their narratives. The student should keep in mind that Sindh was not chosen at random. It was the nearest rich region beyond the frontier, linked by sea and land to Arab-controlled territory, and its rulers were close enough to matter to Umayyad governors. At the same time, the invasion was not inevitable, and earlier campaigns had failed. Al-Hajjaj sent at least two earlier expeditions, according to the sources, before the one that succeeded.

Section 38.8

Muhammad ibn Qasim and the Conquest of Sindh, 711–715

The army that campaigned in Sindh was assembled in Iraq and Fars and marched through Makran, with supplies and siege equipment partly brought by sea. The sources describe forces of several thousand soldiers, and they report that the commander, Muhammad ibn Qasim al-Thaqafi, a relative of al-Hajjaj, was young. His exact age is uncertain, and the popular claim that he was specifically seventeen is not securely established. In 711 the army reached Debal, where, according to the sources, it besieged the town, used siege engines, and captured it after a struggle. The accounts disagree about the treatment of Debal's population and should be read with caution.

From Debal the army moved up the Indus. Several towns, including Nerun, are said to have submitted by negotiation, and the sources describe agreements in which local leaders accepted Arab authority in return for guarantees. The decisive engagement with Dahir took place near a place the sources call Rawar, probably in 712, and ended in Dahir's death. After this defeat, Arab forces took Brahmanabad, Aror, and other strongholds, and the sources describe continued resistance, notably from Dahir's family and followers at Brahmanabad. The army subsequently advanced north to Multan, which, according to the sources, fell after a siege and yielded substantial wealth.

The conquest was thus a combination of siege warfare, pitched battle, and negotiated surrender. It succeeded partly because Arab commanders exploited divisions among local elites, and because many towns and communities preferred negotiation to destruction. It was not a rapid or total conquest of all South Asia. The Arab hold extended along the lower Indus and up to Multan, and not into the Ganges plain or the interior of Rajasthan. The campaign ended with changes at the center of the caliphate: al-Hajjaj died in 714 and the caliph al-Walid I in 715, and under the new caliph Muhammad ibn Qasim was recalled. Traditions about his fate are conflicting, and historians should not rely on any single account.

Students should be careful not to write this history as a heroic biography. Muhammad ibn Qasim was neither a uniquely benevolent civilizer nor a uniquely destructive religious conqueror. He was an imperial commander executing a governor's strategy, and the sources that celebrate or condemn him were composed by later authors with their own purposes.

Cause and Effect

The Arab Conquest of Sindh

Long-term causes

The expansion of the Arab empire to Iran and Makran; the commercial importance of the Arabian Sea; Sindh's position as the nearest rich region beyond the frontier; decades of raiding and frontier experience.

Short-term causes

Frontier insecurity in Makran; the ambitions and authority of al-Hajjaj; the failure of earlier expeditions; the reported attack on ships near Debal; and political divisions in Sindh.

Immediate effects

Arab control of the lower Indus and Multan; negotiated and imposed arrangements for taxation and governance; the establishment of Arab garrisons and officials.

Long-term consequences

Sindh became a frontier province of the caliphate and later a region of regional Muslim rule; Muslim communities slowly grew; Arab expansion stalled before the Ganges plain.

Historical complexity: The conquest was neither the first nor the only way Islam entered South Asia, and it did not itself produce mass conversion. It also did not lead directly to later Muslim states in northern India, which arose centuries later from different political systems.

Section 38.9

The Chachnama and the Problem of Reconstructing the Conquest

The most detailed narrative of the conquest is the Chachnama, an early-thirteenth-century Persian prose work by Ali ibn Hamid Kufi, written in the Sindh region under the patronage of a ruler there. Kufi's own account places his search for source material in AH 613 (1216–17), while Manan Ahmed Asif dates the composition of the surviving Persian work to c. 1224–26. The author states that he was translating an earlier Arabic account, reportedly preserved by the family of the Thaqafi conquerors, and the text is often called the history of "the conquest of Sindh." Its narrative includes the rise of Chach, the reign of Dahir, the campaigns of Muhammad ibn Qasim, and a great deal of dialogue, correspondence, and moral commentary.

Historians recognize the work's value, but they also recognize that it is not an eighth-century eyewitness record. It was written roughly five hundred years after the events, in a different political world, and its speeches, motives, and agreements cannot be taken as verbatim documentation. The surviving text may incorporate earlier material, but its form and content also reflect the concerns of thirteenth-century Persianate political culture, including ideas about kingship, justice, and the treatment of non-Muslim subjects. Some recent historians, notably Manan Ahmed Asif, have argued that the work should be read not primarily as a translation but as a text composed to offer advice and political legitimacy for its patron, which makes its relationship to eighth-century events even more complex.

The responsible method is comparison. Al-Baladhuri (died c. 892), writing in Arabic about a century and a half after the conquest, gives a much shorter account of Sindh and preserves some details that overlap with the Chachnama and others that differ. Other Arabic geographers and historians preserve fragments. Archaeology at Banbhore, often identified with Debal, provides material evidence for early Muslim presence, though its dating and interpretation are debated. Coins and inscriptions add further information. When independent sources agree, historians gain confidence, and when only the Chachnama reports an episode, they must treat it as a possibility with a literary character rather than as an established fact.

Section 38.10

Conquest, Taxation, and Accommodation

The conquerors governed a population that was overwhelmingly non-Muslim, and they had neither the numbers nor the administrative apparatus to replace existing institutions. Sources suggest that Arab commanders and governors retained many local officials, landed families, and village or town leaders, who knew how to collect revenue and keep order. Some notables who submitted by agreement were confirmed in their positions, and the garrisons settled in key towns provided military force. Governance depended on bargaining between conquerors and local elites.

Taxation was central. Arab governors collected revenue from land and trade, and some of it was sent to the caliph's treasury or used to pay soldiers. The terminology of Islamic law, including kharaj (a land tax), jizya (a tax on protected non-Muslims), and the status of dhimma (protection), offered a framework that governors applied to the conquered, and later sources describe Buddhists and Brahmanical communities as being placed in a status analogous to the protected communities of the Near East. The surviving evidence cannot show how consistently this framework was applied. Later jurists described systems more fully than the sources allow us to see in practice, and the actual arrangements in eighth-century Sindh probably varied by place and negotiation.

The overall pattern was one of accommodation without equality. Muslims, and especially the Arab military elite, held political and military power and enjoyed privileges, while non-Muslims paid taxes and, in the legal framework, held a subordinate status. Yet continuity in village life, landholding, and local administration was considerable, and the conquerors' dependence on local intermediaries limited the scope of change. Students should avoid assuming either that conquest swept away existing structures or that it left everything unchanged.

Historical Vocabulary
Dhimma, Jizya, and Kharaj

Dhimma means "protection" or "covenant," and in Islamic law it describes the status of non-Muslim communities, such as Christians and Jews, who were guaranteed protection of life, property, and worship in return for payment of a tax and acceptance of Muslim authority. Jizya is the tax levied on such protected communities. Kharaj is a tax on land, often the principal source of revenue in conquered agrarian regions.

These terms matter because later writers used them to describe the status of non-Muslims in Sindh. But the legal categories developed over time, and applying a fully developed later jurisprudence to the early eighth century risks anachronism. Historians therefore ask what can be shown about Sindh itself rather than assuming that later rules operated unchanged.

Section 38.11

Religious Communities under Arab Rule

Sindh's religious landscape included Buddhist monasteries and stupas, Brahmanical temples and learned families, shrines associated with local cults, and, increasingly, mosques. Chinese accounts of the seventh century portray a region in which Buddhism was prominent, and archaeological remains of stupas and monasteries confirm a Buddhist presence in the lower Indus. The Chachnama and Arabic sources refer to Buddhist communities and temples at towns such as Nerun and Debal. The Arab conquest placed these institutions under a new political authority, and their later fortunes varied.

Sources describe the conquerors guaranteeing the safety of existing places of worship in many places, and some reports describe their being allowed to continue. Other accounts describe the destruction or plundering of temples and the capture of treasure, and archaeological and textual evidence both suggest that violence accompanied the conquest in specific places. Historians must therefore avoid generalization either way. It is not true that all temples and monasteries disappeared under Arab rule, and it is not true that the conquest was peaceful for every community. Buddhist institutions, which often depended on royal and mercantile patronage, probably faced changing circumstances as patrons and political ties shifted, and their gradual decline in the region was a long-term process with multiple causes, not an immediate consequence of 711.

A binary of Muslim and Hindu does not fit the eighth-century evidence. The communities in Sindh included Buddhist monastics and laity, Brahmans, merchants of various religious affiliations, pastoralists, and others, whose relationships with each other and with the new rulers were not governed by a single religious line. Muslims themselves were also diverse, including Arab soldiers, converts, and settlers, and, later, Sunni, Shia, and Ismaili communities. Religious change took place within these relationships.

Section 38.12

Mansura, Multan, and the Urban Geography of Muslim Sindh

Arab Sindh developed its own urban centers. Mansura, established in the lower Indus region during the eighth century, became the principal Muslim city and provincial capital, a garrison and administrative center. Traditions attribute its founding to an Umayyad-era governor, but the date and founder are debated. Arabic geographers of the ninth and tenth centuries describe Mansura as a substantial city with a congregational mosque and a mixed population, and its ruins near the older site of Brahmanabad have been studied archaeologically. It illustrates a pattern common to Arab frontier regions: the creation of garrison towns where soldiers and officials lived apart from, but close to, the existing population.

Multan, farther north, had a different history. It was an old Indus-Punjab city that had been a center of pilgrimage and trade, famed for a temple of the sun, and Arabic geographers describe its wealth and its importance as a route to the north. After the conquest it became the principal Arab city in the north, and its strategic importance persisted through the subsequent centuries. Multan's long history as both a religious center and a commercial town made it a place where different communities met.

The wider urban network included ports such as Debal and riverine towns that served markets and administration. Coinage supplies evidence for the economy: governors and later regional dynasties issued silver and other coins, some in styles that reflect earlier Sasanian models, and these coins provide evidence for administration and monetary circulation. The geography of Muslim Sindh thus consisted of both new and older cities, and its political history after the conquest is a history of these centers. Chapter 39 later shows how Multan and Mansura entered the world of Ghaznavid politics, but that story lies beyond this chapter.

Section 38.13

Conversion and the Formation of Muslim Communities

Conversion is one of the most important and least documented processes in this chapter. The sources record conquests, governors, and battles, but they say little about why ordinary people adopted Islam. What can be said with confidence is that conversion in Sindh was gradual, uneven, and multicausal. The Arab conquest did not produce rapid mass conversion, and the population of the lower Indus remained largely non-Muslim for generations after 711. Arabic geographers of the tenth century, writing more than two centuries later, still describe Sindh and Multan as regions with substantial non-Muslim populations alongside Muslim rulers and communities.

Muslim communities grew by several routes. Migrants and military settlers, mainly Arabs and other Muslims posted to garrison towns, established families and neighborhoods. Merchants settled in ports and river towns and married locally. Local converts adopted Islam for reasons that surely varied: spiritual conviction, marriage, the social and economic advantages of joining the ruling community, relief from the status of non-Muslim taxpayers, patronage and employment with Muslim officials, or the influence of neighbors and kin. The children of mixed marriages formed a growing community with ties to both worlds. Religious teachers and, from the tenth century, Ismaili missionaries at Multan also contributed to the growth of particular communities.

Historians therefore reject the claim that conquest automatically produced conversion, and equally reject any single explanation for why people converted. Later accounts, whether celebratory or hostile, often simplify the process into forced conversion or voluntary liberation. The evidence supports neither simplification as a general description. Violence and coercion occurred in particular episodes of the conquest, but the long-term growth of Muslim communities depended mostly on slow social processes.

Historical Debate

Why Did People in South Asia Become Muslims?

The question: Historians have long debated the main causes of conversion to Islam in South Asia. For early Sindh, where evidence is sparse, which explanations are best supported?

Interpretation A — Political patronage and social incentive: Conversion followed the incentives created by political power. Joining the ruling community offered access to office, protection, tax advantages, and social networks, and conversion spread gradually through communities connected to the new rulers and settlers.
Interpretation B — Religious and social appeal, and gradual integration: People adopted Islam through contact with merchants, teachers, and neighbors, and found its teachings and communal institutions attractive. Others argue that existing communities, including those on the margins of the older religious and social order, were drawn to a new community, and that identities were formed gradually through participation rather than through any single event.

Evidence: Direct testimony from converts is almost absent for the eighth and ninth centuries. Historians rely on geographers' descriptions of mixed populations, on patterns of settlement and intermarriage, on later comparison with other regions (such as Bengal, examined in later chapters), and on later community traditions. Neither forced conversion on a large scale nor a purely voluntary model is supported as a complete explanation by the early evidence from Sindh.

Why it matters: If conversion is attributed to a single cause, political memory can transform it into either a story of coercion or one of simple liberation, and both distort the evidence. Understanding it as multicausal helps explain why religious communities in South Asia have complex and varied histories.

Student task: Identify which kinds of evidence would best allow historians to test each interpretation for Sindh, and explain why that evidence is difficult to obtain.

Section 38.14

From Caliphal Province to Regional Muslim Polities

After the conquest, Sindh was governed as a province of the caliphate. Under the Umayyads, governors sent from Iraq or other centers administered the region, and the Arab presence also reached beyond Sindh. Arab raids into Gujarat and farther east in the 730s met resistance, and a Chalukya copper-plate record from the Navsari region and later Pratihara praise of their ancestors' victories over Arab forces show that expansion beyond Sindh was checked. The inscriptions, which come from the victors, are not neutral, but they corroborate the Arabic reports of raids and suggest that Arab power reached its practical limit in western India.

After the Abbasids replaced the Umayyads in 750, Sindh remained part of the caliphal system. Abbasid governors campaigned in the region and attempted to reassert authority, and the relationship between Sindh and Baghdad continued, as shown by the movement of people and goods. One striking example is the transfer of groups of Jats (Zutt) to Iraq, where they later rebelled in the marshes and were suppressed in the 830s. Over time, however, direct caliphal control weakened. Civil conflict in the caliphate, the distance from Baghdad, and the interests of local governors encouraged autonomy.

By the ninth and tenth centuries, regional rulers dominated. At Mansura, a dynasty known as the Habbarids governed with nominal acknowledgment of the caliph, while Multan was ruled by a separate line. In the later tenth century, Multan's rulers adopted an Ismaili allegiance connected with the Fatimid caliphate of Cairo, as Arabic geographers of the period report, which shows that Sindh was now linked to religious and political rivalries within the Islamic world. Sindh was therefore not a permanently incorporated, tightly administered province. It became a region of Muslim rulers linked by culture and commerce to the wider Islamic world but politically fragmented. These were the polities that the Ghaznavids subsequently encountered, as Chapter 39 explains.

Section 38.15

Muslim Communities beyond Political Conquest

The western coast shows that Muslim communal life could flourish where no Muslim army had created a state. In Gujarat, the Konkan, and Malabar, Muslim merchants lived under Hindu, Buddhist, Jain, and other rulers, and their communities grew through trade, marriage, and settlement. Arabic geographers report mosques, headmen, and community institutions in coastal towns, and they describe local rulers who found the merchants valuable. Local inscriptions and copper-plate grants show that rulers in the region regularly gave privileges to foreign merchant groups, including Jewish and Christian communities in Malabar, which suggests that the Muslim merchants were part of a wider pattern of pluralistic coastal society.

Chapters 31 and 34 examined the Rashtrakuta and Pratihara states in whose territories some of these coastal communities lived, and Chapter 49 returns to the same coasts on the eve of Portuguese arrival. In the interval between, the coastal communities grew in number and complexity. Their fortunes depended on the maritime economy and on local rulers, not on the caliphate.

Section 38.16

Trade, Knowledge, Language, and Cultural Exchange

The early Muslim presence connected South Asia more closely to the Gulf and the Middle East. Commercial links carried goods and people in both directions. Arabic sources of the ninth and tenth centuries preserve accounts of Indian ports, products, and customs, including the compilation Akhbar al-Sin wa'l-Hind and the geographies of Ibn Khurdadhbih and al-Masudi. These sources are valuable but have their own limits: they were written by outsiders, often from reports of sailors and merchants, and they sometimes mix observation with hearsay.

Intellectual exchange is also attested, though claims must be proportional to the evidence. Arabic sources report that, in the Abbasid period, Indian scholars and physicians visited or served at Baghdad, and that Sanskrit astronomical and medical texts were translated into Arabic. A tradition associated with the court of the caliph al-Mansur describes the arrival of an Indian astronomical text from Sindh, which influenced early Arabic astronomy, and Indian numerals and mathematical ideas contributed to the later Arabic mathematics that would transmit them onward (see also Chapter 29). Stories from Indian literature, such as the animal fables later known in Arabic as Kalila wa Dimna, traveled by way of Persian translation. The precise lines of transmission in some of these cases are debated, and Sindh was one among several channels, along with Iran and Central Asia.

Language and religion also moved. Arabic and Persian words entered the languages of Sindh and the coast over time. A tenth-century Arabic collection of sailors' tales reports that a ruler in Sindh asked for a translation of the Quran into a local language, a story that, if accurate, indicates interest in exchange, though the source is anecdotal. Sindh also produced Muslim scholars who worked in the wider Islamic world. These links show that Sindh was not merely a conquered frontier. It was a connector of regions, and its Muslim and non-Muslim communities participated in shared networks of trade and learning.

Section 38.17

What Did the Early Muslim Presence Change?

The early Muslim presence changed South Asia in specific and limited ways. Sindh became a frontier and connector between the Islamic world and South Asia, and it carried Arabic and Persian culture into the Indus region. Durable Muslim communities began to form, in Sindh and on the western coast, though their size remained modest in the period covered here. Arab political expansion reached its limits at the Sindh frontier, repelled in Gujarat and not extended into the Ganges plain.

At the same time, much remained continuous. Local institutions, landholding, and village life persisted, and non-Muslim religious communities continued to exist in large numbers. The conquerors governed in part through existing elites. The distinction between political Islamization and social or religious change is fundamental: a change of rulers did not produce an immediate change in the religion or culture of the governed. Students should therefore avoid describing 711 as a decisive rupture in the whole of South Asian history. It was a regional event with important long-term consequences, whose significance was shaped by what followed.

The chapter ends at a threshold. By around the year 1000, new political forces were emerging in Central Asia, and the Ghaznavid state would soon reach the Punjab and Multan. That story, discussed in Chapter 39, belongs to a different political world, and should not be read back into the eighth century.

Section 38.18

Review and Historical Synthesis

The core question of this chapter is why the establishment of Muslim political rule in Sindh and the growth of Muslim merchant communities elsewhere produced different patterns of religious, social, and political change. The answer begins with the three processes traced above. Merchant settlement on the western coast was an extension of an old maritime world: Muslim traders entered existing networks, were hosted by non-Muslim rulers, and formed communities through marriage and institution-building. These communities depended on commercial opportunity and local goodwill, and they grew without political power.

In Sindh, by contrast, change began with conquest. An Umayyad army, driven by frontier security, imperial expansion, and the ambitions of a governor, took the lower Indus and Multan between 711 and 715. But the conquerors were few and Sindh's society was large and varied. They governed by accommodating local elites and institutions, and they held power over a mostly non-Muslim population. Political rule thus preceded, rather than produced, large-scale community growth, which depended on slower processes of settlement, intermarriage, patronage, and conversion over generations. As caliphal control weakened, regional Muslim dynasties took over, shaped more by local and regional circumstances than by the caliphate.

The comparison reveals a principle: political power and social change operate on different timescales. Conquest could alter rulers in a few years, but altering the religious composition of a society took many generations, and it did not follow a single course. Source criticism reinforces this lesson. The Chachnama, the most vivid source for the conquest, tells us as much about thirteenth-century political culture as about the eighth century, and the history of conversion survives only in fragments.

This chapter therefore corrects the old narrative of Arabs trading, Muhammad ibn Qasim conquering, and Islam spreading. It replaces it with a picture of multiple routes, regional variation, and gradual change. Chapter 39 turns to a different process: the expansion of Central Asian Muslim states under the Ghaznavids and Ghurids, whose campaigns were separated from Sindh's conquest by centuries and arose from different political systems. Students should carry forward the lesson that the early Muslim presence in South Asia was not a single event but a set of overlapping histories.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that Islam became part of South Asian history through multiple routes rather than a single invasion. It began with the Arabian Sea, where Arab, Persian, Indian, and other merchants traded long before Islam. The rise of Islam in the seventh century changed the religious identity of many of those traders and brought the Gulf, Red Sea, and Iran under Muslim rule, but it did not abolish the older commercial system.

On the western coasts of Gujarat, the Konkan, and Malabar, Muslim merchants settled in ports hosted by non-Muslim rulers. They married locally, built mosques, and formed communities under their own headmen. Evidence for these communities comes from Arabic geographers, inscriptions, and later traditions, and the later origin stories, such as that of Cheraman Perumal, must be treated as cultural memory rather than contemporary documentation.

Sindh, the lower Indus region, was a real and varied society before the conquest, with ports, river towns, Buddhist and Brahmanical institutions, pastoral and maritime communities, and a dynasty of the Chach family according to later tradition. It lay between the Iranian and Indian worlds and was linked to the sea. Dahir was a regional ruler, not the representative of a modern nation.

The Arab conquest of Sindh was the culmination of a frontier that developed through Makran during the seventh century. The famous story of seized ships was one narrative tradition within a larger context of frontier security, imperial expansion, trade, and the ambition of al-Hajjaj. Between 711 and 715 the army of Muhammad ibn Qasim took Debal, defeated Dahir, captured towns along the Indus, and reached Multan, through a combination of siege, battle, and negotiation.

Reconstructing this conquest requires source criticism. The Chachnama, completed in the thirteenth century, offers a vivid narrative but is not an eyewitness account, and its speeches and agreements cannot be taken as verbatim. Historians compare it with al-Baladhuri and other evidence, and they treat its details cautiously.

The conquerors governed through accommodation. They retained local officials and elites, collected taxes under frameworks drawn from Islamic law, and placed non-Muslims in a protected but subordinate status, though the actual practice in eighth-century Sindh cannot be assumed to match later jurisprudence. Buddhist and Brahmanical institutions did not universally disappear, and the religious landscape was too diverse for a simple Muslim-Hindu binary.

Mansura and Multan became centers of Muslim Sindh, and conversion occurred gradually through settlement, marriage, patronage, and many other causes. Sindh remained largely non-Muslim for generations. As caliphal control weakened, regional dynasties such as the Habbarids and the rulers of Multan took over, and Arab expansion beyond Sindh was checked, including in Gujarat.

The comparison between Sindh and the western coast is the chapter's key insight. In Sindh, Muslim political rule preceded large-scale community growth, while on the coast Muslim communities developed without any political conquest. Trade, language, and knowledge moved in both directions, and Sindh served as a connector as well as a frontier.

The chapter has therefore distinguished political conquest from social and religious change and has avoided the teleology that links 711 directly to the Ghaznavids and the Delhi Sultanate. Those later developments arose centuries afterward in a different political world, and Chapter 39 examines them.

Chachnama
A Persian prose narrative of the Chach dynasty and the Arab conquest, composed in the early thirteenth century.
Source criticism
The method of evaluating a source's date, authorship, purposes, and reliability before using it as evidence.
Dhimma
The covenant of protection granted in Islamic law to non-Muslim communities in return for a tax and acceptance of Muslim authority.
Jizya
The tax levied on protected non-Muslims under Islamic law.
Kharaj
A tax on agricultural land, a principal source of revenue in conquered regions.
Mawali
Non-Arab converts to Islam who became attached to Arab tribes or patrons in the early caliphate.
Garrison town
A settlement founded or used to house conquering troops, such as Mansura.
Conversion
The adoption of a new religious identity, which in early Sindh occurred gradually and for multiple reasons.
Ismaili
A Shia tradition connected with the Fatimid caliphate, whose missionaries influenced Multan in the later tenth century.
Habbarids
A regional dynasty at Mansura that ruled Sindh with limited ties to the caliphate.
Mappila
The Muslim community of Malabar, shaped by overseas merchants, local converts, and coastal society.
Persianate
Describing cultures shaped by the Persian language and its courtly, literary, and administrative traditions.
Frontier
A zone of contact and contest at the edge of a state, where authority is negotiated rather than uniform.
Accommodation
Governance by negotiation with existing elites and institutions rather than replacing them.

Research or Library Extension

MGU Library Research Project

Mapping Early Muslim Presence in South Asia

This project asks students to compare the sources and evidence for two routes by which Islam entered South Asia.

  1. Choose two cases. Select the conquest of Sindh and one coastal community (for example Malabar, the Konkan, or Gujarat).
  2. Identify sources. For each, list the types of evidence (chronicles, geographies, inscriptions, coins, archaeology, later traditions) and note date, author, and perspective.
  3. Create a map. Produce a historical map showing sea routes, Makran, the Indus towns, and coastal settlements, avoiding modern borders.
  4. Compare patterns. Analyze how political power, trade, marriage, and conversion shaped community formation in each case.
  5. Write. In a 4–6 page essay, explain why the two cases differ, and use careful language where evidence is uncertain.

Library resources: translations of Arabic geographers and historians, studies of the Chachnama, archaeological reports on Banbhore and Mansura, and regional histories of Sindh and the western coast.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a major historical change can be misunderstood when compressed into a date. The early Muslim presence in South Asia was the work of merchants, sailors, soldiers, governors, scholars, families, and converts, whose activities unfolded over centuries and in different places. By separating these strands, the chapter prepares students to read later chapters on the Delhi Sultanate and beyond without assuming a single narrative of arrival.

It also reveals larger processes: the integration of South Asia into a widening Afro-Eurasian world, the importance of frontier regions as connectors, and the difference between political and social change. Conquest altered rulers quickly, while the formation of religious communities took generations. The same lesson recurs in Chapters 44, 48, and 49, which examine conversion, devotion, and maritime change in later centuries.

Finally, the chapter shows what historians do when sources are partial and late. The Chachnama is neither discarded nor trusted blindly. It is read as evidence for the culture that produced it. Students who learn this habit will be better prepared for the contested histories ahead, and for Chapter 39, where Central Asian states build new empires from very different foundations.
