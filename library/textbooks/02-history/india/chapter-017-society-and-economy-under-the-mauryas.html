Part IV — The Mauryan Age
17

Society and Economy under the Mauryas

Indian History: South Asia from the Indus Civilization to the Present

Chapter Opening

Ashoka's inscriptions reveal what an emperor wanted his officials and subjects to hear about kingship, dhamma, justice, and welfare. Yet empires are sustained not by proclamations alone. Behind the pillars and rock edicts stood cultivators who produced food, households that supplied labor, artisans who manufactured goods, merchants who moved commodities, officials who assessed and collected resources, and communities whose relationship to imperial authority ranged from close incorporation to negotiated autonomy. To understand Mauryan power, the historian must turn from the king's voice to the social and economic worlds that made imperial rule possible.

Chapters 15 and 16 looked at the Mauryan Empire largely from the palace outward: the conquests of Chandragupta, the statecraft traditions associated with Chanakya, the remorse and ideology of Ashoka, and the edicts through which a ruler addressed his realm. This chapter deliberately changes the camera angle. It looks from fields, villages, workshops, roads, marketplaces, households, and forest edges back toward the empire, and asks what resources, relationships, and communities stood behind the political structures already examined.

The central question of the chapter is therefore not "what did Mauryan kings do?" but a broader one: what economic resources, social relationships, productive communities, administrative practices, and regional networks made Mauryan imperial power possible, and how much can historians actually know about everyday life beneath the level of kings and inscriptions? The second half of that question matters as much as the first. The honest answer, repeatedly, will be that historians know a good deal about what rulers and statecraft authors wanted, considerably less about what happened several days' travel from a provincial center, and almost nothing about the thoughts of most individual cultivators, weavers, or servants.

The chapter's argument can be stated directly. Mauryan imperial power rested on far more than kings, armies, and proclamations. It depended on agrarian production, village communities, urban craft specialization, merchants and transport networks, systems of revenue and administration, occupational differentiation, household labor, and relationships with forest and frontier populations. Yet these foundations were not uniform across the empire, and the evidence must be used cautiously rather than combined into a single picture of a perfectly centralized Mauryan economy. The empire was built upon many older regional economies and connected, taxed, supervised, and sometimes reorganized them; it did not make them identical.

The evidence for this reconstruction is scattered and of very different kinds. Archaeology supplies settlement patterns, pottery, iron tools, ornaments, coins, and the remains of fortified towns. Ashokan inscriptions supply contemporary Mauryan royal statements about officials, revenue, welfare, and frontier peoples. Coins document exchange. Greek accounts, above all the fragments of Megasthenes preserved in later authors, offer an outsider's description. The Arthashastra, as Chapter 15 explained, is a composite statecraft text of uncertain and layered date, valuable for what it reveals about how a political thinker imagined economic administration, but not a literal Mauryan handbook. No single source gives "Mauryan society"; the chapter must assemble it, carefully, from all of them.

The chapter also resists two opposite temptations. One credits the Mauryas with inventing the early historic economy: cities, coinage, long-distance trade, and craft specialization. In fact, as Chapter 9 showed, these developments were well underway during the Second Urbanization and the Nanda period. The other treats the empire as merely a thin political layer irrelevant to ordinary life. Imperial demands for revenue, labor, supplies, and obedience did reach many communities. The task is to find the right proportion, region by region.

Finally, the chapter prepares the ground for Chapter 18. An empire that depends on moving resources, information, officials, and military power across a vast and varied landscape is impressive when its center is strong. What happens when that center weakens is the subject of the next chapter; here the aim is to understand what was being held together.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how agricultural surplus supported Mauryan courts, officials, armies, cities, and transport, while distinguishing state dependence on agriculture from direct state management of agriculture.
  2. Describe the regional variety of crops, farming methods, and rural settlement across the Mauryan sphere, and explain why no single "Mauryan village" can be reconstructed.
  3. Analyze the relationship between rural production and urban centers such as Pataliputra, Taxila, and Ujjain, and the role of craft specialization in that relationship.
  4. Trace the principal overland, riverine, and coastal routes that carried goods and people, while distinguishing developing transregional exchange from the later Roman-era Indian Ocean trade.
  5. Assess what punch-marked coinage does and does not demonstrate about Mauryan monetization and state economic control.
  6. Distinguish among contemporary Ashokan evidence, Greek accounts, and the composite Arthashastra tradition when reconstructing administration.
  7. Explain why Mauryan social hierarchy cannot be reduced to a four-varna diagram, and evaluate Megasthenes' seven-fold classification as evidence.
  8. Evaluate the limits of evidence for women's lives and household organization, separating elite and normative prescription from lived experience.
  9. Interpret the position of forest peoples and frontier communities as economic and political actors rather than as "outsiders" to the empire.
  10. Evaluate competing interpretations of how centralized Mauryan economic and administrative control actually was.
  11. Synthesize the material, social, and administrative foundations of Mauryan power in a way that sets up the question of imperial fragmentation in Chapter 18.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How does a state turn agricultural production into political and military power, and how much of that process can historians actually document for the Mauryas?
  2. What did imperial rule look like for a village far from any provincial center?
  3. How did cities and villages depend upon one another, and what does craft specialization reveal about that interdependence?
  4. Which economic developments should be credited to the Mauryas, and which belong to the longer history of the Second Urbanization?
  5. What does the existence of coinage prove about an economy, and what does it not prove?
  6. Why must historians keep Ashokan inscriptions, Greek accounts, and the Arthashastra analytically separate?
  7. Why did observers describe Mauryan society in ways that do not match one another, and what does that reveal about categories such as varna, jati, and occupation?
  8. Whose labor and whose voices are most difficult to recover, and what methods allow historians to approach them?
  9. Was the frontier of the Mauryan Empire a boundary or a zone of interaction?

Historical Context

Chapter 9 traced the return of large urban centers in the first millennium BCE, the growth of merchant communities, and the appearance of coinage in the Ganges valley. Chapter 10 followed Magadha's rise, including its control of agricultural resources and of Rajagriha and Pataliputra, and the Nanda Empire's accumulation of wealth and military strength. Chapter 14 placed the northwest within the Achaemenid and Hellenistic worlds. Chapters 15 and 16 then examined the creation of Mauryan rule under Chandragupta, its consolidation under Bindusara, and the transformation of royal ideology under Ashoka. This chapter assumes that political narrative and asks about the material and social conditions beneath it.

The geographic setting shaped these conditions in decisive ways. The middle Ganges plain, with its fertile alluvial soils and reliable rainfall and river water, formed the agricultural and demographic core, examined in Chapter 1. Beyond it lay very different landscapes: the irrigated and semi-arid northwest; the trade-linked regions of Gujarat and Saurashtra; the coastal plains of Kalinga; the hills, forests, and plateaus of central India; and the Deccan and southern regions where Ashokan inscriptions appear but where the pre-existing societies discussed in Chapter 8 continued largely on their own terms. The Mauryan sphere was a political umbrella over economies of very different character.

The evidence is uneven and must be handled with the caution that Chapters 1 and 15 established. Contemporary Mauryan evidence includes the Ashokan inscriptions and a small number of other early Brahmi records. Archaeological evidence includes excavations at Pataliputra and Taxila, distinctive fine pottery such as Northern Black Polished Ware, iron implements, and coin hoards. Greek and Latin writers preserve fragments of Megasthenes, a Greek diplomat probably associated with Seleucus, whose association with Chandragupta is strong but whose exact mission and dates are not securely known. The Arthashastra, discussed in Chapter 15, offers a detailed vision of economic administration but is a composite text; it is used in this chapter as normative and statecraft evidence, never as a Mauryan census. Modern archaeology has also insisted on studying rural and urban settlement together rather than treating urbanization as something that happened in cities alone.

A final piece of context concerns the term "Mauryan." Political authority did not have a single uniform character across the empire. Core regions were probably governed intensively; distant regions were connected by roads, officials, and inscriptions but may have retained large degrees of local arrangement. Keeping this variation in view is the discipline that runs through every section that follows.

Section 17.1

Agriculture and Revenue

Most people in Mauryan South Asia lived by agriculture, herding, or activities tied to them. The empire's courts, officials, armies, cities, craftworkers, roads, and monumental works were all ultimately supported by agricultural surplus: production beyond what cultivating households consumed, transferred by taxation, tribute, exchange, or coercion to those who did not farm. To ask how the Mauryan state worked economically is first to ask how such surplus was generated and how it reached the state.

The crops of the period varied by region. In the wetter Ganges plain and eastern regions rice was a major crop, and archaeological evidence for rice cultivation in the middle and lower Ganges is substantial for the first millennium BCE. Wheat and barley were important in the northwest and the drier western Ganges plain, as they had been since the farming communities traced in Chapter 2. Millets, pulses, oilseeds, sesame, and cotton were cultivated across drier plateau and western zones, and sugarcane, fruit, and vegetables belonged to the fuller agricultural repertoire that textual and archaeobotanical sources attest. Regional variation, not uniformity, is the rule; the monsoon-dependent rhythm described in Chapter 1 meant that the same imperial demand fell on economies with very different harvests and risks.

Iron tools, increasingly widespread since the later Vedic period (Chapter 7), supported forest clearance and cultivation in the Ganges basin, though, as earlier chapters noted, historians debate how decisive iron was compared with demographic and social factors. Animal traction, using oxen, and the plough were central to cultivation in many regions. Irrigation took several forms: river and canal water, tanks and reservoirs, wells, and water-lifting devices, along with the simple reliance on rain. Reliable irrigation evidence for the period is regionally uneven, and historians should avoid assuming that state-built irrigation was characteristic of the entire empire.

The relationship between the state and cultivation was one of dependence and interest rather than universal management. Rulers depended on cultivators for revenue, food supplies, and soldiers' provisioning; consequently they had strong reasons to encourage settlement, protect cultivators from disruption, and tax in ways that kept production going. Yet the Mauryan state did not necessarily direct farming operations. Most cultivating households managed their own land, labor, and cropping within local and kin arrangements, and even the Arthashastra's picture of crown lands under supervised cultivation (the sita lands) describes only one category alongside village lands. The claim that the Mauryan government owned or controlled all land is not supported by the evidence.

Revenue was drawn in several ways. The Arthashastra discusses a share of produce, often traditionally imagined as a sixth, along with other levies, but it should not be treated as proof of an empire-wide Mauryan rate; its numbers reflect normative statecraft, not a documented tax schedule applied everywhere. Contemporary Ashokan evidence is much thinner but more secure. The Lumbini inscription, examined in the Primary Source Window, shows that a share (bhaga) of village produce was a recognized category, and that a ruler could reduce it as a favor. That is contemporary evidence for the principle of a produce share and for royal discretion, not for a uniform rate.

Turning production into political power required storage, transport, and provisioning. Grain collected as revenue had to be stored, moved to courts and garrisons, and distributed to officials, soldiers, and workers. The Arthashastra imagines storehouses and treasuries, and a few early inscriptions, notably the Mahasthan record from Bengal, sometimes dated to the Mauryan period though its date is debated, mention grain storage for use in emergency, suggesting that stockpiling for famine relief was at least conceivable in some regions. A large army, of the kind Chapter 15 described, was possible only where surplus could be gathered and moved.

Water management offers a useful illustration. A much later inscription of Rudradaman, dating to about 150 CE, records that a Mauryan-era official in Saurashtra built the Sudarshana reservoir under Chandragupta and that a later official under Ashoka supplied it with channels. Because the source is roughly four centuries later, it must be used carefully; but it is consistent with the idea that in a productive and water-dependent region, imperial officials could work through local landscapes to shape irrigation. It is an example of the relationship among regional administration, water, and production, not evidence of a single Mauryan irrigation policy.

Historical Vocabulary
Surplus, Revenue, and Bhaga

Surplus is production beyond the immediate consumption of the producers; it is the material basis for specialists who do not farm. Revenue is the portion of that surplus, and of other resources, that a state extracts through taxes, tribute, labor demands, or other levies. Bhaga, "share," is a term used in early Indian sources for a portion of produce due to the ruler, while bali appears in some contexts as another kind of due or offering to the king; their exact operation varied by time and place.

These terms matter because they name how agricultural life connected to political power. Their use in Ashokan and later sources should not be read as evidence that a single uniform system of taxation operated everywhere.

Section 17.2

Villages and Rural Production

The Mauryan Empire was not primarily a collection of great cities. Rural settlements supported the entire system: they produced the food and raw materials on which towns and armies depended, supplied labor, and were the places where most people spent their lives. Yet villages leave the least direct trace in imperial documents, and much of what can be said comes from settlement archaeology and from indirect textual evidence.

A village of the period was a settlement of cultivating and herding households, often joined by specialist families such as potters, smiths, carpenters, and weavers who served the local community. Household production met most needs, but exchange with neighboring villages and market towns supplied salt, metals, fine goods, and tools. Villages also depended on shared resources: pasture, woodland, ponds, and water channels. Access to forests and grazing land was essential to pastoral households and to the fuel, timber, and fodder that agriculture needed, and it connects rural settlements to the frontier and forest worlds examined in Section 17.9.

Modern archaeology has stressed that rural and urban settlement must be studied together. Regional surveys in the Ganges plain and elsewhere show clusters of villages around larger centers, with sites of different sizes suggesting patterns of interaction, but they do not by themselves reveal who governed them. A settlement hierarchy, in other words, is a pattern of size and material culture, not necessarily an administrative chart.

The Arthashastra imagines villages of a stated size, headed by officials, with boundary rules and crown supervision. Such passages describe a statecraft ideal. Real settlement patterns varied considerably across the middle Ganges basin, the northwest, central India, western regions, the Deccan, and frontier landscapes, and there is no basis for a single standardized "Mauryan village." Local headmen, councils of elders, and kin-based authorities probably mediated between households and any imperial official, though evidence for them in the Mauryan period is indirect.

What then did imperial rule look like several days' travel from a provincial center? Often the honest answer is that historians know much less about what happened than about what rulers wanted officials to do. Ashokan edicts show that a king expected officials to tour, inspect, and report. They do not show how often, how effectively, or with what local consequences. In some regions, the presence of a rock edict, a road, or a resident official probably changed the local relationship between village and state; in others, a village may have encountered the empire mainly as an occasional revenue collector or as a demand for grain, labor, or transport animals.

The rural world was also unequal. Landholding cultivators, tenant and dependent laborers, herders, and landless workers occupied different positions, and debt, kinship, and local power shaped these positions. Sources rarely allow the internal life of villages to be reconstructed, and this section should be read as identifying what is known, what is inferred, and what remains invisible.

Section 17.3

Cities and Crafts

Cities were the second pillar of the Mauryan economy. Pataliputra, on the confluence of major rivers, was the capital; Taxila served the northwest; Ujjain occupied a strategic position on routes between the Ganges basin, the west coast, and the Deccan; Kaushambi and other towns of the Ganges and Yamuna region had been urban centers since earlier centuries. Mathura and other centers developed further in later periods and should be included only where evidence supports Mauryan-era occupation. These places were not creations of the Mauryas. They were part of the earlier urbanization traced in Chapter 9, into which the empire inserted political power, demand, and communication.

Pataliputra is described by Greek authors, drawing on Megasthenes, as a long, timber-palisaded city with many towers and gates, administered by a board of officials. At Bulandibagh, excavations exposed substantial wooden palisade remains conventionally associated with Pataliputra's fortifications; at Kumrahar, excavators found the remains of a large Mauryan-period pillared hall whose precise function remains debated. Together they give physical evidence for a monumental capital, though much of the ancient city lies beneath the modern one. At Taxila, occupation on the Bhir Mound spans the period, and the city's position made it a meeting point of Indian, Iranian, and Central Asian goods. Historians should avoid constructing a rigid "Mauryan city hierarchy": the evidence shows several important centers with different functions, not a planned imperial urban system.

Cities were centers of craft production. Ironworking was among the most important crafts, providing agricultural tools, weapons, and construction fittings. Copper and bronze were worked for vessels and ornaments; potters produced everyday wares and the distinctive Northern Black Polished Ware, a fine, lustrous pottery found widely across northern and central regions from the mid-first millennium BCE and which cannot be labeled "Mauryan" merely because it appears in a Mauryan-period context. Textile production was widespread and included cotton, silk-like fibers, and wool. Workers in wood, ivory, stone, shell, and semi-precious stones produced furniture, ornaments, beads, and sculpture. Mauryan-period stone working was remarkable: the polished sandstone pillars of Ashoka, discussed in Chapter 16, demonstrate highly skilled quarrying, transport, and finishing, and hint at the organization of specialist labor required for imperial projects. Food processing, brewing, oil pressing, and building trades rounded out the urban economy.

The key point is interdependence. Cities could exist because rural producers generated food surpluses; villages, in turn, depended on urban and itinerant specialists for metal tools, pottery, cloth, and services. The court and elite consumers created demand for luxury goods, while everyday demand from the wider population sustained the bulk of craft production. Archaeological evidence for specialized production, such as workshop debris, kilns, slag, and standardized forms, is therefore better evidence than later texts describing how crafts ought to be organized.

This distinction matters when discussing occupational organization. Later sources refer to shrenis, corporate bodies of merchants or craftworkers often translated as "guilds." The Arthashastra and Buddhist literature refer to such groups, and their importance in later periods is well established (see Chapters 25 and 27). But for the Mauryan period, historians should avoid assuming universal guilds. Craft groups clearly existed; whether they were formal corporate bodies with specified rights in a given Mauryan city is a separate question that the available evidence does not settle.

Section 17.4

Merchants and Trade Routes

Trade connected cultivators, craftworkers, cities, and regions. The routes of the Mauryan period followed geographic corridors already used for centuries. The most important ran from Pataliputra along the Ganges and Yamuna plain toward Taxila and the northwest, one of the broad northern corridors associated with the ancient term Uttarapatha, "northern route." A second connected the Ganges basin with central India and the Deccan through the Ujjain region, and a third reached the west, the Gujarat and Saurashtra ports. Eastern routes linked Kalinga and the Bay of Bengal coast. Rivers carried heavy goods; pack animals, oxcarts, and porters moved goods across land.

Merchants included local traders in market towns, specialist dealers, and long-distance travelers who moved goods through several regions. They bought agricultural and craft products, moved commodities such as salt, cloth, metals, horses from the northwest, elephants, timber, and precious and semi-precious stones, and connected regions with different endowments. Regional specialization was real: the northwest provided horses and links to Iran and Central Asia, the eastern coast offered access to elephants and coastal products, the west linked to sea trade, and the Ganges plain furnished grain and manufactured goods. Merchants also traveled with caravans where warranted by distance and risk, though the details of organization for this period are limited.

The northwest connected South Asia with Afghanistan, Iran, and the Hellenistic world, a connection prepared by the Achaemenid and Alexander-era developments of Chapter 14 and by Seleucid diplomacy under Chandragupta. The coast connected South Asia to Indian Ocean systems: archaeological research beyond the subcontinent increasingly shows exchange across the Bay of Bengal from the later first millennium BCE, including South Asian materials in Southeast Asian settlements. Sri Lanka, discussed in Chapter 24, maintained close ties with mainland South Asia. Later Sri Lankan Buddhist chronicles associate Ashoka with the transmission of Buddhism to the island; Ashoka's own inscriptions do not document the later tradition of the Mahinda and Sanghamitta mission.

This chapter should not project backward the mature Roman-era Indian Ocean trade of the first centuries CE, examined in Chapter 25, with its monsoon navigation, large-scale Mediterranean demand, and well-documented ports. What can be said for the Mauryan period is that transregional networks were developing, and that South Asian goods and people were moving across land and sea. Not all such movement was Mauryan imperial activity. Goods can travel far beyond the reach of any administration, and trade often followed merchants' initiatives rather than state design. The empire may have supported trade by maintaining order on major roads, provisioning way-stations, and taxing or regulating markets, and Greek sources refer to officials overseeing roads and markets. Ashoka's own inscriptions mention planting trees, digging wells, and building rest-houses along roads, suggesting a concern with travelers and movement, though again the extent of these works cannot be measured.

The result is a picture of a commercial world with strong regional and interregional links, on which the imperial state drew and which it partly shaped, but which did not originate with it.

Section 17.5

Coinage and Exchange

Coinage requires careful wording. Silver punch-marked coins, produced by stamping symbols onto pieces of silver of roughly standard weight, circulated in the Ganges valley and beyond from before the Mauryan period, as Chapter 9 explained. The Mauryan state did not invent coinage in India, and Chandragupta cannot be credited with creating it. What the Mauryan period shows is a wide circulation of punch-marked coins, with series that numismatists have associated with Mauryan authority on grounds of symbols, fabric, and find patterns. Copper punch-marked pieces of lower value also appear. Attribution and chronology are complex: not every punch-marked coin is Mauryan, and the identification of issuing authorities behind particular symbols remains an area of scholarly debate.

Coins were weighed pieces of metal, and their value depended in part on weight and metal content. The standard silver unit in later texts is the karshapana or pana. Exchange, however, was never exclusively monetary. Barter, payments in kind, labor obligations, and grain measures continued alongside coin. Even in places where coins circulated, many transactions, especially in villages, probably did not use them. Monetized and non-monetized exchange existed together.

A useful question for students is therefore: what does the existence of coinage prove, and what does it not prove? Coins can demonstrate exchange, standards of value, and connections among regions; they show that people valued and moved silver and copper in ways that made recurring pricing and payment possible. They do not by themselves prove a fully monetized economy, that the state paid everyone in cash, or that a single empire-wide monetary system operated identically in every region. A silver punch-marked coin found in a southern Deccan landscape, for example, can indicate interaction with northern networks without proving dense Mauryan administrative control there. The Arthashastra imagines mints, assayers, and officials of the treasury, but this is again statecraft description, and it cannot substitute for hoards and excavated finds.

The state's relationship to money is nonetheless important. Revenue collected in coin or grain, payments to officials and soldiers, and the purchase of goods for court and army required systems of measurement, storage, and accounting. The exact balance between cash and kind in these operations is unknown. What can be said is that a monetary economy existed within a wider mixed economy, and that the empire's fiscal operations relied on both.

Section 17.6

Officials and Administration

Chapter 15 asked how Chandragupta could govern an empire. This section asks a different question: what did administration mean economically and socially for the people being governed? Administration, in this sense, was the movement of information, taxes, orders, officials, supplies, and judgments across space, and its effectiveness varied by region.

The contemporary evidence is the Ashokan inscriptions. They mention several categories of officials. Mahamatras were high officials with varied duties, including provincial responsibilities and, in Ashoka's later reign, special roles connected with dhamma and with the care of particular groups. Rajukas are described as officials responsible for large numbers of people, with authority over rewards and punishments and, in Pillar Edict IV, a degree of independence in judicial matters. Other edicts refer to yuktas and pradeshikas who were expected to tour on a regular schedule to teach and inspect. Ashoka's edicts speak of reporters and of the importance of prompt reporting, indicating an intent to build channels of information, and they refer to princes and viceroys in regional centers, discussed in Chapter 15 and Chapter 16.

The Arthashastra offers an extensive taxonomy: superintendents of agriculture, weaving, markets, mines, warehouses, forests, elephants, and many other domains. Greek accounts also describe boards of officials in Pataliputra responsible for supervising crafts, foreigners, births and deaths, trade, and taxation on sales. These sources are not identical and should not be merged into one administrative chart. The Ashokan inscriptions are contemporary Mauryan evidence; the Arthashastra is a later or composite normative tradition that may preserve earlier ideas; Megasthenes is an outsider's report preserved second-hand. Where they overlap, that may reflect shared practice or shared conceptual habits; where they diverge, the divergence is itself evidence of the limits of each.

What administration meant for a subject depended on where he or she lived. Near Pataliputra and along major routes, officials, inspections, and the regulation of markets and crafts may have been visible. In regions like the southern Deccan, Ashokan edicts show that imperial messages reached the area and that some contact existed, yet archaeological surveys have found little sign of large-scale reorganization of settlement or production. The inscriptions demonstrate a reach of communication rather than a uniform depth of control.

For ordinary people, administration meant encounters with revenue collectors, demands for labor or transport, judgments in disputes, and, occasionally, welfare measures such as roadside wells or medical provisions mentioned in Ashoka's edicts. For officials, it meant balancing royal expectations, local power, and personal interest. The edicts themselves, with their repeated exhortations to officials, suggest that royal commands were not automatically obeyed. That is one of the most valuable things such a source can reveal.

Section 17.7

Social Hierarchy and Occupation

Mauryan society included Brahmans, rulers and warriors, cultivators, pastoralists, merchants, artisans, servants, laborers, dependent and enslaved persons, renunciants, officials, and forest communities. But it cannot be reduced to a tidy four-box diagram. As Chapters 6 and 7 explained, normative Brahmanical texts described society through the idealized categories of varna, and these categories had social force. Yet actual occupational and social organization was more complicated than four idealized varnas, and a great deal of local variation and mobility existed.

Ashokan inscriptions themselves reflect a more varied landscape. They address Brahmans and shramanas (renunciants of many traditions) together as recipients of respect and support, and refer to a wide range of people including relatives, servants, the elderly, and the poor. The edicts distinguish masters from servants and slaves (dasas) and hired workers (bhatakas), urging proper treatment of them; this is direct contemporary evidence of dependent and enslaved labor in the Mauryan sphere. Megasthenes, by contrast, is reported to have claimed that slavery did not exist in India, a claim that shows an outsider's misunderstanding, or perhaps a distinction between chattel slavery as he knew it and Indian forms of dependence. The Arthashastra regulates dasas extensively. Together, the sources indicate that servitude and dependence existed in various forms, and that observers disagreed about how to describe them.

Megasthenes' division of Indian society into seven groups is a celebrated case. In the surviving fragments, the groups are usually rendered as philosophers (which may include Brahmans and shramanas), farmers, herdsmen and hunters, artisans and traders, warriors, overseers or inspectors, and councillors or assessors. This is not the Mauryan caste system. It is a Greek attempt to categorize an unfamiliar society, and it appears to mix occupational, political, and functional distinctions. Why does Megasthenes see seven groups where Brahmanical texts describe four varnas? Several answers are plausible: he may have observed occupational groupings rather than ritual ones; he may have drawn on Greek and Egyptian models of classification; something may have been lost in translation; and his text has been transmitted only through later authors. The comparison illustrates that different observers organize a society differently, and that no single scheme captures the reality.

Keeping analytic categories distinct is essential. Varna refers to the four-fold ideal classification. Jati refers to numerous birth-based communities with specific occupations and marriage rules, whose developed form belongs largely to later centuries. Occupation refers to what people did. Caste is a modern English word that has been applied to varna, jati, or both. These do not map neatly onto one another, and the chapter should not assume that occupation was uniformly hereditary or that a specific jati system already operated across the empire.

Status also depended on wealth, land, office, military service, and religious standing. Brahmans held prestige through ritual and learning and could be priests, advisors, landholders, or teachers; renunciant communities, including Buddhists, Jains, and Ajivikas (Chapters 11 and 12), received royal patronage and had their own institutions. Merchants and craftworkers could accumulate wealth, and patronage records of the wider early historic period show them supporting religious institutions. Cultivators and laborers formed the majority but rarely appear as individuals. Mobility, both geographic and social, was possible but poorly documented.

Historical Vocabulary
Varna, Jati, and Occupation

Varna is the ancient ideal classification, in normative Brahmanical texts, of society into four broad categories: Brahmans, Kshatriyas, Vaishyas, and Shudras. Jati refers to the many endogamous, birth-defined communities, often associated with particular occupations, that became increasingly important in social organization over the following centuries. Occupation refers to the actual work a person or household performed. Modern English "caste" has been used for both varna and jati.

Students should not treat these terms as interchangeable, and should not assume that a Mauryan farmer, weaver, or merchant fit neatly into a fixed varna or jati. The terms name different ways of organizing and describing society, and their relationships changed across time and region.

Section 17.8

Women and Household Life

Evidence for women in Mauryan society is thin and uneven, and this section is disciplined about what it can and cannot say. The household was the basic unit of production and consumption for most people. Households organized labor for cultivation, animal care, food preparation, spinning and weaving, and childcare, and many households were connected by kinship, marriage, and property arrangements that varied by region and social group. Women's labor was central in agricultural and textile work, in food preparation, and in markets, though the precise division of labor cannot be reconstructed confidently for the Mauryan period.

Ashokan evidence includes references to members of the royal household, including the Queen's Edict, which orders that donations made by Ashoka's second queen Karuvaki, mother of Tivara, be credited to her, and to women's quarters. In Rock Edict XII, Ashoka refers to officials in charge of women's affairs, and a number of edicts describe the household of the king and other elite families. These references show that elite women held recognized roles, could make donations, and were the subject of official attention. They are evidence for a narrow social stratum, not for all women.

The Arthashastra contains rich material on marriage, property, inheritance, divorce, labor, prostitution, palace women, and textile production, including the employment of women in state-supervised weaving workshops. This material is valuable as normative and statecraft evidence and illustrates how a political thinker imagined regulating women's lives and labor. It cannot simply be described as how Mauryan women lived. It reflects a specific elite, male, Brahmanical perspective, and its rules may have applied unevenly or not at all outside the circles it addresses. Greek accounts add reports of royal women as guards and other details, but these are filtered through outside observers.

Women in religious communities are another area of evidence. Buddhist and Jain traditions, discussed in Chapters 11 and 12, included nuns and laywomen, and some women appear as donors in early inscriptions, though most of this evidence is from slightly later periods. Servants and dependent women, often unnamed, performed domestic and other labor, and the sources mention them only in passing.

This section therefore separates elite and normative prescription from ordinary lived experience. It would be misleading to compose a detailed "day in the life of a Mauryan woman" from these sources. The better history admits where evidence is thin. What can be established is that women were economically productive, that elite women had visible roles, that texts regulated women's conduct and property in various ways, and that the experience of women varied by class, region, and community in ways the surviving evidence cannot fully capture.

Section 17.9

Forest Peoples and Imperial Frontiers

Empire maps create a visual illusion: solid colors suggest uniformly governed territory. In reality, large parts of the Mauryan sphere consisted of forests, hills, pastoral zones, and other landscapes where agrarian states had little direct presence. These zones were home to communities the sources call atavikas, "forest people" or "forest dwellers," and they were essential to the economy and to imperial politics.

Forests supplied timber for construction, shipbuilding, and fuel; elephants, essential to armies, as Chapter 15 described; minerals, honey, resins, lac, hides, and other products; and routes that crossed non-agrarian landscapes. The Arthashastra devotes attention to elephant forests and forest superintendents and to the management of forest products, again as statecraft prescription. Kalinga and other eastern and central forested regions were important sources of elephants and forest products, but Ashoka's inscriptions do not identify resource acquisition as the motive for the Kalinga war. Hunters, gatherers, pastoralists, shifting cultivators, and hill communities supplied these goods, exchanged with agrarian populations, guided travelers, or, in some circumstances, raided or blocked routes.

Ashoka's Rock Edict XIII is especially important. After expressing remorse over the suffering of the Kalinga war, the king nonetheless addresses the forest peoples within his domain, reminding them of his power to punish while urging them to change their ways and avoid the consequences. This shows that frontier governance remained coercive as well as moral, and that the king was aware of forest communities as a political issue. It also shows that these communities were within his concern, not outside it.

Forest peoples should not be described as "tribes outside civilization." They were economic and political actors whose relations with agrarian states varied: they might supply goods in return for salt, metal, or cloth; pay tribute or serve as auxiliaries; negotiate over access to resources; or resist demands. The expansion of cultivation and of state authority could bring conflict over land and forest. Later chapters, including the colonial discussions in Chapters 68 and 73, will show how relationships between states and forest communities continued to shape South Asian history, but the Mauryan case should be understood in its own terms.

Was the frontier a boundary or a zone of interaction? The evidence favors the latter. Direct imperial control probably varied dramatically from one landscape to another: closer in the core river plains, negotiated in forest and hill zones, and mediated by inscriptions, officials, or alliances in distant regions. The frontier was a set of overlapping relationships—economic, military, and political—rather than a line on a map.

Historical Vocabulary
Atavika and Frontier Zone

Atavika is a term used in Ashokan and other early sources for communities of forest or wooded regions. It is not an ethnic label but a descriptive category referring to people living in and drawing resources from forest landscapes. A frontier zone is an area where the presence and authority of a state is partial, negotiated, or contested, and where different economic systems interact.

Both terms help students avoid treating imperial borders as fixed lines and forest communities as passive or primitive. They were participants in the empire's economy and politics.

Section 17.10

Review and Historical Synthesis

This chapter has asked what actually sustained the Mauryan Empire. The answer is not merely that Chandragupta conquered it, nor merely that Ashoka governed morally. It is a combination: agricultural surplus, village production, urban centers and crafts, trade routes, coin and non-coin exchange, administrative extraction, transport, occupational specialization, household labor, and negotiation with regional and frontier populations.

Section 17.1 established that agriculture supported the state without being directly managed by it, and that revenue, storage, and transport converted surplus into power, while cautioning against treating the Arthashastra's tax rates as empire-wide fact. Section 17.2 corrected the impression of an empire of great cities by placing villages at its foundation, and showed how little is known about rural life beyond provincial centers. Section 17.3 traced the interdependence of cities and villages and the significance of craft specialization, without assuming universal guilds. Section 17.4 mapped the corridors and merchants that connected regions, distinguishing developing transregional networks from the later Roman-era trade. Section 17.5 clarified the limits of what coinage proves.

Section 17.6 separated contemporary Ashokan evidence, Greek reports, and the composite Arthashastra tradition, and showed that administrative reach and depth were different things. Section 17.7 argued that social hierarchy cannot be reduced to four varnas, and used Megasthenes' seven groups as a lesson in source method. Section 17.8 showed how thin evidence for women's lives is and distinguished elite prescription from lived experience. Section 17.9 restored forest peoples and frontier zones as economic and political actors.

The bigger conclusion is that the Mauryan Empire was a political structure resting on many pre-existing regional economies and communities. Imperial power connected, taxed, supervised, and sometimes reorganized those worlds, but it did not make them uniform. Central capacity was substantial, but territorial control was uneven. This conclusion prevents the mistake of crediting the Mauryas with inventing every feature of the early historic economy, and also the opposite mistake of treating imperial power as irrelevant to it.

This synthesis quietly sets up a question. If an empire depends on moving resources, information, officials, and military power through a large and varied landscape, what happens when the center becomes weaker? That question belongs to Chapter 18, which examines the Mauryas after Ashoka, problems of succession, provincial autonomy, and fiscal and military limits, and the empire's legacies. The Mauryan state connected an extraordinary range of regions and communities, but those connections required constant flows of revenue, information, officials, supplies, and military power. The very scale that made the empire exceptional also created problems of coordination and control. Chapter 18 asks what happened when the political center could no longer sustain those relationships.

Primary Source Window

Two Kinds of Evidence: Ashoka's Lumbini Pillar Inscription and the Arthashastra

Sources: (1) Lumbini Pillar inscription, Mauryan period, Brahmi script and Prakrit language, at Lumbini in the Nepal Terai; (2) passages on economic administration in the Arthashastra, a Sanskrit statecraft text of composite and debated date · Type: (1) royal inscription; (2) normative statecraft treatise

Context: The Lumbini inscription is a short record, carved on a pillar and attributed to Ashoka, commemorating a visit to the birthplace of the Buddha. Its administrative interest lies in a provision concerning the village: the final clause is commonly interpreted as exempting the village from one kind of due and reducing its revenue share. The Arthashastra, discussed in Chapter 15, contains detailed prescriptions for superintendents, revenue, and the management of crown resources. It is not a record of what happened but a text about what a ruler should do.

Paraphrase: The Lumbini inscription records that the king came to honor the place of the Buddha's birth. Its final clause is commonly interpreted as exempting Lumbini from bali and reducing its revenue share to one-eighth, though interpretations of some terms have varied. The Arthashastra, by contrast, describes in general terms the officers, accounts, and revenue sources that a ruler should organize, and presents crown resources as things to be carefully supervised and increased.

Analysis: The inscription is contemporary and specific: it is commonly interpreted as evidence that a produce share was a recognized category, that a village could be favored for religious reasons, and that a ruler could adjust it. It does not reveal the typical rate or how many villages paid. The Arthashastra reveals how political thinkers conceived economic administration, but it cannot be used to prove that Mauryan officials operated in exactly those ways. Reading the two together shows the difference between what a king proclaimed and what a statecraft author thought rulers should do.

Questions for students:

  1. What does the Lumbini inscription say about the relationship between revenue and royal favor?
  2. Who created each source, and for what possible purpose?
  3. What can historians learn from each, and what can they not establish?
  4. Why is it methodologically important not to merge the two into a single account of Mauryan taxation?
Historical Debate

How Centralized Was the Mauryan Economy and Administration?

The question: Was the Mauryan Empire a highly centralized state that regulated much of economic life through a dense bureaucracy, or a looser political framework that exercised intensive control in some regions and light supervision elsewhere?

Interpretation A — A powerful, extensive administration: Historians who emphasize the scale of Mauryan power point to the extensive officials named in inscriptions, the detailed administrative categories of the Arthashastra and Greek reports, the road networks, the circulation of punch-marked coinage, and the geographic spread of Ashoka's edicts, arguing that the empire had substantial capacity to tax, supervise, and mobilize.
Interpretation B — Uneven control with strong cores and corridors: Others stress that imperial control was intensive in core regions and along key routes but much lighter in peripheral zones, where local economies and political arrangements continued. Archaeological work in parts of the southern Deccan, for instance, finds inscriptions and some coin evidence of Mauryan contact but little sign of sweeping imperial investment or reorganization.

Evidence: Both interpretations use Ashokan inscriptions, coins, settlement archaeology, Greek accounts, and the Arthashastra, but differ in how much weight to give normative texts and how to read the absence of dense archaeological change in some regions.

Why it matters: The answer shapes how historians understand imperial collapse, the nature of early Indian statehood, and the relationship between political power and economic life. This textbook's conclusion is that central capacity was substantial but territorial control was uneven.

Student task: Identify one piece of evidence that supports each interpretation and explain what additional evidence, such as regional excavation data, would help resolve the question.

Multiple Perspectives

Living within the Mauryan Empire: Reconstructed Perspectives

Note: these perspectives are reconstructed analytical illustrations based on patterns in the evidence, not quotations from named individuals. The groups did not hold equal power or freedom of action.

A cultivator in the Ganges basin: Faced seasonal cycles of planting and harvest, and the periodic demand for a share of produce or other dues. Close to major routes and towns, such a household might sell surplus in markets, but also lived closest to the reach of imperial officials.
An artisan in an urban center: Produced tools, cloth, or ornaments for local buyers and elites, depended on rural producers for food, and might benefit from the demand created by a court and army, while facing regulation of markets and crafts where officials were active.
A merchant on a major route: Moved goods across regions, dealt with different local authorities and tolls, and benefited from relative order on main roads, while remaining exposed to risks that no administration could remove.
A provincial official: Was expected to collect, inspect, and report, but depended on local intermediaries, faced difficult distances, and negotiated between royal expectations and local power.
A woman managing household production: Contributed labor in agriculture, textile work, and food preparation, and lived within kinship and property arrangements that varied by community; the sources say little about her own view.
A forest-community leader: Negotiated access to resources, supplied or withheld forest products, and dealt with imperial demands that could combine incentives and threats, as Rock Edict XIII suggests.
Continuity and Change Over Time

From the Second Urbanization and Nandas to the Mauryas

What Changed

  • Unprecedented imperial scale, connecting the Ganges plain with the northwest, west, east, and parts of the Deccan
  • Wider administrative coordination, and greater movement of officials, information, and resources
  • Stronger integration of distant regions into a common political framework, evidenced by inscriptions across a wide geographic range
  • New royal ideologies of welfare and dhamma that addressed economic and social life

What Stayed the Same

  • Agriculture as the foundation of production and revenue
  • Cities, long-distance trade, and craft specialization already developing since the Second Urbanization
  • Punch-marked coinage and mixed monetary and non-monetary exchange
  • Regional markets and local social organization, which continued to shape daily life

Who experienced change most directly? Officials, capital-region residents, and communities along imperial routes probably experienced the most change, while many villages and frontier communities may have experienced more continuity. Why the pattern matters: It prevents crediting the Mauryas with inventing the early historic economy while still recognizing what imperial scale changed.

Cause and Effect

How Did Economic Capacity Make Empire Possible?

Long-term causes

Agricultural expansion in the Ganges basin, iron technology, growing towns, merchant networks, and punch-marked coinage created a productive base before the Mauryas.

Short-term causes

The Nanda inheritance of revenue and military strength, and the conquests of Chandragupta, gave the Mauryas access to these resources and to new regions.

Event or transformation, and immediate effects

Agricultural surplus fed revenue, revenue supported officials and armies, and roads and supply lines enabled territorial power, while imperial security and infrastructure in turn supported movement and exchange.

Long-term consequences

A durable model of empire dependent on many regional economies, whose strengths and limits shaped later states, Chapter 18 and beyond.

Historical complexity: The causal chain ran in both directions, but state expansion did not automatically create prosperity, and economic capacity alone does not explain political success. Environment, military organization, ideology, and regional cooperation also mattered.

Everyday Life and Social History

Who Produced the Resources of Empire?

The resources of empire were produced by cultivators who planted and harvested, craftworkers who shaped metal, clay, cloth, and stone, merchants and transporters who moved goods, household members who supplied unpaid and paid labor, servants and dependent workers, officials who assessed and recorded, pastoralists who managed herds, and forest communities who supplied timber, elephants, and other products. Rulers and inscriptions occupy the foreground of the sources, but these groups made the empire's material life possible.

Because most surviving sources reflect elite, royal, or outside perspectives, historians must read them for indirect evidence and combine them with archaeology. Diet, housing, clothing, health, debt, and the rhythm of seasons and festivals mattered greatly to daily life, but they can often only be reconstructed in outline. Approaching these questions with curiosity and appropriate caution is the historical skill this chapter asks students to practice.

Chapter Summary

This chapter changed the perspective from the palace to the fields, villages, workshops, roads, and frontiers that sustained Mauryan imperial power. Its central argument is that Mauryan power depended on agrarian production, village communities, urban craft specialization, merchants and transport, revenue and administration, social differentiation, household labor, and relationships with forest and frontier populations, and that these foundations varied across the empire.

Agriculture was the economic base. Surplus, transferred through revenue and other means, supported courts, officials, armies, cities, and transport, though the state depended on rather than directly managed most agriculture. Regional crops and farming methods varied widely, and the chapter cautioned against treating Arthashastra tax rates as empire-wide facts. The Lumbini inscription provides rare contemporary evidence for a produce share and royal discretion.

Villages formed the foundation of the empire, yet they are the least documented of its parts. No single standardized Mauryan village can be reconstructed, and historians know far more about what rulers wanted than about what happened far from provincial centers.

Cities such as Pataliputra, Taxila, and Ujjain were centers of craft production and exchange, and were connected to villages in relationships of interdependence. The chapter distinguished archaeological evidence of specialized production from later textual prescriptions, and warned against assuming universal guilds.

Merchants and routes connected the Ganges plain with the northwest, west, east, central India, and the Deccan. The chapter distinguished developing transregional networks from the mature Roman-era Indian Ocean trade, and emphasized that goods could move beyond imperial administration.

Coinage was a real and important feature of exchange, but it predated the Mauryas, and its existence does not prove a fully monetized economy or a uniform empire-wide monetary system. Monetized and non-monetized exchange coexisted.

Administration was examined through Ashokan inscriptions, kept distinct from the Arthashastra and Greek accounts. Administration meant the movement of information, taxes, orders, officials, supplies, and judgments, with effectiveness that varied regionally.

Social hierarchy could not be reduced to four varnas. Megasthenes' seven groups illustrate an outsider's classification, and varna, jati, occupation, and caste were kept analytically distinct. Evidence for women and household life is thin and mostly elite or normative, and the chapter separated prescription from lived experience.

Forest peoples and frontier zones were economic and political actors. Rock Edict XIII shows that frontier governance combined moral rhetoric with the threat of force. The frontier is best understood as a zone of interaction.

The chapter concluded that the Mauryan Empire rested on many pre-existing regional economies and communities, which it connected, taxed, supervised, and sometimes reorganized, without making them uniform. That conclusion prepares Chapter 18's question of what happens when the political center can no longer sustain those relationships.

Mapping and Interpreting the Economic Landscapes of the Mauryan Sphere

This project asks you to build a regional economic case study of one part of the Mauryan sphere, using archaeological, epigraphic, and numismatic evidence.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows that empires are more than the actions of rulers. Behind the famous inscriptions and the dynastic narrative of Chapters 15 and 16 lay cultivators, craftworkers, merchants, households, officials, and forest communities whose labor and relationships made imperial power possible, and whose experiences varied enormously from one region to another.

It also reveals a larger process: the way states in early South Asia built upon existing regional economies rather than replacing them. The Mauryan Empire connected, taxed, and supervised worlds that had their own histories, and this pattern of layered and uneven authority would recur in later empires, from the Guptas to the Mughals, as later chapters show.

The chapter's methodological lesson is equally lasting. No single source gives "Mauryan society." Archaeology, inscriptions, coins, Greek accounts, and normative texts do not always describe the same place, people, or moment, and responsible history requires holding them side by side without collapsing them. Students will need this habit in every later chapter.

Chapter 18 now asks what happened when the political center could no longer sustain the flows of revenue, officials, information, and force that this chapter has described, and how the empire's fragmentation shaped its long-term legacies.
