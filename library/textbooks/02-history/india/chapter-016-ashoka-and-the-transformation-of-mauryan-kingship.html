Part IV — The Mauryan Age
16

Ashoka and the Transformation of Mauryan Kingship

Indian History: South Asia from the Indus Civilization to the Present

Chapter Opening

Chandragupta created the Mauryan imperial framework, and Bindusara demonstrated that it could survive its founder. Ashoka inherited that political system. What makes his reign exceptional is not that he invented Mauryan empire, but that he left an unprecedented body of inscriptions through which a South Asian ruler attempted to explain publicly how kingship ought to be exercised.

On a rock face near the Bay of Bengal, at a place now called Dhauli in Odisha, and on boulders and pillars scattered from the northwestern frontier to the Deccan, a king speaks. He calls himself "Beloved of the Gods" and "Piyadasi," he addresses officials, subjects, frontier peoples, and neighboring kings, and he says things no earlier ruler of South Asia is known to have said in writing: that conquest causes suffering, that a ruler should examine his own conduct, that religious communities should honor one another, and that the highest victory is one won by moral means. The rulers of earlier South Asian states left coins, treasures, ruins, and later memory. Ashoka left messages, addressed to the living, carved in stone.

This chapter studies that king and those messages. Chapter 15 traced how Chandragupta Maurya and Bindusara built a large territorial empire centered on Pataliputra, with provincial governors, an army, a revenue system, and diplomatic ties to the Hellenistic world. Ashoka took over that machinery. His reign, which lasted roughly four decades in the middle of the third century BCE, did not create the Mauryan state, dissolve its army, or abandon its taxes. What changed, as this chapter argues, was the public language of kingship: the way a Mauryan ruler explained his purposes, described his duties, and addressed his subjects.

The chapter's central question is therefore twofold. First, how did Ashoka use conquest, inscriptions, dhamma, and religious patronage to redefine Mauryan kingship? Second, how should historians distinguish the ruler visible in his own inscriptions from the Ashoka remembered in later Buddhist traditions, which credit him with councils, missions, relic distributions, and thousands of stupas? The two Ashokas overlap but are not identical, and much of the chapter's work lies in keeping them apart.

The chapter's thesis can be stated plainly. After the conquest of Kalinga, Ashoka's inscriptions presented "conquest by dhamma," moral self-discipline, welfare, restraint, and concern for diverse religious communities as central duties of rule. Ashoka was personally committed to Buddhism, yet the dhamma proclaimed across the empire was broader than Buddhist doctrine. It is best understood as an imperial ethical and political program rather than simply as a campaign to convert his subjects. Keeping that distinction in view prevents several familiar simplifications: that Ashoka converted instantly at Kalinga, that he became a pacifist, that he made the empire Buddhist, or that he practiced modern secularism.

Ashoka's inscriptions are also a methodological gift. Chapter 1 explained that epigraphy offers some of the most valuable contemporary documentation for ancient South Asia. Chapter 15 showed how heavily Chandragupta is mediated by later literary sources. Ashoka is different, because historians possess documents issued during his own reign. But this chapter insists that royal proclamations are not transparent autobiography. They were composed for political purposes, produced by officials, scribes, and stoneworkers, and directed at particular audiences. They tell us what the king wished to be understood, not necessarily everything that happened or everything he felt.

The chapter follows this sequence: Ashoka's accession and the limits of what can be known about him before the inscriptions begin; the Kalinga War and the remorse expressed in Major Rock Edict XIII; the inscriptions as a distributed, multilingual corpus of rock and pillar edicts; the concept of dhamma; the evidence for Ashoka's Buddhist commitment; the relationship between religious respect and imperial administration; and the long afterlife of Ashoka in memory and historiography. Chapter 17 will then turn from royal proclamations to the society and economy that sustained the Mauryan world, and Chapter 18 will examine the empire's end.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain what can and cannot be established about Ashoka's accession, and why later Buddhist accession narratives must be distinguished from contemporary evidence.
  2. Describe the Kalinga War and explain why its casualty figures are royal claims inscribed by Ashoka rather than audited demographic counts.
  3. Analyze Major Rock Edict XIII as both an ethical statement and an instrument of kingship, including its continuing assertion of coercive royal power.
  4. Distinguish the Major Rock Edicts, Minor Rock Edicts, Kalinga Separate Edicts, and Pillar Edicts as parts of a distributed inscriptional corpus.
  5. Explain why Ashoka's inscriptions use different languages, scripts, and regional versions, including Prakrit, Brahmi, Kharoshthi, Greek, and Aramaic.
  6. Define dhamma as an imperial ethical program and contrast it with Buddhist doctrine.
  7. Evaluate the inscriptional evidence for Ashoka's personal Buddhist commitment, and distinguish it from later traditions about councils, missions, relics, and 84,000 stupas.
  8. Assess Ashoka's policies of religious respect and the role of the dhamma-mahamatras without describing Ashoka as a modern secularist.
  9. Compare the Ashoka of the inscriptions, the Ashoka of Buddhist literary memory, and the Ashoka reconstructed by modern historians.
  10. Identify what changed under Ashoka and what remained continuous from Chandragupta and Bindusara.
  11. Interpret royal inscriptions critically by asking who created them, for whom, and what perspectives they omit.

Historical Context

Chapters 9 and 10 traced the rise of Magadha from the competition among the mahajanapadas of the Ganges valley, and Chapter 15 traced the Mauryan seizure of that inheritance from the Nandas. By the time of Bindusara, the Mauryan state controlled the Gangetic plain and extended over very large parts of the subcontinent, with its capital at Pataliputra, provincial centers, a substantial army, a system of revenue collection, and diplomatic contact with the Hellenistic kingdoms that had emerged from the wars of Alexander's successors (Chapter 14). This was a new kind of political formation in South Asia: an empire whose center could reach, however unevenly, across peoples who spoke different languages, followed different customs, and lived in very different environments.

Governing such a space raised problems that earlier, smaller states had not faced. How could a court in the Gangetic plain communicate with officials and subjects on the northwestern frontier, in the forested interior, on the Deccan plateau, and along the eastern coast? How could an empire reassure populations conquered by force, or restrain officials who acted harshly on the king's behalf? How could a ruler present his authority to communities of Brahmans, Buddhist and Jain monks, ascetics, farmers, merchants, and forest peoples who did not share a single religious identity? These problems form the background against which Ashoka's inscriptions should be read.

The religious environment described in Chapters 11 through 13 is equally important. Buddhism, Jainism, Ajivika and other shramana communities, and Brahmanical traditions coexisted and competed for patronage and prestige. Renunciants and Brahmans were both recognized as bearers of religious authority, and rulers gained legitimacy by supporting them. Ashoka's references to Brahmans and shramanas, which recur across his inscriptions, presuppose this pluralistic world. They should not be read as the simple imposition of one religion on subjects who lacked religious options.

The evidence for Ashoka's reign is unusual among the sources for ancient South Asia. The inscriptions are contemporary, datable by regnal years, and speak in the first person of the king. They are supplemented by archaeological remains, by references in Hellenistic sources to Mauryan diplomacy, and by much later Buddhist literature, including the Sanskrit Ashokavadana and the Pali chronicles of Sri Lanka such as the Dipavamsa and Mahavamsa. Those later texts are valuable as evidence for how Buddhist communities remembered Ashoka, but they were composed centuries after his death and pursue their own religious purposes. This chapter treats the inscriptions as the primary evidence for Ashoka's reign and later tradition as a separate, secondary stream of evidence.

Section 16.1

Ashoka's Accession

Chapter 15 ended with the death of Bindusara and the transfer of the Mauryan throne. Ashoka's accession is conventionally placed in the 270s BCE, with his formal coronation usually dated to around 268 BCE. These dates are reconstructions. Ashoka's own inscriptions count events by years of his reign, and they do not state a date in any external calendar. Historians reach BCE figures by combining regnal years with references in Ashoka's inscriptions to Hellenistic kings whose reigns can be dated, and with later regnal lists whose reliability is uneven. The result is a chronology that is widely used but should be described as approximate, especially for the earliest years.

What can be said about Ashoka before the inscriptions? Very little that rests on contemporary evidence. Later Buddhist traditions offer rich and dramatic stories. In these narratives Ashoka is a prince who serves as a provincial governor, in some accounts at Ujjain or in the northwest, then wins the throne through a struggle with his brothers. Some versions describe him killing many of his brothers. They portray the early Ashoka as violent and cruel, sometimes under the epithet Chandashoka, "Ashoka the Fierce," in contrast with the pious Dharmashoka, "Ashoka the Righteous," he later became. Ministers are said to have chosen him, and miraculous signs foretell his greatness.

These narratives belong principally to later Buddhist literary tradition and should not be treated as straightforward biography. Their structure is instructive: they present a moral drama of transformation, from a wicked king to a righteous one, that suited the religious purposes of the communities that preserved them. A story of dramatic conversion is doing something different from a chronicle of events. Some details may preserve memory of a real succession dispute, and the fact that Ashoka's inscriptions never discuss his rise to power is not proof that it was peaceful. But the specific claims about fratricide, early cruelty, and miraculous election cannot be confirmed from contemporary evidence.

Ashoka's own inscriptions do give one relevant piece of contemporary evidence about his family. They refer to the king's brothers and sisters, and to other relatives and households, among the persons whose welfare his officials are to attend to. This sits uneasily with the tale of wholesale fratricide, though it does not disprove that some conflict occurred. It illustrates the general problem: the inscriptions are silent on the events that most interest later narrators, because a royal proclamation addressed to subjects has purposes other than autobiography.

The key question of this section is therefore what can be established about Ashoka before his inscriptions begin speaking in his own royal voice. The honest answer is that he was a member of the ruling Mauryan house, that he succeeded Bindusara, and that he inherited an empire whose institutions were already in place. This contrasts with the situation examined in Chapter 15. Chandragupta is heavily mediated by later literary sources, Greek, Jain, Buddhist, and Brahmanical. Ashoka gives historians something unusually valuable: royal inscriptions issued during his own reign. Yet students should not conclude that the inscriptions solve every historical problem. They are royal proclamations serving political purposes, and the historian's task is to read them as such.

Section 16.2

The Kalinga War

Ashoka's inscriptions report one military campaign in his own reign: the conquest of Kalinga, which took place in the eighth year after his coronation. Kalinga lay on the eastern coast of the subcontinent, in a region corresponding broadly to parts of present-day Odisha and adjoining territory. Its position mattered strategically. It lay between the Gangetic core of the Mauryan state and the eastern routes leading south toward peninsular India, and it controlled coastal access and overland routes along the Bay of Bengal. A Mauryan empire that dominated the Ganges plain and much of the interior but not this coastal region had an obvious gap in its territorial control. Kalinga had earlier been associated with independent political power, and later traditions and inscriptions of the region preserve its distinctive identity.

Ashoka presents Kalinga as a successful conquest, not a military defeat. This is worth stating plainly, because many modern retellings suggest that Ashoka was overwhelmed by the sight of the battlefield and turned away from empire. The inscriptions say something different. They report that Kalinga was conquered, that many people were killed and deported, and that the king afterward felt remorse. The victory itself is not questioned. The empire's control over Kalinga continued, and later Ashokan inscriptions were carved at sites in the region, notably Dhauli and Jaugada, where separate edicts address the officials responsible for governing conquered Kalinga.

Major Rock Edict XIII is the essential source. It reports the scale of the human cost in numbers that are often quoted: roughly 150,000 people deported, 100,000 killed, and many times that number dead from other causes during and after the conflict. These figures should be taught as Ashoka's own inscribed royal claims, not as an audited demographic count. They may reflect real scale, rhetorical emphasis, or a combination of both. No independent census, casualty list, or administrative record survives to test them. What the numbers reveal with confidence is that Ashoka chose to publicize the suffering caused by his conquest, a choice that was itself a political act.

Students should avoid the familiar and tidy formulation: Ashoka saw the battlefield, immediately converted to Buddhism, and renounced war forever. That version compresses separate streams of evidence into one dramatic episode. The inscriptions connect Kalinga with remorse. They do not describe a conversion on the battlefield. Ashoka's Buddhist commitment has its own inscriptional evidence and its own chronology, examined in Section 16.7, and Minor Rock Edict I indicates that his engagement with the Buddhist community developed over a period of time. Nor do the inscriptions say that Ashoka abandoned war, disbanded the army, or renounced the exercise of coercive power. Section 16.3 examines what they do say.

Kalinga also illustrates the interplay of evidence types. At Dhauli and Jaugada, the sites in Kalinga itself, Major Rock Edicts XI, XII, and XIII are omitted, while Edicts I–X and XIV are retained and two Separate Kalinga Edicts are added. Those edicts address the officials of the newly incorporated region, urging them to govern fairly, to avoid unjust imprisonment and harassment, and to treat the people as the king's children. This suggests that the king used different messages for different audiences. A general statement of remorse could be broadcast to the wider empire, while conquered Kalinga received administrative guidance that spoke directly to how officials should behave there.

Section 16.3

Remorse and Royal Ideology

What does it mean when a conquering emperor publicly expresses regret for conquest? Major Rock Edict XIII should be analyzed as both evidence of a striking ethical claim and as an instrument of kingship. In it Ashoka expresses sorrow for the death, deportation, and separation of families caused by the conquest of Kalinga. He notes that such suffering fell on Brahmans, shramanas, householders, and others, including people who were themselves devoted to their relatives and friends. He states that the king now regards the loss of even a fraction of those killed or deported as a grave matter. He declares that the greatest conquest is the conquest by dhamma, and he says that he has achieved such conquest among neighboring peoples and far beyond his borders, naming Hellenistic kings and distant kingdoms as places where his message reached.

But the edict does not announce the abolition of royal coercion. The same inscription reminds potentially disobedient peoples, especially forest communities within and near the empire, that the king still possesses the power to punish them. It says that Ashoka is forgiving toward wrongdoers as far as he can be, but it warns that he retains power, and it urges these peoples to change their ways so that they are not killed. Modern scholarship therefore stresses that the post-Kalinga rhetoric combines compassion with continuing assertions of imperial authority. It would be a serious misreading to conclude that Ashoka relinquished the state's monopoly on force.

This prevents the chapter from becoming the simplistic story of a violent king who became a pacifist saint. A better description of the transformation is a sequence: military conquest → public critique of the human costs of conquest → preference for "conquest by dhamma" → continued royal power. Each stage is documented in the inscriptions. Ashoka did not say that war was always wrong. He said that this conquest caused grievous suffering, that he felt regret, and that he now valued moral victory over military victory. He did not dissolve the army, nor is there evidence that the empire abandoned its capacity for force. The state continued to tax, to punish, to guard its frontiers, and to demand obedience.

Students should also be careful about Ashoka's private psychology. Historians have an inscription declaring royal remorse. They do not have access to Ashoka's internal emotional state independent of that political text. The edict may express sincere feeling. It may equally serve to legitimize a new style of rule, to reassure conquered peoples, to distinguish Ashoka from his predecessors, and to instruct officials. Royal ideology and personal conviction are not mutually exclusive, and the surviving evidence cannot cleanly separate them. The question worth asking is not "was the remorse real?" but "what work did the public expression of remorse do in Mauryan kingship?"

One answer is that it reframed the ethical justification of power. Earlier Mauryan kingship, as reconstructed in Chapter 15, rested heavily on conquest, dynastic authority, control of revenue, and command of an army. Ashoka's inscriptions do not repudiate these foundations, but they add a new language in which the ruler answers publicly for the consequences of his actions. The ideal of a king who examines his own conduct and regards his subjects as his children recurs across the Pillar Edicts and Separate Edicts. Whether or not Kalinga caused this shift, Edict XIII became its most famous articulation.

Section 16.4

The Major Rock Edicts

Students should understand that the Major Rock Edicts are a distributed corpus, not one book carved identically everywhere. They consist of a group of fourteen proclamations, numbered by modern scholars, inscribed on natural rock surfaces at sites spread across the subcontinent. The proclamations address such subjects as restrictions on animal slaughter, medical care for humans and animals, the appointment of dhamma officials, tours of inspection, the ideal of dhamma conquest, and mutual respect among religious communities. Not every site carries all fourteen edicts, and versions differ in wording, ordering, and emphasis. Some inscriptions appear near roads, settlements, or sacred places. Others stand at sites whose exact audience is difficult to reconstruct.

The language and script of the inscriptions vary by region. Much of the corpus is composed in a Prakrit, a Middle Indo-Aryan dialect, with regional variations that reflect local speech and scribal practice. Most inscriptions in the subcontinent are written in Brahmi script, which became ancestral to later South Asian scripts, as Chapter 1 noted. In the northwest, at sites such as Shahbazgarhi and Mansehra, the script is Kharoshthi, a script derived from Aramaic and associated with the Gandhara region. In the far northwest, at Kandahar, Ashokan material survives in Greek and Aramaic. These versions demonstrate the adaptation of imperial communication to regional audiences, in a frontier that had been shaped by Achaemenid administration and Greek settlement (Chapter 14). They also show that Ashoka's message circulated in multiple registers.

This raises a question: why would an emperor communicate publicly in different languages, scripts, and regional versions rather than issue one uniform text? The answer opens onto imperial communication, audience, translation, and regional governance. A uniform text would have assumed a uniform audience. The Mauryan empire did not have one. Communities in Gandhara, the Gangetic plain, the western coast, and the Deccan used different speech forms and, in some places, different writing traditions. Using local languages and scripts made the message legible where it was carved. The Greek versions, in particular, were composed in a philosophical vocabulary suited to Greek-speaking readers, and they translated the concept of dhamma into terms such as piety. Translation was not merely technical; it involved choices about how to present royal ideas to communities with different conceptual frameworks.

Students also need to learn Ashoka's titulature. In the inscriptions the king normally calls himself Devanampiya Piyadasi, usually translated "Beloved of the Gods, Piyadasi" (or "of Gracious Countenance"). Most inscriptions use this title without giving a personal name. For centuries this made the identification of the author uncertain. The Sri Lankan chronicles had preserved the name Ashoka, and in the nineteenth century scholars connected the name Piyadasi in the edicts with the Mauryan king. The connection was confirmed decisively when a Minor Rock Edict at Maski, in the Deccan, was found to use the personal name Ashoka together with the royal title Devanampiya. That single inscription helped link two bodies of evidence, the royal edicts and the later literary tradition.

Historians should avoid saying that every inscription was personally composed by Ashoka. The edicts speak in a royal voice, but their production involved officials, scribes, translators, stonemasons, and regional transmission. The regional variations in wording, spelling, and content suggest that local scribes adapted drafts sent from the center. Some inscriptions contain phrases that appear to reflect local dialect; the Greek and Aramaic versions at Kandahar reflect translation into other conceptual worlds. The corpus is thus best understood as an official communication system in which the king's voice was mediated by an administrative apparatus. That apparatus itself is evidence for the reach of Mauryan government: to inscribe hundreds of kilometers from Pataliputra in the language of the local population required trained staff, clear instructions, and organized supervision.

Geography Study

Where the Edicts Stand: Mapping the Royal Message

This map study asks students to map the inscriptions rather than merely color an empire. Using a map of South Asia and adjoining regions, locate and identify:

  • Gandhara sites (Shahbazgarhi and Mansehra), with Kharoshthi versions of the Major Rock Edicts
  • Kandahar, where Greek and Aramaic Ashokan inscriptions survive
  • Girnar (Gujarat), Kalsi (in the Himalayan foothills), and other regional copies of the Major Rock Edicts
  • Dhauli and Jaugada in Kalinga, where Major Rock Edicts XI–XIII are omitted and the two Separate Edicts are added
  • Sarnath, Sanchi, and Kosambi, sites associated with the Schism Edict
  • Lumbini and Nigali Sagar near the Himalayan foothills, with inscriptions commemorating sites associated with the Buddha tradition
  • Major pillar locations in the Gangetic plain and adjoining routes
  • Southern Minor Rock Edict sites, including Maski
  • Pataliputra, the imperial center, and the Hellenistic states to the west

Students should treat this as a map of communication: it shows how the royal message varied across geography, languages, scripts, and audiences. Modern borders should not be used; the map should use regional names appropriate to the period.

Section 16.5

Pillar Edicts

The Pillar Edicts are a distinct corpus, and the chapter should not treat them as a continuation of a single catalogue. They are carved on tall, polished sandstone pillars, often surmounted by carved capitals such as lions or bulls. The best-known such capital, the lion capital from Sarnath, later became a modern national emblem; the chapter returns to the modern significance of Ashokan imagery in Section 16.9. The pillars were highly visible royal monuments, associated particularly with the Gangetic heartland and with major routes and sites. Their production required the quarrying of stone, its transport over long distances, and highly skilled polishing, demonstrating both technical resources and the ability to project royal presence. Where a rock edict is tied to a natural feature, a pillar is a constructed marker, meant to be seen from afar.

Pillar Edicts I–VI occur in multiple versions on six principal pillars, at Delhi-Topra, Meerut, Allahabad-Kosambi, Lauriya-Araraj, Lauriya-Nandangarh, and Rampurva; Pillar Edict VII is a later addition preserved only on the Topra pillar. They were issued late in the reign, from about twenty-six years after coronation. What do they reveal about mature Ashokan kingship? They emphasize dhamma administration: the king's concern for the conduct of officials, the fair administration of justice by officials such as the rajukas, and the welfare of subjects. They describe restrictions on the killing of certain animals on specified days, measures to ease the treatment of prisoners, including a period of respite for those condemned to death, and the planting of trees and digging of wells along roads. Above all they present royal self-evaluation: the king reflects on what he has done and on what more is necessary.

Pillar Edict VII is especially valuable as a retrospective statement about Ashoka's efforts to promote dhamma. In it the king looks back on the measures taken by earlier kings and by himself, describes the appointment of officials and the erection of dhamma proclamations, and reviews what he believes he has accomplished. It survives only on the Topra pillar. It is a rare example of a ruler assessing a reign in his own inscribed words, though it remains a royal self-presentation and cannot be treated as an independent audit of results.

The chapter should not say that pillars were simply "Buddhist monuments." Some later became associated with Buddhist sites, and Ashoka certainly patronized Buddhism, but the main Pillar Edict corpus communicates royal dhamma more broadly. The pillars address officials and subjects of all religious communities and do not use specifically Buddhist doctrinal language. A useful comparison is with the Rock Edicts: both corpora present dhamma as a public ethic, but the pillars, placed along major routes and at places where officials and travelers gathered, presented a more permanent and monumental form of royal presence.

A different type of pillar inscription is the Lumbini (Rummindei) inscription, which belongs to the small group of Ashokan inscriptions that mention Buddhist sites directly. It records that in his twentieth year after his coronation Ashoka himself came to Lumbini and worshipped there, because the Buddha had been born at that place. It notes that a stone pillar was erected and that the village is commonly interpreted as exempted from bali and its revenue share reduced to one-eighth. This inscription has been central to scholarly discussions of Ashoka's Buddhist commitment, because it shows the king making a pilgrimage to a Buddhist site and using state resources, including tax remission, to honor it. It is also a reminder that inscriptions could serve administrative as well as devotional purposes. A related inscription at Nigali Sagar reports that Ashoka enlarged a stupa associated with a former Buddha and visited the site.

Section 16.6

Dhamma

This section is the conceptual center of the chapter. Students must understand that Ashoka's dhamma is not simply synonymous with Buddhism. The word dhamma is the Prakrit form of the Sanskrit dharma, a term with a wide range of meanings in South Asian religious and ethical thought, including law, duty, righteousness, and moral order. In Ashoka's inscriptions it names a program of ethical conduct that the king urges on himself, his officials, and his subjects. Its content is described concretely, not through abstract doctrine.

The inscriptions emphasize such things as respect for parents and elders; generosity toward Brahmans, shramanas, friends, and relatives; proper treatment of servants and dependents; restraint in the killing of animals; moderation in spending and possessions; truthfulness; good conduct; gentleness; and restraint in speech. They also emphasize concern for social harmony and mutual respect among different religious communities. Dhamma is described as involving little sin and many good deeds, and includes compassion, liberality, truthfulness, and purity. These are ethical norms broadly intelligible across the traditions of the period. They differ from the specific doctrinal content that Chapter 11 associated with Buddhism.

Modern scholarship commonly treats the imperial dhamma as a broadly framed ethical program influenced by Ashoka's Buddhist commitments but deliberately applicable across a religiously diverse empire. This chapter therefore keeps two things apart. On one side is Buddhist doctrine: the Four Noble Truths, dependent origination, the doctrine of non-self, nirvana, and the detailed soteriology of Chapter 11. None of these appears in the definition of dhamma in the edicts. On the other side is Ashokan imperial dhamma: a public ethic of restraint, welfare, and respect that the king expected officials to promote and subjects to practice. The edicts do speak of heaven as a reward for dhamma conduct, but they do so in general terms, not in the vocabulary of a specific school.

A very good analytical question follows: why would a Buddhist king articulate a public ethic that did not require everyone in his empire to become Buddhist? Several answers are possible and, again, not mutually exclusive. A ruler of a diverse empire had practical reasons to avoid tying royal authority to the doctrine of one community. Communities of Brahmans, Jains, Ajivikas, and other groups were also recipients of royal patronage and possible sources of support or unrest. An ethical program with shared content could unify officials and subjects across religious lines. A king personally drawn to Buddhism might also believe that the moral core of his religion could be expressed in terms accessible to all. The evidence permits all of these interpretations, and historians disagree about the weight to assign each.

Dhamma also included concrete measures of welfare. Major Rock Edict II describes medical provision for humans and animals, the planting of medicinal herbs, the digging of wells, and the planting of trees along roads. Such measures made the ethical program visible in the landscape and connected royal morality with infrastructure. They also reached beyond the empire's borders: the same edict refers to medical care in neighboring lands. Dhamma thus combined moral exhortation with practical benefit, and that combination is part of what made it a political program rather than only a philosophy.

Section 16.7

Ashoka and Buddhism

Only after defining dhamma separately can the chapter address Ashoka's Buddhist identity. There is inscriptional evidence for personal Buddhist commitment. The Minor Rock Edicts describe Ashoka's relationship with the Buddhist community. In Minor Rock Edict I, the king reports that he has been a lay follower (upasaka) for a period of more than two and a half years, and that for more than a year he has drawn closer to the Buddhist sangha and exerted himself. Scholarly analysis treats this edict and the Schism Edicts as important contemporary evidence for his dealings with Buddhist sanghas. The Schism Edict, found at Sarnath, Sanchi, and Kosambi, warns that monks or nuns who cause division within the sangha are to be made to wear white robes and live outside monastic residences, evidence that the king took an interest in the discipline and unity of the Buddhist community.

Other evidence includes the Bairat (Bhabru) Edict, in which Ashoka addresses the Buddhist sangha, expresses faith in the Buddha, the dhamma, and the sangha, and recommends particular texts for monks, nuns, and lay followers to hear and reflect upon. The Lumbini inscription and the Nigali Sagar inscription, discussed in Section 16.5, record pilgrimage and commemoration at sites associated with the Buddha tradition. Taken together, they show a king who honored the Buddha, visited sacred places, patronized monastic communities, and sought to maintain the sangha's unity. These inscriptions therefore provide strong contemporary evidence that Ashoka was personally committed to Buddhism.

Yet this section must strongly distinguish inscriptional evidence from later traditions. Later Buddhist texts credit Ashoka with convening the Third Buddhist Council at Pataliputra, sending missionary teams across Asia, dispatching his son Mahinda and daughter Sanghamitta to Sri Lanka, opening the relic stupas of the Buddha and redistributing the relics, and constructing 84,000 stupas. These stories are historically important, especially for Buddhist memory, and Chapter 24 examines the introduction of Buddhism to Sri Lanka as a historical problem in its own right. But they come from later Buddhist textual traditions, especially the Sanskrit Ashokavadana and the Pali chronicles, and should not be presented as contemporary facts. Ashoka's own inscriptions do not mention a Third Council or the Sri Lankan mission. The number 84,000 is a conventional symbolic number in Buddhist literature, not a census of monuments.

The inscriptions do mention dhamma missions in a different sense: Rock Edict XIII says that Ashoka's dhamma conquest had been achieved in the territories of Hellenistic kings and other distant rulers, and Rock Edict II refers to medical provision abroad. These are claims about the reach of royal dhamma and diplomacy, not about a Buddhist missionary organization. Historians debate how far such diplomatic contacts contributed to the spread of Buddhism. Archaeology and later inscriptions elsewhere show that Buddhism did spread widely in the centuries after Ashoka, but the mechanisms were complex, involving monks, merchants, and regional rulers.

The word "conversion" also needs care. The popular story places Ashoka's conversion at Kalinga. A better formulation is that Ashoka's inscriptions indicate an evolving personal engagement with Buddhism; they do not support a single cinematic conversion moment immediately after Kalinga. Minor Rock Edict I speaks of a gradual process of increasing involvement. Buddhist patronage should not be confused with compulsory Buddhist conversion, and nothing in the edicts requires subjects to adopt Buddhism. Ashoka also honored Brahmans, shramanas, and Ajivikas, and several Barabar cave inscriptions record royal cave donations associated with the Ajivikas. His Buddhism was personal and devotional, while his public dhamma was addressed to a wider constituency.

Section 16.8

Religious Toleration and Imperial Governance

This section integrates ethics with administration. Important evidence appears in Major Rock Edict VII and especially Major Rock Edict XII. In Edict VII, Ashoka expresses the wish that all religious communities may live everywhere in his realm. In Edict XII, he says that he honors all sects with gifts and other forms of respect, but that the most important thing is growth in the essential qualities of all religions. He warns against praising one's own tradition by disparaging others, urges restraint in speech, and encourages people to listen to and respect one another's teachings. He describes mutual respect as a form of mutual benefit and links it to the increase of dhamma.

Modern scholarship sees this as a significant ancient policy of religious pluralism, but cautions against simply labeling Ashoka a modern secularist. The word "secularism," in its modern political senses, refers to specific ideas about the separation of state and religion that did not exist in the third century BCE. Ashoka did not separate the state from religion: he patronized Buddhism, honored other traditions with gifts, and enlisted officials in the promotion of dhamma. It would be inaccurate to say that he established religious freedom. Historically appropriate language is more modest: he promoted restraint in interreligious speech, respect among religious communities, and imperial concern for their coexistence.

Dhamma also had an administrative form. In Rock Edict V, Ashoka reports that thirteen years after his coronation he appointed dhamma-mahamatras, officials associated with the promotion and administration of dhamma. The edict says that they worked among all religious communities and among a wide range of populations, including Greeks, Kambojas, Gandharans, other western peoples, soldiers, chiefs, Brahmans, householders, the poor, and the aged. It also associates them with the welfare and possible release of prisoners in circumstances such as old age or family obligations, and with the affairs of members of the royal household. Pillar Edict VII later gives a more explicit religious list, describing officials concerned with the Sangha, Brahmans, Ajivikas, Niganthas, and other communities. Together, these inscriptions show that dhamma was not merely a private virtue but a matter of institutional practice.

The central question therefore becomes: was dhamma simply personal morality, or was it also a technology of imperial governance? The answer is that it functioned in both ethical and political registers. As an ethical program it expressed values the king evidently held and urged on others. As a political instrument it provided a shared vocabulary for officials, a justification for royal supervision of local conduct, a means of presenting the king as a moral parent to his subjects, and a way of addressing populations who might resent conquest. Officials charged with promoting dhamma also carried the royal presence into regions and communities that the king could not personally visit.

But historians should avoid cynically reducing the entire program to propaganda. Evidence can support multiple functions simultaneously. A king who publicly urges restraint may be sincere and strategic at the same moment. Historians of Mauryan governance also note that the dhamma bureaucracy added another layer of supervision over society, one that could be experienced by subjects as care or as intrusion. This ambiguity is part of the historical reality. What the inscriptions demonstrate is that Ashoka connected moral claims to the machinery of the state, and that this combination was novel in the record of South Asian kingship.

Section 16.9

Ashoka's Historical Reputation

This section is a historiography and memory section rather than another narrative of the reign. It distinguishes at least three Ashokas.

The Ashoka of the inscriptions is a Mauryan king addressing subjects, officials, neighboring rulers, religious groups, and frontier populations. He is confident, moralizing, and administratively engaged. He describes tours of inspection, welfare works, and appointments of officials. He speaks of the suffering caused by Kalinga, the value of restraint, and the goal of dhamma. This Ashoka is not an all-powerful saint: he still threatens punishment, still manages officials, and still speaks with the authority of an emperor.

The Ashoka of Buddhist literary memory is a dramatically transformed ruler: a violent prince who becomes a righteous king, a devoted patron, a distributor of relics, a sponsor of councils, a missionary monarch, and a model of righteous Buddhist kingship. Sanskrit and Pali traditions, produced centuries after his death in northern India and Sri Lanka, developed these portraits according to their own needs. Later Buddhist models of universal Buddhist kingship often developed beyond what Ashoka actually claims for himself in his inscriptions. For example, he never presents himself in his inscriptions as a bodhisattva; that characterization belongs to later Buddhist interpretations of righteous kingship. The Ashoka of memory is nonetheless historically important, because it shaped how Buddhist communities in South and Southeast Asia understood ideal kingship for many centuries.

The Ashoka reconstructed by modern historians is built through comparison of inscriptions, archaeology, classical sources, later Buddhist literature, and modern textual criticism. This third Ashoka was not available for nearly two thousand years. After the decline of Brahmi as a readable script, the inscriptions were unintelligible to later observers. In the 1830s James Prinsep and other scholars deciphered Brahmi, making it possible to read the edicts and to connect the name Piyadasi with the Sri Lankan chronicles' Ashoka. Later discoveries, including the Maski inscription in 1915 that used the name Ashoka directly, confirmed the identification. The rediscovery was a landmark in the modern historical recovery of ancient South Asia.

After rediscovery, Ashoka became an influential symbol. Nationalists, Buddhists, historians, and later governments invoked him for different purposes: as an early example of a righteous state, as a symbol of religious pluralism, as a model of nonviolent politics, or as a figure of national heritage. The Sarnath lion capital and the wheel (chakra) associated with Ashokan imagery were adopted by modern India, whose State Emblem adapts the Sarnath lion capital and whose flag carries a chakra derived from its wheel. These uses are part of the modern history of memory. They should not overwrite the third-century-BCE ruler. A textbook that uses Ashoka as a modern moral lesson without attention to his inscriptions risks projecting present concerns onto the past, just as Chapter 1 warned. The historian's task is to keep the modern symbol and the ancient king distinct.

Section 16.10

Review and Historical Synthesis

This chapter asked what exactly changed under Ashoka. The answer is not the existence of empire. Chandragupta had already created that, and Chapter 15 traced how Bindusara maintained it. Nor did Ashoka create the basic existence of officials, taxation, armies, provincial government, or long-distance diplomacy. All of these were inherited. The transformation was principally in the public articulation of kingship.

Before Ashoka, imperial power was justified heavily through conquest, dynasty, revenue, and territorial rule. Under Ashoka, those foundations remained, but royal authority was publicly connected with moral restraint, welfare, dhamma, interreligious conduct, self-scrutiny, and direct communication to populations through inscriptions. This is the historical payoff of the chapter: Ashoka transformed the way Mauryan kingship represented itself and attempted to govern.

Section 16.1 established the limits of biographical knowledge: the accession narratives are later Buddhist traditions, while the inscriptions begin speaking in a royal voice. Sections 16.2 and 16.3 examined Kalinga and the remorse of Edict XIII, showing a sequence from conquest to public critique of its costs to a preference for conquest by dhamma, alongside continuing royal power. Sections 16.4 and 16.5 treated the inscriptions themselves as a historical subject, a distributed, multilingual corpus of rock and pillar edicts whose forms, languages, and locations reveal the reach and adaptability of Mauryan communication. Section 16.6 defined dhamma as an imperial ethical program distinct from Buddhist doctrine, and Section 16.7 showed that Ashoka's personal Buddhist commitment is documented in inscriptions, but that later traditions about councils, missions, and 84,000 stupas belong to a different evidentiary category. Section 16.8 showed how religious respect and dhamma officials linked ethics to administration, and Section 16.9 separated the Ashoka of inscriptions, memory, and modern historiography.

The synthesis therefore rejects several simplistic conclusions. Ashoka did not abolish warfare, and he did not dissolve the army after Kalinga. He did not convert the empire wholesale to Buddhism. Dhamma was not identical to Buddhist doctrine. Religious toleration was not modern secularism. His inscriptions are not transparent autobiography. Kalinga cannot be treated as a single instantaneous conversion scene, and later Buddhist legends cannot be collapsed into contemporary evidence.

A further lesson concerns the relationship between political power and moral language. Ashoka's inscriptions show that an empire could seek legitimacy through claims of care and restraint as well as through force. They also show the limits of what such claims tell us. The inscriptions communicate how the Mauryan court wanted power, morality, welfare, and religious coexistence to be understood. They tell us much less directly about how ordinary people produced food, traded goods, organized households, worked, paid taxes, or experienced imperial demands. Chapter 17 therefore turns from royal proclamations to the society and economy that sustained the Mauryan world, and Chapter 18 will examine how the empire fragmented after Ashoka and how its legacies were remembered.

Primary Source Window

Major Rock Edict XIII (versions from Shahbazgarhi, Kalsi, Girnar, and other sites)

Creator: Issued in the name of Ashoka, "Beloved of the Gods, Piyadasi," and carved by officials, scribes, and stoneworkers · Date: after the conquest of Kalinga, in the mid-third century BCE, reconstructed from regnal-year references · Type: Royal inscription on natural rock, in Prakrit; Brahmi script in most regions and Kharoshthi in the northwest. Edicts XI, XII, and XIII are omitted at Dhauli and Jaugada, where the two Kalinga Separate Edicts are added to the retained edicts.

Context: Rock Edict XIII is the most famous of the fourteen Major Rock Edicts. It addresses a general audience across the empire and beyond, reviewing the conquest of Kalinga and expressing the king's attitude toward it. Because versions differ from site to site, students should cite a specific version and site when working with the text rather than treating an undifferentiated composite as "what Ashoka said."

Paraphrase: The king reports that in the eighth year after his coronation Kalinga was conquered, and that about a hundred and fifty thousand people were deported, a hundred thousand were killed, and many times that number perished. He states that, having taken Kalinga, he now feels remorse, because the conquest of an unconquered land involves killing, death, and deportation. He notes that suffering also falls on Brahmans, ascetics, householders, and those bound to friends and relatives. He says that even a hundredth or thousandth part of what happened in Kalinga would now weigh heavily upon him. He adds that he will forgive what can be forgiven, warns forest peoples that he retains power and that they should reform so they are not killed, and declares that the best conquest is conquest by dhamma. He says that such conquest has been won in neighboring lands and among distant kings, including Hellenistic rulers, and he hopes that his sons and grandsons will pursue conquest by dhamma.

Analysis: The edict offers contemporary evidence for the scale of the Kalinga conquest as Ashoka chose to present it, and for the emergence of "conquest by dhamma" as a royal ideal. It should not be read as a transparent private confession. It is a public proclamation, composed for political purposes, addressed to subjects, frontier peoples, and foreign rulers. The casualty figures are royal claims. The passage on forest peoples shows that coercive authority remained. The list of Hellenistic kings provides an important chronological anchor and evidence of diplomatic reach. What the edict cannot tell historians is what Ashoka privately felt, how the conquered people of Kalinga experienced Mauryan rule, or how widely the ideals were realized in practice.

Questions for students:

  1. What does Ashoka claim happened at Kalinga?
  2. Whose suffering concerns him, and whose suffering is not mentioned?
  3. What does he mean by conquest by dhamma?
  4. Where does coercive royal authority remain visible in the edict?
  5. Is this confession, policy statement, royal ideology, or some combination?
  6. What can historians not establish from this source alone?
Historical Debate

Did Kalinga Transform Ashoka, or Did Ashoka Use Kalinga to Transform the Public Language of Kingship?

The question: How should historians understand the relationship between the Kalinga War and the ethical program of Ashoka's later inscriptions? Was Kalinga a moral turning point for the king, or was the expression of remorse part of a deliberate strategy of imperial legitimation?

Interpretation A — Genuine ethical transformation: On this view, the intensity of Edict XIII's language, the sustained ethical program that follows, the king's reference to his own conduct, and his personal engagement with Buddhism suggest a real change in outlook. A ruler who publicly criticizes the human costs of his own conquest and pursues welfare and restraint for decades is plausibly sincere. The inscriptions record a shift in priorities that cannot be dismissed merely as rhetoric.
Interpretation B — Royal ideology and imperial governance: On this view, the inscriptions are political texts. Publicly regretting Kalinga distanced Ashoka from violence, reassured conquered populations, provided a shared vocabulary for officials, and supported royal authority across a diverse empire. The edict itself retains threats of force. Dhamma bureaucracy extended state supervision. Historians can analyze what the language did without claiming knowledge of the king's inner feelings.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on the same inscriptions, especially Rock Edict XIII, the Separate Edicts, the Pillar Edicts, and the Minor Rock Edicts, together with comparative evidence about ancient kingship. They differ in how much weight to give the emotional language of the texts and in how far they treat inscriptions as evidence of personal motivation rather than of public communication.

Why it matters: The debate affects how students understand Ashoka's place in world history: as a moral reformer, as a sophisticated ruler of an empire, or as both. This chapter proposes that the evidence allows both dimensions. The inscriptions can reflect real ethical concern and simultaneously perform political work. Forcing a choice between "real" and "strategic" remorse imposes a modern dichotomy on a text that likely served several purposes at once.

Student task: Identify two passages or features of the edicts (paraphrased in this chapter) that best support each interpretation, and explain what additional evidence, if any survived, would help resolve the question.

Historical Vocabulary
Dhamma

Dhamma is the Prakrit form of the Sanskrit dharma. In Ashoka's inscriptions it names a program of ethical conduct and social responsibility that includes respect for elders and parents, generosity, restraint in killing, truthfulness, moderation, and mutual respect among religious communities. It is described through concrete behaviors and welfare measures rather than through detailed doctrine.

The term matters because it is easily confused with Buddhism. In later Buddhist usage, dhamma (Pali) can mean the Buddha's teaching. In Ashoka's public inscriptions, it is broader: an imperial ethic that the king hoped would guide subjects of many traditions. Modern equivalents such as "morality," "piety," or "righteousness" capture parts of the meaning but should not be treated as exact translations.

Historical Vocabulary
Devanampiya Piyadasi and Regnal Years

Devanampiya Piyadasi ("Beloved of the Gods, Piyadasi") is the royal title used in most Ashokan inscriptions. The Maski inscription joins it with the name Ashoka. A regnal year counts time from a king's coronation. Ashoka's inscriptions date events by such years (for example, the eighth year after coronation), which gives the internal chronology of the reign more secure grounding than reconstructed BCE dates.

These terms matter because they explain how historians identified the author of the inscriptions and why the chronology of the reign is expressed in relative rather than absolute terms.

Multiple Perspectives

Hearing the Edicts: Reconstructed Perspectives

Note: the perspectives below are reconstructed analytical illustrations grounded in what the inscriptions and other evidence suggest. They are not quotations or accounts from named historical individuals.

An inhabitant of Kalinga after the conquest: Someone whose family had been killed or deported would have experienced Kalinga as a catastrophe, not a distant moral lesson. The inscription's expression of royal regret would have carried a different meaning for those who lost relatives than for readers elsewhere. The Separate Edicts addressing officials suggest that the state sought to stabilize conquered Kalinga, but the evidence tells us little about how survivors judged such measures.
A dhamma-mahamatra: An official appointed to promote dhamma would have combined moral instruction with administrative tasks such as inspecting the treatment of prisoners and supporting religious communities. Such an official could act as the king's representative, wielding new authority, and might also be responsible for reporting on the conduct of others.
A Buddhist monk encountering royal patronage: Royal support could bring resources and prestige, and the king's concern for the unity of the sangha, expressed in the Schism Edict, meant royal involvement in monastic discipline. Patronage was therefore both an opportunity and a form of state influence.
A Brahman or member of another religious community hearing an edict: The edicts praise respect for Brahmans and shramanas and urge restraint in criticizing others. A listener might have welcomed royal recognition, while also recognizing that the king was defining norms for religious speech. The dhamma ethic left room for Brahmanical practice, but restrictions on animal slaughter could bear on sacrificial and household customs.
A frontier official implementing royal commands: An official in a frontier region had to translate central directives into local circumstances, use local languages and scripts, and manage relations with forest peoples and neighboring communities. The warning to forest peoples in Edict XIII shows that officials remained prepared to use force where necessary.
Continuity and Change Over Time

From Chandragupta and Bindusara to Ashoka

What Changed

  • An unprecedented surviving royal inscriptional program addressed to subjects and officials
  • A public dhamma ethic connecting royal authority with welfare and restraint
  • Self-critical language about conquest, most visible in Major Rock Edict XIII
  • Specialized dhamma officials, the dhamma-mahamatras
  • Greater visibility of royal ethical claims and of royal pilgrimage and patronage

What Stayed the Same

  • The Mauryan dynasty and the capital at Pataliputra
  • Provincial rule, taxation, and a standing army
  • Coercive authority, including punishment and the threat of force
  • Diplomatic contact with the Hellenistic world

Who experienced change most directly? Officials, who received new duties, and conquered populations such as those of Kalinga experienced change most directly. Rural communities and frontier peoples may have experienced greater continuity in the demands of the state. Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both continuity and change prevents the error of treating Ashoka as the founder of a different kind of state.

Cause and Effect

Why Did Ashoka Develop an Empire-Wide Inscriptional and Dhamma Program?

Long-term causes

The difficulty of ruling a vast, diverse empire; an inherited administrative system; a religiously plural environment of Brahmanical, Buddhist, Jain, and other traditions; and increasing use of writing in public life.

Short-term causes

The conquest of Kalinga and its human costs; Ashoka's developing engagement with Buddhism; the need to communicate with officials and new subjects; and the king's own ideology.

Immediate effects

The carving of Rock and Pillar Edicts, the appointment of dhamma-mahamatras, and welfare measures such as wells, trees, and medical provision.

Long-term consequences

A new model of public royal communication; a lasting symbol of ethical kingship in Buddhist and modern memory; and, in the near term, an empire whose administrative unity remained vulnerable, a subject for Chapter 18.

Historical complexity: Students should weigh Kalinga, imperial governance, Buddhist commitment, inherited administration, the need for royal communication, personal ideology, and political integration. No single cause explains the program, and the sources do not allow historians to rank them with confidence.

Everyday Life and Social History

Living with the Edicts

The edicts, addressed to the public, offer glimpses of ordinary life: wells dug along roads, trees planted for shade, medicinal herbs, restrictions on the slaughter of animals on particular days, and rules about prisoners. They reveal concern with travelers, animals, servants, the elderly, and those held in confinement. They also imply the presence of officials who inspected, judged, and reported, and of ordinary people who might experience such officials as protectors or as agents of the state.

Many people could not read the inscriptions. Rock Edict messages were probably communicated by reading aloud, by officials, and by local intermediaries; literacy was limited, and many subjects probably encountered inscriptions through officials, readers, or other intermediaries rather than by reading the texts themselves. Historians therefore have to ask how the message reached those who never read it. Chapter 17 examines the social and economic foundations of the Mauryan world, including village life, cities, crafts, and trade, which the royal inscriptions describe only indirectly.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that Ashoka inherited the imperial system created by Chandragupta and maintained under Bindusara, but transformed the public language of Mauryan kingship. After the conquest of Kalinga, his inscriptions presented conquest by dhamma, moral self-discipline, welfare, restraint, and concern for diverse religious communities as central duties of rule. Ashoka was personally committed to Buddhism, yet the dhamma proclaimed across the empire was broader than Buddhist doctrine, and should be understood as an imperial ethical and political program rather than simply as a campaign to convert his subjects.

The chapter began with Ashoka's accession, conventionally placed in the 270s BCE with a coronation around 268 BCE. Later Buddhist stories of fratricide, a wicked early Ashoka, and miraculous election are not contemporary evidence. The contrast with Chandragupta is that Ashoka's own inscriptions supply contemporary, though political, documentation.

The Kalinga War, in Ashoka's eighth year after his coronation, is the chapter's major narrative turning point. Ashoka presents Kalinga as a successful conquest. Major Rock Edict XIII reports large numbers of deportations and deaths, which are royal claims rather than audited counts. The familiar story of instant conversion and pacifism compresses separate streams of evidence.

Edict XIII expresses remorse but also asserts continuing royal power, especially over forest peoples. The transformation is better described as military conquest, then public critique of the human costs of conquest, then preference for conquest by dhamma, alongside continued royal power. Ashoka's private psychology is not accessible independently of the political text.

The Major Rock Edicts form a distributed corpus, in Prakrit and mostly Brahmi, with Kharoshthi in the northwest and Greek and Aramaic at Kandahar. Their variety demonstrates adaptation to regional audiences and a sophisticated administrative apparatus. Ashoka's title, Devanampiya Piyadasi, and the Maski inscription allowed scholars to identify the author with the Mauryan king. The inscriptions were not personally composed and carved by Ashoka.

The Pillar Edicts, distinguished from the Rock Edicts by form and location, communicate mature dhamma in the Gangetic heartland and along routes. Pillar Edict VII offers a retrospective assessment. The pillars are not simply Buddhist monuments, though the Lumbini inscription shows the king honoring the Buddha's birthplace.

Dhamma is an imperial ethic emphasizing respect for elders, generosity, restraint in killing, moderation, truthfulness, and respect for religious communities. It is not synonymous with Buddhist doctrine. Ashoka's own Buddhist engagement is documented by inscriptions such as Minor Rock Edict I, the Schism Edict, the Bairat Edict, and the Lumbini and Nigali Sagar inscriptions, but later traditions about the Third Council, missions, relics, and 84,000 stupas are retrospective Buddhist memory.

Ashoka's policies of religious respect, expressed in Edicts VII and XII, are best described as promoting restraint in interreligious speech and concern for coexistence, not modern secularism or religious freedom. The dhamma-mahamatras show that ethics was linked to administration, and that dhamma operated in both ethical and political registers.

Finally, the chapter distinguished the Ashoka of the inscriptions, the Ashoka of Buddhist literary memory, and the Ashoka reconstructed by modern historians after the decipherment of Brahmi. Ashoka did not abolish warfare, dissolve the army, convert the empire, or found the Mauryan state. He transformed how Mauryan kingship represented itself and sought to govern. Chapter 17 turns to the society and economy that sustained his empire.

Conquest by dhamma
Ashoka's expression for the moral persuasion that he described as superior to military conquest, in Major Rock Edict XIII.
Devanampiya Piyadasi
The royal title used in most Ashokan inscriptions, usually rendered "Beloved of the Gods, Piyadasi."
Edict
A royal proclamation, here inscribed on rock or pillar and addressed to officials and the public.
Major Rock Edicts
A group of fourteen proclamations carved on rock surfaces in regional versions across the empire.
Minor Rock Edicts
Shorter inscriptions, including Minor Rock Edict I on Ashoka's relationship with the Buddhist community and the Maski inscription naming Ashoka.
Kalinga Separate Edicts
Edicts at Dhauli and Jaugada addressed to officials governing conquered Kalinga, added at those sites, where Major Rock Edicts XI–XIII are omitted.
Pillar Edicts
Later proclamations on polished stone pillars, including the retrospective Pillar Edict VII.
Regnal year
A year counted from a king's coronation, used in Ashoka's inscriptions to date events.
Brahmi
The script of most Ashokan inscriptions, ancestral to most later South Asian scripts.
Kharoshthi
A script derived from Aramaic, used for Ashokan inscriptions in the northwest.
Prakrit
Middle Indo-Aryan dialects; the main language of the Ashokan inscriptions.
Aramaic
A Semitic language used in Achaemenid administration; attested in Ashokan material at Kandahar.
Dhamma-mahamatra
An official appointed to promote and administer dhamma among varied communities, described in Rock Edict V.
Rajuka
An official named in the Pillar Edicts in connection with justice and local administration.
Shramana
A renunciant ascetic tradition, including Buddhist, Jain, and Ajivika communities, honored in Ashoka's inscriptions alongside Brahmans.
Sangha
The Buddhist monastic community, whose unity concerned Ashoka in the Schism Edict.
Upasaka
A Buddhist lay follower; the status Ashoka claims in Minor Rock Edict I.
Royal ideology
The ideas and public claims by which a ruler explains and justifies authority.
Titulature
The titles a ruler uses in official texts.
Kalinga
Eastern coastal region conquered by Ashoka in his eighth year after his coronation.
Lumbini inscription
A pillar inscription recording Ashoka's visit to the Buddha's birthplace in his twentieth year after his coronation.
Schism Edict
An edict warning against division in the Buddhist sangha, found at Sarnath, Sanchi, and Kosambi.
Bairat (Bhabru) Edict
An inscription addressed to the Buddhist sangha recommending particular texts.
Ashokavadana
A Sanskrit Buddhist narrative tradition about Ashoka, composed centuries after his death.
Mahavamsa
A Pali chronicle of Sri Lanka that preserves Buddhist memory of Ashoka and Mahinda.
84,000 stupas
A traditional symbolic number attributed by later Buddhist texts to Ashoka's stupa building; not a documented count.
Anachronism
Applying later concepts, such as modern secularism, to an earlier period.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows a ruler of a very large ancient empire addressing his subjects in writing, and because it demonstrates how much historians can learn, and how carefully they must read, when a state speaks in its own voice. Ashoka's inscriptions are among the earliest surviving public statements of political ideals in South Asia. They reveal an empire that sought legitimacy not only through force and revenue but through claims about morality, welfare, and coexistence.

The chapter also reveals how historical understanding is built from separate streams of evidence. The inscriptions, later Buddhist narratives, archaeology, and modern scholarship each contribute, and confusing them produces the familiar myths of instant conversion and pacifism. The habit of distinguishing contemporary evidence from later memory, introduced in Chapter 1 and practiced in Chapters 11 through 13, is a skill this chapter puts to use in a case where the stakes of memory are unusually high.

Ashoka's legacy shaped later developments in several ways. His patronage contributed to the institutional strength of Buddhism, whose spread across South Asia and beyond is traced in later chapters, including Chapter 24 on Sri Lanka. His model of a king answering publicly for the moral consequences of rule became an enduring reference point in Buddhist political thought. And his rediscovery in the nineteenth century made him a modern symbol, a subject that Chapter 111 will revisit in its discussion of historical memory. Yet the empire he ruled proved vulnerable, and Chapter 18 turns to its fragmentation.

Chapter 17 now turns from the royal voice to the world beneath it, examining the agriculture, villages, cities, crafts, trade, officials, and social hierarchies through which the Mauryan state actually operated. Only by combining the inscriptions with that wider evidence can students understand both what Ashoka said and the society in which he said it.
