Lire la littérature comme discours

Literary Discourse · Speak and Write French: Specialized Mastery and Expert Communication

Chapter Objectives

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

  1. Distinguish reading a text as literary discourse — where a genuine narrative technique, a genuine impression, a genuine implication, and a genuine adopted perspective must each be named precisely, without inventing an interpretation the text does not support — from Unité II’s specialized-domain precision, while recognizing that a single text can call for both kinds of reading at once.
  2. Use a newly productive depiction frame — donner à voir + nom — to state precisely what a passage's narrative technique actually depicts or makes visible to the reader, as distinct from what the passage is merely "about."
  3. Use a newly productive impersonal-impression frame — il se dégage de + source + une impression / un sentiment / un thème — to name an impression, a mood, or a theme that genuinely emerges from a passage, grounded in specific textual evidence rather than in a free-floating reaction.
  4. Use a newly productive implication frame — laisser entendre que + indicatif — to name what a text suggests without stating outright, distinguishing an implication actually supported by the text from a claim the reader has simply added.
  5. Use a newly productive perspective frame — adopter le point de vue de + nom — to identify precisely whose perspective a passage is organized around at a given moment, and to notice when that perspective shifts.
  6. Use a newly productive literary-discourse vocabulary set: un narrateur, un point de vue, un procédé, le ton, symboliser, une nuance, l’implicite.
  7. Recycle, without any new tense, mood, subjonctif trigger, or clitic pattern, the complete Unité I toolkit, the complete Unité II specialized-domain toolkit (Chapters 6–9), and the full Volume I–IV grammar inventory, selecting from among these rather than treating any of them as newly taught.
  8. Receive further controlled, receptive-only exposure to a small set of more technical literary-analytical vocabulary encountered in literary commentary.
  9. Read a short story excerpt and a short literary commentary for their stated narrative technique, impression, implication, and perspective, distinguishing an interpretation grounded in the text from an unsupported claim about the author's intentions.
  10. Complete a short mediation exercise (drafting notes for the newspaper's new literary supplement about a short story evoking the covered market) using all four of this chapter's newly productive frames at least once each.
  11. Move to literary reading and listening — a short story excerpt, a literary commentary, and an editorial-meeting listening excerpt — opening Unité III toward the Chapter 15 integration.

Communication Scenario

La Revue du quartier is launching a small literary supplement, and its first commission is a short story by a local writer, set partly at the covered market whose renovation Sophie and Karim have covered for five chapters. The story follows an elderly vendor closing her stall on an ordinary evening, but the editor wants more than a plot summary: readers should understand how the story tells its story — whose eyes the reader sees through, what the closing paragraph implies without ever stating it outright, and what impression the piece leaves once it is finished. Sophie, who has spent five chapters stating exactly what is required, permitted, verified, and promised, finds this unfamiliar: a literary commentary cannot simply report facts, because a short story's most important content is often precisely what it does not say directly. Karim, more at ease with ambiguity, worries about the opposite risk — reading meanings into the story that the text does not actually support. Chapter 11 gives Sophie, Karim — and you — the tools to read a text as discourse: naming what a passage genuinely depicts, what impression genuinely emerges from it, what it genuinely implies, and whose perspective it is organized around, all grounded in the text itself rather than in an unchecked private reaction.

  1. State precisely what a passage's narrative technique depicts or makes visible to the reader.
  2. Name an impression, mood, or theme that genuinely emerges from a passage, grounded in evidence.
  3. Name what a text implies without stating outright, distinguishing implication from invention.
  4. Identify whose perspective a passage is organized around, and notice when that perspective shifts.

Receptive Only — More technical literary-analytical vocabulary

This chapter opens a receptive set of more technical literary-analytical vocabulary, encountered in literary commentary but not yet expected productively: la focalisation (focalization; the technical term for whose perceptions and knowledge organize a given passage), un leitmotiv (a leitmotif; a recurring image, phrase, or detail that gathers meaning across a text), une ellipse narrative (a narrative ellipsis; a deliberate gap where the story skips over time or events), and le discours indirect libre (free indirect discourse; a technique blending a narrator's voice with a character's unspoken thoughts, without quotation marks). Une ellipse narrative appears directly in the Reading Practice passage below, marked by the phrase trois mois plus tard; le discours indirect libre does not appear in this chapter's texts and is introduced here for recognition only, ahead of a controlled example in a later chapter. Do not use any of these items productively yet; these items remain available receptively for later chapters and the Chapter 15 integration.

Pronunciation Focus

Pronunciation Focus — Delivering an interpretation as a reading, not a verdict

No new sound-spelling or vowel feature is introduced in this chapter. The task is delivery: a literary claim can sound like an overreach if delivered with the same flat certainty as a fact, when in fact literary analysis at this level should sound like a careful, examined reading rather than an assertion of the one correct meaning. No single pitch contour is required for any of this chapter’s frames. A slightly more reflective, unhurried pace — with a brief pause before naming the impression or the implication — helps a listener hear that a genuine act of interpretation, not a recitation of fact, is taking place.

  • Naming a depiction: Ce passage donne à voir la fatigue d’une fin de journée, sans jamais nommer directement le mot « fatigue ». — measured pace, slight emphasis on donne à voir.
  • Naming an impression: Il se dégage de ce paragraphe une impression de calme précaire. — a brief pause before il se dégage de, then an even delivery through to the named impression.
  • Naming an implication: Le passage laisse entendre qu’elle hésite à quitter son emplacement. — slight emphasis on laisse entendre to mark that what follows is an inference, not a stated fact in the story itself.
  • Naming a perspective: Dans ce passage, le texte adopte le point de vue de la vendeuse, et non celui d’un observateur extérieur. — steady, declarative pace, since this is a claim about textual organization that can be checked against the text.

Cumulative review: this chapter’s spoken practice also recycles active sound-spelling, liaison, enchaînement, elision, and rhythm systems from earlier volumes. As a short recycling drill, practice il adopte for enchaînement between the normally pronounced final consonant of il and the vowel-initial adopte, un narrateur for liaison between the article and the vowel-initial noun, l’implicite for elision, and symboliser une nuance for nasal-vowel control and phrase rhythm.

Grammar: Décrire un texte comme discours

Purpose: introduce a newly productive depiction frame (donner à voir + nom), a newly productive impersonal-impression frame (il se dégage de + source + une impression/un sentiment/un thème), a newly productive implication frame (laisser entendre que + indicatif), and a newly productive perspective frame (adopter le point de vue de + nom), while recycling the complete Unité I and Unité II toolkits. No new tense, mood, subjonctif trigger, or clitic pattern is introduced in this chapter.

Key distinction — literary discourse is an independent dimension from Unité II's specialized domains

Chapters 6–9 each added a precision dimension oriented toward the real world: legal obligation, institutional responsibility, technical accuracy, and diplomatic commitment. This chapter adds a dimension oriented toward how a text itself is constructed: what a passage's technique depicts, what impression genuinely emerges from it, what it implies without stating, and whose perspective organizes it. Note that laisser entendre que takes the indicative, exactly as il convient de se demander si did in Chapter 8; this chapter introduces no new mood. This literary dimension can combine with earlier tools — a passage's implied meaning (Ch. 11) can itself be explained for a general newspaper readership using Chapter 2's audience-adaptation habits — but it is distinct because it concerns how a text means, not what a person, institution, or technology does.

11.1 Naming what a technique depicts: donner à voir

Grammar Focus — What the passage actually shows, through its technique

Donner à voir + noun is newly productive. It states precisely what a passage's narrative technique — its choice of detail, its rhythm, its imagery — makes visible to the reader, as distinct from simply summarizing what the passage is "about." Naming the technique's effect, rather than only the plot content, is what distinguishes literary commentary from a plot summary.

  • Par l’accumulation de gestes précis et répétés — ranger, compter, verrouiller — le passage donne à voir la fatigue d’une fin de journée, sans jamais nommer directement ce mot. Through the accumulation of precise, repeated gestures — tidying, counting, locking up — the passage depicts the fatigue of a long day, without ever naming that word directly. donne à voir (newly productive) names what the technique shows, not merely what happens in the plot

11.2 Naming a genuine impression: il se dégage de

Grammar Focus — An impression grounded in the text, not a free-floating reaction

Il se dégage de + source (a passage, a paragraph) + une impression / un sentiment / un thème is newly productive, as in il se dégage de ce passage une impression de…. This impersonal construction names an impression, mood, or theme that genuinely emerges from a passage as a whole, rather than from any single sentence. Because the construction is impersonal, it invites the reader to check the claim against the text itself, rather than presenting the impression as the writer's private, unverifiable reaction.

  • Il se dégage de ce paragraphe une impression de calme précaire, comme si la tranquillité du marché fermé pouvait, à tout moment, être rompue. A sense of precarious calm emerges from this paragraph, as if the stillness of the closed market could be broken at any moment. il se dégage de (newly productive) names an impression drawn from the whole passage, not a single detail

11.3 Naming an implication and a perspective: laisser entendre que / adopter le point de vue de

Building Block — Depiction, impression, implication, and perspective together
[depiction: le passage donne à voir…] + [impression: il s’en dégage…] + [implication: le texte laisse entendre que + indicatif] + [perspective: le passage adopte le point de vue de…]
Laisser entendre que + indicative is newly productive. It names what a text suggests without stating outright. Laisser entendre que normally introduces its proposition in the indicative; the expression does not itself trigger the subjonctif, so no new mood is required here. This differs from simply asserting the implied content as though the text stated it directly, which would overstate what is actually written. Adopter le point de vue de + noun is newly productive. It identifies precisely whose perceptions organize a given passage at a given moment — a claim a reader can check by asking whose eyes the reader seems to be looking through — and is distinct from asking who narrates. A story can be told by a narrator who remains outside the events (third person, uninvolved) while the passage is nonetheless organized around, or focalized on, one character's perceptions; the narrator and the adopted point of view are not the same question, and a passage's point of view may shift elsewhere in the same text.
Dans ce passage, le texte adopte le point de vue de la vendeuse : le lecteur ne sait que ce qu’elle remarque, dans l’ordre où elle le remarque. Par l’accumulation de gestes précis et répétés, le passage donne à voir la fatigue d’une fin de journée. Il s’en dégage une impression de calme précaire. Le silence prolongé avant la dernière phrase laisse entendre qu’elle hésite à quitter définitivement son emplacement, sans que le texte le dise jamais explicitement.
In this passage, the text adopts the vendor's point of view: the reader knows only what she notices, in the order she notices it. Through the accumulation of precise, repeated gestures, the passage depicts the fatigue of a long day. A sense of precarious calm emerges from it. The prolonged silence before the last sentence suggests that she hesitates to leave her spot for good, though the text never states this explicitly.

Model Dialogue: La réunion de rédaction du supplément littéraire

Context: the editorial team of La Revue du quartier meets to plan the first literary supplement, built around a short story about a market vendor closing her stall. M. Fontaine, the supplement's editor, guides Sophie and Karim through their first literary-commentary draft. Register: professionnel neutre, reflective and precise.

M. Fontaine : Alors, votre premier brouillon résume surtout l’histoire. Une vendeuse ferme son étal, un point c’est tout. Ce n’est pas encore un commentaire littéraire. So, your first draft mostly summarizes the story. A vendor closes her stall, that's it. That's not a literary commentary yet.
Sophie : C’est vrai — j’ai du mal à dire autre chose que ce qui se passe. Comment est-ce qu’on parle de ce que le texte fait, plutôt que de ce qu’il raconte ? That's true — I have trouble saying anything beyond what happens. How do we talk about what the text does, rather than what it tells?
M. Fontaine : Regardez le passage sur les gestes de fermeture. Que donne-t-il à voir, exactement, au-delà du simple fait qu’elle range son étal ? Look at the passage about the closing gestures. What does it actually depict, beyond the simple fact that she's tidying her stall?
Karim : Par l’accumulation de gestes précis et répétés, le passage donne à voir la fatigue d’une fin de journée, sans jamais employer le mot « fatigue ». Through the accumulation of precise, repeated gestures, the passage depicts the fatigue of a long day, without ever using the word "fatigue."
M. Fontaine : Voilà — c’est exactement ce genre d’observation qu’il faut. Et l’ensemble du paragraphe, quelle impression s’en dégage, selon vous ? There you go — that's exactly the kind of observation we need. And the paragraph as a whole, what impression emerges from it, in your view?
Sophie : Il s’en dégage une impression de calme précaire — comme si ce calme pouvait être rompu à tout moment. A sense of precarious calm emerges from it — as if that calm could be broken at any moment.
Karim : Et il y a ce long silence juste avant la dernière phrase, quand elle regarde une dernière fois son emplacement. Je pense que ça laisse entendre qu’elle hésite à partir pour de bon — mais le texte ne le dit jamais. And there's that long silence right before the last sentence, when she looks at her spot one last time. I think that suggests she hesitates to leave for good — but the text never says so.
M. Fontaine : Bonne lecture, mais attention : dites bien que c’est ce que le texte laisse entendre, pas ce qu’il affirme. Et de qui est-ce le point de vue, ici ? Good reading, but be careful: say clearly that this is what the text suggests, not what it states. And whose point of view is this, here?
Sophie : Le narrateur reste extérieur aux événements, mais le passage est focalisé sur la vendeuse : le lecteur ne sait que ce qu’elle remarque, dans l’ordre où elle le remarque, sans commentaire venu d’ailleurs. The narrator stays outside the events, but the passage is focalized on the vendor: the reader knows only what she notices, in the order she notices it, with no commentary from elsewhere.
M. Fontaine : Exactement. Vous avez les quatre éléments maintenant : ce que le texte donne à voir, l’impression qui s’en dégage, ce qu’il laisse entendre, et le point de vue qu’il adopte. C’est ça, un commentaire littéraire. Exactly. You now have the four elements: what the text depicts, the impression that emerges from it, what it implies, and the perspective it adopts. That's what a literary commentary is.

This dialogue models all four newly productive frames — depiction, impression, implication, and perspective — while recycling Chapter 4's real-time repair and clarification habits as M. Fontaine guides Sophie and Karim's first attempt.

Reading Practice

Nouvelle (extrait) et commentaire littéraire

Nouvelle, « La dernière heure » (extrait) : Elle range les cageots un à un, dans l’ordre où elle les a vidés le matin même. Elle compte les pièces restées dans la petite boîte de fer, deux fois, comme chaque soir. Elle entend, plus loin, le dernier balai racler le pavé, et se demande, sans y penser vraiment, si Mounir a fini son propre rangement. Elle tire la bâche, vérifie le cadenas, le vérifie encore. Le marché, autour d’elle, se vide lentement ; les derniers pas s’éloignent vers la rue. Elle reste un instant devant son étal fermé, la main posée sur le vieux tabouret qu’elle n’utilise plus depuis des années mais qu’elle n’a jamais pensé à jeter. Puis elle s’en va, sans se retourner. Trois mois plus tard, le même tabouret est toujours là, à la même place exacte, comme si personne, entre-temps, n’y avait touché.

Commentaire littéraire (extrait) : Jusqu’à l’ellipse finale, le passage adopte le point de vue de la vendeuse elle-même : le narrateur reste extérieur aux événements, mais le lecteur ne perçoit que ce qu’elle perçoit et pense — le bruit du balai, la question sur Mounir — procédé classique de focalisation interne. Par l’accumulation de gestes précis et répétés — ranger, compter, vérifier — le texte donne à voir la fatigue et la vigilance d’une fin de journée, sans jamais nommer directement ni l’une ni l’autre. Il se dégage de l’ensemble du paragraphe une impression de calme précaire, renforcée par le ralentissement progressif du rythme des phrases. Le détail du vieux tabouret, jamais expliqué, laisse entendre un attachement au lieu que la vendeuse elle-même ne formule pas. L’indication « trois mois plus tard » marque une véritable ellipse narrative : le texte saute directement par-dessus tout un intervalle de temps, sans en rapporter aucun événement. Après l’ellipse, le point de vue se déplace brièvement : ce n’est plus ce que perçoit la vendeuse, absente de la scène, mais une observation extérieure et générale sur le tabouret resté en place, signe que le point de vue d’un texte peut changer d’un passage à l’autre, voire à l’intérieur d’un même court extrait.

Culture and Communication

Culture in Context — The explication de texte tradition

The close, evidence-grounded reading practiced in this chapter draws on a tradition of close textual reading long emphasized in French secondary and higher education, sometimes called l’explication de texte. This tradition favors interpretation that can be traced back to specific words, structures, or techniques in the text itself, rather than general impressions offered without textual support — the same discipline this chapter's four frames are designed to build.

Regional Variation — Francophone literary traditions are not a single tradition

Literary production and literary-critical conventions differ significantly across France, Québec, Belgium, Switzerland, and Francophone Africa, the Caribbean, and the Indian Ocean, each with distinct literary histories, canons, and critical vocabularies. This chapter's brief invented excerpt reflects a simple, classroom-appropriate metropolitan-French narrative style; the underlying skill — grounding interpretation in specific textual evidence — transfers across these varied Francophone literary traditions, which Unité III will continue to sample as it develops.

Common Mistakes

Common Mistake — Summarizing plot instead of naming a depiction

Error: Using donner à voir to introduce what is really just a restatement of a plot event (le passage donne à voir une vendeuse qui range son étal), rather than naming what the technique makes the reader perceive beyond the bare event.

Correction: Ask what the technique itself contributes: par l’accumulation de gestes précis, le passage donne à voir la fatigue d’une fin de journée names an effect of technique, not merely an event of plot.

Common Mistake — Stating an implication as though the text said it outright

Error: Dropping laisser entendre que and simply asserting the implied content as fact (elle hésite à quitter son emplacement), which overstates what the text actually states.

Correction: Keep the frame that marks the claim as an interpretation: le texte laisse entendre qu’elle hésite à quitter son emplacement, sans jamais le dire explicitement lets a reader see exactly which claims are grounded in explicit text and which are a supported reading.

Common Mistake — Naming an impression without textual evidence

Error: Using il se dégage de to name an impression that reflects the reader's personal reaction rather than something the text's specific choices actually support, with no detail offered as evidence.

Correction: Pair the impression with the detail that grounds it, as in il se dégage de ce paragraphe une impression de calme précaire, renforcée par le ralentissement progressif du rythme des phrases, so the claim can be checked against the text rather than taken on faith.

  6.   7. Une ellipse narrative means a gap where the story skips over a specific interval of time without describing it, as the passage does with the three months; le discours indirect libre means a technique blending a narrator's voice with a character's unspoken thoughts, without quotation marks, based on the definition given in the Receptive Only box.

Writing Workshop — Model text

Modèle — Commentaire littéraire pour le supplément

Ce court passage adopte le point de vue d’un jeune commerçant qui observe, depuis son étal, le vieux marché se vider peu à peu ; le narrateur, lui, reste extérieur aux événements et ne commente jamais directement ce que ressent le personnage. Par la longueur croissante des phrases, à mesure que la foule s’éloigne, le texte donne à voir un ralentissement du temps lui-même, plutôt qu’une simple description du lieu. Il se dégage de l’ensemble du passage une impression de solitude douce, renforcée par l’absence presque totale de dialogue. Le geste répété de ranger, puis de reranger, une même caisse déjà vide laisse entendre une réticence à quitter les lieux, sans que le texte ne le formule jamais directement. Un bref silence, juste avant qu’il n’éteigne la dernière lumière de son étal, renforce cette même impression sans jamais l’expliquer. Ce commentaire se limite volontairement à ce que le passage donne réellement à lire, plutôt qu’à ce qu’un lecteur pourrait vouloir y ajouter.

This short passage adopts the point of view of a young vendor who watches, from his stall, the old market gradually empty out; the narrator, meanwhile, stays outside the events and never directly comments on what the character feels. Through the increasing length of the sentences, as the crowd moves away, the text depicts a slowing of time itself, rather than a simple description of the place. A gentle sense of solitude emerges from the passage as a whole, reinforced by the near-total absence of dialogue. The repeated gesture of tidying, and re-tidying, an already-empty crate suggests a reluctance to leave, though the text never states this directly. A brief silence, just before he switches off his stall's last light, reinforces this same impression without ever explaining it. This commentary deliberately limits itself to what the passage actually offers to read, rather than what a reader might wish to add to it.
