Écrire pour publier

Publication-Level Writing · Speak and Write French: Specialized Mastery and Expert Communication

Chapter Objectives

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

  1. Distinguish publication-level revision — where a specific improvable point, a house-style requirement, an audience-appropriate register and genre, and a careful edit that preserves the writer's voice must each be named precisely — from Unité III's reading, arguing, translating, and historiographical work, while recognizing that preparing a text for print draws on all of Unité III's habits of precision and honesty.
  2. Use a newly productive revision-suggestion frame — le texte gagnerait à + infinitif — to identify a specific point in a draft that would benefit from a named change, without dismissing the draft as a whole.
  3. Use a newly productive house-style justification frame — les normes de la maison imposent de + infinitif plutôt que de + infinitif — to explain an editorial requirement by naming the house convention and the alternative it rules out.
  4. Use a newly productive audience-style-matching frame — un texte destiné à + public appelle un style plus/moins + adjectif — to state precisely how an intended readership and genre shape an appropriate style, without treating style as a matter of personal taste alone.
  5. Use a newly productive revision-without-distortion frame — on a resserré/allégé X sans dénaturer Y — to describe a specific edit (tightening or streamlining) while stating explicitly what was deliberately preserved.
  6. Use a newly productive publication vocabulary set: la ligne éditoriale, une coquille, resserrer, alléger, la cohérence stylistique, une épreuve, valider, publiable.
  7. Recycle, without any new tense, mood, subjonctif trigger, or clitic pattern, the complete Unité I, Unité II, and Unité III toolkits and the full Volume I–IV grammar inventory, selecting from among these rather than treating any of them as newly taught.
  8. Receive further controlled, receptive-only exposure to a small set of more technical editorial and publishing vocabulary encountered in a marked-up proof.
  9. Read a marked-up proof of the literary supplement's second issue and a house style memo for their stated revisions, editorial standards, and audience-style reasoning, distinguishing a substantive edit from a superficial preference.
  10. Complete a short editorial exercise (drafting a short passage, revising it for publication, and writing an editorial note explaining the revision) using all four of this chapter's newly productive frames at least once each.
  11. Move to editorial reading and listening — a marked-up proof, a house style memo, and an editorial-meeting listening excerpt — opening Unité IV toward the Chapter 20 final integration.

Communication Scenario

With the first issue of the literary supplement in print, M. Fontaine, Sophie, and Karim turn to the second issue — and this time, Sophie's own long-form piece on the neighborhood's disappearing workshops is on the table for revision. Karim has marked up a proof with specific suggestions; the newspaper's own house style memo sets requirements Sophie hasn't always followed; and M. Fontaine keeps asking who, exactly, the piece is for, and in what genre it is meant to work, since a version written for fellow journalists reads very differently from one written for the general readers who actually buy La Revue du quartier. Sophie is proud of her draft and initially resists any change as a threat to her voice; Karim insists that a careful edit can tighten a piece without erasing what makes it hers. Chapter 16 gives Sophie, Karim — and you — the tools of publication-level revision: naming a specific point that would benefit from change, justifying a house requirement against the alternative it rules out, matching style and genre to the intended readership, and describing an edit that preserves a writer's voice even as it tightens the prose.

  1. Name a specific point in a draft that would benefit from a stated revision.
  2. Justify a house-style requirement by naming the convention and the alternative it rules out.
  3. State how an intended readership and genre call for a more or less particular style.
  4. Describe a specific edit while naming what it deliberately preserved.

Pronunciation Focus

Pronunciation Focus — Delivering a suggested edit as collegial, not corrective

No new sound-spelling or vowel feature is introduced in this chapter. The task is delivery: a suggested revision can sound like an attack on the writer if delivered too flatly or too quickly, when in fact a good editorial suggestion at this level should sound collaborative even while being specific. A warm but precise tone on gagnerait à, and a level, non-apologetic tone when citing a house requirement, helps a listener hear that revision is a shared professional habit, not a personal criticism.

  • Suggesting a revision: Ce paragraphe gagnerait à préciser la date de la fermeture dès la première phrase. — warm, collegial tone on gagnerait à, not a tone of correction.
  • Citing a house requirement: Les normes de la maison imposent de citer une source précise plutôt que de parler d’une rumeur. — even, matter-of-fact delivery, treating the norm as shared rather than personally imposed.
  • Matching style to audience: Un texte destiné au grand public appelle un style moins technique que celui d’un rapport professionnel. — neutral, explanatory pace, not a tone of talking down to any reader.
  • Describing a careful edit: On a resserré l’introduction sans dénaturer le ton personnel du texte. — reassuring, confident delivery on sans dénaturer, so the writer hears that the edit protected, not erased, their voice.

Cumulative review: this chapter’s spoken practice also recycles active sound-spelling, liaison, enchaînement, elision, and rhythm systems from earlier volumes, with corrected classification for each recycling item below. Practice l’épreuve for elision of the definite article before a vowel-initial noun; une épreuve for enchaînement of the normally pronounced final consonant of une into the vowel-initial noun, together with the oral front-rounded vowel in épreuve; les épreuves for required liaison between the plural article and the vowel-initial noun; on a resserré for the nasal vowel in on together with the obligatory liaison between on and a; and cohérence stylistique for phrase rhythm across a longer noun phrase.

Key distinction — editorial revision is a fifth dimension, not a repetition of Unité III

Unité III asked what a text depicts (Ch. 11), how an argument holds together (Ch. 12), how meaning crosses languages (Ch. 13), and how a contested history is presented responsibly (Ch. 14). This chapter asks a different, practical question: given a finished draft, what specific change would make it ready for print, for this house, for this readership and genre — without losing what makes the writing worth publishing in the first place? An editor might use Chapter 12’s dans la mesure où to scope how far a suggested cut should go, or Chapter 14’s on aurait tort de réduire… à… to caution against over-trimming a nuanced point — but the editorial judgment itself, deciding what a text needs before it can be published, is this chapter’s own contribution.

16.1 Naming a specific improvable point: le texte gagnerait à + infinitif

Grammar Focus — A suggestion, not a verdict

Le texte gagnerait à + infinitive is newly productive. It names a specific point in a draft that would benefit from a stated change, framing the observation as an improvement the text could gain rather than a flaw it currently has. This differs from a blunt criticism (ce paragraphe est mal écrit), which names a problem without proposing a constructive path forward.

  • Le texte gagnerait à préciser, dès le premier paragraphe, la date exacte de la fermeture de l’atelier. The piece would benefit from specifying, right in the first paragraph, the exact date the workshop closed. le texte gagnerait à (newly productive) names a specific, constructive point for revision

16.2 Justifying a house requirement: les normes de la maison imposent de X plutôt que de Y

Grammar Focus — A shared convention, not a personal preference

Les normes de la maison imposent de + infinitive plutôt que de + infinitive is newly productive. It explains an editorial requirement by naming the house convention explicitly and the specific alternative it rules out, so a writer can see the requirement as a shared professional standard rather than an editor's personal taste. This differs from simply asserting a preference (je préfère qu’on dise…), which frames the same requirement as arbitrary.

  • Les normes de la maison imposent de vérifier chaque date historique dans une source écrite plutôt que de s’appuyer sur une mémoire non vérifiée. House standards require verifying every historical date in a written source rather than relying on unverified memory. les normes de la maison imposent de… plutôt que de… (newly productive) names the convention and the rejected alternative together

Note on collocation: vérifier auprès de fits naturally when checking with a person, authority, or organization (vérifier auprès du service des archives); for a written document itself, prefer vérifier dans, à partir de, or à l’aide de (vérifier une date dans un registre, à l’aide d’une source écrite).

16.3 Matching style and genre to readership, and editing without distortion

Building Block — Suggestion, house standard, audience/genre fit, and preserved voice together
[suggestion: le texte gagnerait à…] + [house standard: les normes de la maison imposent de… plutôt que de…] + [audience/genre fit: un texte destiné à… appelle un style plus/moins…] + [edit performed: on a resserré/allégé… sans dénaturer…]
Un texte destiné à + public appelle un style plus/moins + adjective is newly productive. It states precisely how an intended readership and genre shape an appropriate style, treating register as a functional fit between text, audience, and genre rather than a matter of personal taste; naming the genre explicitly (un portrait, un reportage, un essai) helps confirm that structure and tone actually fit the kind of text being prepared. On a resserré/allégé X sans dénaturer Y is newly productive. It describes a specific completed edit (tightening or streamlining) while stating explicitly what the edit deliberately preserved, so a writer can see that revision protected their voice rather than replacing it — a habit of honest, checkable description consistent with Chapter 13's naming of a translation rendering and Chapter 14's naming of a source-grounded claim.
Le texte gagnerait à préciser, dès le premier paragraphe, la date exacte de la fermeture de l’atelier, plutôt que de la mentionner seulement à la fin. Les normes de la maison imposent par ailleurs de vérifier chaque date dans une source écrite ou un registre professionnel plutôt que de s’appuyer uniquement sur un souvenir de famille. Comme ce texte est un portrait destiné à un lectorat de quartier plutôt qu’un rapport destiné à des historiens, il appelle un style moins technique et plus proche du ton d’un récit personnel. On a donc resserré le deuxième paragraphe, jugé trop long, sans dénaturer le ton intime des phrases sur l’odeur du bois et de la colle, qui restent le cœur du texte.
The piece would benefit from specifying the exact closing date of the workshop right in the first paragraph, rather than only mentioning it at the end. House standards also require verifying each date in a written source or professional register rather than relying solely on a family memory. Since this piece is a portrait intended for a neighborhood readership rather than a report intended for historians, it calls for a less technical style, closer in tone to a personal narrative. We therefore tightened the second paragraph, judged too long, without distorting the intimate tone of the sentences about the smell of wood and glue, which remain the heart of the piece.

Model Dialogue: La relecture avant le bouclage du deuxième numéro

Context: Karim reviews a marked-up proof of Sophie's piece on the neighborhood's disappearing workshops with Sophie and M. Fontaine. Register: professionnel neutre, collegial and specific.

Karim : J’ai relu ton épreuve. Dans l’ensemble, c’est très fort. Une seule chose : le texte gagnerait à préciser la date de fermeture dès le premier paragraphe, pas seulement à la fin. I read through your proof. Overall, it's very strong. One thing: the piece would benefit from specifying the closing date right in the first paragraph, not only at the end.
Sophie : J’ai gardé le suspense exprès ! Mais bon, je comprends l’idée. I kept the suspense on purpose! But okay, I get the idea.
M. Fontaine : Et il y a une autre question : la date vient de ton grand-oncle, si je me souviens bien ? And there's another question: the date comes from your great-uncle, if I remember correctly?
Sophie : Oui, c’est un souvenir de famille. Yes, it's a family memory.
M. Fontaine : Les normes de la maison imposent de vérifier chaque date dans une source écrite plutôt que de s’appuyer uniquement sur un souvenir de famille, même fiable. House standards require verifying each date in a written source rather than relying solely on a family memory, even a reliable one.
Karim : On peut chercher dans les registres professionnels, en complément des archives municipales que Sophie a déjà consultées pour l’article sur l’histoire du marché. We could look in the professional registers, in addition to the municipal archives Sophie already consulted for the piece on the market's history.
Sophie : D’accord. Et pour le style ? Karim trouve que mon deuxième paragraphe est trop technique. All right. And what about the style? Karim thinks my second paragraph is too technical.
Karim : Ce n’est pas un rapport pour des historiens, c’est un portrait pour nos lecteurs. Un texte destiné au lectorat du quartier appelle un style moins technique, plus proche du ton d’un récit personnel. This isn't a report for historians, it's a portrait for our readers. A piece intended for the neighborhood readership calls for a less technical style, closer to the tone of a personal narrative.
Sophie : Bon, d’accord. Mais je tiens absolument à garder la phrase sur l’odeur du bois et de la colle. Fine, all right. But I absolutely want to keep the sentence about the smell of wood and glue.
Karim : Bien sûr. On a resserré le paragraphe, mais sans dénaturer cette phrase-là ; elle reste le cœur du texte. Of course. We tightened the paragraph, but without distorting that sentence; it remains the heart of the piece.
M. Fontaine : Voilà un texte publiable : une date précisée, une source vérifiée, un style adapté au lectorat et au genre du portrait, et une voix préservée. Je valide. Now that's a publishable piece: a clarified date, a verified source, a style suited to the readership and the genre of the portrait, and a preserved voice. I'm signing off on it.

This dialogue models all four newly productive frames — suggestion, house standard, audience/genre fit, and preserved edit — while recycling Chapter 4's real-time repair habits as the team negotiates Sophie's resistance to change.

Culture and Communication

Culture in Context — Editorial revision as a collaborative professional norm

The habit modeled in this chapter — naming a specific suggestion, citing a shared house standard rather than a personal preference, matching style and genre to readership, and describing an edit honestly as preserving rather than erasing a writer's voice — reflects a broader professional norm found in many French-language publishing and journalistic settings: revision is treated as a collaborative craft rather than a hierarchy of correction. A writer who understands why a change is suggested, and what is deliberately kept, is more likely to trust the editorial process.

Regional Variation — House style varies by publication and region

House style conventions of the kind modeled in this chapter — source verification requirements, house preferences for register, or standard proofing procedures — vary from one publication to another and across French, Belgian, Swiss, Québécois, and other Francophone editorial traditions. Learning to justify a house requirement explicitly, rather than presenting it as an unquestionable universal rule, prepares learners to adapt to different editorial contexts across the Francophone world.

Common Mistakes

Common Mistake — Delivering a bare criticism instead of a constructive suggestion

Error: Stating a problem without a path forward (ce paragraphe est confus), which names a flaw but gives the writer nothing actionable.

Correction: Use the full frame: ce paragraphe gagnerait à séparer les deux idées en deux phrases distinctes names both the issue and a specific, actionable improvement.

Common Mistake — Presenting a house requirement as personal preference

Error: Framing an editorial standard as a personal wish (je préfère qu’on cite une source), which makes a shared professional standard sound negotiable or arbitrary.

Correction: Name the standard and its rationale: les normes de la maison imposent de citer une source vérifiable plutôt que de s’appuyer sur une rumeur presents the requirement as a shared convention, not a personal taste.

Common Mistake — Claiming a preserved voice without naming what was preserved

Error: Reassuring a writer vaguely (ne t’inquiète pas, on a gardé ton style) without naming any specific element that was protected, leaving the claim unverifiable.

Correction: Name the specific preserved element: on a resserré l’introduction sans dénaturer la phrase sur l’odeur du bois, qui reste intacte makes the claim checkable against the actual text.

Writing Workshop — Model text

Modèle — Rédiger, réviser, et justifier un texte destiné à la publication

Genre et lectorat : Un portrait destiné aux lecteurs du quartier, non à des chercheurs en histoire urbaine.

Brouillon original : L’ancienne blanchisserie de la rue des Tanneurs a fermé il y a plusieurs décennies. On y utilisait des machines à vapeur selon des procédés de blanchissage industriel typiques du début du siècle dernier. Les cuves étaient imposantes, et certains séchoirs mécaniques dataient d’avant la Première Guerre mondiale. Le travail y était bruyant et humide, rythmé par le bruit constant des machines. Plusieurs générations d’une même famille du quartier y ont travaillé, souvent dès l’adolescence. Aujourd’hui encore, la vapeur semble parfois monter, certains matins, de la cheminée éteinte depuis longtemps. Ce n’est qu’à la toute fin de ce texte que je révélerai le nom du dernier propriétaire.

Note éditoriale : Le texte gagnerait à préciser, dès la première phrase, le nom du dernier propriétaire plutôt que de le révéler seulement à la fin. Les normes de la maison imposent par ailleurs de vérifier chaque nom cité dans un annuaire professionnel de l’époque plutôt que de s’appuyer sur la seule mémoire de l’auteur. Comme ce texte est un portrait destiné à des lecteurs du quartier plutôt qu’un rapport destiné à des chercheurs en histoire urbaine, il appelle un style plus narratif et moins chargé de références techniques, même si le sujet touche à des méthodes de blanchisserie aujourd’hui disparues. On a donc resserré les deuxième et troisième phrases, initialement trop détaillées sur les machines utilisées, sans dénaturer la phrase sur la vapeur qui montait encore, certains matins, de la cheminée éteinte depuis longtemps, qui reste intacte et demeure le cœur du texte.

Version révisée : M. Antoine Berger, dernier propriétaire de la blanchisserie de la rue des Tanneurs, a fermé l’établissement il y a plusieurs décennies, un nom confirmé dans un annuaire professionnel de l’époque. On y lavait le linge à la vapeur, à l’ancienne, dans des cuves imposantes. Le travail y était bruyant et humide, rythmé par le bruit constant des machines. Plusieurs générations d’une même famille du quartier y ont travaillé, souvent dès l’adolescence. Aujourd’hui encore, la vapeur semble parfois monter, certains matins, de la cheminée éteinte depuis longtemps.

Genre and readership: A portrait intended for neighborhood readers, not urban history researchers.

Original draft: The former laundry on Rue des Tanneurs closed several decades ago. It used steam-powered machines following industrial laundering processes typical of the early twentieth century. The vats were imposing, and some mechanical dryers dated from before the First World War. The work there was loud and damp, paced by the constant noise of the machines. Several generations of the same neighborhood family worked there, often starting in adolescence. Even today, the steam sometimes seems to rise, on some mornings, from the long-extinguished chimney. Only at the very end of this piece will I reveal the last owner's name.

Editorial note: The piece would benefit from naming the last owner right in the first sentence rather than revealing it only at the end. House standards also require verifying each named person in a period professional directory rather than relying solely on the author's memory. Since this piece is a portrait intended for neighborhood readers rather than a report intended for urban history researchers, it calls for a more narrative style with fewer technical references, even though the subject touches on now-vanished laundering methods. We therefore tightened the second and third sentences, initially too detailed about the machinery used, without distorting the sentence about the steam that still seems to rise, on some mornings, from the long-extinguished chimney, which remains intact and remains at the heart of the piece.

Revised version: Mr. Antoine Berger, the last owner of the laundry on Rue des Tanneurs, closed the establishment several decades ago, a name confirmed in a period professional directory. Laundry was washed there with steam, the old way, in imposing vats. The work there was loud and damp, paced by the constant noise of the machines. Several generations of the same neighborhood family worked there, often starting in adolescence. Even today, the steam sometimes seems to rise, on some mornings, from the long-extinguished chimney.
