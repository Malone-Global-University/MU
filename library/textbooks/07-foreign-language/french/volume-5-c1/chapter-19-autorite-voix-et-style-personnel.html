Autorité, voix et style personnel

Authority, Voice, and Personal Style · Speak and Write French: Specialized Mastery and Expert Communication

Chapter Objectives

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

  1. Distinguish stylistic self-awareness — recognizing and naming one's own recurring choices across several pieces of writing — from Chapter 18's public argument and Chapter 16's single-document revision, while recognizing that all three depend on close, honest rereading of one's own text.
  2. Use a newly productive style-naming frame — ce qui caractérise mon style, c’est + groupe nominal — to name a specific, recurring feature of one's own writing precisely, rather than describing style only in vague terms such as « personnel » or « naturel ».
  3. Use a newly productive self-revision frame — en relisant, j’ai remplacé X par Y pour + infinitif — to describe a specific change made to one's own draft and the precise reason for it, distinct from Chapter 16's description of an edit made to someone else's text.
  4. Use a newly productive cross-text pattern frame — d’un texte à l’autre, on retrouve + groupe nominal — to identify a recurring feature across two or more of one's own pieces, grounding a style claim in more than a single example.
  5. Use a newly productive choice-justification frame — j’ai délibérément choisi de X plutôt que de Y, parce que Z — to defend a stylistic choice as deliberate rather than accidental, naming the rejected alternative and the reason.
  6. Use a newly productive style/voice vocabulary set: la voix, une signature stylistique, un tic d’écriture, la sobriété, une facilité, retravailler, épurer, assumer.
  7. Recycle, without any new tense, mood, subjonctif trigger, or clitic pattern, the complete Unité I, Unité II, Unité III, Chapter 16, Chapter 17, and Chapter 18 toolkits, selecting from among these rather than treating any of them as newly taught.
  8. Receive further controlled, receptive-only exposure to a small set of more technical stylistic-criticism vocabulary introduced for later recognition.
  9. Read a comparative self-assessment note in which a writer identifies patterns across a portrait, a synthesis, and a tribune, and identify a named style feature, a self-revision, a cross-text pattern, and a justified choice.
  10. Complete a short self-assessment exercise comparing at least two of the learner's own earlier pieces (from this volume or invented) and writing a short reflective note using all four of this chapter's newly productive frames at least once each.

Communication Scenario

With three pieces now published in the literary supplement — Sophie's portrait of the closed woodworking workshop (Chapter 16), the synthesis on the neighborhood's disappearing artisanal trades (Chapter 17), and the tribune arguing for a heritage-preservation fund (Chapter 18) — M. Fontaine asks Sophie and Karim to do something neither has done before: reread their own published work, side by side, and describe what makes it recognizably theirs. He is preparing a short editor's note for the supplement's final issue, introducing Sophie and Karim as writers rather than only as reporters of other people's stories, and he wants them to write their own paragraph for it. Sophie is uncomfortable at first — noticing her own habits feels like exposing them — but Karim points out that a habit named on purpose, and kept deliberately, is a strength, while the same habit left unnoticed can become a crutch. Together they reread all three pieces, name what recurs across them, decide which recurring choices to keep and which to revise, and write a short, honest account of their own developing voices. Chapter 19 gives Sophie, Karim — and you — the tools of stylistic self-awareness: naming a specific feature of one's own style, describing a deliberate self-revision, identifying a pattern across several texts, and justifying a stylistic choice as intentional.

  1. Name a specific, recurring feature of one's own writing.
  2. Describe a specific change made to one's own draft and why.
  3. Identify a pattern that recurs across more than one of one's own texts.
  4. Justify a stylistic choice as deliberate, naming the rejected alternative.

Receptive Only — More technical stylistic-criticism vocabulary

This chapter opens a receptive set of more technical stylistic-criticism vocabulary, introduced here for later recognition: l’idiolecte (an individual's characteristic language use, as distinct from a regional dialect), une anaphore stylistique (a deliberate repetition of the same word or phrase at the beginning of successive clauses or sentences for effect), le rythme de la phrase (sentence rhythm, as a stylistic feature in its own right), and une autocritique (a piece of self-criticism). Do not use any of these items productively yet; these items remain available receptively for later chapters and the Chapter 20 integration.

Pronunciation Focus

Pronunciation Focus — Speaking honestly about one's own habits

No new sound-spelling or vowel feature is introduced in this chapter. The task is delivery: naming one's own style should sound observant and confident, not falsely modest and not boastful. A level, curious tone on ce qui caractérise mon style, c’est… should sound like an honest observation, not a claim to greatness. A matter-of-fact tone on en relisant, j’ai remplacé… par… treats revision as ordinary craft, not confession. A tone of quiet recognition on d’un texte à l’autre, on retrouve… signals that the pattern was actually found, not invented for the exercise. A steady, unapologetic tone on j’ai délibérément choisi de… parce que… signals that the choice is owned, not merely explained away.

  • Naming a style feature: Ce qui caractérise mon style, c’est une préférence pour des phrases courtes. — observant, not boastful.
  • Describing a self-revision: En relisant, j’ai remplacé cet adjectif par un verbe plus précis, pour éviter une facilité. — matter-of-fact, craft-focused.
  • Naming a cross-text pattern: D’un texte à l’autre, on retrouve la même attention aux petits objets du quotidien. — quiet recognition, not surprise.
  • Justifying a choice: J’ai délibérément choisi de garder cette répétition plutôt que de la corriger, parce qu’elle imite le rythme du geste décrit. — steady, unapologetic.

Cumulative review: this chapter’s spoken practice also recycles active sound-spelling, liaison, enchaînement, elision, and rhythm systems from earlier volumes. Practice ce qui caractérise for phrase rhythm across the sequence of function words and the oral vowel contrasts in caractérise (no liaison applies at this boundary, since caractérise begins with a consonant); practice en relisant for the nasal vowel in en followed by a clean /ʁ/ onset in relisant, with smooth phrase rhythm rather than any enchaînement, since en ends in a nasal vowel with no consonant to resyllabify; and practice j’ai délibérément choisi for elision of je before the vowel-initial auxiliary and for phrase rhythm across a longer adverb.

Grammar: Nommer et retravailler sa propre voix

No new tense, mood, subjonctif trigger, or clitic pattern is introduced in this chapter.

Key distinction — style analysis turns the writer's own critical eye on their own work

Chapter 16 asked how someone else's draft could be improved for publication. Chapters 17 and 18 asked how evidence could be synthesized and argued honestly. This chapter asks a different question, turned inward: given several pieces I have already written, what actually recurs in my own choices, which of those recurring choices do I want to keep on purpose, and which have become an unexamined habit I should revise? A writer who cannot answer this question is at the mercy of accident; a writer who can answer it is beginning to write with authority.

19.1 Naming a specific feature: ce qui caractérise mon style, c’est

Grammar Focus — A precise claim, not a vague self-description

Ce qui caractérise mon style, c’est + groupe nominal is newly productive. It names one specific, checkable feature of a writer's own work — a preference for short sentences, a habit of returning to sensory detail, a tendency toward direct address — rather than reaching for a vague, unfalsifiable label such as un style personnel or un style naturel, which name nothing a reader could actually verify against the text.

  • Ce qui caractérise mon style, c’est une attention constante aux petits objets qui portent la mémoire d’un lieu. What characterizes my style is a constant attention to the small objects that carry a place's memory. ce qui caractérise mon style, c’est (newly productive) names a specific, checkable feature

A named feature should be checkable against actual sentences the writer can point to; a feature no reader could locate in the text is not yet a real observation.

19.2 Describing a self-revision: en relisant, j’ai remplacé X par Y pour + infinitif

Grammar Focus — A specific change and its reason

En relisant, j’ai remplacé X par Y pour + infinitive is newly productive. It describes one specific change a writer made to their own draft, and states precisely why, treating self-revision as a deliberate craft decision rather than an admission of an original mistake. This differs from Chapter 16’s on a resserré/allégé… sans dénaturer…, which describes an edit made collaboratively to someone else’s text; here the writer revises their own work and states their own reasoning.

  • En relisant mon portrait, j’ai remplacé l’adjectif « triste » par le geste précis de la main posée sur l’établi, pour éviter de nommer l’émotion directement. Rereading my portrait, I replaced the adjective "sad" with the precise gesture of the hand resting on the workbench, in order to avoid naming the emotion directly. en relisant, j’ai remplacé… par… pour… (newly productive) names the original, the replacement, and the reason

19.3 Finding a pattern across texts and owning a choice

Building Block — Naming, revising, comparing, and owning together
[naming: ce qui caractérise mon style, c’est…] + [self-revision: en relisant, j’ai remplacé… par… pour…] + [cross-text pattern: d’un texte à l’autre, on retrouve…] + [ownership: j’ai délibérément choisi de… plutôt que de…, parce que…]
D’un texte à l’autre, on retrouve + groupe nominal is newly productive. It grounds a style claim in more than a single example by identifying a feature that recurs across at least two of the writer’s own pieces, which is what distinguishes a genuine signature stylistique from a feature of a single text. J’ai délibérément choisi de X plutôt que de Y, parce que Z is newly productive. It defends a specific stylistic choice as intentional, naming the rejected alternative and the reason, so that a recurring feature reads as a deliberate signature rather than an unexamined tic; this differs from Chapter 18’s reframing frame, which corrects a reader’s likely misreading of an argument, whereas this frame defends a stylistic choice as intentional craft.
Ce qui caractérise mon style, c’est un retour constant aux matières concrètes du travail artisanal — le bois, la colle, le cuir, le métal — plutôt qu’à des catégories abstraites comme « le patrimoine ». En relisant ma tribune, j’ai remplacé la formule abstraite « quelques bâtiments » par les mots concrets « le travail du bois, du cuir, du métal, transmis d’une génération à l’autre », pour ancrer l’argument dans des gestes précis plutôt que dans une abstraction. D’un texte à l’autre — de mon portrait à ma tribune — on retrouve cette même attention aux matières : l’odeur du bois et de la colle dans le portrait, le bois, le cuir et le métal dans la tribune. J’ai délibérément choisi de nommer les matières et les métiers plutôt que de parler simplement de « patrimoine artisanal », parce qu’un lecteur retient mieux un geste ou une matière précise qu’une catégorie abstraite.
What characterizes my style is a constant return to the concrete materials of craft work — wood, glue, leather, metal — rather than to abstract categories like "heritage." Rereading my op-ed, I replaced the abstract phrase "a few buildings" with the concrete words "the work of wood, leather, and metal, passed down from one generation to the next," in order to ground the argument in precise gestures rather than in an abstraction. Across my texts — the portrait of my great-uncle's workshop and the op-ed on the preservation fund — this same attention to materials recurs: the smell of wood and glue in the portrait, wood, leather, and metal in the op-ed. I deliberately chose to name the materials and trades rather than simply speak of "artisanal heritage," because a reader remembers a precise gesture or material better than an abstract category.

Practice — Naming, self-revision, cross-text pattern, or ownership?

Decide which newly productive frame best fits each statement.

  1. Une chose revient sans cesse dans mes textes : je commence toujours par une image concrète avant d’introduire l’idée générale. (naming)
  2. Au deuxième brouillon, j’ai changé « très fatiguée » en « les mains tremblantes », afin de montrer plutôt que d’affirmer. (self-revision)
  3. Dans mon portrait comme dans ma tribune, je reviens toujours à une matière concrète du travail artisanal. (cross-text pattern)
  4. J’ai gardé cette phrase un peu longue au lieu de la couper en deux, car elle imite le souffle du témoin qui parle sans s’arrêter. (ownership)

Model Dialogue: Trois textes, une voix ?

Context: Sophie and Karim reread their three published pieces together to draft a short paragraph about their own writing for M. Fontaine's editor's note. Register: professionnel neutre, reflective and candid.

M. Fontaine : Relisez vos trois textes ensemble. Qu’est-ce qui vous appartient, à chacun, dans cette écriture ? Reread your three pieces together. What belongs to each of you, in this writing?
Sophie : C’est étrange à dire, mais ce qui caractérise mon style, c’est un retour constant aux matières concrètes du travail artisanal — le bois, la colle, le cuir, le métal. It's strange to say, but what characterizes my style is a constant return to the concrete materials of craft work — wood, glue, leather, metal.
Karim : C’est vrai. D’un texte à l’autre, on retrouve cette même attention : l’odeur du bois et de la colle dans ton portrait, et le bois, le cuir, le métal dans ta tribune. That's true. Across your texts, this same attention recurs: the smell of wood and glue in your portrait, and wood, leather, metal in your op-ed.
Sophie : En relisant la tribune, justement, j’avais d’abord écrit « quelques bâtiments », une formule assez abstraite. Je l’ai remplacée par « le travail du bois, du cuir, du métal, transmis d’une génération à l’autre », pour ancrer l’argument dans des gestes précis. Rereading the op-ed, in fact, I had first written "a few buildings," a fairly abstract phrase. I replaced it with "the work of wood, leather, and metal, passed down from one generation to the next," to ground the argument in precise gestures.
M. Fontaine : Et ce n’est pas juste un hasard, cette fois ? Une lectrice m’a dit que ta tribune parlait davantage de gestes concrets que de politique municipale. And this isn't just chance, this time? A reader told me your op-ed spoke more of concrete gestures than of municipal politics.
Sophie : J’ai délibérément choisi de nommer les matières et les métiers plutôt que de parler simplement de « patrimoine artisanal », parce qu’un lecteur retient mieux un geste précis qu’une catégorie abstraite. I deliberately chose to name the materials and trades rather than simply speak of "artisanal heritage," because a reader remembers a precise gesture better than an abstract category.
Karim : Moi, c’est plutôt l’inverse : ce qui caractérise mon style, c’est une tendance à nommer une source précise à chaque affirmation, presque par réflexe. For me, it's more the opposite: what characterizes my style is a tendency to name a precise source for every claim, almost by reflex.
Sophie : C’est vrai, et c’est une force dans ton article sur le marché. Mais parfois, ça devient un peu un tic : tu nommes même des sources évidentes. That's true, and it's a strength in your piece on the market. But sometimes it becomes a bit of a tic: you even name obvious sources.
Karim : Juste. En relisant ma synthèse, j’ai remplacé deux mentions de source redondantes par une seule note en fin de paragraphe, pour épurer le texte sans perdre la rigueur. Fair. Rereading my synthesis, I replaced two redundant source mentions with a single end-of-paragraph note, in order to pare down the text without losing rigor.
M. Fontaine : Voilà exactement ce que je voulais entendre : deux voix reconnaissables, chacune assumée, et chacune retravaillée quand il le fallait. Écrivez ce paragraphe pour le numéro final. That's exactly what I wanted to hear: two recognizable voices, each owned, and each reworked when necessary. Write that paragraph for the final issue.

This dialogue models all four newly productive frames — naming, self-revision, cross-text pattern, and ownership — while recycling Chapter 16's revision vocabulary and Chapter 17's honesty about limitations, now turned toward the writers' own habits.

Speaking Practice

Pratique contrôlée — Nommer un trait de style

With a partner, each person names one specific, checkable feature of their own writing (in French or in a language they know well), using ce qui caractérise mon style, c’est…, then gives one concrete example that shows it.

Pratique contrôlée — Décrire une autorévision

With a partner, describe one real or invented change you made to a piece of your own writing, using en relisant, j’ai remplacé… par… pour….

Item 1

en relisant mon brouillon, j’ai remplacé une longue explication par un exemple concret, pour rendre le passage plus vivant

Item 2

en relisant mon texte, j’ai remplacé trois adjectifs par un seul détail précis, pour éviter une facilité

Tâche communicative — L’atelier de relecture croisée

In a group of three, exchange two short invented or previously written texts each and discuss recognizable patterns across them. Your discussion must include, in any order, at least one instance of each:

  • a style feature named with ce qui caractérise mon style, c’est…;
  • a self-revision described with en relisant, j’ai remplacé… par… pour…;
  • a cross-text pattern named with d’un texte à l’autre, on retrouve…;
  • a choice defended with j’ai délibérément choisi de… plutôt que de…, parce que….

Pratique individuelle — Solo alternative

Record yourself rereading two of your own past pieces (from this volume's writing workshops or elsewhere) aloud and commenting on them, including every required element above.

Note d’auto-évaluation — Trois textes, deux voix

Sophie : Ce qui caractérise mon style, c’est une attention constante aux matières concrètes du travail artisanal — le bois, la colle, le cuir, le métal — plutôt qu’à des catégories abstraites comme « le patrimoine ». J’ai délibérément choisi de garder cette rigueur plutôt que d’écrire de façon plus fluide mais moins vérifiable, parce que la confiance du lecteur dans mon travail en dépend directement.

Writing Workshop

Complete the following writing task. A credible style claim rests on real evidence from your own text, so ground every claim in an actual sentence you can point to.

Tâche principale — Rédiger une note d’auto-évaluation stylistique : Choose at least two pieces of your own writing (from this volume's earlier writing workshops, or invented for this exercise) and write a short reflective note (ten to fourteen sentences total) that includes all of the following.

  • one style feature named with ce qui caractérise mon style, c’est…, supported by a specific example;
  • one self-revision described with en relisant, j’ai remplacé… par… pour…, naming the original wording, the replacement, and the reason;
  • one cross-text pattern named with d’un texte à l’autre, on retrouve…, naming which texts it recurs across;
  • one choice defended with j’ai délibérément choisi de… plutôt que de…, parce que…, naming a real rejected alternative.

Use only this chapter's tools and the recycled Unité I, Unité II, Unité III, Chapter 16, Chapter 17, and Chapter 18 toolkits; do not attempt a new tense, mood, subjonctif trigger, or clitic pattern. Before submitting, confirm that each claim is checkable against an actual sentence you can point to, that the self-revision names a genuine original and replacement, and that the defended choice names a real alternative you considered and rejected, not an invented strawman.

  1. Choose your texts. Select at least two pieces of your own writing to compare.
  2. Reread closely. Look for a feature that actually recurs, not one you assume must be there.
  3. Name the feature. State it precisely and point to an example.
  4. Find the pattern. Confirm the feature recurs across at least two texts.
  5. Describe a self-revision. Name a specific change you made and why.
  6. Defend a choice. Name a real alternative you rejected and your reason.
  7. Revise the note itself. Confirm every claim is checkable and that no unscheduled productive system appears.

Liste de révision — Editing checklist:

  • The named style feature is specific and supported by a real example.
  • The cross-text pattern genuinely recurs across at least two named texts.
  • The self-revision names a real original wording, a real replacement, and a specific reason.

Culture and Communication

Culture in Context — Voice as a recognized professional asset in French writing culture

French journalistic and literary culture often explicitly names and discusses a writer's voix or signature stylistique as a professional asset, not merely as an incidental byproduct of writing quickly. A columnist, essayist, or reporter developing a recognizable style over time is a familiar figure across French-language print culture, from national dailies to small regional and neighborhood publications like the fictional Revue du quartier in this volume's scenario. Naming one's own habits honestly, rather than either denying them out of false modesty or overselling them, reflects this broader professional norm.

Regional Variation — What counts as strong style varies

What is valued as a strong, distinctive style — spare and understated, richly descriptive, formally precise, or conversationally direct — varies across French, Belgian, Swiss, Québécois, and other Francophone literary and journalistic traditions, and across genres within any one of them. Learning to name one's own recurring choices precisely, rather than measuring them against a single fixed ideal of "good style," prepares learners to adapt their voice to different editorial contexts across the Francophone world.

Common Mistakes

Common Mistake — Naming style vaguely instead of specifically

Error: Describing one's own style only in unfalsifiable terms (j’ai un style assez personnel), which names nothing a reader could check against the actual text.

Correction: Use the full frame with a checkable feature: ce qui caractérise mon style, c’est une préférence pour des phrases courtes names something a reader can verify.

Common Mistake — Claiming a pattern from a single example

Error: Treating one instance in one text as if it already proved a signature stylistique, when a single occurrence could simply be an accident of that particular piece.

Correction: Use d’un texte à l’autre, on retrouve… only once the feature is confirmed across at least two genuinely distinct texts, as Sophie does across her portrait and tribune.

Common Mistake — Apologizing for a choice instead of owning it

Error: Explaining a stylistic choice defensively (je sais que ce n’est peut-être pas l’idéal, mais…), which undercuts the claim that the choice was deliberate.

Correction: State it as a genuine decision: j’ai délibérément choisi de garder cette répétition plutôt que de la corriger, parce qu’elle imite le rythme du geste décrit owns the choice rather than apologizing for it.

Writing Workshop — Model text

Modèle — Note d’auto-évaluation stylistique

J’ai relu un portrait que j’avais écrit sur l’ancien atelier de réparation de vélos de la rue Voltaire et mon reportage sur le marché aux puces du quartier, deux textes écrits à plusieurs mois d’intervalle et pour des publics légèrement différents. En les comparant, j’ai remarqué quelque chose que je n’avais jamais nommé consciemment auparavant. Ce qui caractérise mon style, c’est une tendance à décrire un lieu par le son qu’il produisait avant de dire ce qui s’y passait : le grincement d’une chaîne, le bruit d’un outil, avant même le nom du lieu. Dans le portrait de l’atelier de vélos, le nom du réparateur n’apparaît qu’au deuxième paragraphe ; c’est le cliquetis répété d’une chaîne qu’on remonte qui ouvre le texte. D’un texte à l’autre — le portrait de l’atelier de vélos et le reportage sur le marché — on retrouve cette même entrée par le son plutôt que par la description visuelle : le reportage sur le marché, lui aussi, commence par le bruit des sacs plastiques avant de décrire un seul étal. En relisant mon reportage sur le marché, j’ai remplacé une longue description visuelle des étals, qui occupait tout le premier paragraphe, par le simple bruit des sacs plastiques et des voix mêlées, pour rester fidèle à cette manière d’entrer dans un lieu. Ce changement m’a demandé de supprimer plusieurs phrases auxquelles je tenais pourtant beaucoup au premier brouillon. J’ai également remarqué, en comparant les deux textes, que je reviens souvent à des sons très ordinaires plutôt qu’à des sons spectaculaires, ce qui donne, je crois, une impression de proximité plutôt que de mise en scène. J’ai délibérément choisi de garder cette entrée sonore plutôt que de commencer, plus classiquement, par une description visuelle d’ensemble, parce qu’un lieu, avant d’être vu, est souvent d’abord entendu, et parce que cette approche me semble plus fidèle à la façon dont on découvre réellement un endroit nouveau. Un lecteur pressé pourrait juger cette entrée trop lente, mais j’assume ce choix, parce qu’il correspond à ce que je veux vraiment faire ressentir avant d’expliquer quoi que ce soit. C’est en tout cas ce que cette relecture croisée m’a permis de comprendre sur ma propre manière d’écrire.

I reread a portrait I had written about the old bicycle-repair workshop on Rue Voltaire, and my report on the neighborhood flea market, two pieces written several months apart and for slightly different audiences. Comparing them, I noticed something I had never consciously named before. What characterizes my style is a tendency to describe a place through the sound it made before saying what happened there: the creak of a chain, the noise of a tool, even before the name of the place. In the bicycle-workshop portrait, the repairman's name does not appear until the second paragraph; it is the repeated clicking of a chain being rewound that opens the piece. Across my texts — the bicycle-workshop portrait and the flea-market report — this same entry point through sound rather than visual description recurs: the flea-market report, too, opens with the noise of plastic bags before describing a single stall. Rereading my flea-market report, I replaced a long visual description of the stalls, which had taken up the whole first paragraph, with the simple noise of plastic bags and mingled voices, to stay true to this way of entering a place. This change required me to cut several sentences I had actually been quite attached to in the first draft. Comparing the two texts, I also noticed that I often return to very ordinary sounds rather than dramatic ones, which gives, I think, an impression of closeness rather than staging. I deliberately chose to keep this sound-based entry rather than begin, more conventionally, with an overall visual description, because a place, before it is seen, is often first heard, and because this approach seems to me truer to how one actually discovers a new place. A hurried reader might find this entry too slow, but I stand behind this choice, because it matches what I really want the reader to feel before I explain anything. That, in any case, is what this cross-reading allowed me to understand about my own way of writing.
