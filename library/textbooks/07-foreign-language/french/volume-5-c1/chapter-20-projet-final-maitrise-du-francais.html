Projet final : Maîtrise du français

Final C2 Integration · Speak and Write French: Specialized Mastery and Expert Communication

Chapter Objectives

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

  1. Combine, in a single comprehensive portfolio, Chapter 16's revision-suggestion, house-style, audience/genre, and preserved-edit frames; Chapter 17's convergence, divergence, positioning, and gap frames; Chapter 18's responsibility, objection/answer, stakes, and reframing frames; and Chapter 19's style-naming, self-revision, cross-text-pattern, and choice-justification frames.
  2. Select the right Unité IV tool for a given editorial moment from the complete toolkit, rather than treating any single chapter's frames as the default for every task.
  3. Revise a piece of writing for publication using le texte gagnerait à, les normes de la maison imposent de… plutôt que de…, un texte destiné à… appelle un style plus/moins…, and on a resserré/allégé… sans dénaturer…, as in Chapter 16.
  4. Synthesize multiple sources using plusieurs sources convergent pour montrer que, les sources divergent… sur la question de, cette synthèse s’inscrit dans le prolongement de…, tout en…, and il subsiste une lacune quant à…, as in Chapter 17.
  5. Argue a public position using il appartient à… de…, on objectera que…, il n’empêche que…, ce qui est en jeu, c’est…, and loin de…, il s’agit plutôt de…, as in Chapter 18.
  6. Reflect honestly on one's own developing voice using ce qui caractérise mon style, c’est…, en relisant, j’ai remplacé… par… pour…, d’un texte à l’autre, on retrouve…, and j’ai délibérément choisi de… plutôt que de…, parce que…, as in Chapter 19.
  7. Produce no new tense, mood, subjonctif trigger, or clitic pattern in this chapter; rely entirely on Volumes I–IV, the complete Unité I, Unité II, and Unité III toolkits, and Chapters 16–19 of this volume.
  8. Complete a comprehensive expert portfolio requiring listening, reading, oral, and written evidence across all four Unité IV dimensions within a single realistic editorial scenario, without a chapter-by-chapter script to follow.
  9. Self-assess overall C2 readiness across the full volume using the consolidated review grid at the end of this chapter, identifying any dimension of Volume V that would benefit from further practice.

Communication Scenario

La Revue du quartier's literary supplement is closing its first full year with a final dossier: an editor's retrospective drawing together everything Sophie and Karim have produced since Chapter 16. M. Fontaine wants four linked pieces finished and defended in one closing session: a short piece revised for publication (echoing the portrait from Chapter 16, but on a new topic Sophie chooses), a synthesis note comparing at least three sources on a shared local question (as in Chapter 17), a public op-ed built on that synthesis (as in Chapter 18), and a stylistic self-assessment comparing at least two of their own pieces from across the year (as in Chapter 19). None of these four tasks is new; what is new is that Sophie and Karim must move among all four in a single evening, deciding for themselves which Unité IV tool a given editorial question calls for, exactly as they already learned to do across Unité III's Chapter 15 integration. Because this is the volume's final chapter, the dossier also serves as the capstone for the entire C1→C2 progression: expert discourse and idiom from Unité I, specialized mediation from Unité II, literary and historiographical interpretation from Unité III, and publication-level authority from Unité IV all remain available and are expected to surface naturally wherever the editorial work calls for them. Chapter 20 does not teach new tools; it asks Sophie, Karim — and you — to use the complete Volume V toolkit, together, to close the year responsibly.

  1. Revise a short piece for publication, naming a suggestion, a house requirement, an audience/genre fit, and a preserved edit.
  2. Synthesize at least three sources, naming a convergence, a divergence, a positioning claim, and a gap.
  3. Argue a public position grounded in that synthesis, naming a responsibility, an anticipated objection and answer, a stake, and a reframing.
  4. Reflect on one's own voice across the year's work, naming a style feature, a self-revision, a cross-text pattern, and a deliberate choice.

Pronunciation Focus

Pronunciation Focus — Cumulative review, no new feature

No new sound-spelling, vowel, liaison, or delivery feature is introduced in Chapter 20. This chapter's integrated task instead requires learners to move fluidly among the four delivery styles practiced separately in Chapters 16–19: the warm, collegial tone that frames a revision suggestion as constructive rather than corrective (Chapter 16); the level, unhurried tone that distinguishes a convergence claim from a more evenly weighted divergence claim (Chapter 17); the firm-but-fair public delivery that voices a real objection honestly before answering it, and that names stakes without melodrama (Chapter 18); and the observant, unapologetic tone that names one's own style honestly, without false modesty or overselling (Chapter 19). Learners should treat all of these as previously practiced tendencies to draw on as needed, not as a checklist to perform mechanically in a fixed order.

Because this is the volume's final assessment chapter, this chapter's spoken tasks are also evaluated for cumulative intelligibility built up across all five volumes: accurate oral and nasal vowels and front rounded vowels, an intelligible French r, correctly applied required and forbidden liaison, natural enchaînement and elision in connected speech, and rhythm and intonation appropriate to statements, questions, concessions, and emphasis. No new pronunciation system is taught here; the requirement is sustained control of everything already active, demonstrated at C2-level fluency and naturalness.

Grammar: L’ensemble de l’Unité IV, mobilisé

Purpose: consolidate, without introducing any new productive system, the four independent dimensions built across Unité IV — publication-level revision (Chapter 16), research synthesis (Chapter 17), public intervention (Chapter 18), and stylistic self-awareness (Chapter 19) — and practice selecting the right tool for a given editorial moment, exactly as Chapter 15 asked for Unité III.

Key distinction — four independent dimensions, not four steps in a sequence

Chapters 16–19 each added a dimension that operates independently of the others: a single draft can be revised for publication without any multi-source comparison at all; several sources can be synthesized honestly without the writer ever taking a public position on them; a public argument can be built and defended without any explicit reflection on the writer's own style; and a writer can examine their own recurring habits across several pieces without revising, synthesizing, or arguing anything new in the process. This chapter does not ask learners to apply the four dimensions in a fixed order. It asks learners to recognize, moment by moment, which dimension (or combination of dimensions) a given editorial question actually calls for, and to reach for the corresponding tool — the same discipline Chapter 15 asked of Unité III, now applied to the full arc of Unité IV.

20.1 Recognizing which Unité IV dimension is in play

Grammar Focus — A diagnostic question for each dimension

Before choosing a tool, ask which question the moment is actually posing:

  • Publication-level revision (Ch. 16): Does the question concern a specific point that would benefit from change, a house convention and its rejected alternative, how readership and genre shape style, or an edit that must be described as preserving the writer's voice? Reach for le texte gagnerait à…, les normes de la maison imposent de… plutôt que de…, un texte destiné à… appelle un style plus/moins…, and on a resserré/allégé… sans dénaturer….
  • Research synthesis (Ch. 17): Does the question concern what several independent sources actually agree on, a genuine and nameable point of disagreement, how new work relates to existing work, or what the sources, taken together, do not yet establish? Reach for plusieurs sources convergent pour montrer que…, les sources divergent… sur la question de…, cette synthèse s’inscrit dans le prolongement de…, tout en…, and il subsiste une lacune quant à….
  • Public intervention (Ch. 18): Does the question concern who specifically should act, a real objection that deserves a fair hearing and a direct answer, what is genuinely at stake beyond the surface topic, or a likely misreading of the writer's own position? Reach for il appartient à… de…, on objectera que…, il n’empêche que…, ce qui est en jeu, c’est…, and loin de…, il s’agit plutôt de….
  • Stylistic self-awareness (Ch. 19): Does the question concern a specific, checkable feature of the writer's own work, a concrete change made and the reason for it, a pattern confirmed across more than one of the writer's own texts, or a stylistic choice the writer wants to defend as intentional? Reach for ce qui caractérise mon style, c’est…, en relisant, j’ai remplacé… par… pour…, d’un texte à l’autre, on retrouve…, and j’ai délibérément choisi de… plutôt que de…, parce que….

More than one dimension can apply to the same editorial moment. A synthesis claim (Ch. 17) can ground a responsibility claim in the same paragraph (Ch. 18), just as Sophie and Karim moved fluidly among all four dimensions in assembling the final dossier.

Building Block — One closing dossier moving through all four Unité IV dimensions
[revision: suggestion + house standard + audience/genre fit + preserved edit] → [synthesis: convergence + divergence + positioning + gap] → [public intervention: responsibility + objection/answer + stakes + reframing] → [style: naming + self-revision + cross-text pattern + ownership]
No single new frame is introduced here; every tool below was already newly productive in Chapters 16–19. The same closing dossier — a revised piece, a synthesis, a tribune, and a self-assessment — is treated through all four dimensions in turn, showing how a single year's editorial work can require careful revision, honest synthesis, responsible advocacy, and clear-eyed self-reflection all within the same closing session.
Karim, sur la révision d’un court portrait de Sophie sur une ancienne mercerie du quartier : Ce paragraphe gagnerait à nommer la mercière dès la première phrase. Les normes de la maison imposent de vérifier ce nom dans un registre professionnel plutôt que de s’appuyer sur un souvenir de voisinage. Comme ce texte est un portrait destiné à nos lecteurs de quartier, il appelle un style narratif et non un style de rapport. On a donc resserré le deuxième paragraphe sans dénaturer la phrase sur le bruit des ciseaux, qui reste le cœur du texte. [Ch. 16] Sophie, sur la synthèse qui accompagne ce portrait : Plusieurs sources convergent pour montrer que les merceries de quartier ont presque toutes fermé depuis 1995. Les sources divergent en revanche sur la question de la cause principale. Cette synthèse s’inscrit dans le prolongement de notre article sur les ateliers artisanaux, tout en apportant un éclairage nouveau fondé sur les registres commerciaux de la ville. Il subsiste néanmoins une lacune quant au nombre exact d’emplois perdus. [Ch. 17] Karim, sur la tribune qui suit : Il appartient à la mairie de faciliter l’installation de nouveaux commerces de proximité dans les locaux vacants. On objectera que le marché immobilier ne s’y prête pas facilement ; il n’empêche qu’un dispositif municipal existait déjà pour ce type de local avant d’être abandonné. Ce qui est en jeu, ce n’est pas seulement la présence de vitrines vides, mais un lien de proximité entre habitants et commerçants. Loin de proposer un retour nostalgique au passé, il s’agit plutôt de donner à ces commerces une chance réelle de s’installer. [Ch. 18] Sophie, en relisant l’ensemble de l’année : Ce qui caractérise mon style, c’est un retour constant aux gestes et aux matières concrètes du travail artisanal. D’un texte à l’autre — de mon portrait de menuisier à ce portrait de mercière — on retrouve cette même attention aux objets du métier. En relisant ce dernier portrait, j’ai remplacé « une femme dévouée » par le geste précis de ranger un dernier bouton dans sa boîte, pour montrer plutôt que d’affirmer. J’ai délibérément choisi de garder cette entrée par le geste plutôt que par une présentation biographique, parce qu’un lecteur retient mieux un geste précis qu’une généralité. [Ch. 19]
Karim, on revising a short portrait Sophie wrote about a former neighborhood haberdashery: This paragraph would benefit from naming the shopkeeper in the first sentence. House standards require verifying this name in a professional register rather than relying on a neighborhood memory. Since this piece is a portrait intended for our neighborhood readers, it calls for a narrative style rather than a report style. We therefore tightened the second paragraph without distorting the sentence about the sound of the scissors, which remains the heart of the piece. [Ch. 16] Sophie, on the synthesis accompanying this portrait: Several sources converge in showing that neighborhood haberdasheries have almost all closed since 1995. The sources disagree, however, on the main cause. This synthesis builds on our piece on artisanal workshops, while adding a new perspective grounded in the city's commercial registers. A gap nonetheless remains regarding the exact number of jobs lost. [Ch. 17] Karim, on the following op-ed: It falls to the municipality to facilitate the installation of new local shops in vacant premises. One will object that the real-estate market does not easily allow for this; it remains the case that a municipal program already existed for this type of premises before being abandoned. What is at stake is not just empty storefronts, but a bond of proximity between residents and shopkeepers. Far from proposing a nostalgic return to the past, the point is rather to give these shops a real chance to open. [Ch. 18] Sophie, rereading the whole year's work: What characterizes my style is a constant return to the gestures and concrete materials of craft work. Across my texts — from my portrait of the woodworker to this portrait of the shopkeeper — this same attention to the objects of a trade recurs. Rereading this latest portrait, I replaced "a devoted woman" with the precise gesture of putting a last button away in its box, in order to show rather than assert. I deliberately chose to keep this entry through gesture rather than a biographical presentation, because a reader remembers a precise gesture better than a generality. [Ch. 19]

Model Dialogue: La dernière séance avant le bouclage final

Context: the final editorial session of the year, closing the literary supplement's dossier. M. Fontaine, Sophie, and Karim review the revised portrait, the synthesis, the tribune, and the stylistic self-assessment as a single closing dossier. Register: professionnel neutre to soutenu, reflective and precise throughout.

M. Fontaine : Alors, le dossier de clôture. Sophie, ton portrait de la mercière : où en est la révision ? So, the closing dossier. Sophie, your portrait of the shopkeeper: where does the revision stand?
Sophie : Karim m’a fait remarquer que le texte gagnerait à nommer Mme Aziz dès la première phrase plutôt qu’à la fin. Les normes de la maison imposent aussi de vérifier son nom dans un registre plutôt que de s’appuyer sur mon souvenir. On a resserré le deuxième paragraphe sans dénaturer la phrase sur le bruit des ciseaux. Karim pointed out that the piece would benefit from naming Mme Aziz in the first sentence rather than at the end. House standards also require verifying her name in a register rather than relying on my memory. We tightened the second paragraph without distorting the sentence about the sound of the scissors.
M. Fontaine : Bien. Et la synthèse qui l’accompagne ? Good. And the synthesis that goes with it?
Sophie : Plusieurs sources convergent pour montrer que les merceries de quartier ont presque toutes fermé depuis 1995, mais les sources divergent sur la cause principale. Cette synthèse s’inscrit dans le prolongement de notre article sur les ateliers artisanaux, tout en apportant un éclairage fondé sur les registres commerciaux. Il subsiste une lacune quant au nombre exact d’emplois perdus. Several sources converge in showing that neighborhood haberdasheries have almost all closed since 1995, but the sources disagree on the main cause. This synthesis builds on our piece on artisanal workshops, while adding a perspective grounded in commercial registers. A gap remains regarding the exact number of jobs lost.
M. Fontaine : Karim, ta tribune s’appuie là-dessus ? Karim, does your op-ed build on this?
Karim : Oui. Il appartient à la mairie de faciliter l’installation de nouveaux commerces dans les locaux vacants. On objectera que le marché ne s’y prête pas ; il n’empêche qu’un dispositif municipal existait déjà avant d’être abandonné. Ce qui est en jeu, c’est le lien de proximité entre habitants et commerçants. Loin de proposer un retour nostalgique, il s’agit plutôt de donner une vraie chance à ces commerces. Yes. It falls to the municipality to facilitate the installation of new shops in vacant premises. One will object that the market doesn't allow for this; it remains the case that a municipal program already existed before being abandoned. What's at stake is the bond of proximity between residents and shopkeepers. Far from proposing a nostalgic return, the point is rather to give these shops a real chance.
M. Fontaine : Et pour finir, votre bilan de style à tous les deux ? And to finish, your stylistic self-assessment, both of you?
Sophie : Ce qui caractérise mon style, c’est un retour constant aux gestes concrets du travail artisanal. D’un texte à l’autre, on retrouve cette attention : du menuisier à la mercière. En relisant ce dernier portrait, j’ai remplacé « une femme dévouée » par le geste de ranger un dernier bouton dans sa boîte. J’ai délibérément choisi cette entrée par le geste plutôt qu’une présentation biographique, parce qu’un lecteur retient mieux un geste précis. What characterizes my style is a constant return to the concrete gestures of craft work. Across my texts, this attention recurs: from the woodworker to the shopkeeper. Rereading this latest portrait, I replaced "a devoted woman" with the gesture of putting a last button away. I deliberately chose this entry through gesture rather than a biographical presentation, because a reader remembers a precise gesture better.
Karim : Et moi, ce qui caractérise mon style reste ma tendance à nommer une source précise à chaque affirmation — mais j’ai appris, cette année, à épurer quand la rigueur tournait au tic. And for me, what characterizes my style remains my tendency to name a precise source for every claim — but I learned, this year, to pare it down when the rigor turned into a tic.
M. Fontaine : Voilà un an de travail qui se referme sur un dossier cohérent : un texte révisé, une synthèse honnête, une tribune assumée, et deux voix reconnaissables. Le numéro est prêt. Here's a year of work closing on a coherent dossier: a revised piece, an honest synthesis, an owned op-ed, and two recognizable voices. The issue is ready.

This dialogue moves through all four Unité IV dimensions within a single closing editorial session, recycling Chapter 15's model of integration and Chapter 19's stylistic honesty now applied to the full year's portfolio.

Writing Workshop

The closing dossier modeled in this chapter — where careful revision, honest synthesis, responsible public argument, and clear-eyed self-reflection all surface within a single evening's work — reflects a recognizable arc in French-language journalism, publishing, and civic life: writing that moves from private craft to public responsibility. Regional Variation — A cumulative reminder for the whole series

As throughout this volume, this chapter uses a simplified metropolitan-French editorial setting; the specific portrait, synthesis, and tribune are fictional, while the broader habits of careful revision, honest synthesis, responsible advocacy, and stylistic self-awareness reflect genuine practices across the Francophone world. Belgium, Switzerland, Québec, and Francophone Africa, the Caribbean, and the Indian Ocean each bring their own editorial, argumentative, and stylistic traditions to bear on comparable questions. The underlying skills transfer across Francophone contexts even where the specific texts and disputes differ, and learners are encouraged to seek out authentic Francophone journalism, essays, and literature from across the Francophone world as they continue beyond this volume.

Common Mistakes

Common Mistake — Reaching for the wrong chapter's tool

Error: Using a synthesis frame (Chapter 17) when the actual question is about revising a single draft (Chapter 16), or using a style-reflection frame (Chapter 19) when the actual question is about naming public responsibility (Chapter 18).

Correction: Ask the diagnostic question for each dimension before choosing a tool (see Grammar 20.1). A question about one draft's readiness for print is a revision question, not a synthesis question; a question about who should act is a responsibility question, not a style question.

Common Mistake — Treating this chapter as a place for new grammar

Error: Introducing a new tense, mood, subjonctif trigger, or clitic pattern in this chapter's integrated task because the capstone scenario feels demanding enough to justify it.

Correction: Chapter 20 is the volume's final integration chapter by design. Its challenge is combining Chapters 16–19's tools fluently under a single scenario, not expanding the grammar inventory. Everything needed is already active from Volumes I–IV, Unité I, Unité II, Unité III, and Chapters 16–19.

Common Mistake — Letting the op-ed quietly overwrite the synthesis's honesty

Error: In the rush of an integrated task that also covers revision and style reflection, using the op-ed's confident argument (Ch. 18) to imply that a gap the synthesis honestly named (Ch. 17) has actually been resolved.

Correction: Keep the two moves distinct: an op-ed may argue confidently for action while still naming, explicitly, any gap the underlying synthesis identified, exactly as Chapter 18's own model op-ed did with Chapter 17's unresolved causes.

Writing Workshop — Model text

Modèle — Dossier de clôture

Partie 1 (Ch. 16) — Brouillon original : Un café populaire du quartier a fermé le mois dernier après plusieurs décennies d’activité. Chaque matin à sept heures, l’odeur du café moulu envahissait la salle avant l’arrivée des premiers habitués, toujours assis aux mêmes tables. On y servait un café assez ordinaire, préparé selon des méthodes traditionnelles qui n’avaient pas beaucoup changé depuis les années 1970. Le lieu était modeste, un peu sombre, mais chaleureux, avec un vieux comptoir en bois et des chaises dépareillées. Plusieurs générations de la même famille de commerçants ont tenu ce café avant sa fermeture. Ce n’est qu’à la fin de cet article que je révélerai le nom du dernier tenancier.

Note éditoriale : Le texte gagnerait à remplacer l’étiquette vague « un café populaire » par l’identification précise de M. Ferrand dès la première phrase plutôt qu’à la fin. Les normes de la maison imposent de vérifier ce nom auprès de la chambre de commerce plutôt que de s’appuyer sur une rumeur de quartier. Comme ce texte est un portrait destiné à nos lecteurs, et non un rapport économique, il appelle un style narratif et sensoriel plutôt qu’un style de rapport, même si le sujet touche à des données commerciales vérifiables. On a donc resserré la troisième phrase, consacrée à la préparation du café, et la quatrième, trop détaillée sur le mobilier, sans dénaturer la deuxième phrase sur l’odeur du café moulu chaque matin à sept heures, qui reste le cœur du texte.

Version révisée : M. Ferrand, dernier tenancier du café de la rue des Tanneurs, a fermé l’établissement le mois dernier après plusieurs décennies d’activité, un nom confirmé auprès de la chambre de commerce. Chaque matin à sept heures, l’odeur du café moulu envahissait la salle avant même l’arrivée des premiers habitués, toujours assis aux mêmes tables. Le lieu était modeste, un peu sombre, mais chaleureux. Plusieurs générations de la même famille de commerçants ont tenu ce café avant sa fermeture.

Partie 2 (Ch. 17) : Notre corpus comprend un article de presse régionale, un rapport de la chambre de commerce, et deux témoignages d’anciens tenanciers de cafés voisins — quatre sources en tout. Plusieurs sources convergent pour montrer que les cafés indépendants du quartier ont diminué de moitié en quinze ans : l’article, le rapport, et les deux témoins décrivent tous, indépendamment les uns des autres, cette même tendance. Les sources divergent en revanche sur la question du rôle exact des grandes chaînes de café dans ce déclin : le rapport de la chambre de commerce l’attribue en grande partie à la concurrence de ces chaînes, tandis que les deux tenanciers interrogés insistent plutôt sur la hausse des loyers commerciaux survenue à partir de 2005. Cette synthèse s’inscrit dans le prolongement du rapport de la chambre de commerce, qui reste la source la plus systématique sur la période, tout en apportant un éclairage nouveau fondé sur l’expérience directe de deux professionnels que ce rapport n’avait pas interrogés. Il subsiste néanmoins une lacune quant à l’effet précis du télétravail sur la fréquentation de ces cafés depuis 2020, qu’aucune des quatre sources consultées, toutes antérieures à cette période, ne documente de façon satisfaisante.

Partie 3 (Ch. 18) : Il appartient à la mairie de simplifier les démarches administratives pour l’ouverture d’un café indépendant dans le quartier. On objectera, non sans raison, que la concurrence des grandes chaînes relève du marché et non de la politique municipale, et que la mairie ne peut pas garantir la survie de chaque commerce. Il n’empêche qu’un dispositif municipal d’aide à l’installation des commerces de centre-ville existait déjà il y a dix ans, avant d’être discrètement restreint sans explication publique. Ce qui est en jeu, ce n’est pas seulement la présence de vitrines vides ou occupées, mais un lieu de rencontre quotidien entre voisins que ni une chaîne ni un local vide ne peuvent remplacer. Loin de proposer d’interdire ou de limiter les grandes chaînes, cette tribune demande seulement que les commerces indépendants reçoivent, eux aussi, une chance réelle de s’installer et de durer.

Partie 4 (Ch. 19) : Ce qui caractérise mon style, c’est une attention aux odeurs et aux sons ordinaires d’un lieu, que je place avant sa description visuelle. D’un texte à l’autre — de ce portrait du café à mon portrait de l’atelier de menuiserie écrit plus tôt cette année — on retrouve cette même entrée sensorielle : l’odeur du café moulu ici, l’odeur du bois et de la colle là-bas. En relisant le portrait du café, j’ai remplacé l’étiquette vague « un café populaire » par l’identification précise de M. Ferrand, pour ancrer le texte dans une personne réelle plutôt que dans une généralité. J’ai délibérément choisi de garder la phrase sur l’odeur du café moulu avant toute description visuelle de la salle, parce qu’un lecteur se souvient mieux d’une odeur précise que d’un décor détaillé.

Part 1 (Ch. 16) — Original draft: A popular neighborhood café closed last month after several decades in business. Every morning at seven, the smell of ground coffee filled the room before the first regulars arrived, always seated at the same tables. It served fairly ordinary coffee, prepared using traditional methods that hadn't changed much since the 1970s. The place was modest, a little dark, but warm, with an old wooden counter and mismatched chairs. Several generations of the same shopkeeping family ran this café before its closing. Only at the end of this piece will I reveal the last owner's name.

Editorial note: The piece would benefit from replacing the vague label "a popular café" with the precise identification of M. Ferrand in the first sentence rather than at the end. House standards require verifying this name with the chamber of commerce rather than relying on neighborhood rumor. Since this piece is a portrait intended for our readers, not an economic report, it calls for a narrative, sensory style rather than a report style, even though the subject touches on verifiable commercial data. We therefore tightened the third sentence, about the coffee's preparation, and the fourth, too detailed about the furniture, without distorting the second sentence about the smell of coffee ground every morning at seven, which remains the heart of the piece.

Revised version: M. Ferrand, the last owner of the café on Rue des Tanneurs, closed the establishment last month after several decades in business, a name confirmed with the chamber of commerce. Every morning at seven, the smell of ground coffee filled the room even before the first regulars arrived, always seated at the same tables. The place was modest, a little dark, but warm. Several generations of the same shopkeeping family ran this café before its closing.

Part 2 (Ch. 17): Our corpus includes a regional press article, a chamber-of-commerce report, and two testimonies from owners of neighboring cafés — four sources in total. Several sources converge in showing that independent cafés in the neighborhood have halved in fifteen years: the article, the report, and the two witnesses all independently describe this same trend. The sources disagree, however, on the exact role of large chains in this decline: the chamber-of-commerce report attributes it largely to competition from these chains, while the two owners interviewed instead emphasize the rise in commercial rents starting in 2005. This synthesis builds on the chamber-of-commerce report, which remains the most systematic source for the period, while adding a new perspective grounded in the direct experience of two professionals the report had not interviewed. A gap nonetheless remains regarding the precise effect of remote work on these cafés' foot traffic since 2020, which none of the four sources consulted, all predating this period, documents satisfactorily.

Part 3 (Ch. 18): It falls to the municipality to simplify the administrative steps for opening an independent café in the neighborhood. One will object, not without reason, that competition from large chains is a market matter, not municipal policy, and that the municipality cannot guarantee every business's survival. It remains the case that a municipal program supporting downtown businesses already existed ten years ago, before being quietly restricted without public explanation. What is at stake is not just the presence of empty or occupied storefronts, but a daily meeting place between neighbors that neither a chain nor an empty space can replace. Far from proposing to ban or limit large chains, this op-ed asks only that independent businesses also be given a real chance to open and last.

Part 4 (Ch. 19): What characterizes my style is an attention to a place's ordinary smells and sounds, which I place before its visual description. Across my texts — this café portrait and my portrait of the woodworking workshop written earlier this year — this same sensory entry recurs: the smell of ground coffee here, the smell of wood and glue there. Rereading the café portrait, I replaced the vague label "a popular café" with the precise identification of M. Ferrand, to ground the piece in a real person rather than a generality. I deliberately chose to keep the sentence about the smell of ground coffee before any visual description of the room, because a reader remembers a precise smell better than a detailed setting.

No unscheduled productive system appears. Other responses are acceptable when they meet all task requirements.
