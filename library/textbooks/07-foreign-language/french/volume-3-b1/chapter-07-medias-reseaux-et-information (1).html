Médias, réseaux et information

Media and Information · Speak and Write French: Independent Communication and Advanced Expression

Chapter Objectives

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

  1. Use vocabulary for comparing sources and platforms — une chaîne, un site d’information, les réseaux sociaux, fiable, vérifier, confirmer, contredire — to discuss how outlets cover the same story.
  2. Report what someone said or wrote using indirect speech (le discours indirect) with a present-tense reporting verb, making the required pronoun and possessive adjustments.
  3. Report what someone said using a past-tense reporting verb, shifting the embedded verb from présent to imparfait, from passé composé to plus-que-parfait, and from futur simple to conditionnel, using only tenses already active from earlier chapters.
  4. Ask and report indirect questions using si, ce que, ce qui, and retained question words, distinguishing them from direct questions.
  5. Continue to recognize (without producing new instances of) the passive voice, and continue to use the two fixed il faut que phrases from Chapter 6 exactly as given, with no new subjonctif form.
  6. Recycle selon, d’après, and the Chapter 6 formulaic reporting expressions while comparing two accounts of the same event.
  7. Read and listen to short passages that present two versions of one story, and identify where they agree, disagree, or add different details.
  8. Write a short comparison of six to nine sentences that reports what two different sources said, using indirect speech and at least one indirect question.

Communication Scenario

The second article that Karim predicted in Chapter 6 — « Il paraît qu’un autre article va paraître le mois prochain » — has now appeared, this time on a regional website. But the online version tells the story a little differently from the original newsletter piece, and a colleague has also posted about the conference on social media. Sophie and Karim need to compare what each source actually said before they mention any of it to Monsieur Lefèvre, who has already asked Karim directly what the new article says. Chapter 6 asked you to cite a source and recognize unattributed reports; this chapter asks you to report, in your own words, what a specific source said or asked — and to compare two sources that do not fully agree.

  1. Compare how two sources describe the same event, using media and platform vocabulary.
  2. Report what a source said, in your own words, using indirect speech with correct pronoun, possessive, and tense shifts.
  3. Report a question someone asked, using an indirect question rather than repeating the original question mark.
  4. Keep the passive receptive and the subjonctif limited to the two fixed Chapter 6 phrases, exactly as before.

Pronunciation Focus

Pronunciation Focus — Statement intonation in indirect questions

A yes-or-no question in French — like the example practiced below — usually rises in pitch toward the end, whether formed with intonation alone, est-ce que, or inversion. (Not every type of direct question behaves this way; French interrogative intonation varies with question type, a detail this chapter does not require you to manage.) Once a yes-or-no question is reported indirectly, it becomes part of a statement and takes falling, statement-style intonation instead — even though the sentence still concerns a question.

Practice the shift from question intonation to statement intonation:

  • Est-ce que l’article dit la vérité ? (direct question, rising pitch on the final syllable)
  • Karim demande si l’article dit la vérité. (indirect question, falling pitch, read as a statement)

Liaison, enchaînement, and rhythm-group pacing continue exactly as in earlier chapters. Note one connected-speech detail relevant to this chapter’s reporting verbs: il a dit and elle a demandé link smoothly (il‿a dit) exactly like any other subject-pronoun-plus-auxiliary sequence you already control. No new sound-spelling feature is introduced.

Grammar: Médias, réseaux et information

Purpose: introduce productive indirect speech (le discours indirect) using reporting verbs in the présent and in the passé composé/imparfait, and productive indirect questions with si, ce que, and ce qui. Every tense used in the reported clause — présent, passé composé, imparfait, plus-que-parfait, futur simple, and conditionnel — is already active from Volume II and earlier Volume III chapters; this chapter teaches how to shift between them when reporting, not the tenses themselves. The passive voice remains receptive only, exactly as in Chapter 6. The subjonctif is not explained here and is not extended beyond the two fixed il faut que phrases from Chapter 6.

7.1 Reporting what someone said: le discours indirect

Building Block — From direct to indirect speech
[Sujet] + [dire/expliquer/confirmer/ajouter] + que + [proposition, avec ajustements]
When the reporting verb is in the présent (il dit que, elle explique que), the reported verb keeps the tense it had in the original statement, exactly as in English. Pronouns and possessives change to match the new speaker’s point of view, and que introduces the reported clause. When the reporting verb is in a past tense (il a dit que, elle a expliqué que) and the reported information is anchored to that earlier past situation, three shifts recur most often: a présent in the original statement becomes an imparfait in the reported version, a passé composé becomes a plus-que-parfait, and a futur simple becomes a conditionnel. The imparfait, the plus-que-parfait, and the conditionnel are all systems you have already built in Volume III and Volume II; this chapter asks you to choose among them for a new communicative reason — reporting — not to learn their forms for the first time.
Karim dit : « Je n’ai pas encore lu le site. » → Karim dit qu’il n’a pas encore lu le site. Une collègue a écrit : « Je suis très fière du projet. » → Elle a dit qu’elle était très fière du projet. L’article a annoncé : « Une deuxième édition aura lieu l’année prochaine. » → L’article a annoncé qu’une deuxième édition aurait lieu l’année suivante.
Karim says: "I haven't read the site yet." → Karim says (that) he hasn't read the site yet. A colleague wrote: "I'm very proud of the project." → She said she was very proud of the project. The article announced: "A second edition will take place next year." → The article announced that a second edition would take place the following year.

This three-part shift (présent→imparfait, passé composé→plus-que-parfait, futur simple→conditionnel) is the controlled pattern taught in this chapter for reporting something anchored to an earlier past situation; it is not an exceptionless mechanical rule that applies to every past-tense reporting verb regardless of context — a detail you are not asked to manage yet, since every example and task in this chapter uses the anchored-past pattern. Time expressions may shift the same way: l’année prochaine becomes l’année suivante when the speaker’s point of reference has moved into the past along with the reported statement. This chapter only asks you to recognize and apply this shift with the specific expressions modeled here (l’année prochaine → l’année suivante, hier → la veille); a fuller inventory of shifted time expressions is developed later in the volume.

7.2 Reporting a question: la question indirecte

Building Block — From a direct question to an indirect question
[Sujet] + [demander] + si / ce que / ce qui / [mot interrogatif] + [proposition]
A yes-or-no question becomes indirect with si. A question with qu’est-ce que (asking about a direct object) becomes indirect with ce que; a question with qu’est-ce qui (asking about a subject) becomes indirect with ce qui. Question words that already stand alone — où, quand, pourquoi, comment — are retained as-is. In every case, the question mark disappears, inversion disappears, and the reported clause follows normal statement word order.
Monsieur Lefèvre demande : « Est-ce que l’article est positif ? » → Monsieur Lefèvre demande si l’article est positif. « Qu’est-ce que le site ajoute ? » → Il demande ce que le site ajoute. « Qu’est-ce qui a changé depuis le premier article ? » → Il demande ce qui a changé depuis le premier article. « Pourquoi les deux versions sont-elles différentes ? » → Il demande pourquoi les deux versions sont différentes.
Monsieur Lefèvre asks: "Is the article positive?" → Monsieur Lefèvre asks whether the article is positive. "What does the site add?" → He asks what the site adds. "What has changed since the first article?" → He asks what has changed since the first article. "Why are the two versions different?" → He asks why the two versions are different.

Qu’est-ce que and qu’est-ce qui belong to direct questions only; once a question becomes indirect, they are replaced by ce que (object) and ce qui (subject). A direct question that already uses qu’est-ce qui — such as Qu’est-ce qui se dit sur les réseaux sociaux ? in the Model Dialogue — remains a direct question until it is reported; only its indirect counterpart (il demande ce qui se dit…) would count as an indirect question.

Reminder — Still receptive and still formulaic

Two Chapter 6 boundaries stay exactly in place. First, the passive voice remains receptive: continue to recognize it in headlines and reports, but do not build new passive sentences of your own, even inside a reported clause — prefer le site dit que les organisateurs ont financé le projet (active) over an attempted new passive. Second, the subjonctif is not explained in this chapter either. You may continue to use il faut que ce soit clair and il faut qu’on en parle exactly as modeled in Chapter 6, but do not extend the pattern to new phrases, and do not attempt il faut que with a verb other than those two fixed forms.

Model Dialogue: Deux versions de la même histoire

Context: Karim has just read the new website article and compares it with the original newsletter piece from Chapter 6. Register: informal, close friends; tu throughout.

Sophie : Alors, qu’est-ce que dit le nouveau site ? So, what does the new site say?
Karim : Il confirme ce que disait le bulletin : il dit que notre conférence a attiré un nombre record de participants. It confirms what the bulletin said: it says our conference attracted a record number of participants.
Sophie : Bien. Mais est-ce qu’il ajoute quelque chose de nouveau ? Good. But does it add anything new?
Karim : Oui, justement : il ajoute que l’organisatrice a annoncé qu’une deuxième édition aurait lieu l’année suivante. Ça, le bulletin ne le disait pas. Yes, actually: it adds that the organizer announced a second edition would take place the following year. The bulletin didn't say that.
Sophie : Intéressant. Et sur les réseaux sociaux, qu’est-ce qui se dit ? Interesting. And on social media, what's being said?
Karim : Une collègue a publié un message ce matin. Elle a dit qu’elle était très fière du projet, mais elle n’a rien confirmé sur la deuxième édition. A colleague posted a message this morning. She said she was very proud of the project, but she didn't confirm anything about the second edition.
Sophie : Monsieur Lefèvre va nous demander si le site est fiable. Il faut qu’on en parle avant de lui répondre. Monsieur Lefèvre is going to ask us whether the site is reliable. We need to talk about it before we answer him.
Karim : D’accord. On lui dira que les deux sources confirment l’essentiel, même si elles n’ajoutent pas exactement les mêmes détails. Okay. We'll tell him that the two sources confirm the main point, even if they don't add exactly the same details.

This dialogue models indirect speech with both a présent reporting verb (il dit que, il ajoute que) and a passé composé reporting verb with tense shift (elle a dit qu’elle était…, a annoncé qu’une deuxième édition aurait lieu…); one indirect question with si (va nous demander si le site est fiable); the recycled fixed phrase il faut qu’on en parle from Chapter 6; and the new comparison vocabulary confirmer, ajouter, and fiable. Note that Sophie’s qu’est-ce qui se dit ? is a direct question, not an indirect one — compare it with the true indirect-question pattern practiced in section 7.2.

Culture and Communication

Culture in Context — Comparing sources across platforms

National dailies, regional websites, community bulletins, and individual social media posts do not carry the same editorial standards, and readers in every French-speaking community learn to weigh them differently. A social media post from a single person, however sincere, is not held to the same verification standard as an article from an established outlet, even when both describe the same event. Learning to say, in French, that two sources confirment, se contredisent, or that one ajoute a detail the other omits is a practical skill for anyone navigating French-language information, in France or elsewhere in the Francophone world.

Regional Variation — Talking about social media

Les réseaux sociaux is understood everywhere in the Francophone world as the general term for social media. Some Francophone regions and institutions prefer French-language names for specific platforms where an official terminology exists, while everyday speech in France, Belgium, Switzerland, and Québec often keeps the platform’s original name unchanged; this chapter uses only the general term, which remains understood everywhere regardless of local platform-naming habits.

Common Mistakes

Common Mistake — Keeping the question mark and inversion in an indirect question

Error: Writing *Il demande est-ce que le site est fiable ?, carrying the direct-question structure into a reported clause.

Correction: Once a question becomes indirect, the question mark, inversion, and est-ce que all disappear: Il demande si le site est fiable. The reported clause follows ordinary statement word order.

Common Mistake — Forgetting the tense shift after a past-tense reporting verb

Error: Writing *Elle a dit qu’elle a fini l’article when reporting, after the fact, something originally said in the passé composé.

Correction: With a past-tense reporting verb, a passé composé in the original statement becomes a plus-que-parfait in the report: Elle a dit qu’elle avait fini l’article. Compare this with a présent-tense reporting verb, which requires no shift: Elle dit qu’elle a fini l’article.

Common Mistake — Extending il faut que or the passive beyond this chapter’s scope

Out of scope for productive use: Il faut que le site soit plus clair. This is grammatical French, but it extends il faut que productively beyond one of the two authorized fixed phrases from Chapter 6 (il faut que ce soit clair) into a new productive subjonctif construction, and Chapter 7 does not explain the subjonctif or authorize that extension. Similarly, constructing a new passive sentence such as L’information a été confirmée par le site on your own is outside this chapter’s productive scope, even though it is grammatical: Chapter 7 keeps passive production inactive.

Correction: Use the already-active constructions instead: le site doit être plus clair; le site confirme l’information.

Modèle — Comparer deux sources

Deux sources ont parlé du nouveau café associatif cette semaine. Selon l’article que j’ai lu, le projet a mis presque un an à se concrétiser. Le journaliste confirme que plusieurs bénévoles ont rénové le local avant l’ouverture. Sur les réseaux sociaux, une habitante du quartier a écrit qu’elle était très contente des nouveaux ateliers gratuits. Elle a ajouté qu’un deuxième café ouvrirait l’année suivante, une information que l’article ne mentionnait pas. Un voisin lui a demandé si elle savait où se trouverait le nouveau café, et elle a répondu qu’elle ne le savait pas encore. Les deux sources se complètent bien, même si elles n’ajoutent pas exactement les mêmes détails.

Two sources reported on the new community café this week. According to the article I read, the project took almost a year to come together. The journalist confirms that several volunteers renovated the space before the opening. On social media, a neighborhood resident wrote that she was very happy with the new free workshops. She added that a second café would open the following year, information the article did not mention. A neighbor asked her whether she knew where the new café would be located, and she answered that she didn't know yet. The two sources complement each other well, even if they don't add exactly the same details.

This seven-sentence model includes one recycled selon citation from Chapter 6, one comparison verb (confirmer), two instances of indirect speech with tense shift, one indirect question, and a closing comparison sentence using ajouter. No newly constructed passive sentence and no unscheduled subjonctif form appear anywhere. Other responses are acceptable when they meet all task requirements and remain within the grammar and vocabulary activated through Chapter 7.

Practical Performance Task

Successful completion requires both components: a spoken comparison meeting every listed element, and a written comparison of six to nine sentences meeting every required element listed in the rubric above. Partial completion of either component does not meet the task's requirements.

Looking Ahead

Ce que vous savez faire maintenant — What you can do now

You can compare how two sources describe the same event, report what someone said with correct pronoun and tense adjustments, and report a question someone asked rather than repeating it directly.
