Évaluer et recommander

Evaluation and Recommendation · Speak and Write French: Independent Communication and Advanced Expression

Chapter Objectives

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

  1. Discuss criteria and options using vocabulary already active from the personal and practical/work domains, expanded with evaluation-specific items: un critère, un avantage, un inconvénient, peser le pour et le contre, envisager, and related terms.
  2. Introduce the relative pronoun dont productively, using it to link two ideas around a noun governed by de, joining the already-active qui, que, and où from earlier volumes. Lequel and its family remain receptive only.
  3. Recycle comparative and superlative structures from earlier volumes to rank options against stated criteria.
  4. Make a nuanced recommendation using the fixed formula je recommanderais de plus the infinitive, and the conditionnel of the already-authorized trigger il vaudrait mieux que plus the subjonctif, as a softer alternative to the present-tense il vaut mieux que.
  5. Continue productive use of all eleven Chapter 8–11 subjonctif triggers, the purpose-clause contrast, article omission with professions, the impersonal structures from Chapter 13, and the sequencing connectors, applied to evaluation and recommendation.
  6. Recycle the already-consolidated double-object pronoun order to evaluation and recommendation contexts, while keeping the further stylistic extension of denser clitic stacking at the Introduced stage, building on the two Chapter 12 modeled sentences.
  7. Read and listen to short evaluations and identify the criteria, the comparison, and the recommendation.
  8. Write a short evaluation of six to nine sentences that states criteria, compares at least two options, and closes with a clear, appropriately softened recommendation.

Communication Scenario

Following her presentation, Sophie has been asked to help choose an outside trainer to lead a short workshop for her team, building on what she learned in her own training program. Monsieur Lefèvre has narrowed the choice to two candidates and asks Sophie and Karim to compare them and make a recommendation. Chapter 13 asked Sophie to present a plan; Chapter 14 asks her to weigh two options against clear criteria and recommend one — carefully, since a direct recommendation to a supervisor calls for softer, more nuanced language than a flat statement of fact.

  1. State the criteria that matter for the decision.
  2. Compare the two candidates using comparative and superlative language.
  3. Link an idea back to a noun governed by de using dont.
  4. Recommend one option, softened appropriately for a workplace audience.

Pronunciation Focus

Pronunciation Focus — The three pronunciations of plus

The word plus is pronounced differently depending on its function and its neighbor — a pattern that becomes especially noticeable in this chapter's comparative and superlative language. Before a consonant in a comparative, the final s is silent: plus cher [ply]. Before a vowel, liaison activates a [z] sound: plus avantageux [plyz]. When plus means "more" (davantage) and is followed by a pause or receives contrastive emphasis, the final s is pronounced [s]: j’en veux plus [plys]. This is distinct from negative ne…plus ("no more, not anymore"), which follows the negation pronunciation pattern rather than this comparative one and should not be generalized from this rule.

  • Cette formatrice est plus convaincante. (silent s, before a consonant)
  • Cette option est plus avantageuse. (liaison, [z], before a vowel)

Dont does not elide before a vowel; there is no apostrophe form. In dont elle parle, liaison after dont is optional: careful or formal speech may pronounce the final t as a liaison consonant, while pronunciation without liaison is equally standard, especially in ordinary conversation. Liaison and rhythm-group pacing otherwise continue exactly as in Chapters 11–13.

14.1 The relative pronoun dont

Building Block — Joining two ideas around de
[noun] + dont + [rest of clause, replacing de + noun]
Dont replaces de plus a noun phrase — the object of a verb, adjective, or noun construction built with de — joining it to the noun it refers back to. It joins the family of relative pronouns already active since Volume II (qui for subjects, que for direct objects, où for place and time); dont is used specifically where the verb, adjective, or noun construction requires de. Word order after dont follows normal declarative order; unlike English "whose," it does not reverse subject and object.
C’est la formatrice dont Karim m’a parlé. (parler de quelqu’un) Voici les deux critères dont nous avons besoin. (avoir besoin de quelque chose)
This is the trainer Karim told me about. Here are the two criteria we need.
Grammar Focus — Using dont in evaluation

Dont is especially useful in evaluation, since many evaluative expressions are built with de: avoir besoin de, parler de, être satisfait de, se souvenir de, among others.

  • La candidate dont le dossier est le plus convaincant a déjà formé deux équipes similaires. The candidate whose file is the most convincing has already trained two similar teams. dont + noun phrase (le dossier), replacing "de cette candidate"
  • Voici l’avantage dont je suis le plus sûre. Here is the advantage I am most sure of. dont (être sûr de) + normal declarative order

Lequel, laquelle, and their plural forms — used after most other prepositions (avec lequel, pour laquelle) — remain receptive only in this volume. You may recognize them in a reading or listening text, but this chapter does not teach or assess their production.

14.2 Recycled comparison, and two nuanced ways to recommend

Grammar Focus — Ranking options, then softening the recommendation

The comparative and superlative patterns from Chapter 4 (plus…que, moins…que, aussi…que, le/la/les plus…, le/la/les moins…) are not new; this chapter simply applies them to criteria and options. What is new is how to move from a ranking to a recommendation without sounding blunt in a professional context.

  • Cette formatrice est plus expérimentée, mais l’autre a un meilleur rapport qualité-prix. This trainer is more experienced, but the other has better value for money. recycled comparative
  • Je recommanderais de retenir la première candidate. I would recommend selecting the first candidate. je recommanderais de + infinitif (fixed formula, conditionnel softens the verb; no subject change involved, so no que/subjonctif)
  • Il vaudrait mieux que nous choisissions la formatrice la plus expérimentée. It would be better if we chose the more experienced trainer. conditionnel of the already-authorized trigger il vaut mieux que + subjonctif (choisissions, transparent present-derived stem)

This chapter teaches and assesses je recommanderais de plus the infinitive, following the same shape as the formal request formulas from Chapter 12. Standard French also permits recommander que plus a subordinate clause with a named subject (for example, je recommanderais que nous retenions cette candidate), so de plus the infinitive is not an absolute rule of the verb itself; it is simply the only pattern introduced and assessed in this chapter, since the que version would expand the authorized subjonctif-trigger inventory beyond the eleven from Chapters 8–11. Il vaudrait mieux que is not a twelfth subjonctif trigger; it is the conditionnel of the eighth Chapter 8 trigger, il vaut mieux que, and it still requires the same transparent present-derived stems or the five memorized irregular paradigms. Putting the trigger itself in the conditionnel — rather than adding a new expression — is what softens the recommendation.

14.3 Recycled and consolidated: double-object pronouns in evaluation and recommendation

Recycled and Consolidated — The general pronoun-order rule

Double-object pronoun order is not new at this level: it has been consolidated (level C) since earlier in the volume, and Chapter 12 already applied it productively to correspondence, beyond the two formulaic confirmation sentences it modeled for stylistic substitution. This chapter simply recycles the already-consolidated general rule and applies it to evaluation and recommendation.

  • me / te / nous / vous  before  le / la / les  before  lui / leur First-and-second-person object pronouns come before third-person direct objects, which come before third-person indirect objects.
  • Karim me la recommande. Karim recommends it to me. (me before la)
  • Sophie nous les explique. Sophie explains them to us. (nous before les)

What remains bounded at this level is not ordinary double-object order itself, but the denser, more stylistically advanced clitic stacking sometimes seen in formal prose. That further stylistic extension stays at the Introduced stage this chapter, building on the two Chapter 12 modeled sentences; a fuller stylistic account is developed later in the volume.

Reminder — Eleven triggers, one new relative pronoun, still bounded

All eleven subjonctif triggers from Chapters 8–11 remain productive, with no twelfth trigger added; il vaudrait mieux que is the conditionnel of an already-authorized trigger, not a new one. Dont joins qui, que, and où as a productive relative pronoun; lequel and its family remain receptive only. The purpose-clause contrast, article omission with professions, the impersonal structures from Chapter 13, and the sequencing connectors all recycle directly into this chapter's evaluation tasks. The passive voice remains receptive only. Ordinary double-object pronoun order is already consolidated and recycles freely here; only the denser, more advanced stylistic clitic stacking of formal prose remains at the Introduced stage, building on the two patterns modeled in Chapter 12.

Model Dialogue: Le choix d’un prestataire

Context: Sophie and Karim compare two candidate trainers before reporting their recommendation to Monsieur Lefèvre. Register: semi-formal throughout, since the conversation will be summarized for a supervisor.

Karim : Il s’agit de choisir entre deux candidates. Quels sont les critères les plus importants pour toi ? It's about choosing between two candidates. What criteria matter most to you?
Sophie : L’expérience, d’abord, et le rapport qualité-prix. La formatrice dont tu m’as parlé la semaine dernière a un excellent dossier. Experience, first, and value for money. The trainer you told me about last week has an excellent file.
Karim : Oui, elle est plus expérimentée que l’autre candidate, mais son tarif est aussi plus élevé. L’autre a un point fort : elle est plus disponible. Yes, she's more experienced than the other candidate, but her rate is also higher. The other one has a strong point: she's more available.
Sophie : C’est vrai. Mais l’avantage dont je suis le plus sûre, c’est la qualité de ses formations passées. Karim, tu me la recommandes vraiment ? That's true. But the advantage I'm most sure of is the quality of her past training programs. Karim, do you really recommend her to me?
Karim : Oui, je te la recommande sans hésiter. Il vaudrait mieux que nous choisissions la plus expérimentée, même si elle coûte un peu plus cher. Yes, I recommend her to you without hesitation. It would be better if we chose the more experienced one, even if she costs a bit more.
Sophie : D’accord. Je vais peser le pour et le contre une dernière fois, mais je pense que nous avons trouvé la candidate la plus convaincante. Okay. I'll weigh the pros and cons one last time, but I think we've found the most convincing candidate.
Karim : Pour conclure, je recommanderais de retenir cette candidate dès cette semaine, pour que la formation puisse commencer à temps. To conclude, I would recommend selecting this candidate this week, so that the training can begin on time.

This dialogue models dont twice (la formatrice dont tu m’as parlé; l’avantage dont je suis le plus sûre), recycled comparatives and superlatives, two recycled, already-consolidated double-object patterns (tu me la recommandes, je te la recommande), both nuanced recommendation formulas, a recycled sequencing connector (pour conclure), and a recycled purpose clause (pour que). No unauthorized subjonctif trigger and no new passive sentence appear.

Culture and Communication

Culture in Context — Softening a recommendation to a supervisor

In many French professional settings, a direct recommendation to someone in a position of authority is often phrased more tentatively than a simple statement of preference, particularly in a first or formal exchange. The conditionnel — as in je recommanderais or il vaudrait mieux — softens the force of the statement without weakening the underlying recommendation; it signals professional care rather than uncertainty.

Regional Variation — Directness in evaluation

Expectations about how directly to state a recommendation vary across the Francophone world and across individual workplaces; some contexts favor a brisker, more direct style even toward a supervisor. This chapter's softened pattern remains a safe, broadly transferable default when the expected style is uncertain.

Common Mistakes

Common Mistake — Using que instead of dont

Error: Writing *C’est la formatrice que Karim m’a parlé, treating parler de like a direct-object verb.

Correction: Since parler takes de, use dont: C’est la formatrice dont Karim m’a parlé. Check whether the verb, adjective, or noun construction requires de before choosing between que and dont.

Common Mistake — Using que with recommander at this level

Out of scope for this chapter: Writing *Je recommanderais que nous retenions cette candidate in chapter tasks.

Correction: This chapter teaches and assesses the infinitive pattern: je recommanderais de retenir cette candidate. Standard French does allow recommander que plus the subjonctif with a named subject, so the que sentence above is not ungrammatical in general French; it is simply outside this chapter's scope, since it would expand the authorized subjonctif-trigger inventory beyond the eleven from Chapters 8–11. Use de plus the infinitive for this chapter's tasks.

Common Mistake — Overextending the double-object rule

Watch for: Assuming that because the general rule (section 14.3) is now explained, it can be applied freely to compound tenses, infinitive constructions, imperatives, or negated sentences.

Correction: This chapter authorizes production only in simple declarative sentences with a single conjugated verb, following me/te/nous/vous before le/la/les before lui/leur. More complex clitic environments are developed later in the volume.
