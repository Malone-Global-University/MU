Comprendre l’actualité

Understanding Current Events · Speak and Write French: Independent Communication and Advanced Expression

Chapter Objectives

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

  1. Use core news vocabulary to talk about an article, a report, or a news item: un article, un titre, une source, un reportage, une enquête, and related terms.
  2. Cite sources appropriately using selon and d’après, both of which function as interchangeable defaults in this chapter's sourcing pattern.
  3. Recognize the passive voice in headlines and short news reports, and understand what it communicates, without being required to produce new passive sentences.
  4. Recognize a small set of fixed, formulaic expressions used to report information without a named source — il paraît que, on dit que, on raconte que — as whole phrases, not as patterns to analyze or extend.
  5. Recognize, as a small set of fixed expressions only, a first formulaic exposure to il faut que with a small number of common set phrases, without any explanation of subjonctif formation.
  6. Maintain consistent tu/vous register while discussing a news item informally with a friend and more carefully with a colleague or manager.
  7. Read and listen to short news-style texts and identify the main event, its source, and how confidently the information is presented.
  8. Write a short news-style summary of six to nine sentences that reports an event, cites a source with selon or d’après, and uses at least one recognized formulaic reporting expression appropriately.

Communication Scenario

A few weeks after the Bilan meeting with Monsieur Lefèvre, Sophie sends Karim a link: a local business newsletter has published a short article mentioning their conference by name. Karim reads it, then talks to Sophie about what it says — and later has to mention it briefly to Monsieur Lefèvre as well. Unit I asked you to narrate, give an opinion, and compare; Unit II opens by asking you to talk about something you read or heard, rather than something you did yourself — naming where the information came from, and noticing, without yet producing, some of the ways French marks that a piece of information is not your own firsthand account.

  1. Talk about a short news item using the vocabulary of articles, sources, and reports.
  2. Cite where a piece of information comes from, using selon or d’après.
  3. Recognize passive headlines and understand what they report, without needing to produce new passive sentences of your own.
  4. Recognize il paraît que and similar fixed phrases as ways of reporting something without a named source.

Pronunciation Focus

Pronunciation Focus — Reading a headline aloud

News headlines in French are often clipped — articles and some function words are dropped for economy — but they are still read aloud with the full rhythm-group phrasing you already control. This chapter's pronunciation focus is register-sensitive delivery: the same information sounds different read as a casual mention to a friend versus read aloud carefully, as if presenting it to a colleague.

Practice both deliveries of the same headline:

  • Conférence régionale : un format salué par les participants. (read carefully, evenly paced, as in a formal presentation)
  • T’as vu ? Notre conférence, elle est mentionnée dans le journal ! (read casually, faster, with informal contraction in speech)

Liaison remains fully active in this chapter, but note one exception worth flagging explicitly: selon does not take liaison with a following vowel — selon elle is pronounced with no linking sound at all, unlike most short function words. Liaison and enchaînement continue to apply normally elsewhere, for example in nous‿avons and les‿organisateurs. Nasal vowels and rhythm-group pacing continue exactly as in earlier chapters. No new sound-spelling feature is introduced.

Grammar: Comprendre l’actualité

Purpose: activate sourcing language with selon/d’après, develop receptive recognition of the passive voice in news contexts, and introduce, purely as recognized formulas, reported-information expressions and a first, unanalyzed exposure to il faut que. No subjonctif explanation is given in this chapter. The passive voice remains receptive; reported speech with tense shift remains formulaic and receptive, exactly as in Chapters 1–5.

6.1 Citing a source: selon and d’après

Building Block — Naming where information comes from
[Selon / D’après] + [source], + [information, indicative]
In the source-citation pattern taught here, selon and d’après are followed directly by the source — a noun, a name, or a stressed pronoun — not by que: selon l’article, not *selon que l’article dit. (A separate conjunction, selon que, exists in French with a different meaning, roughly “depending on whether”; it is outside this chapter's scope and is not part of the sourcing pattern taught here.) The verb that follows the source phrase continues to use the indicative tenses you already control. Selon and d’après are interchangeable defaults for this chapter's sourcing pattern; either is acceptable unless a task specifies one.
Selon l’article, notre conférence a été l’un des événements les plus réussis de l’année. D’après la journaliste, le nouveau format attire de plus en plus de participants.
According to the article, our conference was one of the most successful events of the year. According to the journalist, the new format is attracting more and more participants.

6.2 Recognizing the passive voice in headlines and reports

Receptive Focus — The passive voice: recognize, do not yet produce

News writing often uses the passive voice to foreground the event rather than the person doing the action — useful when the actor is unknown, unimportant, or already obvious. You are asked in this chapter to recognize the passive when you read or hear it and understand what it communicates. You are not asked to construct new passive sentences of your own; that skill develops later in the volume.

Recognition pattern: a form of être + a past participle that agrees with the subject, often with par introducing the agent when one is stated. Watch out for a false friend: a form of être followed by a noun phrase, rather than a past participle, is not passive — it is simply être used as a copula, exactly as in je suis étudiant.

  • Notre conférence, elle est mentionnée dans le journal local. Our conference is mentioned in the local paper. passive: est + mentionnée (agreement with the feminine subject); no agent stated
  • Le nouveau format a été salué par plusieurs participants. The new format was praised by several participants. passive: a été + salué; agent introduced by par
  • Notre conférence a été l’un des événements les plus réussis de l’année. Our conference was one of the most successful events of the year. NOT passive: a été here is simply passé composé of être as a copula before a noun phrase, exactly like j'ai été content

When you speak or write this chapter, prefer the active sentences you already control (l’article mentionne notre conférence) rather than attempting a new passive construction. Treat the first two examples above, and others like them that you encounter in this chapter's texts, as receptive input to understand, not as a pattern to generate freely yet.

6.3 Reporting information without a named source: formulaic expressions

Receptive/Formulaic Focus — Il paraît que, on dit que, on raconte que

French has several fixed, whole-phrase ways to report something without naming a precise source — roughly equivalent to English “apparently…” or “people say that…” This chapter introduces three of them as formulas to recognize and, when useful, reuse exactly as given — not as patterns to analyze grammatically or extend to new forms.

  • Il paraît que notre conférence va être mentionnée dans un autre article le mois prochain. Apparently, our conference is going to be mentioned in another article next month.
  • On dit que ce genre de format devient de plus en plus populaire. People say that this kind of format is becoming more and more popular.

Use these three formulas as fixed openers only. Do not attempt to build new sentences on an unfamiliar grammatical pattern extracted from them; treat each as a memorized whole, exactly as you treated the Chapter 1 formulaic expressions.

6.4 First glimpse: il faut que (formulaic only, not explained)

Formulaic First Exposure — A small, closed set of fixed phrases

You may encounter il faut que followed by a verb form that looks different from anything you have studied so far. This chapter does not explain that form. Instead, recognize the following two fixed phrases as whole memorized units, useful for talking about what a situation requires:

  • Il faut que ce soit clair : je n’ai jamais dit ça à la journaliste. Let's be clear: I never said that to the journalist. (fixed phrase, "il faut que ce soit clair")
  • Il faut qu’on en parle à l’équipe. We need to talk to the team about it. (fixed phrase, "il faut qu'on en parle")

Treat both phrases exactly as you would any other fixed expression: usable as-is, not a springboard for new sentences. A full account of this verb form, called the subjonctif, begins with controlled introduction in Chapter 8 and moves toward developing production in Chapter 9. Nothing about its formation is taught here, and no other subjonctif form — including after expressions of emotion or evaluation such as c'est bien que or ça fait plaisir que — is introduced or modeled anywhere in this chapter.

The passive voice remains receptive only in this chapter; prefer the active constructions you already control in your own speech and writing. The reported-information formulas in section 6.3 and the il faut que phrases in section 6.4 are closed, memorized sets — do not generate new sentences on their underlying patterns. The subjonctif is not explained anywhere in this chapter.

Model Dialogue: Vous avez vu cet article ?

Context: Sophie has just sent Karim the link to the newsletter article. Register: informal, close friends; tu throughout.

Sophie : T’as vu ? Notre conférence, elle est mentionnée dans le journal local ! Did you see? Our conference is mentioned in the local paper!
Karim : Sérieux ? Attends, je lis… Ah oui ! Selon l’article, notre conférence a été l’un des événements les plus réussis de l’année. Seriously? Wait, let me read… Oh yes! According to the article, our conference was one of the most successful events of the year.
Sophie : D’après la journaliste, le nouveau format attire de plus en plus de participants chaque année. According to the journalist, the new format is attracting more and more participants each year.
Karim : Il paraît qu’un autre article va paraître le mois prochain, avec plus de détails. Apparently, another article is going to come out next month, with more details.
Sophie : On dit que ce genre de format devient vraiment populaire en ce moment. People say this kind of format is really becoming popular right now.
Karim : Il faut qu’on en parle à l’équipe demain, franchement. Ça pourrait être une bonne nouvelle pour le projet. We really need to talk to the team about it tomorrow. This could be good news for the project.
Sophie : Bonne idée. Et il faut que ce soit clair avec Monsieur Lefèvre : on n’a rien demandé à la journaliste, elle nous a trouvés toute seule ! Good idea. And we need to be clear with Monsieur Lefèvre: we didn't ask the journalist for anything, she found us on her own!

This dialogue models selon and d’après; the receptive passive construction elle est mentionnée dans le journal local (contrast this with notre conférence a été l’un des événements…, which is NOT passive — it is être used as a copula before a noun phrase, exactly as covered in section 6.2); the formulaic reporting expressions il paraît que and on dit que; and both fixed il faut que phrases from section 6.4, used exactly as given.

Culture and Communication

Culture in Context — Reading news critically

Noticing how information is sourced — selon l’article versus il paraît que — is a basic media-literacy skill in any language. French-language news outlets, like those in any linguistic community, vary widely in editorial standards, ownership, and regional focus, from national dailies to local bulletins to community newsletters. Learning to distinguish a named, checkable source from an unattributed formula (“apparently…”) is as much a communicative skill as a grammatical one, and this distinction matters equally across every French-speaking region.

Regional Variation — News vocabulary

Un article, un titre, une source, selon, and d’après are used and understood throughout the Francophone world in professional and everyday news contexts. Some outlets favor un·e journaliste while others use profession-specific titles (un·e reporter, un·e chroniqueur·euse); the general vocabulary in this chapter remains the safer, broadly recognized default.

Common Mistakes

Common Mistake — Following selon or d’après with que to cite a source

Error: Writing *selon que l’article dit, adding que as if selon worked like an opinion opener.

Correction: For source citation, selon and d’après are followed directly by a noun, name, or stressed pronoun: selon l’article, d’après elle. If you want to report the content of what the article says, a separate clause follows: selon l’article, la conférence a été un succès. (Note that selon que does exist as a conjunction meaning “depending on whether,” but it has a different function from the source-citation pattern taught here and is not covered in this chapter.)

Common Mistake — Trying to construct a new passive sentence

Error: Attempting to build an original passive sentence not modeled in this chapter, such as guessing at participle agreement or auxiliary choice without controlled instruction.

Correction: This chapter asks you to recognize the passive, not to produce new instances of it. Rephrase using the active voice you already control: instead of guessing at a new passive, say l’article mentionne notre conférence rather than attempting an unfamiliar passive construction.

Common Mistake — Extending il faut que beyond the two fixed phrases

Out of scope for this chapter: Il faut qu’on le fasse demain. This is grammatical French — fasse is the correct present subjonctif of faire after il faut que — but it uses a new subjonctif form that has not been introduced in this chapter. In Chapter 6, use only the two fixed expressions from section 6.4 (il faut que ce soit clair, il faut qu’on en parle).

Correction: For this meaning, use the already-active construction: il faut le faire demain.

Writing Workshop — Model text

Modèle — Résumé d’actualité

La semaine dernière, un nouveau café associatif a ouvert dans notre quartier. Selon les organisateurs, le projet a mis presque un an à se concrétiser. D’abord, plusieurs bénévoles ont rénové le local, et ensuite, ils ont trouvé les premiers partenaires. D’après l’article que j’ai lu, l’endroit propose déjà des ateliers gratuits pour les habitants du quartier. Il paraît qu’un deuxième café du même genre va ouvrir l’année prochaine dans un autre quartier. Je pense que c’est une très bonne nouvelle pour la vie locale.

Last week, a new community café opened in our neighborhood. According to the organizers, the project took almost a year to come together. First, several volunteers renovated the space, and then they found the first partners. According to the article I read, the place already offers free workshops for neighborhood residents. Apparently, a second café of the same kind is going to open next year in another neighborhood. I think this is very good news for local life.
