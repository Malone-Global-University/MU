Vie professionnelle

Professional Life · Speak and Write French: Independent Communication and Advanced Expression

Chapter Objectives

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

  1. Talk about workplace situations using vocabulary already active from the personal and practical/work domains, expanded with correspondence-specific items: un supérieur / une supérieure hiérarchique, une demande, un congé, joindre, convenir (à), and related terms.
  2. Open and close a formal professional letter or email using standard French correspondence conventions.
  3. Make a formal workplace request using the fixed patterns je vous serais reconnaissant / reconnaissante de and je vous prie de, each followed by the infinitive.
  4. Apply the already-consolidated double-object pronoun order (je vous le confirme, je le lui transmettrai) to formal correspondence, and, in two closely modeled sentences only, extend to the more stylistically advanced clitic combinations of formal written register.
  5. Continue productive use of all eleven Chapter 8–11 subjonctif triggers, the purpose-clause contrast (pour + infinitif versus pour que + subjonctif), and article omission with professions, applied to workplace contexts.
  6. In this chapter's formal request to a supervisor, maintain vous consistently throughout the written communication, distinguishing this deliberately formal register choice from the mixed tu/vous conversation registers of earlier chapters.
  7. Read and listen to short workplace correspondence and identify the request, the justification, and the level of formality.
  8. Write a formal professional email of six to nine sentences that opens and closes correctly, makes a clear request, and justifies it with at least one recycled subjonctif trigger or purpose clause.

Pronunciation Focus

Pronunciation Focus — The rhythm of formal request formulas

Je vous serais reconnaissant / reconnaissante de is a long rhythm group, and French speakers do not pause in the middle of it. Practice it as a single breath group, with the main stress falling on the final syllable of reconnaissant or reconnaissante, exactly as you would stress the last syllable of any French rhythm group.

  • Je vous serais reconnaissant / reconnaissante de bien vouloir confirmer. (one rhythm group up through de, then a second group for the infinitive phrase)
  • Je vous prie de bien vouloir examiner ma demande. (shorter, brisker rhythm group; formal but less heavy than the first pattern)

The double-object combinations applied in this chapter (je vous le confirme, je le lui transmettrai) place both clitics tightly before the verb with no pause between them, exactly as with the single object pronouns you already control — only the number of pronouns changes, not the rhythm principle. Liaison and enchaînement continue exactly as in earlier chapters, for example vous‿informer. No new sound-spelling feature is introduced.

Grammar: Vie professionnelle

Purpose: teach the conventions of formal French correspondence, add two fixed formal-request patterns, apply the already-consolidated double-object pronoun order to correspondence with a formulaic, closely modeled stylistic extension, and maintain vous consistently in this chapter's formal written request. No new subjonctif trigger and no new irregular paradigm are introduced. The passive voice remains receptive only, exactly as in Chapters 6–11.

12.1 Opening and closing a formal email or letter

Building Block — The formal correspondence frame
Madame, Monsieur, … [corps de la lettre] … Je vous prie d’agréer, Madame, Monsieur, mes salutations distinguées.
Formal French correspondence opens with a title alone (no name is required if the recipient is not personally known) and closes with a fixed, somewhat elaborate formula rather than a short English-style sign-off. A simpler and very common alternative closing is Cordialement, appropriate for most everyday professional email exchanges once contact is established; the fuller je vous prie d’agréer… formula suits a first, more formal contact or a written letter. Both closings are authorized productively in this chapter; the choice depends on how formal the exchange is, not on personal preference.
Madame, je me permets de vous écrire au sujet de ma formation. … Dans l’attente de votre réponse, je vous prie d’agréer, Madame, l’expression de mes salutations distinguées.
Madam, I am writing to you regarding my training program. … Awaiting your reply, please accept, Madam, the expression of my highest regards.

In everyday professional email, Bonjour Madame, or simply Bonjour, plus Cordialement at the close is common and fully acceptable; the fuller formula above remains the safer default for a first formal contact, an official request, or a written letter.

12.2 Formal requests: je vous serais reconnaissant / reconnaissante de and je vous prie de

Grammar Focus — Making a polite formal request

Both patterns are followed by the infinitive, never by que plus a conjugated verb, because the subject of the request and the subject of the requested action are understood to be the same — the recipient. This is a fixed formula, not an instance of the pour/pour que contrast from Chapter 11.

  • Je vous serais reconnaissante de bien vouloir aménager mon horaire le jeudi soir. I would be grateful if you could adjust my schedule on Thursday evenings. je vous serais reconnaissant / reconnaissante de + infinitif (heavier, very formal)
  • Je vous prie de bien vouloir examiner ma demande dans les meilleurs délais. I kindly ask you to review my request as soon as possible. je vous prie de + infinitif (formal, slightly brisker)

Bien vouloir plus the infinitive is itself a formal politeness marker recycled here in fixed combination with both patterns above; it softens the request without changing its structure. Both formulas require vous; neither has a natural tu equivalent at this register.

12.3 Double-object pronouns in correspondence, and a formulaic stylistic extension

Recycled and Consolidated — Double-object order applied to formal writing

Double-object pronoun order is not new: it has been consolidated (level C) since earlier in the volume, and you already combine two clitics correctly before a conjugated verb. This chapter simply applies that already-active system to the short, fixed confirmations typical of formal correspondence, where a supervisor or colleague expects a quick, precise reply.

  • Je vous le confirme par ce message. I confirm it to you with this message. (indirect object vous + direct object le/la/les + verb, the order you already control)
  • Je le lui transmettrai dès que possible. I will pass it along to him/her as soon as possible. (direct object le/la/les + indirect object lui/leur + verb, the order you already control)

What is new at this level is not the pronoun order itself but its formulaic use as a compact confirmation move in formal writing. A further stylistic refinement — stacking clitics with even greater density and precision in advanced formal prose — remains at the formulaic stage in this chapter; the two sentences above are its closely modeled starting point, and a fuller stylistic account is developed later in the volume.

Reminder — Eleven triggers, vous throughout, still bounded

All eleven subjonctif triggers from Chapters 8–11 remain productive, with no twelfth trigger added in this chapter. The purpose-clause contrast (pour + infinitif versus pour que + subjonctif) and article omission before an unmodified profession after être/devenir both recycle directly into workplace contexts. In this chapter's scenario — Sophie's formal written request to her supervisor — maintain vous consistently throughout the formal communication. The passive voice remains receptive only. The advanced stylistic extension of double-object clitics in section 12.3 is limited to the two modeled sentences; ordinary double-object order itself is already fully consolidated.

Model Dialogue: Une lettre à rédiger

Context: Sophie and Karim draft Sophie's formal request email to her supervisor, Monsieur Lefèvre. Register: this segment shows their informal planning conversation (tu between Sophie and Karim), followed by the formal email text itself (vous throughout, addressed to Monsieur Lefèvre).

Karim : Commence par « Monsieur », sans le nom si tu veux rester très formelle, puis explique ta demande clairement. Start with "Monsieur," without the name if you want to stay very formal, then explain your request clearly.
Sophie : D’accord. Je vais utiliser « je vous serais reconnaissante de » pour l’horaire, et « je vous prie de » pour la question du financement. Okay. I'll use "je vous serais reconnaissante de" for the schedule, and "je vous prie de" for the funding question.
Karim : Bonne idée. Et termine avec « je vous prie d’agréer, Monsieur, mes salutations distinguées » — c’est la formule la plus sûre pour un premier contact formel. Good idea. And end with "je vous prie d'agréer, Monsieur, mes salutations distinguées" — it's the safest formula for a first formal contact.
Sophie rédige, puis lit son message à voix haute.
Sophie : « Monsieur, je me permets de vous écrire au sujet de ma formation en gestion de projet, qui commence dans trois semaines. Je vous serais reconnaissante de bien vouloir aménager mon horaire le jeudi soir pendant la durée de la formation. Il est important que je puisse suivre chaque séance sans manquer mes responsabilités habituelles. Par ailleurs, je vous prie de bien vouloir examiner la possibilité d’un financement partiel, pour que cette formation me coûte moins cher. Je joins à ce message le programme complet de la formation. Dans l’attente de votre réponse, je vous prie d’agréer, Monsieur, mes salutations distinguées. » "Sir, I am writing to you regarding my project-management training program, which begins in three weeks. I would be grateful if you could adjust my schedule on Thursday evenings for the duration of the program. It is important that I be able to attend every session without neglecting my usual responsibilities. In addition, I kindly ask you to consider the possibility of partial funding, so that this program costs me less. I am attaching the full program schedule to this message. Awaiting your reply, please accept, Sir, my highest regards."
Karim : Parfait. C’est clair, poli, et bien organisé. Une fois qu’il te répondra, je te le confirmerai tout de suite si ça te rassure. Perfect. It's clear, polite, and well organized. Once he replies, I'll confirm it to you right away if that reassures you.

The planning exchange between Sophie and Karim uses tu; Sophie's email itself uses vous throughout, without exception. The email models both formal request patterns, the formal opening and closing, one recycled subjonctif trigger (il est important que), one recycled purpose clause (pour que), and Karim's closing line models the double-object pattern (je te le confirmerai, a further modeled variant of section 12.3 with te in place of vous/lui).

Culture and Communication

Culture in Context — Formality in French professional writing

French professional correspondence tends to preserve more elaborate formal conventions than everyday English business email, particularly in first contact with a supervisor or an institution. The closing formula je vous prie d’agréer… mes salutations distinguées can feel stiff translated literally, but it functions simply as the expected, respectful way to end this kind of message — not as an unusually strong statement of feeling.

Regional Variation — Correspondence formality

The general shape of French formal correspondence — title-only opening, vous throughout, elaborate closing — is broadly recognized across the Francophone world, though everyday email culture in some workplaces and some countries has grown noticeably less formal, favoring Bonjour and Cordialement even in a first professional contact. This chapter's fuller formula remains the safer default when formality is uncertain.

Common Mistakes

Common Mistake — Using que after a formal request formula

Error: Writing *Je vous prie que vous examiniez ma demande, treating je vous prie de like a pour que-style trigger.

Correction: Both formal request formulas take the infinitive, not que plus the subjonctif: je vous prie de bien vouloir examiner ma demande. This is a fixed formula, distinct from the eleven authorized subjonctif triggers.

Common Mistake — Mixing tu into a formal email

Error: Writing most of a formal email with vous but slipping into a tu form partway through, especially when addressing someone you know well informally in speech.

Correction: For this chapter's scenario — a formal request to a supervisor, especially a first or official written contact — keep vous consistent from the opening to the closing formula. This is not a universal rule of all French workplace writing: some workplaces and some Francophone contexts use tu more freely, even in writing, once a working relationship is established. But mixing tu and vous within the same message, as opposed to choosing one deliberately, reads as an error rather than a register choice. Proofread specifically for stray tu forms before sending.

Common Mistake — Treating advanced clitic stacking as fully generalized

Watch for: Assuming that because ordinary double-object order (vous le, le lui) is already consolidated, every dense, multi-clitic formal-style combination is equally safe to improvise.

Correction: Ordinary double-object combinations like those in section 12.3 follow the pronoun order you already control confidently. For the more stylistically advanced, denser clitic stacking sometimes seen in formal prose, reuse the two modeled sentences (or Karim's te-variant) through substitution only; a fuller stylistic account is developed later in the volume.
