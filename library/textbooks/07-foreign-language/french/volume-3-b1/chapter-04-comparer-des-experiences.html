Comparer des expériences

Comparing Experiences · Speak and Write French: Independent Communication and Advanced Expression

Chapter Objectives

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

  1. Compare two people, choices, or experiences using plus… que, moins… que, and aussi… que with adjectives and adverbs, including irregular comparatives such as meilleur(e) and mieux.
  2. Form and use the superlative (le/la/les plus…, le/la/les moins…) to single out the best, worst, most, or least of a set.
  3. Avoid repeating a noun already established in the conversation by recycling direct object, indirect object, y, and en pronouns across a longer comparison.
  4. Build a two-part comparison — two experiences held side by side — that reaches an explicit conclusion, recycling the opinion and cause/consequence connectors from Chapter 3.
  5. Continue to apply consistent tu/vous register while comparing experiences with people at different levels of formality.
  6. Read and listen to a comparison of two experiences and identify the point of comparison, the conclusion, and any pronoun used to avoid repetition.
  7. Write a comparison of six to nine sentences that evaluates two experiences and reaches a clear conclusion.

Communication Scenario

Karim's team has now presented at two conferences in the same year — the one from Chapters 1–3, and an earlier one in the spring. Sophie wants to know which one went better, and why. Meanwhile, in the Reading Practice, Karim also compares this year's conference format to last year's for Madame Rousseau, in a more careful register. Chapter 3 asked you to defend a single opinion; this chapter asks you to hold two experiences up against each other, decide which one comes out ahead on a given point, and say so clearly — without repeating the name of everything you are comparing every time you mention it.

  1. Compare two experiences on at least one specific point, using a comparative or superlative structure.
  2. Avoid repeating a noun you have already named, using an object pronoun, y, or en instead.
  3. Reach an explicit conclusion about which experience was better, worse, or equivalent, and why.
  4. Adjust your comparison's tone and directness to the person you are speaking with.

Pronunciation Focus

Pronunciation Focus — Stress and contrast in comparison

This chapter develops contrastive stress: when you compare two things, the words carrying the comparison — plus, moins, meilleur, mieux — and the two things being compared usually receive extra emphasis, so the listener hears exactly what is being weighed against what.

Two features deserve attention this chapter:

  • The final s of plus is usually silent before a consonant (plus intéressant is pronounced with liaison, plu-z-intéressant, since intéressant begins with a vowel) but the word itself is pronounced [ply] before a consonant-initial adjective (plus long) and often [plys] when it stands alone at the end of a sentence to mean "more" (j'en veux plus).
  • In a two-part comparison with tandis que or alors que, place a clear pause before the connector, letting the first half of the comparison stand as its own rhythm group before the contrast begins.

Practice the following aloud, stressing the comparison words:

  • Cette conférence était plus intéressante que la dernière.
  • Notre présentation de printemps s'est moins bien passée, ¦ tandis que celle-ci était bien meilleure.

No new sound-spelling feature is introduced. Liaison, nasal vowels, and rhythm-group pacing remain fully active from earlier chapters and volumes.

Grammar: Comparer des expériences

Purpose: activate comparative and superlative structures with adjectives and adverbs, including irregular forms, and review recycled object, y, and en pronouns for avoiding repetition across a longer comparison. No subjonctif is introduced this chapter. The plus-que-parfait continues in guided, recycled production; the passive voice and reported speech remain formulaic and receptive, exactly as in Chapters 1–3.

4.1 Comparatives: plus, moins, aussi + adjective/adverb + que

Building Block — Weighing two things against each other
[Sujet] + être/verbe + plus / moins / aussi + [adjectif ou adverbe] + que + [second élément]
With an adjective, the adjective still agrees with the noun it describes, exactly as in ordinary description: cette conférence était plus longue que la dernière agrees longue with the feminine conférence. With an adverb, there is no agreement at all: elle s'est déroulée plus rapidement que prévu. Bon(ne) and bien do not follow this pattern productively: their comparatives are irregular (see section 4.2).
Cette conférence était plus intéressante que celle du printemps. Le public a posé moins de questions que la dernière fois, mais il était aussi attentif qu'avant.
This conference was more interesting than the one in the spring. The audience asked fewer questions than last time, but it was just as attentive as before.

4.2 Irregular comparatives: meilleur/mieux, pire/plus mauvais

Language Focus

Bon(ne) and bien do not take plus productively. Instead, French uses two distinct irregular forms, and choosing between them depends on whether you are describing a noun or modifying a verb.

  • Cette présentation était meilleure que la dernière. This presentation was better than the last one. (adjective, agrees with présentation)
  • Ça s'est mieux passé que je le pensais. It went better than I thought. (adverb, modifies the verb, never agrees)
  • Le public était pire — ou plutôt plus difficile — que la dernière fois. The audience was worse — or rather more difficult — than last time. (pire evaluates the situation or quality negatively.)

A useful test: if you can replace the word with an adjective describing a noun (a good/better conference), reach for meilleur(e)(s). If you are describing how an action went (it went well/better), reach for mieux. Do not say *plus bon or *plus bien in either case.

4.3 Avoiding repetition: recycling object, y, and en pronouns

Review and Application — Comparisons without repeating the noun

You have controlled direct object pronouns, indirect object pronouns, y, and en since Volume II. A longer comparison gives them real work to do: once you have named an experience, a place, or a quantity once, French strongly prefers a pronoun over repeating the noun.

  • La conférence de printemps ? Je m'en souviens très bien, mais je la trouve moins mémorable que celle-ci. The spring conference? I remember it very well, but I find it less memorable than this one. (en with se souvenir de; la replacing la conférence as a direct object)
  • Nous avons parlé à beaucoup plus de participants cette fois — nous leur avons même demandé leur avis. We spoke to a lot more participants this time — we even asked them their opinion. (leur, indirect object, replacing aux participants)
  • Tu es allé à la conférence de printemps ? — Oui, j'y suis allé, mais celle-ci était bien meilleure. Did you go to the spring conference? — Yes, I went there, but this one was much better. (y replacing à la conférence)

Nothing about the form of these pronouns is new in this chapter; what is new is applying them deliberately across a longer stretch of comparative discourse, so that a comparison does not collapse into naming the same noun four or five times.

4.4 The superlative: le/la/les plus… (de), le/la/les moins… (de)

Language Note — Singling out the best, worst, most, or least

The superlative is built on the comparative: add the appropriate definite article before plus or moins. To introduce the comparison set in this pattern, use de; do not mechanically translate English "in" with dans. The article and adjective still agree with the noun; the placement of the superlative phrase follows ordinary adjective placement (before or after the noun).

  • C'était la meilleure présentation de l'année. It was the best presentation of the year.
  • C'est la conférence la plus utile que j'ai suivie jusqu'ici — enfin, dans l'ensemble. It's the most useful conference I've attended so far — well, overall.

To introduce the comparison set in this pattern, use de (la meilleure conférence de l'année); do not mechanically translate English "in" with dans.

The subjonctif is not active anywhere in this chapter. Continue building comparisons and conclusions with the indicative structures already active, including after a superlative, as in the example above. Formulaic subjunctive exposure begins in Chapter 6; controlled introduction follows in Chapter 8.

Model Dialogue: C'était bien mieux que je le pensais

Context: Sophie asks Karim to compare this year's conference to the one his team attended in the spring. Register: informal, close friends; tu throughout.

Sophie : Alors, comparée à celle du printemps, comment était cette conférence ? So, compared to the one in the spring, how was this conference?
Karim : Bien meilleure, honnêtement ! Le public était plus attentif, et nous avons eu plus de temps pour présenter. Much better, honestly! The audience was more attentive, and we had more time to present.
Sophie : Tu te souviens encore de la conférence de printemps ? Do you still remember the spring conference?
Karim : Oui, je m'en souviens très bien — ça s'était moins bien passé que prévu. Le public était aussi nombreux, mais bien moins attentif. Yes, I remember it very well — it went less well than expected. The audience was just as numerous, but far less attentive.
Sophie : Et cette fois, qu'est-ce qui a changé, à ton avis ? And this time, what changed, in your opinion?
Karim : Je pense qu'on était mieux préparés, tandis qu'au printemps, on avait un peu improvisé. C'est pour cela que ça s'est tellement mieux passé. I think we were better prepared, whereas in the spring, we had improvised a bit. That's why it went so much better.
Sophie : Alors c'était la meilleure des deux ? So it was the better of the two?
Karim : Sans hésitation. Dans l'ensemble, c'était bien mieux que je le pensais. Without hesitation. Overall, it was much better than I thought.

This dialogue models comparatives with adjectives (plus attentif) and adverbs (mieux passé), the irregular comparative meilleure/mieux, the superlative (la meilleure des deux), the contrast connector tandis que, and the recycled pronoun en (je m'en souviens) avoiding repetition of la conférence de printemps.

Culture and Communication

Culture in Context — Comparing without dismissing

Comparing two experiences openly — a job, a school, a country, a conference format — is a normal, everyday part of French conversation and professional feedback, not a sign of disloyalty to the option that came out worse. That said, the same softening instinct from Chapter 3 often applies: a strong superlative claim (c'est de loin la meilleure) lands more comfortably among close friends than in a formal evaluation addressed to a wider audience, where a more measured comparative (plus efficace, nettement amélioré) is often preferred.

Regional Variation — Comparative and evaluative expressions

Plus… que, meilleur, mieux, and the superlative pattern in this chapter are standard throughout the Francophone world. Bien meilleur is itself a standard, ordinary way to intensify a comparison and is appropriate across most registers, including careful writing. Super bien, by contrast, is a familiar, spoken-only intensifier; in more formal writing, nettement meilleur or considérablement meilleur is often preferred instead.

Common Mistakes

Common Mistake — Saying *plus bon or *plus bien

Error: Writing *cette conférence était plus bonne or *ça s'est plus bien passé, applying the regular comparative pattern to bon(ne) and bien.

Correction: Use the irregular forms instead: cette conférence était meilleure (adjective, agrees with the noun) and ça s'est mieux passé (adverb, never agrees). Bon(ne)/meilleur(e) and bien/mieux are among a small set of French adjectives and adverbs with irregular comparatives (mauvais(e)/pire is another, taught in this chapter's vocabulary), so this pair is worth memorizing as a fixed exception rather than assuming the regular plus… pattern applies.

Common Mistake — Using dans instead of de in a superlative

Error: Writing *la meilleure conférence dans l'année, following the English "in."

Correction: To introduce the comparison set in this pattern, use de: la meilleure conférence de l'année. Do not mechanically translate English "in" with dans here, regardless of what the group is (a year, a team, a class, a family).

Common Mistake — Repeating the noun instead of using a pronoun

Error: Writing *Je me souviens de la conférence de printemps. La conférence de printemps était moins bien organisée., repeating the full noun phrase in back-to-back sentences.

Correction: Once a noun is established, replace it: je me souviens de la conférence de printemps ; elle était moins bien organisée, or, since se souvenir de governs de, je m'en souviens ; elle était moins bien organisée. Repeating a full noun phrase across consecutive sentences reads as unnatural and overly formal in French, exactly as it would in English.

J'ai habité dans deux appartements très différents cette année. Le premier était plus grand, mais je le trouvais moins lumineux que le second. Le second, en revanche, est de loin le meilleur appartement où j'ai habité jusqu'ici. Le quartier est plus calme, et je m'y sens beaucoup mieux le soir. Tandis que dans le premier appartement je me sentais souvent isolé, dans le second, je me suis fait des amis dès la première semaine. Dans l'ensemble, l'emplacement compte bien plus que la taille d'un logement.

I lived in two very different apartments this year. The first was bigger, but I found it less bright than the second. The second, on the other hand, is by far the best apartment I've ever lived in: the neighborhood is quieter, and I feel much better there in the evening. Whereas in the first apartment I often felt isolated, in the second, I made friends within the first week. Overall, location matters much more than the size of a place.

This six-sentence model includes two adjective comparatives, one adverb comparative (mieux), one superlative in the indicative, two pronouns avoiding repetition (le, y), a tandis que contrast, and a concluding sentence with dans l'ensemble. No subjonctif, passive, or freely generated reported-speech form appears anywhere.
