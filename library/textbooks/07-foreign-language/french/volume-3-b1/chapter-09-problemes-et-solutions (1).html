Problèmes et solutions

Problems and Solutions · Speak and Write French: Independent Communication and Advanced Expression

Chapter Objectives

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

  1. Use core problem-solving vocabulary: un problème, un obstacle, un défi, résoudre, empêcher, une solution, and related terms.
  2. Form and use hypotheses about likely or possible situations with si + présent, + futur simple, expanding the conditionnel you have already built.
  3. Form and use hypotheses about unlikely or hypothetical situations with si + imparfait, + conditionnel présent, choosing correctly between the two si-clause patterns.
  4. Continue productive use of the eight Chapter 8 subjonctif triggers, and extend the subjonctif to two new bounded triggers of possibility, il est possible que and il se peut que, using only verbs already authorized for productive subjonctif use.
  5. Understand and produce, in a small set of controlled, closely modeled sentences, the paired negative structure ni…ni, without yet extending it to its full grammar.
  6. Use ne…que confidently and consistently to express restriction in problem-solving contexts.
  7. Read and listen to short problem/solution texts and identify the problem, the proposed solution, and the degree of certainty expressed.
  8. Write a short set of six to nine sentences that presents a problem, proposes at least one hypothesis, and recommends a solution using the subjonctif appropriately.

Communication Scenario

The neighborhood festival that Sophie proposed in Chapter 8 is now three weeks away, and a real obstacle has appeared: the outdoor space the association hoped to use is also booked by another group on the same date. Madame Dubois calls an emergency planning session with Sophie and Karim. Chapter 8 asked you to recommend and react; Chapter 9 asks you to reason about possibilities — what will probably happen if nothing changes, what could happen under different, less likely conditions, and what remains genuinely uncertain.

  1. Describe an organizational problem using the vocabulary of obstacles, challenges, and solutions.
  2. Form a realistic hypothesis about what will happen if a likely condition is met, using si + présent/futur.
  3. Form a more distant hypothesis about what would happen under a less likely condition, using si + imparfait/conditionnel.
  4. Express possibility, not just necessity or emotion, using il est possible que or il se peut que.

Pronunciation Focus

Pronunciation Focus — Distinguishing the two si-clause patterns aloud

The two hypothesis patterns in this chapter sound different in a way that reinforces the meaning difference between them. Si + présent, read with ordinary present-tense pronunciation, sounds immediate and matter-of-fact. Si + imparfait carries the familiar imparfait -ais/-ait/-aient sound you already control, which signals a more distant, less certain frame before the conditionnel verb even arrives.

Practice both patterns aloud, listening for the shift in the verb right after si:

  • Si le parc est disponible, nous y organiserons la fête. (si + present, likely condition)
  • Si le parc n’était pas disponible, nous chercherions un autre lieu. (si + imparfait, less likely condition)

Liaison, enchaînement, and rhythm-group pacing continue exactly as in earlier chapters, for example nous‿organiserons. Note one detail worth flagging: si elides to s’ only before il/ils (s’il pleut), never before elle, on, or other vowel-initial words (si on trouve, no elision). No new sound-spelling feature is introduced.

Grammar: Problèmes et solutions

Purpose: activate the two core hypothesis patterns with si, extend the subjonctif to two new bounded possibility triggers using only verbs and irregular paradigms already authorized in Chapter 8, and give a controlled introduction to ni…ni alongside consolidated ne…que for problem-solving discourse. No new irregular subjonctif paradigm is introduced beyond the five presented in Chapter 8. The passive voice remains receptive only, exactly as in Chapters 6–8.

9.1 Realistic hypothesis: si + présent, + futur simple

Building Block — What will probably happen
Si + [présent], + [futur simple]
This pattern presents a condition as realistic and likely, then states its probable result. The verb after si stays in the présent even though the meaning is about the future; the result clause uses the futur simple, a tense you already control from Volume II. The order of the two clauses can be reversed without changing the meaning.
Si le parc est disponible, nous y organiserons la fête. Nous trouverons une solution si tout le monde reste calme.
If the park is available, we'll organize the festival there. We'll find a solution if everyone stays calm.

9.2 Distant hypothesis: si + imparfait, + conditionnel présent

Building Block — What would happen under a less likely condition
Si + [imparfait], + [conditionnel présent]
This second pattern presents a condition as less likely, contrary to the current situation, or simply more hypothetical, then states what would result. The verb after si is in the imparfait, and the result clause uses the conditionnel présent, both systems you already control from Volume II and earlier Volume III chapters. Never combine si with a conditionnel verb directly after it; that combination does not occur in either pattern.
Si le parc n’était pas disponible, nous chercherions un autre lieu. Que ferions-nous si la météo ne coopérait pas ?
If the park weren't available, we would look for another location. What would we do if the weather didn't cooperate?

Choosing between the two patterns is a matter of how likely the speaker considers the condition, not a strict grammatical rule tied to a specific situation: the same real-world condition can sometimes be framed either way depending on the speaker's outlook. This chapter teaches these two clean, matched pairings (présent/futur and imparfait/conditionnel) as the controlled default; French also permits other combinations, such as a conditionnel result after si + présent, which this chapter does not require you to produce. What remains fixed in every pattern is that si itself is never directly followed by a conditionnel verb. This chapter's practice and tasks model clearly likely or clearly hypothetical conditions so the choice remains unambiguous; a fuller treatment of borderline cases is not required here.

9.3 Possibility: il est possible que and il se peut que

Grammar Focus — Saying something might happen

Chapter 8 authorized eight subjonctif triggers of necessity, emotion, and recommendation. This chapter adds two triggers of simple possibility, still using only the subjonctif-formation rules and the five memorized irregular paradigms (être, avoir, faire, pouvoir, aller) you learned in section 8.1. No new stem-formation rule and no new irregular paradigm is introduced.

  • Il est possible que l’autre groupe accepte de changer de date. It's possible that the other group will agree to change the date. il est possible que + subjonctif (transparent stem: ils acceptent → accepte)
  • Il se peut que nous devions trouver un autre lieu. It may be that we'll have to find another location. il se peut que + subjonctif; devions recycled from the devoir-family stem you already recognize

Il est possible que and il se peut que specifically require the subjonctif; this is a property of these two constructions, not a general rule that every expression of possibility triggers the subjonctif. Do not generalize this to other ways of expressing possibility: peut-être que, for example, normally keeps the indicative, exactly as in Peut-être que le conseil dira oui (Speaking Practice, below). Compare this also with selon/d’après from Chapter 6, which keep the indicative because they report a stated fact.

9.4 Controlled introduction: ni…ni

Controlled Introduction — Limited production

You will encounter ni…ni in this chapter's texts and dialogue, used to say that neither of two things is true. This chapter does not fully explain its placement rules with different verb forms and object types. Understand and produce the following two controlled patterns through closely modeled substitution:

  • Le parc n’est ni assez grand ni assez central pour tout le monde. The park is neither big enough nor central enough for everyone. (fixed pattern: ne + verb + ni + adjectif + ni + adjectif)
  • Nous n’avons ni le temps ni le budget pour tout changer. We have neither the time nor the budget to change everything. (fixed pattern: ne + avoir + ni + nom + ni + nom)

Use both sentences as models for controlled substitution with similar nouns or adjectives in a parallel structure, not as a springboard for the full grammar of ni…ni, which is developed later in the volume alongside the rest of the negation system.

9.5 Consolidating ne…que

Grammar Focus — Restriction with ne…que

Ne…que, introduced earlier in the volume, is fully productive by this chapter and useful for stating limits on a solution: it means “only,” not a true negation, and does not change the following noun's article the way ne…pas de does.

  • Nous n’avons qu’une seule autre date possible. We only have one other possible date.
Reminder — Still bounded

All eight Chapter 8 triggers remain productive, now joined by il est possible que and il se peut que — ten authorized triggers in total, always with a transparent present-derived stem or one of the five memorized irregular paradigms from section 8.1. Ni…ni remains limited to controlled production within the two introduced patterns above. The passive voice remains receptive only.

Practice — Si + présent ou si + imparfait ?

  2. Si l’autre groupe ne ___ (vouloir, imparfait) pas changer de date, nous ___ (devoir, conditionnel) tout reporter.
  3. Il est possible que Madame Dubois ___ (avoir, subjonctif) une autre idée.

Model Dialogue: Un problème d’organisation

Context: Sophie, Karim, and Madame Dubois discuss the scheduling conflict for the neighborhood festival. Register: semi-formal; vous with Madame Dubois, tu between Sophie and Karim.

Mme Dubois : Nous avons un problème. Un autre groupe a réservé le parc pour la même date que notre fête. We have a problem. Another group has booked the park for the same date as our festival.
Karim : Il est possible que l’autre groupe accepte de changer de date, non ? On pourrait leur demander. It's possible the other group will agree to change the date, right? We could ask them.
Mme Dubois : Si l’autre groupe accepte, nous garderons la même date. Sinon, il se peut que nous devions trouver un autre lieu. If the other group agrees, we'll keep the same date. Otherwise, we may have to find another location.
Sophie : Et si personne ne cédait ? Le parc n’est ni assez grand ni assez central pour partager l’espace de toute façon. And if no one gives way? The park is neither big enough nor central enough to share the space anyway.
Karim : Si personne ne cédait, nous chercherions un autre lieu — la cour de l’école, par exemple. If no one gave way, we would look for another location — the school courtyard, for example.
Mme Dubois : Bonne idée. Mais nous n’avons qu’une seule autre date possible avant l’été, donc il faut que nous décidions vite. Good idea. But we only have one other possible date before summer, so we need to decide quickly.
Sophie : D’accord. Je propose que Karim contacte l’autre groupe aujourd’hui. Si ça ne marche pas, on envisagera la cour de l’école. Agreed. I propose Karim contact the other group today. If that doesn't work, we'll consider the school courtyard.

This dialogue models both hypothesis patterns (si l’autre groupe accepte, nous garderons…; si personne ne cédait, nous chercherions…); both new possibility triggers (il est possible que, il se peut que); one ni…ni sentence exactly as modeled in section 9.4; consolidated ne…que; and recycled Chapter 8 triggers (il faut que, je propose que).

Culture and Communication

Culture in Context — Negotiating shared resources

Community groups across the Francophone world regularly negotiate shared public spaces, budgets, and schedules with local institutions and with each other. The possibility and hypothesis constructions practiced in this chapter — il est possible que, si…, nous… — are useful tools in this kind of collaborative problem-solving because they let speakers state conditions, possible outcomes, and alternatives explicitly, which helps a group work toward a shared decision.

Regional Variation — Problem-solving vocabulary

Un problème, une solution, and résoudre are used and understood throughout the Francophone world. Some regions and professional contexts prefer un enjeu (a stake, a key issue) where this chapter uses un défi; the vocabulary in this chapter remains the safer, broadly recognized default.

Common Mistakes

Common Mistake — Using the conditionnel directly after si

Error: Writing *Si nous trouverions un autre lieu…, placing the conditionnel immediately after si.

Correction: Si is never directly followed by a conditionnel verb in either hypothesis pattern taught in this chapter. Use the imparfait after si when the result clause is in the conditionnel: Si nous trouvions un autre lieu, nous serions ravis.

Common Mistake — Losing track of which pattern you started

Watch for: Starting with si + présent and then drifting into an imparfait-style result, or the reverse, so the sentence no longer matches either pattern cleanly.

Correction: For the realistic pattern practiced in this chapter, pair présent with futur simple: si…est…, nous organiserons. For the more distant pattern, pair imparfait with conditionnel: si…était…, nous organiserions. This is the clean, controlled pairing this chapter asks you to produce — but it is not an absolute rule that si + présent can never appear with a conditionnel result clause elsewhere in French; French also allows a conditionnel result after si + présent in some contexts. This chapter simply keeps the two patterns separate so the meaning contrast (likely versus more hypothetical) stays clear while you are first learning them.

Common Mistake — Extending ni…ni or the subjonctif beyond this chapter’s scope

Out of scope for this chapter: Building a new ni…ni sentence with a structure not modeled in section 9.4, or using a subjonctif trigger of doubt such as je doute que, which remains outside authorized use until Chapter 18.

Correction: Reuse the two modeled ni…ni patterns only, and stay within the ten authorized subjonctif triggers (the eight from Chapter 8 plus il est possible que and il se peut que from this chapter).

Writing Workshop — Model text

Modèle — Un problème et ses solutions

Notre association fait face à un problème d’organisation pour la prochaine fête de quartier. Il est possible que le lieu habituel ne soit pas disponible cette année. Si le lieu est libre, nous y organiserons la fête comme d’habitude. Si le lieu n’était pas disponible, nous chercherions un espace plus petit dans le quartier. Il faut que nous prenions une décision avant la fin du mois. Il se peut aussi que plusieurs voisins proposent leur jardin pour la fête. Nous n’avons pas encore de réponse définitive, mais nous restons optimistes.

Our association is facing an organizational problem for the next neighborhood festival. It's possible the usual location won't be available this year. If the location is free, we'll organize the festival there as usual. If the location weren't available, we would look for a smaller space in the neighborhood. We need to make a decision before the end of the month. It may also be that several neighbors will offer their garden for the festival. We don't have a definite answer yet, but we remain optimistic.

This seven-sentence model includes both hypothesis patterns, two possibility triggers, one recycled Chapter 8 necessity trigger (il faut que), and no ni…ni or passive construction. Other responses are acceptable when they meet all task requirements and remain within the grammar and vocabulary activated through Chapter 9.
