Expliquer un problème complexe

Explaining Complex Problems · Speak and Write French: Professional, Academic, Media, and Institutional French

Chapter Objectives

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

  1. Explain a complex workplace problem in speech and in writing: what happened, why it happened, what it caused, and what should be done, recycling and expanding this chapter's cause, consequence, and clarification connectors.
  2. Recycle and expand the already-active cause/consequence/contrast/concession connector system — parce que, car, puisque, donc, par conséquent, ainsi — adding étant donné que and du fait que as newly productive, more formal causal connectors.
  3. Use a first controlled instance of impersonal, fronted information packaging — ce qui pose problème, c’est que… and ce qui explique…, c’est… — to place emphasis on the cause or the consequence of a problem, without treating clefting as a fully productive general system.
  4. Recycle the eleven Chapter 8–11 subjonctif triggers, the Chapter 18 negative-belief/negative-certainty environment, and the Chapter 19 bien que/quoique concession environment inside an extended problem explanation, without any new productive trigger.
  5. Add and recycle a curated cause/consequence/clarification vocabulary set: être dû à, provenir de, engendrer, entraîner, poser problème, à l’origine de, dans la mesure où, de ce fait, and autrement dit.
  6. Recycle dont, the Chapter 2 impersonal-plus-infinitive frames, and the Chapter 3 reported-speech verbs and turn-taking formulas, applied to a problem-explanation task.
  7. Write a short explanatory memo (six to nine sentences) analyzing a workplace problem: cause, consequence, and a recommended next step, preparing directly for the Chapter 5 Unité I integration.

Communication Scenario

A shipment of training materials for the professional development program has arrived three weeks late, and Mme Girard asks Karim to explain to the Paris head office what happened and why, since several colleagues have already asked about it informally. Sophie, meanwhile, needs to explain to her own manager why the badge request from Chapter 2 is still pending, tracing the delay back to a missing document. Chapter 4 turns from the language of requests and meetings to the language of explanation: naming a cause, tracing a consequence, clarifying a point that might be misunderstood, and recommending what should happen next.

  1. State clearly what the problem is.
  2. Explain what caused it, using this chapter's cause connectors.
  3. Explain what it led to, using this chapter's consequence connectors.
  4. Clarify a point that could be misread, and recommend a next step.

Pronunciation Focus

Pronunciation Focus — Signaling cause and consequence with intonation

No new sound-spelling or vowel feature is introduced in this chapter. The task is using already-active rhythm and intonation to help a listener track the shift from cause to consequence across a longer explanation — the first formulaic step toward the more advanced listening-pronunciation variation work that begins later in the volume.

  • Cause, then consequence: Étant donné que le fournisseur a eu un problème logistique, la livraison a pris trois semaines de retard. — a level rhythm through the étant donné que clause, then a brief pause before the main clause, with a falling pitch at the end.
  • Review the elision in qu’un problème, the required liaison in trois_ans and dans_un délai, and the enchaînement in cela a entraîné. Note that dû keeps its circumflex to distinguish it in writing from the partitive article du.

4.1 Naming a cause: parce que, car, puisque, étant donné que, du fait que

Grammar Focus — Recycled and newly productive causal connectors

Parce que, car, and puisque are recycled from earlier volumes. Étant donné que and du fait que are newly productive in this chapter, adding a more formal register for stating a cause in professional and institutional writing. All five connectors introduce a clause in the indicatif, since a cause is presented as an established fact.

  • Le colis est arrivé en retard parce que le fournisseur a eu un problème logistique. The package arrived late because the supplier had a logistics problem. parce que + indicatif, recycled
  • Étant donné que le formulaire n’était pas complet, le dossier a été retourné. Given that the form was not complete, the file was returned. étant donné que + indicatif, newly productive, formal register
  • Du fait que le transporteur a changé d’itinéraire, la livraison a pris du retard. Owing to the fact that the carrier changed its route, the delivery was delayed. du fait que + indicatif, newly productive, formal written register

4.2 Naming a consequence and clarifying a point

Building Block — A useful problem-explanation shape (this chapter's practice template)
[the problem stated] + [cause: parce que / car / puisque / étant donné que / du fait que] + [consequence: donc / par conséquent / ainsi / de ce fait] + [optional clarification: c’est-à-dire / autrement dit] + [recommended step]
The cause connectors are recycled and newly extended; the consequence connectors recycle Volume III material directly; the clarification connectors are new to this chapter and useful whenever a first statement of cause or consequence risks being misread. This is a useful template for this chapter's practice, not a fixed rule: some explanations need no clarification step at all, and a short problem may not require every element.
Le dossier de Sophie est toujours en attente. Cela est dû à un justificatif de domicile manquant, un document dont le service a besoin pour traiter toute demande. De ce fait, le badge ne pourra pas être délivré avant la semaine prochaine. Autrement dit, il ne s’agit pas d’un refus, mais d’un dossier incomplet. Il conviendrait de renvoyer le document dès que possible.
Sophie's file is still pending. This is due to a missing proof of address, a document the department needs in order to process any request. As a result, the badge cannot be issued before next week. In other words, this is not a refusal, but an incomplete file. It would be advisable to resend the document as soon as possible.

4.3 A first controlled fronted structure for emphasis

Grammar Focus — Ce qui pose problème, c’est que… / Ce qui explique…, c’est…

French can front an idea for emphasis using ce qui plus a verb, followed by c’est que plus a clause, or c’est plus a noun phrase. This chapter introduces this pattern at a controlled level, limited to two fixed frames useful for problem explanation: ce qui pose problème, c’est que… (what is the problem is that…) and ce qui explique…, c’est… (what explains… is…). This is not a general introduction to clefting as a productive system; other fronted structures remain receptive until later in the volume.

  • Ce qui pose problème, c’est que le formulaire n’a pas été rempli à temps. What's the problem is that the form wasn't filled out in time. ce qui pose problème, c’est que + indicatif, newly introduced fixed frame
  • Ce qui explique le retard, c’est un changement d’itinéraire chez le transporteur. What explains the delay is a change of route on the carrier's part. ce qui explique X, c’est Y, newly introduced fixed frame

The eleven Chapter 8–11 subjonctif triggers, the Chapter 18 negative-belief/negative-certainty environment, and the Chapter 19 bien que/quoique concession environment remain fully active alongside this chapter's recycled and newly extended causal connectors; no further trigger is introduced. Model Dialogue: Un retard qui s’explique

Context: Karim explains the late training-materials shipment to Mme Girard before she reports it to the Paris head office. Register: formal, professional.

Mme Girard : Karim, pouvez-vous m’expliquer ce qui s’est passé avec la livraison des supports de formation ? Karim, can you explain to me what happened with the training materials delivery?
Karim : Bien sûr. Le retard est dû à un problème logistique chez le fournisseur. Étant donné que le transporteur a changé d’itinéraire, la livraison a pris trois semaines de retard. Of course. The delay is due to a logistics problem at the supplier. Given that the carrier changed its route, the delivery ended up three weeks late.
Mme Girard : Je vois. Et quelle en a été la conséquence pour le programme de formation ? I see. And what was the consequence of that for the training program?
Karim : De ce fait, la première session a dû être reportée d’une semaine, un changement dont plusieurs participants ont déjà été informés. As a result, the first session had to be postponed by a week, a change several participants have already been informed about.
Mme Girard : Bien que ce contretemps soit regrettable, il faut que nous rassurions l’équipe de Paris. Ce qui pose problème, c’est surtout le manque de communication du fournisseur. Although this setback is unfortunate, we need to reassure the Paris team. The main problem is the supplier's lack of communication.
Karim : Tout à fait. Autrement dit, il ne s’agit pas d’une erreur de notre côté. Je vais rédiger une courte note explicative pour Paris. Exactly. In other words, this isn't an error on our end. I'll write a short explanatory note for Paris.
Mme Girard : Parfait. Il conviendrait d’y ajouter une brève recommandation pour prévenir ce problème à l’avenir. Perfect. It would be advisable to add a brief recommendation to it to prevent this problem in the future.

This dialogue models the recycled and newly extended cause connectors (étant donné que), the recycled consequence connector de ce fait, the newly introduced fronted frame ce qui pose problème, c’est…, the clarification connector autrement dit, a recycled dont, a recycled Chapter 19 concession, a recycled Chapter 8–11 trigger, and a recycled Chapter 2 impersonal frame. No unauthorized subjonctif trigger appears, and vous is used throughout.

Culture and Communication

Culture in Context — Explaining a problem without assigning blame prematurely

In many French professional settings, an explanatory note separates the factual account of what happened from any judgment about responsibility, especially before all the facts are confirmed. Phrases like autrement dit, il ne s’agit pas d’une erreur de notre équipe serve to clarify scope carefully rather than to deflect responsibility outright. This is a useful communicative habit, not a fixed rule for every situation.

Regional Variation — Formality of causal connectors

The relative formality of parce que, car, puisque, étant donné que, and du fait que can shift somewhat across Francophone professional and academic contexts, and personal or institutional habit plays a role as well. This chapter's guidance reflects common metropolitan French professional usage; other Francophone institutional cultures may lean toward slightly different defaults.

Common Mistakes

Common Mistake — Using the subjonctif after étant donné que or du fait que

Error: Writing *Étant donné que le formulaire soit incomplet…, using the subjonctif after a connector that states an established fact.

Correction: Étant donné que le formulaire est incomplet… Like parce que, car, and puisque, both new connectors take the indicatif, since they present a cause as fact rather than doubt, necessity, or emotion.

Common Mistake — Confusing dû (past participle) with du (partitive article)

Error: Writing *le retard est du à un problème logistique, dropping the circumflex.

Correction: Le retard est dû à un problème logistique. The circumflex on dû (past participle of devoir, masculine singular) distinguishes it in writing from du, the contracted partitive article; the feminine and plural forms (due, dus, dues) lose the circumflex, since no ambiguity with the article arises there.

Common Mistake — Overusing the fronted frame

Error: Using ce qui pose problème, c’est que… in every sentence of an explanation, where a simple statement would communicate the same idea more directly.

Correction: Reserve this chapter's fronted frames for the single point that most needs emphasis within an explanation. Used once, deliberately, the frame highlights what matters; used repeatedly, it loses its emphatic force.
