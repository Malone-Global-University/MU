Projet final : Agir en français avancé

Final C1 Integration · Speak and Write French: Professional, Academic, Media, and Institutional French

Chapter Objectives

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

  1. Combine, without learning anything new, the institutional and administrative register of Chapters 1–5, the media and public-debate register of Chapters 6–10, the academic and professional research register of Chapters 11–15, and the stylistic, narrative, negotiation, and argumentative register of Chapters 16–19 into a single integrated professional or academic dossier.
  2. Select, deliberately and economically, from the full recycled toolkit of Volume IV — the Chapter 4 emphatic cleft, the Chapter 6 source-framing and reported-speech toolkit, the Chapter 8 concession-and-reassertion frame, the Chapter 9 civic-institutional framing, the Chapter 11–15 academic and professional toolkit, the Chapter 16 nuance-and-tone toolkit, the Chapter 17 attribution toolkit, the Chapter 18 negotiation toolkit, and the Chapter 19 concession/escalation/refutation toolkit — rather than displaying every tool in a single text.
  3. Produce a written capstone dossier (a cover memo plus a short report or article, eight to twelve sentences of connected prose beyond the memo) that draws on at least four different chapters' toolkits and integrates at least two specifically attributed pieces of evidence.
  4. Deliver a multi-part oral presentation (at least twelve connected sentences) that states a position, supports it with evidence, addresses at least one counterpoint, and closes with synthesis.
  5. Correctly use, without introducing any new trigger, at least two of the eleven Volume III Chapter 8–11 subjonctif triggers, the Volume III Chapter 18 negative-belief/negative-certainty environment, or the Volume III Chapter 19 bien que/quoique concession environment, across the written and spoken capstone tasks combined.
  6. Review, through a representative cumulative Chapter Review and demonstrate, across the Chapter Review and the capstone assessment together, the full range of vocabulary, grammar, pronunciation, orthography, and register systems activated across Volume IV.
  7. Read a short adapted capstone dossier and listen to a short excerpt of a final public presentation, both synthesizing the neighborhood market storyline that has run through Chapters 1–19.
  8. Complete the Volume IV unit and volume assessment: a graded, cumulative performance task rather than a formative check, evaluated only against material already active by Chapter 19.

Communication Scenario

A full year of work on the neighborhood association's affairs comes together in one final task. Mme Girard asks Sophie and Karim to prepare a capstone dossier for the association's annual general meeting: a short written report on the market's future, accompanied by a cover memo, and a spoken presentation to the assembled members and the town hall's liaison officer. The dossier must draw on everything the two of them have built since Chapter 1 — the formal self-presentation and institutional register from Unité I, the media synthesis and public-debate skills from Unité II, the research and professional-writing skills from Unité III, and the stylistic, narrative, negotiation, and argumentative tools from Unité IV — without inventing anything new. Nothing in this chapter is unfamiliar; the task is choosing well from what Sophie and Karim, and you, already control, and combining it into one coherent, C1-level professional and academic performance.

  1. Assemble a short written dossier that moves smoothly between registers (a formal memo, then a report or article).
  2. Select, rather than exhaustively list, tools from at least four different earlier chapters.
  3. Deliver a multi-part oral presentation that states a position, supports it, answers a counterpoint, and closes with synthesis.
  4. Demonstrate, across the whole capstone, accurate control of the full range of Volume IV's active systems.

Pronunciation Focus

Pronunciation Focus — Register-shifting across a single performance

No new sound-spelling, vowel, or connected-speech feature is introduced in this chapter. The task is holding together, across one longer spoken performance, the register-sensitive delivery practiced separately in earlier chapters: the more formal, careful pacing appropriate to the institutional memo and report sections (Unité I and Unité III), the measured, source-citing delivery of media synthesis (Unité II), and the contrastive deliveries practiced in Unité IV — the light, tentative quality of a floated proposal (Chapter 18), and the paced contrast among concession, escalation, and refutation (Chapter 19). A strong capstone presentation shifts smoothly among these deliveries as the content shifts, rather than flattening the whole presentation into one uniform tone.

  • Institutional opening: Mesdames et messieurs, je vous remercie de votre présence à cette assemblée générale. — even, formal pacing, recycled from Unité I.
  • Evidence-based synthesis: Selon le rapport du service de la voirie, la fréquentation a augmenté de manière significative. — measured, source-citing delivery, recycled from Unité II and III.
  • Concession and pivot: Certes, ce projet représente un coût réel. Il n’en demeure pas moins qu’il profite à l’ensemble du quartier. — the Chapter 19 concession-pivot contrast.

20.1 The complete Volume IV toolkit, by unit

Grammar Focus — Unité I: Discours formel et vie institutionnelle (Chapters 1–4)

Formal self-presentation and institutional politeness (Chapter 1); formal requests, forms, and impersonal structures for administrative procedures (Chapter 2); turn-taking, reported speech, and agenda language for meetings (Chapter 3); and cause, consequence, concession, and clarification for explaining complex problems (Chapter 4), including the Chapter 4 emphatic cleft (ce qui frappe, c’est que / ce qui compte, c’est que), which recycles directly into this chapter's oral and written synthesis.

Grammar Focus — Unité II: Médias, société et débat public (Chapters 6–9)

Journalistic structure and source framing for news (Chapter 6), including the source-framing and reported-speech toolkit (selon, d’après); data commentary and cautious claims for social trends (Chapter 7); concession, counterargument, rhetorical questions, and modalization for nuanced debate (Chapter 8), including the concession-and-reassertion frame (il n’en demeure pas moins que); and institutional discourse and public argument for civic life (Chapter 9), including the civic-institutional framing recycled throughout Unité IV.

Grammar Focus — Unité III: Recherche, études et communication professionnelle (Chapters 11–14)

Abstract prose, nominalization, and citation language for academic reading (Chapter 11); summary, paraphrase, and source comparison for synthesis (Chapter 12); report structure, recommendations, and passive/impersonal style (Chapter 13); and oral presentation, visual explanation, and audience management for research presentations (Chapter 14). Together these form the academic and professional toolkit this chapter's written dossier and oral presentation both draw on directly.

Culture and Communication

Culture in Context — Register control as a marker of C1 competence

Across French professional, academic, and civic life, the ability to move deliberately among institutional, evidence-based, and rhetorical registers within a single coherent piece of communication — without losing coherence or overusing any one tool — is often treated as a hallmark of advanced competence. This is a general tendency this volume draws on, not a claim that a single fixed template applies to every professional or academic context.

Regional Variation — One volume, many Francophone contexts

The specific institutional, media, academic, and negotiation conventions active across this volume reflect common metropolitan French usage. Across French, Belgian, Swiss, Québécois, and other Francophone administrative, academic, and professional traditions, expectations for formality, directness, and document structure vary, sometimes considerably. The underlying skills this volume has developed — presenting formally, citing evidence, conceding honestly, negotiating, and arguing — transfer across these contexts even where specific conventions differ, and Volume V continues to develop this awareness at a more specialized level.

Common Mistakes

Common Mistake — Trying to use every tool from every chapter

Error: Attempting to fit certes, non seulement…mais encore, loin de, peut-être conviendrait-il de, les deux parties s’accordent à reconnaître que, sous réserve de, and every Chapter 16/17 tool into one short dossier, producing a text that reads as a checklist rather than genuine communication.

Correction: Select a small number of tools that genuinely fit the content, as modeled throughout this chapter. A capstone dossier that uses four well-chosen tools accurately is stronger evidence of C1 competence than one that crams in every tool from Volume IV.

Common Mistake — Letting register drift within a single section

Error: Opening the cover memo formally and then sliding into an informal, conversational tone partway through, or mixing the informal spoken ne deletion into formal written prose.

Correction: Keep the cover memo consistently formal throughout, as in Chapters 1–5, and reserve any deliberate register shift for a clearly marked transition, such as moving from the memo into the report. Written French in this dossier retains ne in negation throughout.

Common Mistake — Leaving evidence unattributed

Error: Writing a general claim such as *de nombreuses études montrent que… without naming a specific source, report, or person.

Correction: Attribute every piece of evidence specifically, as practiced since Chapter 6: selon le rapport du service de la voirie, à en croire l’enquête de l’association de quartier. If no real source is available for an invented scenario, use clearly labeled classroom source material rather than presenting an invented source as real.

Writing Workshop — Model text

Modèle — Dossier final

Mémo : À l’attention des membres de l’association du quartier. Veuillez trouver ci-joint le rapport final sur l’avenir du marché, préparé en vue de l’assemblée générale annuelle. Il est nécessaire que ce rapport soit examiné avant la réunion de jeudi.

Rapport : Le marché du quartier doit rester une priorité pour les cinq prochaines années. Selon le rapport du service de la voirie, la fréquentation des commerces a augmenté de manière significative depuis la rénovation engagée l’an dernier. À en croire le témoignage de Mme Toussaint recueilli au chapitre 17, le marché joue depuis plusieurs décennies un rôle central dans la vie du quartier. Certes, cette rénovation a représenté une dépense importante pour la ville. Il n’en demeure pas moins que cet investissement profite directement aux commerçants et aux familles du quartier. Peut-être conviendrait-il, à présent, d’envisager un plan de développement sur cinq ans, sous réserve de l’accord formel du conseil municipal. Il se peut que certains membres du conseil hésitent devant un tel engagement à long terme, mais cette hésitation mérite d’être prise au sérieux plutôt qu’écartée. Ce qui frappe surtout, en relisant l’histoire de ce marché, c’est qu’il a toujours su évoluer sans jamais perdre ce qui fait son âme.

Memo: To the members of the neighborhood association. Please find attached the final report on the market's future, prepared for the annual general meeting. It is necessary that this report be reviewed before Thursday's meeting.

Report: The neighborhood market must remain a priority for the next five years. According to the roads-department report, business foot traffic has increased significantly since the renovation undertaken last year. If Mme Toussaint's testimony gathered in Chapter 17 is to be believed, the market has played a central role in the neighborhood's life for several decades. Admittedly, this renovation represented a significant expense for the city. It nonetheless remains the case that this investment directly benefits the neighborhood's shopkeepers and families. Perhaps it would be advisable, at this point, to consider a five-year development plan, subject to the formal agreement of the town council. It may be that some council members hesitate before such a long-term commitment, but that hesitation deserves to be taken seriously rather than dismissed. What is most striking, looking back over this market's history, is that it has always known how to evolve without ever losing what makes it what it is.

It integrates two specifically attributed pieces of evidence: the roads-department report (selon le rapport du service de la voirie) and Mme Toussaint's Chapter 17 testimony (à en croire le témoignage de Mme Toussaint) — both fictional classroom sources introduced in earlier chapters, not real, verifiable sources. It recycles two Volume III Chapter 8–11 subjonctif environments (il est nécessaire que…soit and il se peut que…hésitent). No subjonctif trigger beyond those already activated in Volumes I–III and Volume IV Chapters 1–19 appears.
