Argumenter à un niveau avancé

Advanced Argumentation · Speak and Write French: Professional, Academic, Media, and Institutional French

Chapter Objectives

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

  1. Distinguish a single nuanced opinion (Chapter 16), a cultural narrative (Chapter 17), and a neutral mediation summary (Chapter 18) from an extended, multi-paragraph argumentative essay that states a thesis, develops it, answers a counterargument, and closes with synthesis.
  2. Use a newly productive concessive opener — certes — to grant a point fully and explicitly, and pivot from it into the recycled Chapter 8/16 reassertion frame il n’en demeure pas moins que, now used as an essay's central pivot rather than a single sentence's.
  3. Use a newly productive escalating connector — non seulement…mais encore — to build a claim in two deliberate steps, so that the second half lands with more force than the first.
  4. Use a newly productive contrastive refutation frame — loin de + infinitif — to reject a mistaken assumption sharply before offering the correct account.
  5. Recycle, without any new productive subjonctif trigger, the Chapter 4 emphatic cleft, the Chapter 6 source-framing and reported-speech toolkit, the Chapter 8 concession-and-reassertion frame, the Chapter 9 civic-institutional framing, the full Chapter 11–15 academic and professional toolkit, the Chapter 16 nuance toolkit, the Chapter 17 attribution toolkit, and the Chapter 18 negotiation toolkit, selecting from among them rather than using all of them in a single essay.
  6. Receive further controlled, receptive-only exposure to a small rhetorical-essayistic vocabulary set.
  7. Add and recycle a curated argumentative-essay vocabulary set: un argument, une thèse, réfuter, concéder, une objection, and une conclusion.
  8. Put the recycled Chapter 6, 12, and 17 synthesis and source-attribution toolkit to active use, integrating at least two specifically attributed pieces of evidence into an extended argument rather than leaving claims unsupported.
  9. Plan and write an extended, multi-paragraph argumentative essay (four paragraphs, ten to fourteen sentences total) defending a position on a topic of your choice, using all three newly productive tools and at least two attributed pieces of evidence.
  10. Move to a short adapted argumentative essay about the neighborhood market's future and a short excerpt of a public presentation defending a position, continuing Unité IV toward its Chapter 20 capstone.

Communication Scenario

The anniversary newsletter now needs a closing piece: a full argumentative essay, not a single opinion paragraph, making the case for how the neighborhood market should develop over the next ten years. Sophie and Karim have gathered everything — the reports and data from Chapters 6–10, the research dossier from Chapters 11–15, the tone-control tools from Chapter 16, Mme Toussaint's history from Chapter 17, and the parking mediation from Chapter 18 — and now have to weave a subset of it into a single sustained argument with a real thesis, not just a nuanced restatement of an opinion. Mme Girard warns them that an essay this long lives or dies on its structure: a clear claim, an honest concession, an escalating case, a sharp refutation of the obvious objection, and a conclusion that does not simply repeat the introduction. Chapter 19 gives Sophie and Karim the tools to build and defend that argument from start to finish.

  1. State a clear thesis and develop it across multiple paragraphs.
  2. Concede a strong opposing point honestly before pivoting back to the thesis.
  3. Build a claim in two escalating steps rather than asserting it once.
  4. Refute a likely objection sharply, then close with synthesis rather than repetition.

Pronunciation Focus

Pronunciation Focus — Three deliveries for three rhetorical moves

No new sound-spelling or vowel feature is introduced in this chapter. The task is matching delivery to rhetorical function across a longer stretch of speech: certes is typically set off with a brief pause immediately after it, marking the concession as genuinely separate from what follows; the two halves of non seulement…mais encore are usually delivered with rising stress on seulement and a stronger, slightly slower stress on encore, so the second half audibly outweighs the first; and loin de is usually delivered crisply and a little more sharply than surrounding speech, since it exists to reject an assumption before the correct account follows. These are performance possibilities to draw on, not fixed rules of French pronunciation; real delivery varies with speaker and context.

  • Conceding, then pivoting: Certes, la rénovation a coûté cher. Il n’en demeure pas moins qu’elle a sauvé le marché. — a small pause after Certes, then a firmer, more deliberate pace on the pivot sentence.
  • Escalating a claim: Non seulement le marché attire les habitants du quartier, mais encore il attire des visiteurs de toute la ville. — light stress on seulement, stronger and slightly slower stress on encore.
  • Refuting sharply: Loin de nuire au quartier, cette évolution l’a renforcé. — a crisp, slightly clipped delivery on Loin de nuire, before settling into an even pace for the correction that follows.

19.1 Conceding fully before pivoting: certes

Grammar Focus — A concession with no hedging

Certes is newly productive as a sentence-opening concessive adverb. French uses certes more broadly than this chapter's single task — it can simply reinforce an affirmation, or carry this concessive, oppositional value — but in the concessive use practiced in this chapter, it grants a point fully and without qualification: unlike an attenuator such as tout au plus (Chapter 16), which caps a claim, certes used this way does not soften anything; it simply admits that an opposing point is true before the writer pivots away from it. In an extended essay, this concessive certes is most useful at the start of a paragraph that exists specifically to acknowledge the strongest point on the other side, immediately followed by the recycled Chapter 8/16 pivot il n’en demeure pas moins que, which reasserts the thesis without dismissing the concession. The two together form this chapter's central concession-pivot pattern; certes is the newly productive half, and the pivot recycles directly.

  • Certes, la rénovation du marché a coûté cher à la ville. Il n’en demeure pas moins qu’elle a permis de sauver un lieu que beaucoup croyaient condamné. Admittedly, the market's renovation cost the city a great deal. It nonetheless remains the case that it saved a place many believed was doomed. certes (newly productive) + il n'en demeure pas moins que (recycled Ch. 8/16 pivot)

19.2 Building a claim in two steps: non seulement...mais encore

Grammar Focus — Making the second half do more work than the first

Non seulement…mais encore is newly productive. It presents a claim in two deliberate stages, where the second stage is stronger, broader, or more surprising than the first. Mais aussi is also fully grammatical after non seulement, and can itself participate in an escalating construction; this chapter simply targets mais encore specifically, since it lends itself especially well to this kind of reinforcement (renchérissement) in formal written argumentation. For this chapter's task, the choice of connector matters less than the content: make the second element genuinely stronger or broader than the first, not merely an additional, equally weighted point. Unlike some fronted adverbials elsewhere in this volume, non seulement does not trigger subject-verb inversion; keep ordinary French word order after it (non seulement le marché attire…, not *non seulement attire-t-il…), and keep the two coordinated parts structurally parallel.

  • Non seulement le marché attire les habitants du quartier, mais encore il est devenu, ces dernières années, une destination pour des visiteurs de toute la ville. Not only does the market attract residents of the neighborhood, but in recent years it has further become a destination for visitors from across the city. non seulement...mais encore, newly productive; second clause escalates beyond the first

19.3 Rejecting an assumption sharply: loin de + infinitif

Building Block — An extended-essay shape (this chapter's practice template)
[thèse: the essay's central claim] + [certes + concession, then il n’en demeure pas moins que: honoring the strongest counterpoint] + [non seulement…mais encore: escalating the case for the thesis] + [loin de + infinitif: refuting the likely objection] + [Ch. 4 cleft or Ch. 16 reinforcement: closing on the point that matters most, without repeating the introduction]
Loin de + infinitif is newly productive. It rejects a mistaken assumption sharply — often the essay's anticipated objection — before immediately supplying the correct account in the same sentence or the one that follows. The infinitive names the mistaken claim as an action or state that did not in fact occur or hold; what follows corrects it. Loin de is stronger and more contrastive than a plain negation: *ce n’a pas nui au quartier merely denies a claim, while loin de nuire au quartier, cette évolution l’a renforcé actively overturns it and replaces it with the opposite. This chapter's essay template uses exactly one instance, reserved for the strongest, most expected objection — not scattered across minor points.
Le marché du quartier devrait rester au cœur de la politique municipale pour la prochaine décennie. Certes, son entretien représente une dépense continue pour la ville. Il n’en demeure pas moins que cet investissement profite directement aux commerçants et aux familles du quartier. Non seulement le marché soutient l’économie locale, mais encore il est devenu, au fil du temps, un symbole d’identité collective pour tout le quartier. On pourrait objecter qu’un marché en plein air appartient à une autre époque. Loin d’appartenir au passé, ce marché s’est révélé, à en croire les chiffres comme les habitants, l’un des espaces les plus vivants de la ville. Ce qui frappe surtout, c’est que peu d’investissements municipaux offrent un tel retour, à la fois économique et humain.
The neighborhood market should remain at the heart of municipal policy for the next decade. Admittedly, its upkeep represents an ongoing expense for the city. It nonetheless remains the case that this investment directly benefits the neighborhood's shopkeepers and families. Not only does the market support the local economy, but it has further become, over time, a symbol of collective identity for the whole neighborhood. One might object that an open-air market belongs to another era. Far from belonging to the past, this market has proven, according to both the figures and residents, one of the liveliest spaces in the city. What is most striking is that few municipal investments offer such a return, both economic and human.

Model Dialogue: Relire l’essai avant publication

Context: Mme Girard reads through Sophie and Karim's draft essay before it goes to print, checking that its structure holds together. Register: formal editorial review, close to a writing-workshop conversation.

Mme Girard : Votre thèse est claire dès la première phrase. C’est un bon départ. Your thesis is clear from the first sentence. That's a good start.
Karim : Merci. Ensuite, on concède que la rénovation a coûté cher — certes, on ne le nie pas. Thanks. Then we concede that the renovation cost a lot — admittedly, we don't deny it.
Mme Girard : Bien. Et vous revenez ensuite à votre thèse ? Good. And you come back to your thesis after that?
Sophie : Oui : il n’en demeure pas moins que cet investissement profite au quartier. Ensuite, on développe l’argument en deux étapes. Yes: it nonetheless remains the case that this investment benefits the neighborhood. Then we build the argument in two steps.
Karim : Non seulement le marché soutient l’économie locale, mais encore il est devenu un symbole du quartier. Not only does the market support the local economy, but it's further become a symbol of the neighborhood.
Mme Girard : Bien construit. Et l’objection évidente — qu’un marché en plein air, c’est démodé ? Well built. And the obvious objection — that an open-air market is outdated?
Sophie : On la réfute directement : loin d’appartenir au passé, ce marché est l’un des espaces les plus vivants de la ville. We refute it directly: far from belonging to the past, this market is one of the liveliest spaces in the city.
Mme Girard : Très bien. Et la conclusion — elle ne répète pas simplement l’introduction, j’espère ? Very good. And the conclusion — it doesn't just repeat the introduction, I hope?
Karim : Non. Ce qui frappe surtout, c’est que peu d’investissements municipaux offrent un tel retour, économique et humain à la fois. No. What's most striking is that few municipal investments offer such a return, both economic and human at once.
Mme Girard : Voilà une vraie conclusion, pas une simple répétition. C’est prêt pour publication. Now that's a real conclusion, not a simple repetition. It's ready for publication.

This dialogue models the three newly productive tools (certes, non seulement…mais encore, loin de + infinitif), the recycled Chapter 8/16 pivot (il n’en demeure pas moins que), and the recycled Chapter 4 emphatic cleft (ce qui frappe, c’est que) used as a closing move rather than a mid-paragraph one. No unauthorized subjonctif trigger appears.

Reading Practice

Essai : L’avenir du marché du quartier

Le marché du quartier devrait rester au cœur de la politique municipale pour la prochaine décennie. Certes, son entretien représente une dépense continue pour la ville, et l’on comprend que certains élus hésitent à renouveler cet engagement chaque année. Il n’en demeure pas moins que cet investissement profite directement aux commerçants et aux familles du quartier, ainsi qu’à l’ensemble de la vie locale. Non seulement le marché soutient l’économie de proximité, mais encore il est devenu, au fil du temps, un symbole d’identité collective auquel les habitants restent particulièrement attachés. On pourrait objecter, non sans une certaine assurance péremptoire, qu’un marché en plein air appartient à une autre époque et constitue presque l’antithèse du commerce moderne. Loin d’appartenir au passé, ce marché s’est révélé, à en croire les chiffres comme les habitants eux-mêmes, l’un des espaces les plus vivants de la ville. Ce texte n’est pas un simple plaidoyer sentimental : ce qui frappe surtout, c’est que peu d’investissements municipaux offrent un tel retour, à la fois économique et humain.

Culture and Communication

Culture in Context — The concession as a mark of rigor, not weakness

In French academic and public argumentation, an essay that acknowledges the strongest opposing point directly, rather than ignoring or minimizing it, is often read as more rigorous and more persuasive, not less. A writer who states certes and then pivots is generally seen as someone who has genuinely weighed the other side, which tends to build more credibility with a careful reader than an essay that never admits any counterpoint at all. This is a tendency in the argumentative tradition this chapter draws on, not a universal rule of persuasive writing.

Regional Variation — Argumentative conventions across the Francophone world

Expectations for essay structure, directness, and the use of an explicit thesis statement vary somewhat across French, Belgian, Swiss, Québécois, and other Francophone educational and professional traditions; some traditions favor a more visibly announced structure, while others prefer the structure to emerge more gradually. This chapter's vocabulary and patterns reflect common metropolitan French usage; the underlying skills of conceding honestly, escalating a claim, and refuting an objection transfer across contexts even where specific conventions differ.

Common Mistakes

Common Mistake — Using certes without a genuine pivot afterward

Error: Writing certes, la rénovation a coûté cher and then simply moving on to an unrelated point, so the concession is never actually answered.

Correction: In the concessive pattern practiced in this chapter, always follow certes with a clear pivot back to the thesis, typically using the recycled il n’en demeure pas moins que: certes, la rénovation a coûté cher. Il n’en demeure pas moins qu’elle a sauvé le marché. A concession with no pivot reads as an admission that undermines the thesis rather than strengthens it.

Common Mistake — Pairing non seulement with mais aussi and calling it this chapter's target

Not incorrect, but off-target: non seulement le marché soutient l’économie, mais aussi il est devenu un symbole. Mais aussi is fully grammatical and can itself support an escalating construction.

Correction: This chapter specifically targets the more formal mais encore pattern: non seulement le marché soutient l’économie, mais encore il est devenu un symbole. Regardless of which connector you choose, make sure the second element is genuinely stronger or broader than the first, not merely a second, equally weighted point.

Common Mistake — Following loin de with a conjugated verb instead of an infinitive

Error: Writing *loin qu’il nuit au quartier, cette évolution l’a renforcé, treating loin de as though it needed a clause.

Correction: Loin de takes an infinitive directly: loin de nuire au quartier, cette évolution l’a renforcé. Reserve it for the essay's single strongest objection rather than using it repeatedly.

Writing Workshop — Model text

Modèle — Essai argumentatif

La ville devrait investir davantage dans les pistes cyclables du centre-ville au cours des cinq prochaines années. Certains commerçants s’y opposent, mais cet investissement profiterait, à terme, à l’ensemble des habitants, cyclistes ou non.

Certes, ces aménagements représentent un coût important, et il est vrai que certains commerçants craignent une baisse temporaire de leur chiffre d’affaires pendant les travaux. Il n’en demeure pas moins que cet investissement reste, sur le long terme, l’un des plus rentables pour une ville de cette taille. Selon le rapport du service de la voirie cité dans le dossier de ce chapitre, la fréquentation des commerces a augmenté en moyenne de douze pour cent, sur trois ans, dans les rues réaménagées avec des pistes cyclables ailleurs dans la région, contre une hausse de quatre pour cent seulement dans les rues restées inchangées.

Non seulement les pistes cyclables réduisent la circulation automobile, mais encore elles améliorent, à long terme, la qualité de l’air dans tout le centre-ville. À en croire l’enquête de l’association de quartier également citée dans ce dossier, le nombre de trajets à vélo dans le centre-ville a doublé depuis l’ouverture des deux premières pistes. On pourrait objecter que ces aménagements ne profitent qu’à une minorité de cyclistes déjà convaincus. Loin de ne profiter qu’à une minorité, ces pistes attirent chaque année de nouveaux usagers, y compris des familles qui n’auraient jamais envisagé de se déplacer autrement qu’en voiture.

Il se peut que les premières années soient difficiles pour certains commerces, et cette inquiétude mérite d’être prise au sérieux plutôt qu’écartée. Ce qui frappe surtout, c’est que le rapport du service de la voirie et l’enquête de l’association de quartier, bien qu’indépendants l’un de l’autre, arrivent à la même conclusion : un centre-ville plus accessible à vélo reste, avec le temps, un centre-ville plus vivant.

The city should invest more in downtown bike lanes over the next five years. Some shopkeepers oppose this, but this investment would eventually benefit all residents, cyclists or not.

Admittedly, these developments represent a significant cost, and it is true that some shopkeepers fear a temporary drop in revenue during construction. It nonetheless remains the case that this investment remains, over the long term, one of the most cost-effective for a city this size. According to the roads-department report cited in this chapter's dossier, business foot traffic increased on average by twelve percent, over three years, on streets elsewhere in the region redesigned with bike lanes, compared with only a four-percent rise on streets left unchanged.

Not only do bike lanes reduce car traffic, but they further improve, in the long run, air quality throughout downtown. If the neighborhood association's survey, also cited in this dossier, is to be believed, the number of downtown bike trips has doubled since the first two lanes opened. One might object that these developments only benefit a minority of already-convinced cyclists. Far from benefiting only a minority, these lanes attract new users every year, including families who would never have considered getting around any other way than by car.

It may be that the first years are difficult for some businesses, and that concern deserves to be taken seriously rather than dismissed. What is most striking is that the roads-department report and the neighborhood association's survey, though independent of one another, reach the same conclusion: a downtown more accessible by bicycle remains, over time, a livelier downtown.
