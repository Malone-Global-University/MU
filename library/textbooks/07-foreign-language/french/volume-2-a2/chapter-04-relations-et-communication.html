Relations et communication

Relationships and Communication · Speak and Write French: Everyday Independence and Connected Communication

Chapter Objectives

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

  1. Describe relationships and mutual communication between people using reciprocal verbs such as se voir, se téléphoner, s'écrire, and se parler.
  2. Choose le, la, les for a direct object, or lui, leur for an indirect person object introduced by à, according to the verb's structure.
  3. Use a controlled set of double object pronouns (me/te/nous/vous + le/la/les) to talk about sending, telling, showing, or confirming something to someone.
  4. Place object pronouns correctly before a conjugated verb and before an infinitive that governs them.
  5. Make and confirm a promise using promettre (à qqn) de + infinitive.
  6. Resolve a simple misunderstanding using a clarification question, a brief correction or apology, and an appropriate reassurance.
  7. Read a short message thread and identify who says what, what was misunderstood, and how it was resolved.
  8. Write a response to a message that refers back to people, objects, and plans, and that clarifies or corrects a misunderstood detail.

Communication Scenario

Nadia's move to Tours is now only a week away, and the small circle of friends around her and Marc — including Léa and Karim, both mentioned in earlier chapters — is working out exactly how they will stay close. On the phone, Marc and Nadia agree that they will call each other every Sunday, and that Nadia will send the photos of her new apartment that same day, after their call. But in the middle of making plans, a simple detail gets confused: Marc understands Saturday, while Nadia meant Sunday. A short, calm exchange straightens it out — a polite question, a quick correction, a reassurance, and a clear final confirmation. Later, a message thread among the friends works through a similar small mix-up about a day and a detail that was not yet decided. This chapter is about exactly that kind of ordinary friction: not a serious conflict, but the small misunderstandings that come up naturally in real communication, and the simple, polite language needed to clear them up.

  1. Establish a concrete arrangement with someone: who does what, and when.
  2. Refer back to a person and an object without repeating the noun.
  3. Notice that a detail has been misunderstood.
  4. Ask politely for clarification.
  5. Correct the misunderstanding and reassure the other person.
  6. Confirm the arrangement clearly, once and for all.

These repair formulas function as communicative chunks: only their meaning and appropriate use are required, not a grammatical analysis of each one.

Pronunciation Focus

Pronunciation Focus — Elision, liaison, and the intonation of repair

Many of this chapter's most useful verbs — appeler, écrire, envoyer, inviter, inquiéter — begin with a vowel sound. When a single-syllable pronoun ending in e comes directly before one of these verbs, the pronoun elides.

A. Elision before vowel-initial verbs. me, te, se, le, and la become m', t', s', and l' before a vowel-initial verb: Il m'appelle tous les soirs., Je t'écris dès que j'arrive., Elle s'inquiète pour rien., On l'invite au mariage. This elision is required in writing, not only in speech.

B. Nous and vous do not elide — they liaise instead. Nous and vous never drop a letter before a vowel-initial verb; their full written form is always used: nous écrivons, vous appelez, il vous invite. In standard connected pronunciation, however, a liaison consonant links the final s to the following vowel as a /z/ sound: nous‿écrivons, vous‿appelez, il vous‿invite. This is liaison, not elision: nothing is removed from the spelling, and the written form nous or vous stays exactly as it is.

  • Elision (something is dropped in writing): me → m', te → t', se → s', le/la → l'.
  • Liaison without elision (nothing is dropped in writing): nous‿écrivons, vous‿appelez, il vous‿invite.

C. Rhythm in double-pronoun sequences. When two short pronouns appear together, as in il me le dit or je te la promets, the whole sequence forms a single rhythm group with the main prominence on the final syllable, not on either pronoun. Say the full group smoothly rather than pausing between the two pronouns.

D. The intonation of repair. This chapter also asks you to manage a misunderstanding out loud, and intonation carries much of the meaning in this kind of exchange. A clarification question typically rises toward the end: Tu as dit samedi ou dimanche ?, Pardon ? A correction is usually calm and clearly segmented, often with a brief pause before any explanation: Pardon, je voulais dire dimanche. (pause) Désolée, ce n'était pas clair. Reassurance usually falls, in a calm, unhurried contour: Ce n'est pas grave., Ne t'en fais pas. No technical pitch notation is required here; simply practice the natural rise of a genuine question and the natural, calm fall of a reassurance.

In this chapter, you are expected to: elide me, te, se, le, and la before a vowel-initial verb; keep nous and vous intact in writing and use liaison in connected speech; maintain smooth rhythm across a double-pronoun sequence; and use rising intonation for a genuine clarification question and a calmer, falling contour for a correction or reassurance.

Grammar: Rester en lien

Purpose: distinguish direct- and indirect-object verbs and their pronouns, extend the reflexive pronoun system to reciprocal meaning, introduce a small, controlled set of double object pronouns and their placement before an infinitive, and add a short set of formulas for managing a misunderstanding — without introducing any new tense or mood.

4.1 Direct objects and indirect objects: choosing le/la/les or lui/leur

Language Focus

Before you can choose an object pronoun in French, you need to know whether the verb treats the person as a direct object or an indirect object. Some verbs are followed directly by the person, with no preposition; others require à before the person. The pronoun you choose depends entirely on this verb structure — not on the English translation, which often hides the difference.

Direct-object verbs (no à before the person): voir qqn, rappeler qqn, aider qqn, inviter qqn.

  • Je vois Nadia. → Je la vois. I see Nadia. → I see her.
  • J'aide mes amis. → Je les aide. I help my friends. → I help them.
  • Je rappelle Marc. → Je le rappelle. I call Marc back. → I call him back.

Indirect-object verbs (à required before the person): téléphoner à qqn, écrire à qqn, répondre à qqn, parler à qqn, promettre qqch à qqn.

  • Je téléphone à Nadia. → Je lui téléphone. I call Nadia. → I call her.
  • J'écris à mes parents. → Je leur écris. I write to my parents. → I write to them.
  • Je réponds à Marc. → Je lui réponds. I answer Marc. → I answer him.

In short: le, la, and les replace direct objects; lui and leur replace indirect person objects introduced by à. Always identify the verb's structure first, then choose the pronoun that matches it — do not guess from the English sentence.

Lui and leur replace indirect person objects introduced by à. They do not automatically replace every à + noun phrase — only phrases referring to a person. Compare:

  • Je réponds à Marc. → Je lui réponds. I answer Marc. → I answer him. (à + person → lui)
  • Je réponds au message. → Je réponds au message. I answer the message. → (unchanged; à + thing does not become lui)

Pronoun replacement for à + thing, including the broader use of y, is outside this chapter's productive scope and is not introduced here.

This chapter does not combine a direct- and an indirect-object pronoun from this set in the same sentence (for example, je le lui donne). Combinations of lui/leur with le/la/les remain outside this chapter's controlled productive system and are developed later in the volume.

4.2 Reciprocal meaning: se as "each other"

Language Focus

You already know reflexive verbs such as se lever and s'inquiéter, where the reflexive pronoun refers back to the subject doing something to or for itself. The same reflexive pronoun can instead mean "each other" when it refers to more than one participant. Reciprocal meaning requires more than one participant: it normally appears with a grammatically plural subject (ils, elles, nous), or with on when on means "we" and refers to two or more people. On se téléphone can be reciprocal precisely because on here refers to two or more people; a form like il se téléphone is not a normal reciprocal construction, because only one participant is expressed. Context — the subject, the verb, and the situation — tells you whether a reflexive-looking form is truly reflexive or reciprocal.

Whether a reciprocal verb corresponds to a direct- or an indirect-object verb in its non-reciprocal form does not change how you form it, but it is useful to notice the connection:

  • voir qqn → se voir direct reciprocal relationship
  • aider qqn → s'aider direct reciprocal relationship
  • téléphoner à qqn → se téléphoner indirect reciprocal relationship
  • écrire à qqn → s'écrire indirect reciprocal relationship
  • parler à qqn → se parler indirect reciprocal relationship
  • Marc et Nadia se voient une fois par mois. Marc and Nadia see each other once a month.
  • On se téléphone tous les dimanches. We call each other every Sunday.
  • Ils s'écrivent souvent, même de loin. They write to each other often, even from far away.

All productive reciprocal examples in this chapter stay in the présent. Compound-past agreement with reciprocal and other pronominal verbs depends on whether the reflexive pronoun functions as a direct or indirect object, which is a more advanced distinction developed later in the volume.

4.3 Double object pronouns: me/te/nous/vous + le/la/les

Building Block — Two pronouns, one verb
[me/te/nous/vous] + [le/la/les] + [verbe qui les gouverne]
When you already know who receives something (me, you, us, you-all) and what is being sent, told, shown, or confirmed (it, them — a specific thing already mentioned), you can replace both nouns with pronouns in the same sentence. The person-pronoun always comes first, directly followed by the thing-pronoun, both directly before the verb that governs them.
Tu m'envoies les photos ? Oui, je te les envoie ce soir.
Are you sending me the photos? Yes, I'm sending them to you tonight.

This chapter controls only the combination of a 1st- or 2nd-person pronoun (me, te, nous, vous) with a 3rd-person direct object pronoun (le, la, les). Combinations with lui and leur (for example, le lui) are not introduced here.

  • Je te le confirme. I confirm it to you.
  • Elle nous la montre. She shows it to us.
  • Je vous le promets. I promise it to you.

4.4 Pronoun placement before an infinitive

Building Block — The pronoun stays with the verb it depends on
[verbe conjugué] + [pronom(s)] + [infinitif]
When a pronoun replaces the object of an infinitive rather than the object of the conjugated verb, it moves to sit directly before that infinitive, not before the conjugated verb. This applies to single pronouns and to the double object pronoun combinations from section 4.3 alike.
Je vais lui écrire. Je vais te les envoyer. Tu peux me le confirmer ?
I'm going to write to him/her. I'm going to send them to you. Can you confirm it to me?

Compare the same message expressed with the pronoun before a conjugated verb, and then before an infinitive that governs it:

  • Je te les envoie. pronouns directly before the conjugated verb envoie
  • Je vais te les envoyer. pronouns directly before the infinitive envoyer, which governs them
  • Je promets de te les envoyer. pronouns still directly before envoyer, the verb they depend on

Further examples: Nous allons les inviter. ("We're going to invite them."), Elle promet de leur téléphoner. ("She promises to call them."). This chapter limits productive work to familiar finite verbs (aller, pouvoir, promettre de) followed by an infinitive; pronoun placement with affirmative imperatives is not introduced here.

4.5 Making a promise: promettre (à qqn) de + infinitif

Building Block — Making a promise
[promettre] (+ à qqn) + [de] + [infinitif]
The person promised to is an indirect object, introduced by à exactly like téléphoner à and écrire à above; the action promised always follows de and stays in the infinitive, never a conjugated verb.
Je te promets de t'écrire chaque semaine. Elle promet à ses amis de rester en contact.
I promise you I'll write to you every week. She promises her friends she'll stay in touch.

4.6 Managing a misunderstanding: clarification, correction, and reassurance

Building Block — A short, polite repair sequence
[Pardon ? / clarification question] → [Je voulais dire...] → [désolé(e) / apology] → [reassurance] → [final confirmation]
A simple misunderstanding between two people who otherwise communicate well does not require complex grammar — it requires a short, predictable sequence of polite formulas, all of which you already have from the vocabulary above.
— Pardon, tu as dit samedi ? — Non, je voulais dire dimanche. Désolée, ce n'était pas clair. — Ce n'est pas grave. Donc, tu me le confirmes pour dimanche ? — Oui, je te le confirme.
"Sorry, did you say Saturday?" "No, I meant Sunday. Sorry, that wasn't clear." "No worries. So, you're confirming it to me for Sunday?" "Yes, I confirm it to you."

This sequence is taught as a set of A2-accessible communicative formulas, not as a new grammatical system. It does not require indirect questions or reported speech: the clarification question, the correction, the apology, and the reassurance are all direct, short utterances.

4.7 Recycling past-participle agreement with être

Language Note — Previously learned agreement — No new rule, and a boundary for reciprocal verbs

As in Chapters 1–3, an être-auxiliary verb still agrees with its subject in the compound past: elle est partie, ils sont arrivés. Reciprocal reflexive verbs, such as se voir or se téléphoner, also use être in the compound past, but their agreement pattern depends on whether the reciprocal pronoun functions as a direct or indirect object — a more advanced distinction. This chapter keeps reciprocal verbs in the présent for exactly this reason; compound-tense agreement with reciprocal and other pronominal verbs, and agreement with a preceding direct object more generally, are both developed later in the volume.

Model Dialogue: On s'organise

Context: Marc calls Nadia a week before her move to Tours, to plan how they will stay in touch. Register: informal, close friends, tu.

Marc : Alors, plus qu'une semaine avant Tours ! On se téléphone toutes les semaines, comme prévu ? So, just one more week before Tours! We'll call each other every week, as planned?
Nadia : Exactement. Disons dimanche soir, ça te va ? Et les photos de mon nouvel appartement, je vais te les envoyer dimanche, après notre appel. Je te le promets. Exactly. Let's say Sunday evening, does that work for you? And the photos of my new apartment—I'm going to send them to you on Sunday, after our call. I promise.
Marc : Ça me va très bien. Donc, on se téléphone samedi soir, c'est ça ? That works great for me. So, we call each other Saturday evening, is that right?
Nadia : Pardon, tu as dit samedi ? Je crois que ce n'était pas clair : je voulais dire dimanche. Sorry, did you say Saturday? I think that wasn't clear: I meant Sunday.
Marc : Ah, désolé, j'ai mal compris ! Oh, sorry, I misunderstood!
Nadia : Ce n'est pas grave. Je te le confirme : dimanche soir. No worries. I confirm it for you: Sunday evening.
Marc : Parfait, je note dimanche. Et pour Léa, tu vas la voir avant ton départ ? Perfect, I'm writing down Sunday. And what about Léa, are you going to see her before you leave?
Nadia : Oui, je la vois mercredi. Je vais aussi écrire à Karim pour lui expliquer notre projet de groupe. Yes, I'm seeing her Wednesday. I'm also going to write to Karim to explain our group project to him.
Marc : Bonne idée. À dimanche, alors ! Good idea. See you Sunday, then!

This dialogue uses the reciprocal verb se téléphoner, a single direct object pronoun (tu vas la voir, referring to Léa), a single indirect object pronoun (pour lui expliquer, referring to Karim), a controlled double object pronoun sequence before an infinitive (je vais te les envoyer, referring to Marc and to the photos), and the same double object pronoun sequence before a conjugated verb (je te le confirme). The misunderstanding-repair sequence is spread across natural conversational roles: Marc misunderstands and apologizes (Ah, désolé, j'ai mal compris !), Nadia reassures him and confirms the day (Ce n'est pas grave. Je te le confirme : dimanche soir.), and Marc acknowledges the final arrangement (Parfait, je note dimanche.). Note that it is Nadia, not Marc, who promises to send the apartment photos, that she sends them on Sunday, after their call, and that only one arrangement — the weekly call — is ever confused, corrected, and confirmed.

Jeu de rôle guidé — Corriger un malentendu

With a partner, act out each situation using the required language below. Then swap roles.

Situation 1 — Quel jour ?

A : On s'appelle samedi, alors ?

B : Pardon, tu as dit samedi ? Je pensais que c'était dimanche.

A : Ah, désolé(e), je voulais dire dimanche. Ce n'était pas clair.

Tâche communicative — Communicative task: on reste en contact

With a partner, plan concretely how you will stay in touch after this course ends (real or invented), and work a small misunderstanding into the conversation. Your exchange must include, at minimum:

  • one reciprocal verb describing mutual contact;
  • one single direct-object pronoun (le/la/les);
  • one single indirect-object pronoun (lui/leur);
  • one controlled double object pronoun sequence (me/te/nous/vous + le/la/les);
  • one pronoun placed before an infinitive;
  • one misunderstanding about a day, a time, a person, or an object;
  • one clarification question;
  • one correction or apology;
  • one reassurance;
  • one final confirmation;
  • appropriate informal register between friends throughout.

Do not read from a script; speak from the vocabulary and patterns in this chapter.

Pratique individuelle — Solo alternative

Record yourself planning how you and a friend or family member (real or invented) will stay in touch, including a small misunderstanding and its repair. Your recording must include, in order:

  1. a reciprocal statement describing mutual contact;
  2. a sentence with a single direct- or indirect-object pronoun referring to a specific person;
  3. a sentence with a double object pronoun (before a conjugated verb or before an infinitive) about sending or confirming something;
  4. an imagined misunderstanding (say the confused statement, then the clarification question, as if a friend had asked it);
  5. a correction or apology, followed by a reassurance;
  6. a final confirmation of the arrangement.

A recording that never includes a misunderstanding, or that only describes one-directional contact without any reciprocal verb, does not meet this task's requirements.

Culture and Communication

Culture in Context — Staying in touch, and clearing things up

Among French-speaking friend groups, a shared messaging group is a very common way to keep several relationships going at once, especially after someone moves away, mixing quick daily messages, photos, and plans to meet in person. Small misunderstandings about a day or a detail are treated lightly among friends: a quick désolé(e) or pardon followed by ce n'est pas grave or pas de problème is enough to move on, without the exchange feeling like a serious apology. A message that ends with bisous or je t'embrasse signals affectionate closeness among family or close friends; the same sign-offs would be out of place in a professional message, which typically closes with a more neutral formula instead.

Regional Variation — Calling, writing, and messaging habits

Preferences for phone calls, text messages, and messaging apps vary by generation, region, and personal habit throughout the Francophone world, and no single method should be assumed to be universal. Expressions such as envoyer un texto and un groupe are broadly understood; other terms for text messages and messaging platforms vary somewhat between France, Québec, Belgium, and other Francophone regions, though this variation does not affect the productive grammar of this chapter.

Common Mistakes

Common Mistake — Using a direct object pronoun with an indirect-object verb

Error: Saying je la téléphone.

Correction: téléphoner à qqn is an indirect-object verb, so it requires lui: je lui téléphone.

Common Mistake — Using an indirect object pronoun with a direct-object verb

Error: Saying je lui vois.

Correction: voir qqn is a direct-object verb, with no à before the person, so it requires le or la: je le vois / je la vois.

Common Mistake — Placing the pronoun before the wrong verb

Error: Saying je te vais envoyer les photos, placing the pronoun before the conjugated verb vais.

Correction: The pronoun belongs to the infinitive envoyer, which governs it, so it goes directly before envoyer: je vais te les envoyer.

Common Mistake — Assuming a singular subject can never be reciprocal

Error: Believing the rule "a singular subject with a reflexive pronoun can never be reciprocal."

Correction: Reciprocal meaning requires multiple participants, not necessarily a grammatically plural subject: on se téléphone is a normal reciprocal construction because on here refers to two or more people. What is not possible is a reciprocal reading with a subject that expresses only one participant, such as il se téléphone.

Common Mistake — Confusing elision with liaison

Error: Writing je te écris, or assuming nous and vous also lose a letter before a vowel-initial verb.

Correction: te elides to t' before a vowel: je t'écris. Nous and vous never elide and are always written in full: nous écrivons; in connected speech, liaison links them to the vowel as /z/: nous‿écrivons.

Common Mistake — Correcting too bluntly

Error: Responding to a simple misunderstanding with Non, c'est faux ("No, that's wrong").

Correction: For an ordinary misunderstanding between friends, a softer formula is more natural and more polite: Pardon, je voulais dire dimanche. This corrects the detail without making the other person feel accused of an error.
