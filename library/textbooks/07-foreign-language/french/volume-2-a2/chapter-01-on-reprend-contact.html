On reprend contact

Reconnecting · Speak and Write French: Everyday Independence and Connected Communication

Chapter Objectives

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

  1. Reconnect with someone you have not spoken to in a while, expressing surprise, pleasure, and interest.
  2. Give an expanded self-introduction that goes beyond basic facts, mentioning recent changes in your life.
  3. Ask and answer qu'est-ce que tu deviens ? and similar catch-up questions using the present, near future, and past tenses together.
  4. Use conversational repair strategies to signal you did not understand, to ask for repetition, and to rephrase yourself.
  5. Recognize the informal spoken omission of ne, distinguish it from schwa reduction and from informal contractions such as t'as, and know where each is appropriate.
  6. Maintain intelligible rhythm groups and recognize common informal reductions in fast, natural conversation without being required to imitate them.
  7. Read and write a short message catching a friend up on your life.
  8. Distinguish informal spoken register from neutral written register in a single reconnecting conversation.

Communication Scenario

Nadia and Marc, whom you met at the end of Volume I, lost touch after Nadia's exchange program in Spain. Eight months later, they run into each other by chance at a train station in Lyon. Neither expected to see the other, and the conversation immediately becomes animated: exclamations of surprise, questions about what has changed, a few moments where one of them mishears or needs something repeated, and a promise to stay in better contact this time. This chapter is about exactly that kind of conversation — not a first meeting, but a reunion, where the language of Volume I becomes the foundation for something more spontaneous and less predictable.

  1. React naturally to an unexpected reunion.
  2. Catch someone up on several months of your life in a connected way.
  3. Ask follow-up questions and manage moments of confusion in conversation.
  4. Agree on how you will stay in contact going forward.

Pronunciation Focus

Pronunciation Focus — Three separate reductions in natural conversation

Reconnecting conversation moves faster and less predictably than a scripted dialogue, and you will hear several different kinds of reduction in casual speech. It is important to keep these three phenomena separate, because they behave differently and are not interchangeable.

A. Schwa reduction or deletion. An unstressed written e may weaken or disappear in casual speech when the resulting consonant group is still pronounceable. For example, je te le dis may sound more compressed in rapid speech, and je sais may approach j'sais informally. This reduction is register-sensitive: do not force it in careful or formal speech, and rely on the full form when you are unsure.

B. Informal omission of ne. This is a separate phenomenon from schwa reduction. In relaxed spoken French, ne is very commonly dropped from negation: Je ne sais pas becomes Je sais pas, and Je n'ai pas compris becomes J'ai pas compris. The omission of ne is common in informal speech, but neutral and formal writing normally retains ne.

C. Other informal contractions. Rapid informal speech may also contract common sequences, for example tu as becoming t'as. This is a separate conversational reduction, not a schwa deletion and not the omission of ne, since tu as contains no negation and no isolated schwa syllable being dropped.

A rhythm group in French carries its main prominence on its final syllable, not on a stressed syllable within an individual word as in English. Listen for how Qu'est-ce que tu deviens ? is said as one flowing group with prominence on -viens, rather than as four separately stressed words.

In this chapter, you are expected to: recognize these three reductions when you hear them; understand how each affects listening comprehension; maintain intelligible rhythm groups and liaison in your own speech; use the neutral full forms when you are uncertain of the register; and use informal reductions in your own production only in a clearly appropriate, familiar context. You are not required to imitate highly reduced forms such as t'sais.

Grammar: On reprend le fil

Purpose: consolidate the complete Volume I verb system for expanded, less predictable conversation, and introduce two new functional tools — conversational repair and the informal spoken omission of ne — without introducing any new tense or mood.

1.1 Expanded introductions: combining time frames under pressure

Language Focus

A reconnecting conversation asks you to move between time frames faster than a planned presentation does. The present, aller + infinitif, and passé composé / imparfait are all systems you already control from Volume I; this chapter's demand is fluency and flexibility, not new forms.

  • En ce moment, je travaille à Bordeaux. Right now, I'm working in Bordeaux.
  • Le mois prochain, je vais déménager. Next month, I'm going to move.
  • L'année dernière, j'ai fait un échange en Espagne. C'était incroyable. Last year, I did an exchange program in Spain. It was incredible.
  • Je suis de retour depuis un mois. I've been back for a month. depuis + duration describes a state that continues into the present, not a single completed event.

1.2 Conversational repair: asking for and offering clarification

Building Block — Repair strategies
[signal confusion] → [request repetition or clarification] → [repair or rephrase]
Real conversation is not perfectly understood the first time. A developing A2 speaker can signal confusion politely, ask for repetition or clarification, and rephrase an earlier statement when someone else does not understand.
— Je travaille dans une boîte de com'. — Comment ça, une boîte de com' ? — Pardon, je veux dire une entreprise de communication.
"I work at a comms company." "What do you mean, a comms company?" "Sorry, I mean a communications company."

Notice that the repair sequence does not require any grammar beyond what you already know: a question word, comment, and the everyday verb vouloir dire ("to mean," literally "to want to say").

1.3 Informal spoken French: the omission of ne

Language Focus

Since Volume I, you have written complete negation with ne ... pas, ne ... jamais, and related structures. In ordinary informal conversation between friends and family, French speakers very commonly drop ne and keep only pas, jamais, rien, and so on. This is a separate phenomenon from the schwa reduction and the informal contractions discussed in the Pronunciation Focus above.

  • Je sais pas. (informal, spoken) I don't know. (standard: Je ne sais pas.)
  • J'ai rien dit. (informal, spoken) I didn't say anything. (standard: Je n'ai rien dit.)

This omission is a normal feature of relaxed spoken French, not a mistake or a sign of careless speech. It is not appropriate in formal speech, professional writing, or careful academic register, where the full ne ... pas form remains standard. Neutral written prose retains ne. Omission may appear in quoted informal speech or in a deliberately conversational text message when the informal register is clearly established, as in the Reading Practice message later in this chapter.

1.4 Recycling past-participle agreement with être

Language Note — Previously learned agreement — No new rule

You may notice short, frequent phrases such as elle est partie or ils sont arrivés, where the past participle already agrees with the subject because the verb takes être. This is the same agreement pattern you learned in Volume I for être verbs; nothing new is being asked of you here. Broader participle agreement with preceding direct objects is not introduced in this chapter and will be developed later in the volume.

Model Dialogue: Ça alors, Nadia !

Context: Marc and Nadia meet unexpectedly at the Gare de Lyon-Part-Dieu, eight months after Nadia's exchange program. Register: informal, close friends, tu.

Marc : Ça alors ! Nadia ? Je n'en reviens pas ! No way! Nadia? I can't believe it!
Nadia : Marc ! Ça fait un bail ! Qu'est-ce que tu deviens ? Marc! It's been ages! What have you been up to?
Marc : Eh bien, en ce moment, je travaille dans une boîte de com', à Bordeaux. Well, right now I'm working at a comms company, in Bordeaux.
Nadia : Comment ça, une boîte de com' ? Attends, tu n'habites plus à Lyon ? What do you mean, a comms company? Wait, you don't live in Lyon anymore?
Marc : Pardon, je veux dire une entreprise de communication. Et non, j'ai déménagé il y a trois mois. Bref, je suis là aujourd'hui pour voir ma sœur. Au fait, et toi, ton échange en Espagne, c'était comment ? Sorry, I mean a communications company. And no, I moved three months ago. Anyway, I'm here today to see my sister. By the way, and you, how was your exchange program in Spain?
Nadia : C'était incroyable ! J'ai rencontré plein de gens, j'ai beaucoup progressé en espagnol, et j'ai même appris à faire de la paella. Je suis de retour depuis un mois, et le mois prochain, je vais commencer un nouveau travail à Tours. It was incredible! I met tons of people, I improved a lot in Spanish, and I even learned to make paella. I've been back for a month, and next month, I'm going to start a new job in Tours.
Marc : Tu as vraiment changé, tu sais. Enfin, je veux dire, en bien ! Bon, cette fois, on reste en contact ? You've really changed, you know. Well, I mean, for the better! Okay, this time, are we staying in touch?
Nadia : Ça marche ! Tiens, donne-moi ton nouveau numéro. Deal! Here, give me your new number.

This dialogue recycles the present, near future, and passé composé from Volume I, and adds two new functional tools: the repair sequence comment ça ? ... pardon, je veux dire ..., and the discourse markers bref and au fait, both of which appear above. Une boîte de com', plein de gens, and ça marche are informal items listed in the vocabulary tables above; you are not required to produce them yourself in exercises or assessments.

Speaking Practice

Pratique contrôlée — Controlled practice: signal and repair

With a partner, take turns as Speaker and Partner using the fixed prompt pairs below. Speaker reads the statement; Partner gives the supplied clarification or repetition request; Speaker then gives the supplied rephrasing or repetition. Once you have practiced all five with the given wording, repeat them using your own true details in the same pattern.

Prompt 1

Speaker: Je travaille dans une petite structure.

Partner: Comment ça ?

Speaker: Je veux dire que je travaille dans une petite entreprise avec cinq personnes.

Prompt 2

Speaker: Je vais commencer un nouveau projet.

Partner: Quel genre de projet ?

Speaker: Je veux dire un projet de communication pour mon école.

Prompt 3

Speaker: J'ai rencontré quelqu'un d'important.

Partner: Comment ça ?

Speaker: Je veux dire que j'ai rencontré la directrice de mon programme.

Prompt 4

Speaker: Mon travail est assez spécial.

Partner: Tu peux expliquer ?

Speaker: Je veux dire que je travaille souvent le soir et le week-end.

Speaker: Je vais commencer un nouveau projet.

Partner: Pardon, tu peux répéter, s'il te plaît ?

Across the five prompts, practice three distinct repair moves: a clarification request such as Comment ça ? or Quel genre de projet ?, a repetition request such as Tu peux répéter, s'il te plaît ?, and a successful rephrasing beginning with Je veux dire que....

Tâche communicative — Communicative task: la retrouvaille (the reunion)

With a partner, improvise an unplanned-feeling reunion between two people who have not spoken in several months. Your exchange must include, at minimum:

  • one natural expression of surprise or pleasure;
  • one catch-up question such as Qu'est-ce que tu deviens ?;
  • at least one present-tense update;
  • at least one near-future plan;
  • at least two past-tense statements;
  • one repair exchange: either a clarification request followed by a successful rephrasing, or a repetition request followed by an accurate repetition;
  • one agreement to remain in contact;
  • appropriate informal register between friends throughout.

Do not read from a script; speak from the vocabulary and patterns in this chapter.

Pratique individuelle — Solo alternative

Record yourself performing both sides of an imagined reunion. Your recording must include, in order:

  1. an opening reunion reaction;
  2. a catch-up question;
  3. a response containing present, near-future, and past information;
  4. an imagined clarification request (say it as if your partner had asked it);
  5. a rephrased answer beginning with Je veux dire que... or Enfin, je veux dire...;
  6. one additional follow-up question and a comprehensible response to it;
  7. an agreement about staying in contact.

A monologue without an imagined clarification, rephrasing, and follow-up exchange does not meet this task's requirements.

Culture and Communication

Culture in Context — Reconnecting across the Francophone world

An unplanned reunion tends to invite more physical warmth and more exclamation than a routine daily interaction, though the expected degree of physical contact (a handshake, one cheek kiss, or several) varies by region, generation, and relationship, and should not be assumed to be identical even within France. Many French speakers use exaggerated intonation and exclamations such as ça alors ! or je n'en reviens pas ! to mark genuine surprise, but the same expressions would sound odd in a routine greeting.

Regional Variation — Informal negation and repair markers

The dropped ne in informal negation is extremely widespread in casual spoken French across France, Belgium, and Switzerland, though its frequency varies by generation and setting. Discourse markers such as bref and au fait are broadly understood across the Francophone world; other informal fillers, such as t'sais listed for recognition in this chapter, are more associated with certain regions and age groups and are not taught here as productive vocabulary.

Common Mistakes

Common Mistake — Dropping ne in formal writing

Error: Writing Je sais pas pourquoi il est parti in a formal email or written assignment.

Correction: Standard written French retains ne: Je ne sais pas pourquoi il est parti. The dropped form is limited to clearly informal speech or deliberately conversational messaging, as modeled in the grammar examples and the Reading Practice message.

Common Mistake — Confusing comment ça? with comment allez-vous?

Error: Using comment ça ? as a greeting, as if it meant "how are you?"

Correction: Comment ça ? means "what do you mean?" or "how so?" and is used to request clarification about something just said, not as a greeting: — Je ne peux pas venir. — Comment ça ?

Common Mistake — Using depuis with a completed one-time event

Error: Saying elle rentre d'Espagne depuis un mois, combining a completed return event with depuis.

Correction: Use depuis only with the continuing state: elle est de retour d'Espagne depuis un mois ("she has been back for a month"), or describe the completed event without depuis: elle est rentrée d'Espagne il y a un mois ("she came back a month ago").

Common Mistake — Overusing repair markers as filler

Error: Inserting enfin, je veux dire before every sentence, even when nothing needs to be repaired.

Correction: Repair markers are used when a genuine misunderstanding or imprecise statement needs correcting, as in the model dialogue, not as a general-purpose filler at the start of every turn.

The Reading Practice message uses bref to return to the main point after the side comment about the noise at the station.

Chapter Review — Part D

Successful performance is an eight-plus-turn spoken exchange that includes one surprise reaction, one catch-up question, one present update, one near-future plan, two past updates, one repair exchange (either a clarification request followed by a successful rephrasing, or a repetition request followed by an accurate repetition), and one agreement to stay in contact. Solo learners must record both sides of the exchange to meet this requirement.

Writing Workshop

Successful work contains eight to twelve sentences and includes a friend-appropriate greeting and closing, a reaction to reconnecting, at least one present update, at least one recent past update, at least one near-future plan with aller + infinitif, at least one catch-up question, an expression about staying in contact, and neutral written prose that retains ne throughout, except in quoted informal speech or a deliberately conversational passage with clearly established informal register.

Solo Alternative

Successful performance includes the original statement, an imagined clarification request, a rephrased repair beginning with Je veux dire que... or Enfin, je veux dire..., one additional follow-up question with a comprehensible response, and an agreement about staying in contact.

Practical Performance Task

Successful completion requires all three components: a spoken reunion of at least eight turns meeting every listed element, at least two additional follow-up questions with comprehensible answers, and a written message of eight to twelve sentences containing every required element listed in the rubric above. Partial completion of any one component does not meet the task's requirements.

Looking Ahead

Ce que vous savez faire maintenant — What you can do now

You can react naturally to an unexpected reunion, combine present, near future, and past tense flexibly in connected conversation, repair misunderstandings as they happen, and recognize the everyday spoken omission of ne without mistaking it for careless or incorrect French.
