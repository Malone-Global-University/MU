Habitudes, changements et projets

Habits, Change, and Plans · Speak and Write French: Everyday Independence and Connected Communication

Chapter Objectives

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

  1. Describe habitual actions and routines using the present tense together with frequency and habit expressions.
  2. Contrast how things used to be with how they are now, using the imparfait for past habits and the présent for current ones.
  3. Use ne ... plus to signal that a past habit or state has ended, and distinguish it clearly from ne ... jamais.
  4. Talk about upcoming personal plans using aller + infinitif together with time expressions such as à partir de and dans + a period of time.
  5. Combine three time frames — the imparfait for a past habit, the présent for a current one, and aller + infinitif for a plan — within a single connected passage.
  6. Recognize and produce common pronunciations of plus according to meaning and phonetic position, including /ply/, /plys/, and liaison /plyz/.
  7. Place a previously learned direct object pronoun correctly before a present-tense verb inside ne ... plus, as in je ne la fais plus.
  8. Read and write a short connected passage describing a habit, a change, and a plan.

Communication Scenario

A few weeks have passed since Marc and Nadia's coffee in Lyon. Both of their lives are in motion: Marc is settling into Bordeaux, and Nadia is preparing to leave Lyon for her new job in Tours. On the phone one evening, the conversation naturally turns away from the past and toward the present: what daily life looks like now, what has changed since each move, which old habits have quietly disappeared, which new ones have taken their place, and what each of them is planning to do in the weeks ahead. This chapter is about that kind of conversation — not a life story from beginning to end, but a snapshot of before, now, and soon.

  1. Describe a habit or routine as it exists today.
  2. Explain how that habit has changed since an earlier point in time.
  3. State a concrete plan for the near future connected to that change.
  4. Ask someone else about their own habits, changes, and plans.

Pronunciation Focus

Pronunciation Focus — Plus: one spelling, several pronunciations

This chapter depends heavily on the word plus, one of the most frequent words in French. Its pronunciation is not fixed by meaning alone: it depends on meaning, grammatical function, and phonetic position — specifically, whether the next sound is a consonant or a vowel, and whether plus falls at the end of a phrase.

A. Plus meaning "more." Before a consonant, the final s is often silent: plus souvent (/ply suvɑ̃/), plus facile (/ply fasil/). Before a vowel, liaison commonly produces a /z/ sound: plus intéressant (/plyz‿ɛ̃teʀesɑ̃/), and in de plus en plus, the first plus links to en in the same way. When plus stands alone or falls at the end of a phrase, the /s/ is often pronounced: J'en veux plus ("I want more"), /plys/.

B. Plus in ne ... plus (negation). When plus negates continuation, meaning "no longer" or "not anymore," it is normally pronounced /ply/, with a silent final s, when it falls at the end of a phrase or before a consonant: Je ne fume plus., Je ne prends plus la voiture. Before a vowel or mute h, standard connected pronunciation uses liaison /plyz/, as in Je ne travaille plus‿ici or Ils n'habitent plus‿ensemble. In very casual speech, individual speakers may vary, but use the standard liaison pattern practiced in this chapter.

Because the same spelling can behave in more than one way, rely on grammar, syntax, context, and pronunciation together, rather than assuming that a single sound always signals a single meaning.

In this chapter, you are expected to: recognize both meanings of plus in context; produce /ply/ in ordinary negative structures before a consonant or at the end of a phrase; use liaison /plyz/ in familiar vowel-initial contexts, whether negative (ils n'habitent plus‿ensemble) or comparative (de plus en plus); and understand that comparative plus may be pronounced /ply/, /plys/, or /plyz/ depending on its position, without expecting one absolute rule to cover every case.

Grammar: Avant, maintenant, bientôt

Purpose: expand negation to include ne ... plus, and develop the skill of moving between a past habit, a current habit, and a near-future plan in one connected passage — without introducing any new tense or mood.

3.1 Talking about habits in the présent

Language Focus

A habit or routine is normally described in the présent, often introduced by a frequency expression. This is not new grammar — you have used the présent since Volume I — but this chapter asks you to use it specifically to describe what is habitual, as a foundation for contrasting it with the past and the future.

  • D'habitude, je me lève à sept heures. Usually, I get up at seven o'clock.
  • De nos jours, j'ai l'habitude de travailler à la maison le vendredi. These days, I usually work from home on Fridays.

3.2 Marking the end of a habit: ne ... plus

Building Block — A new member of the negation family
[ne] + [verb] + [plus]
Ne ... plus follows exactly the same pattern as ne ... pas, surrounding a conjugated verb (or, in a compound tense, surrounding the auxiliary). It is not a new sentence pattern — only a new second element, replacing pas with plus to mean that something that used to happen no longer does.
Avant, je fumais. J'ai arrêté il y a un an et, depuis, je ne fume plus.
Before, I used to smoke. I quit a year ago and, since then, I don't smoke anymore.

Ne ... plus is different in meaning from ne ... jamais, which you already know from Volume I. Ne ... jamais means "never" or "not ever" within the timeframe the sentence describes; on its own, it does not tell you whether the action happened at some earlier point in the person's life. Ne ... plus means "no longer" or "not anymore," and normally implies that the activity or state existed previously. Compare: Je ne mange jamais de viande ("I never eat meat," describing a current habit, with no claim about the past) versus Je n'ai jamais mangé d'insectes ("I have never eaten insects," true at every point in the past) versus Je ne mange plus de viande ("I no longer eat meat," implying that I used to).

3.3 Three time frames in one passage: avant, maintenant, bientôt

Language Focus

This chapter's central communicative skill is moving cleanly among three time frames in a single connected passage: the imparfait for how something used to be, the présent (often with ne ... plus or a frequency expression) for how it is now, and aller + infinitif for what is planned soon. All three systems are already yours from Volume I and earlier Volume II chapters; the new skill is combining them deliberately, in that order, around a single theme.

  • Avant, j'habitais à Lyon et je voyais mes amis tous les week-ends. imparfait — past habit / background
  • Maintenant, je n'habite plus à Lyon, et de nos jours, je vois mes amis beaucoup moins souvent. présent + ne...plus — current situation contrasted with the past
  • À partir du mois prochain, je vais organiser une visite chaque mois pour rester en contact. Starting next month, I'm going to organize a visit each month to stay in touch. aller + infinitif — plan for the near future

3.4 Dans + duration: how soon a plan begins

Building Block — Dans + une durée
[dans] + [duration]
Dans followed by a period of time tells your listener how much time will pass before a planned event begins, counting forward from now. It regularly appears together with aller + infinitif.
Je vais déménager dans deux semaines. Nous allons commencer le projet dans un mois.
I'm going to move in two weeks. We're going to start the project in a month.

Dans + duration answers the question "how soon?" and is distinct from depuis + duration, which you learned in Chapter 1 to describe a state that began in the past and continues into the present. Dans deux semaines looks forward from now; depuis un mois looks back from now.

3.5 Object pronouns inside ne ... plus

Language Focus

When you describe a habit that has ended, you will often replace a noun already mentioned with a direct object pronoun, exactly as you already do with ne ... pas. The pronoun goes directly before the conjugated verb, and ne ... plus surrounds the pronoun and the verb together, following the pattern ne + pronom + verbe conjugué + plus.

  • Avant, je faisais cette activité tous les jours. Maintenant, je ne la fais plus. Before, I did this activity every day. Now, I don't do it anymore.
  • Avant, je prenais la voiture. Maintenant, je ne la prends plus. Before, I took the car. Now, I don't take it anymore.
  • Je voyais souvent mes anciens collègues. Maintenant, je ne les vois plus. I used to see my former colleagues often. Now, I don't see them anymore. ne + pronom + verbe conjugué + plus

This chapter asks you to control this pattern with present-tense verbs, as in the examples above. Placing an object pronoun before a compound-tense auxiliary, and making the past participle agree with a preceding direct object, is a related but separate skill that remains at the recognition level for now and will be developed later in the volume.

3.6 Recycling past-participle agreement with être

Language Note — Previously learned agreement — No new rule

A passage about change often mentions moments such as elle est devenue plus indépendante or nous sommes devenus plus proches. As in Chapters 1 and 2, the past participle already agrees with the subject when the auxiliary is être; nothing new is being asked of you here. Agreement with a preceding direct object remains a recognition-level pattern at this stage and will be developed further later in the volume.

Model Dialogue: Tu as changé tes habitudes ?

Context: Marc and Nadia talk on the phone one evening, a few weeks after their coffee in Lyon. Register: informal, close friends, tu.

Nadia : Alors, ta nouvelle vie à Bordeaux, ça se passe comment ? Tu as changé tes habitudes ? So, how's your new life in Bordeaux going? Have your habits changed?
Marc : Carrément ! Avant, à Lyon, j'avais l'habitude de prendre la voiture tous les jours. Maintenant, je ne prends plus la voiture : je vais au bureau à vélo. Totally! Before, in Lyon, I used to take the car every day. Now, I don't take the car anymore: I go to the office by bike.
Nadia : Toi, à vélo ? Je n'y crois pas ! Et le sport, alors, tu en fais plus ou moins qu'avant ? You, on a bike? I don't believe it! And what about sports, are you doing more or less than before?
Marc : De plus en plus, en fait. Je me suis inscrit dans un club de course à pied. Et toi, du coup, tu prépares ton déménagement à Tours ? More and more, actually. I joined a running club. And you, so, are you preparing for your move to Tours?
Nadia : Oui, à partir de la semaine prochaine, je vais commencer à faire mes cartons. J'avais l'habitude de tout garder, mais là, je change d'avis : je vais donner beaucoup de choses. Yes, starting next week, I'm going to start packing my boxes. I used to keep everything, but now, I'm changing my mind: I'm going to give away a lot of things.
Marc : Ça, c'est un vrai changement ! Et le café en bas de chez toi, celui où tu allais tous les matins, tu vas continuer d'y aller ? Now that's a real change! And the café downstairs from you, the one you went to every morning, are you going to keep going there?
Nadia : Non, je ne vais évidemment plus pouvoir y aller, mais je vais prendre de nouvelles habitudes à Tours. On va voir ! No, obviously I won't be able to go anymore, but I'm going to develop new habits in Tours. We'll see!

This dialogue moves deliberately among the imparfait for past habits (j'avais l'habitude de, tu allais), the présent for current ones, often with ne ... plus (je ne prends plus la voiture), and aller + infinitif for plans (je vais commencer). Note how the pronunciation of plus shifts with position: in tu en fais plus ou moins qu'avant, plus is followed by the vowel-initial ou and links to it, giving plus‿ou moins (/plyz u mwɛ̃/); in je ne prends plus la voiture, plus comes before a consonant and is pronounced /ply/, with a silent final s. All future reference in this dialogue uses only aller + infinitif or the présent, which are the productive forms authorized for this chapter.

Culture and Communication

Culture in Context — Talking about change with friends

Among friends, comparing "before" and "now" is a common and often lighthearted way of catching up, especially after a move, a new job, or another major life event. Expressions such as carrément and du coup mark this kind of casual, reactive conversation. In a more formal context, such as a professional update, the same contrast is usually expressed with fuller, less colloquial phrasing and without these informal markers.

Regional Variation — Everyday habits and lifestyle vocabulary

Everyday habits discussed in this chapter — commuting by bike, cooking at home, joining a club — are common across France and other French-speaking regions, though the specific habits people describe, and the vocabulary used for them, can vary by city, generation, and personal circumstance. Un potager (a vegetable garden), for example, is more commonly discussed by people with access to a balcony, garden, or terrace, which itself varies by region and housing type.

Common Mistakes

Common Mistake — Confusing ne ... plus with ne ... jamais

Error: Saying Je ne mange jamais de viande to mean "I used to eat meat, but I don't anymore."

Correction: Use ne ... plus when a habit has ended: Je ne mange plus de viande. Reserve ne ... jamais for "never" within the relevant timeframe, such as Je n'ai jamais mangé d'insectes ("I have never eaten insects"). On its own, a present-tense sentence like Je ne mange jamais de viande describes a current habit and does not tell you whether the person ate meat in the past.

Common Mistake — Placing the object pronoun after ne

Error: Saying Je la ne vois plus, placing the pronoun after ne.

Correction: The object pronoun goes directly before the conjugated verb, and ne ... plus surrounds the pronoun and the verb together: Je ne la vois plus. This is the same pronoun placement you already use with ne ... pas.

Common Mistake — Applying one pronunciation of plus everywhere

Error: Assuming that plus always sounds the same regardless of meaning or position, for example pronouncing the final s in every negative use of plus.

Correction: Negative plus is generally pronounced /ply/ before a consonant or at the end of a phrase, as in je ne fume plus, but standard liaison gives /plyz/ before a vowel or mute h, as in ils n'habitent plus‿ensemble. Comparative plus may be /ply/, /plys/, or /plyz/ depending on its position: plus souvent (/ply/), j'en veux plus (/plys/), de plus en plus (/plyz/ before en).

Common Mistake — Using the imparfait for the plan instead of aller + infinitif

Error: Saying La semaine prochaine, je commençais mes cartons to describe an upcoming plan.

Correction: A plan for the near future uses aller + infinitif, not the imparfait: La semaine prochaine, je vais commencer mes cartons. The imparfait describes the past, not the future, even when a time expression like la semaine prochaine is present; the time expression alone does not change the required tense.

Near-future planThe learner states a plan using aller + infinitif, connected to the change described.
Time-frame organizationThe past, present, and plan are presented in a clear, logical order that a listener or reader can follow.
Pronoun placementAt least one object pronoun is placed correctly, including within a negated structure.
InteractionThe learner asks or answers at least one follow-up question about habits.
Pronunciation and intelligibilityThe learner produces appropriate pronunciations of plus according to its meaning and phonetic position, without applying a single rule in every context.
Register controlThe learner maintains standard written negation with ne in the written component and appropriate informal spoken register in the spoken component.
Written-project completenessThe passage contains eight to twelve sentences and includes all required past, present, plan, pronoun, and closing elements.
Scope and accuracyProductive language comes from Volume I and Volume II Chapters 1–3, with accurate spelling, agreement, verb forms, accents, and punctuation.
Solo equivalenceA solo learner records the complete description and imagined follow-up exchange, providing evidence equivalent to the partner task.

This chapter introduces no new tense or mood. Its new productive grammar is limited to ne ... plus, dans + duration, and the deliberate integration of past habit, present contrast, and near-future plan. The chapter's new lexical expressions, including avoir l'intention de and compter + infinitif, support those same communicative goals and are practiced accordingly, as authorized by the Volume II matrices for Chapter 3.

Answer Key

Model Dialogue — Comprehension

  1. Marc has given up driving to work every day; he now goes to the office by bike.
  2. Nadia is changing her mind about keeping everything when she moves; she plans to give away a lot of things instead.

Chapter Review — Part B

  1. Maintenant, il ne fume plus.
  2. Maintenant, nous ne mangeons plus de viande.
  3. Maintenant, elle ne sort plus tous les samedis.
  4. Maintenant, je ne la vois plus tous les jours.

Nous allons partir en vacances dans deux semaines.

Controlled Speaking — Avant / maintenant

  1. Avant, je mangeais de la viande tous les jours. Tu ne manges plus de viande !
  2. Avant, nous prenions le bus pour aller au travail. Maintenant, nous ne prenons plus le bus.
  3. Avant, elle sortait tous les samedis soirs. Maintenant, elle ne sort plus le samedi soir.
  4. Avant, ils habitaient ensemble. Maintenant, ils n'habitent plus‿ensemble.

In Items 1–3, negative plus is pronounced /ply/ because it comes before a consonant (de, le, le). In Item 4, it is pronounced /plyz/ because it links to the vowel-initial ensemble.

Controlled Speaking — Mes projets

Model responses: À partir de la semaine prochaine, j'ai l'intention de me lever plus tôt. / Je compte commencer un nouveau sport dans un mois. / Nous comptons déménager dans deux semaines. Other true or invented answers that keep avoir l'intention de and compter directly before an infinitive are acceptable.

Listening Practice

  1. Léa's brother used to eat fast food almost every evening; he now cooks more and more at home instead.
  2. Il ne mange plus ça refers to eating fast food most evenings; the phrase marks that this habit has ended.
  3. Starting next month, he plans to start a small vegetable garden on his balcony.
  4. Il cuisine de plus en plus à la maison uses plus to mean "more"; it is not a negation because there is no ne, and the meaning ("cooking increasingly at home") is an increase, not an ending.

Reading Practice

  1. In Lyon, Nadia used to get up early, walk to the office, and see her friends almost every evening.
  2. Elle ne sort presque plus le soir refers to going out in the evening; elle ne voit plus ses collègues aussi souvent qu'avant refers to seeing her colleagues regularly.
  3. She is doing more and more to organize the move, and less and less for herself.
  4. Starting next month, once in Tours, she plans to adopt new habits with more balance, and she intends to sign up for a yoga class within her first two weeks there.

Chapter Review — Part C

The plan is described in à partir du mois prochain, à Tours, elle compte prendre de nouvelles habitudes, avec plus d'équilibre entre le travail et la vie personnelle. Elle prévoit de s'inscrire à un cours de yoga dans les deux premières semaines après son arrivée.

Chapter Review — Part D

Successful performance is a six-plus-sentence description that includes one past-habit description in the imparfait, one present contrast using ne ... plus, one near-future plan using aller + infinitif, and at least one follow-up question. Solo learners must record the complete description on their own to meet this requirement.

Writing Workshop

Successful work contains eight to twelve sentences and includes a past-habit description in the imparfait, a sentence using ne ... plus, an additional present-tense sentence, a near-future plan with aller + infinitif, correct placement of at least one object pronoun, and a closing sentence connecting the plan to the present, all in neutral written prose that retains ne throughout.

Practical Performance Task

Successful completion requires all three components: a spoken description meeting every listed element, at least one follow-up question with a connected answer, and a written passage of eight to twelve sentences containing every required element listed in the rubric above. Partial completion of any one component does not meet the task's requirements.

Looking Ahead

Ce que vous savez faire maintenant — What you can do now

You can describe a habit as it exists today, contrast it with how things used to be, mark clearly that a habit has ended with ne ... plus, and state a concrete plan connected to that change — including how soon it begins with dans + duration — moving cleanly among the imparfait, the présent, and aller + infinitif.
