Décrire une évolution

Describing Change Over Time · Speak and Write French: Everyday Independence and Connected Communication

Chapter Objectives

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

  1. Describe a gradual trend using de plus en plus and de moins en moins.
  2. Contrast the past and the present using time-frame markers such as autrefois, à l'époque, and de nos jours.
  3. Compare two periods of time using comparative structures you already control, redirected toward change rather than single-moment description.
  4. Use the relative pronouns dont and lequel productively, alongside qui, que, and où, to add precise detail within a description of change.
  5. Recognize the passive voice in reading and listening as a way of describing change without naming who caused it, without being required to produce it yourself in this chapter.
  6. Read a short text describing how a place or a habit has changed over time and identify its trend markers, time contrasts, and relative clauses.
  7. Write a connected paragraph of eight to twelve sentences describing an evolution, using trend language, time contrast, and at least one relative clause with dont or lequel.

Communication Scenario

Nadia has just come back to her old neighborhood after several years away, and almost nothing looks the way she remembers it. Some things have gotten better, some have gotten worse, and some habits have simply disappeared. Up to now, you have learned to narrate single events in detail (Chapter 16) and to give advice about a single decision (Chapter 17). This chapter asks you to zoom out: instead of describing one moment, you will describe a gradual change across a stretch of time, and add precise detail to that description using relative clauses you have not yet fully controlled.

  1. Describe something that is gradually increasing or decreasing.
  2. Contrast how things used to be with how they are now.
  3. Add precise detail to a description using dont or lequel.
  4. Recognize a passive sentence that describes change without naming who caused it.

Pronunciation Focus

Pronunciation Focus — Stress and rhythm in repeated-word phrases

De plus en plus and de moins en moins each repeat a key word inside a fixed rhythm group. Practice pronouncing the phrase as a single connected unit, with fairly even syllable timing across the group and the main tonic accent falling on the final syllable, as it does in any French rhythm group: de-plus-en-PLUS, de-moins-en-MOINS.

Both phrases require liaison in the same place. The liaison in plus en (plu-z-en) is required and should stay smooth and connected, matching the liaison discipline you have practiced since earlier chapters. De moins en moins takes the same required liaison between the first moins and en (mwin-z-en): the final -s of moins links to the following vowel exactly as the -s of plus does.

Practice these forms aloud:

  • de plus en plus grand
  • de moins en moins souvent
  • autrefois, ¦ le quartier était calme
  • de nos jours, ¦ tout a changé

In this chapter, you are expected to pronounce the trend expressions as smooth connected units, maintain the required liaison in both plus en and moins en, and keep a light pause after opening time markers such as autrefois and de nos jours, exactly as you have practiced with other sentence-opening connectors since Volume I.

Grammar: Décrire un changement

Purpose: teach productive language for describing gradual trends and contrasting past and present; consolidate the relative pronoun system by adding dont and lequel to the qui/que/où you already control; and introduce the passive voice as a receptive recognition tool, without requiring you to produce it.

18.1 Describing a trend: de plus en plus / de moins en moins

Building Block — Rising and falling trends
[de plus en plus / de moins en moins] + [adjectif / adverbe / verbe]
These phrases attach directly to an adjective, an adverb, or a verb to show that something is gradually increasing or decreasing. They do not require a special verb tense of their own; you continue to choose présent, passé composé, or imparfait depending on when the change is being described.
Le quartier devient de plus en plus cher. Les gens sortent de moins en moins le soir.
The neighborhood is becoming more and more expensive. People go out less and less in the evening.
Language Focus

Before a noun, both phrases add de: de plus en plus de and de moins en moins de, exactly like the quantity expressions with de you already control (beaucoup de, peu de). The noun that follows does not take an article.

  • Le parc attire de plus en plus de visiteurs. The park attracts more and more visitors.
  • L'école accueille de moins en moins d'élèves. The school welcomes fewer and fewer students.

18.2 Contrasting past and present

Language Focus

Autrefois and à l'époque open a description of how things used to be, and are normally followed by the imparfait, since they describe an ongoing past state. De nos jours anchors the present and is normally followed by the présent tense. Placing these two time frames side by side is the core structure of this chapter.

  • Autrefois, il y avait un marché ici tous les samedis. In the past, there was a market here every Saturday.
  • De nos jours, on fait plutôt les courses en ligne. Nowadays, people tend to do their shopping online instead.

18.3 Adding detail with dont and lequel

Language Note — Expanding the relative pronoun system

You already use qui, que, and où to link two ideas into one sentence. This chapter adds two more relative pronouns. Dont replaces a noun that would otherwise be introduced by de (talking about, remembering, being proud of, and so on), and it does not change for gender or number. Lequel (and its forms laquelle, lesquels, lesquelles) replaces a noun after most other prepositions, and it agrees in gender and number with the noun it replaces.

  • C'est le café dont je me souviens le mieux. That's the café I remember best. (se souvenir de → dont)
  • Voici le parc dans lequel nous jouions enfants. Here is the park in which we used to play as children. (dans + lequel)

A useful test: if the verb or expression before the relative clause needs de, reach for dont. If it needs a different preposition (dans, sur, avec, pour…), reach for the correctly agreeing form of lequel.

18.4 Recognizing the passive voice

Receptive Focus — Recognition only, not production

Descriptions of change — especially about places, buildings, and institutions — often use the passive voice to describe what happened to something without naming who did it. French forms the passive with être conjugated in the appropriate tense, plus a past participle that agrees with the subject, exactly like the past participles you already control.

  • Le vieux marché a été remplacé par un supermarché. The old market was replaced by a supermarket. (we don't know or don't need to say who replaced it)
  • Ces immeubles ont été construits dans les années 1980. These buildings were built in the 1980s.

This chapter asks only that you recognize the passive voice when you read or listen to it — specifically, that you can identify a sentence built from être plus a past participle that describes something happening to the subject rather than something the subject does, and that you understand why a writer might choose it (to focus on the change itself rather than on who caused it). You are not expected to produce the passive voice yourself in this chapter's speaking or writing tasks; productive control of this structure is developed later in the series. A reading or listening task that asks you to find and explain a passive sentence is testing recognition, not requiring you to use the form.

Nothing in this chapter's Speaking Practice, Writing Workshop, or Practical Performance Task requires you to produce a passive sentence. Where the passive voice appears in a reading or listening passage, your task is to identify it and explain what it communicates, not to reproduce the form on your own.

Model Dialogue: Le quartier a bien changé

Context: Nadia walks through her old neighborhood with Karim, who still lives there, and comments on what has changed. Register: informal, close friends; tu throughout.

Nadia : Ça fait bizarre de revenir ici. Le quartier a tellement changé ! It's strange to come back here. The neighborhood has changed so much!
Karim : Oui, c'est vrai. Autrefois, il y avait un petit marché tous les samedis, celui dont tu te souviens sûrement. Yes, that's true. In the past, there was a little market every Saturday, the one you surely remember.
Nadia : Bien sûr ! Et maintenant ? Of course! And now?
Karim : Le marché a été remplacé par un grand supermarché, il y a environ cinq ans. De nos jours, les gens font de moins en moins leurs courses au marché. The market was replaced by a big supermarket, about five years ago. Nowadays, people do their shopping at the market less and less.
Nadia : Dommage. Et ce petit parc dans lequel on jouait, enfants ? Il existe toujours ? Too bad. And that little park we used to play in, as kids? Does it still exist?
Karim : Oui, heureusement ! Il est même devenu de plus en plus populaire. Les familles dont les enfants sont petits l'adorent. Yes, fortunately! It's even become more and more popular. Families whose children are young love it.
Nadia : Ça me rassure. Tout ne change pas, alors. That reassures me. Not everything changes, then.
Karim : Exactement. Au fil du temps, certaines choses disparaissent, et d'autres restent, tout simplement. Exactly. Over time, some things disappear, and others just stay the same.

This dialogue models the trend expressions de moins en moins and de plus en plus; the time-contrast markers autrefois and de nos jours; the relative pronouns dont (twice) and lequel; and one passive sentence (le marché a été remplacé) shown here for recognition only.

Writing Workshop

Complete the following writing task.

Tâche principale — Une évolution : Write a connected paragraph of eight to twelve sentences describing how a real or invented place, habit, or aspect of daily life has changed over time (your neighborhood, a family tradition, how people communicate, or a similar topic).   • at least one trend expression (de plus en plus or de moins en moins);
  • at least one time contrast using autrefois, à l'époque, or de nos jours;

  1. Choose your topic. Pick a place, habit, or aspect of life with a clear "before" and "now."

Culture and Communication

Culture in Context — Talking about neighborhood change

Conversations about how a neighborhood, village, or city has changed are common across the Francophone world, and they often mix nostalgia with practical observation. Phrases like autrefois and de nos jours let speakers acknowledge both what has been lost and what has genuinely improved, without the conversation becoming one-sided.

Regional Variation — Talking about change

De plus en plus, de moins en moins, autrefois, and de nos jours are used and understood throughout the Francophone world. What has changed — markets giving way to supermarkets, villages emptying as young people move to cities — varies a great deal by region and country, so the specific content of a description of change should not be assumed to be universal even when the language describing it is.

Common Mistakes

Common Mistake — Using que instead of dont

Error: Saying *c'est le livre que je me souviens, using que where the verb actually requires de.

Correction: Se souvenir de requires dont as its relative pronoun: c'est le livre dont je me souviens.

Common Mistake — Forgetting agreement on lequel

Error: Saying *la maison dans lequel j'ai grandi, leaving lequel in its masculine singular form regardless of the noun it replaces.

Correction: Lequel agrees with the noun it replaces: la maison dans laquelle j'ai grandi.

Common Mistake — Trying to produce the passive before it is productive

Error: Feeling that a description of change is incomplete without a self-produced passive sentence, and forcing an uncertain form into speaking or writing.

Correction: This chapter asks only for recognition of the passive voice. A strong description built on trend expressions, time contrast, and relative clauses fully meets this chapter's productive requirements without it. You can express the same idea actively: instead of *le marché a été remplacé, say on a remplacé le marché or simply il y a maintenant un supermarché à la place du marché.

Writing Workshop — Model paragraph

Modèle — Une évolution

Autrefois, ma famille se réunissait chaque dimanche chez mes grands-parents, dans une petite maison dont je garde d'excellents souvenirs. À l'époque, tout le monde venait sans exception, et le repas durait des heures. De nos jours, nous sommes de plus en plus dispersés, et il devient de plus en plus difficile de trouver un dimanche libre pour tout le monde. Mes cousins, dont plusieurs habitent maintenant loin de la ville, ne peuvent venir que quelques fois par an. Le jardin dans lequel nous jouions, enfants, existe toujours, mais il est de moins en moins souvent plein d'enfants. Ma grand-mère, dont la cuisine attirait autrefois toute la famille dès midi, cuisine aujourd'hui pour un groupe beaucoup plus petit. Malgré tout, quand nous nous retrouvons, le plaisir reste le même qu'autrefois. Je pense que ces réunions, même rares, resteront toujours importantes pour notre famille.

In the past, my family gathered every Sunday at my grandparents' house, in a little house I keep excellent memories of. At that time, everyone came without exception, and the meal lasted for hours. Nowadays, we are more and more spread out, and it's becoming harder and harder to find a free Sunday for everyone. My cousins, several of whom now live far from the city, can only come a few times a year. The garden we used to play in as children still exists, but it is less and less often full of children. My grandmother, whose cooking used to draw the whole family in by noon, now cooks for a much smaller group. Despite everything, when we do get together, the pleasure remains the same as it used to be. I think these gatherings, even rare ones, will always remain important to our family.

The paragraph contains eight sentences and demonstrates every required element: a time contrast between autrefois/à l'époque and de nos jours, two trend expressions with de plus en plus and one with de moins en moins, and three relative clauses with dont and one with lequel, all correctly agreeing. No passive-voice form and no subjunctive form appears in this model, consistent with their recognition-only status in this chapter. Other responses are acceptable when they meet all task requirements and remain within the grammar and vocabulary activated through Chapter 18.
