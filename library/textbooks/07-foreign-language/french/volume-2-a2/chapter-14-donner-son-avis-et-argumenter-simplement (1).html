14

Donner son avis et argumenter simplement

Opinions and Simple Argument · Speak and Write French: Everyday Independence and Connected Communication

Chapter Objectives

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

  1. Give a simple opinion using je pense que, je trouve que, and à mon avis.
  2. Agree and disagree politely, including softened disagreement appropriate to a friendly conversation.
  3. Give a reason for an opinion using parce que and car, and distinguish their use in speech and in writing.
  4. State a result or conclusion using donc and alors.
  5. Introduce a limiting or opposing point with a fixed concession connector, même si, without using the subjunctive.
  6. Name an advantage and a disadvantage of a simple, familiar topic using un avantage and un inconvénient.
  7. Read a short opinion text and identify the writer's position, reasons, and any concession.
  8. Write a short, organized paragraph stating and supporting a simple opinion on a familiar topic.

Communication Scenario

Marc, Nadia, and Karim are having one of their regular conversations, this time about a simple, familiar question: is it better to live in a big city or a small town? Marc prefers the city; Nadia misses the calm of smaller places; Karim can see good reasons on both sides. None of them is trying to win a formal debate — they are doing what people do every day: stating an opinion, giving a reason, reacting to what someone else said, and sometimes admitting that the other side has a point. This chapter gives you the tools for exactly this kind of everyday argument: opinion formulas, reason and result connectors, and one controlled way to say "even if" without needing the subjunctive.

  1. State a simple opinion on a familiar topic.
  2. Agree or disagree, including softened disagreement.
  3. Support an opinion with a reason and a result.
  4. Concede a limiting point using même si.

Pronunciation Focus

Pronunciation Focus — Linking opinion and reason smoothly

Opinion sentences in French often link several short clauses together in one breath group: je pense que la ville est plus pratique parce qu'il y a plus de transports. Practice keeping je pense que and je trouve que smoothly connected to the clause that follows, without a pause after que.

The final c of donc varies. In the traditional pattern, it is pronounced sentence-initially or before a vowel, and may be silent before a consonant (donc, c'est pratique). In contemporary French, pronouncing the /k/ in all positions is increasingly common, so learners should expect to hear both. Car keeps its final r pronounced. Both car and parce que introduce a reason, but car is more common in careful speech and writing, and typically follows a short pause or a comma, while parce que is common and neutral in ordinary conversation.

Practice these forms aloud:

  • je pense que
  • je trouve que
  • parce qu'il y a
  • car c'est plus calme
  • donc, c'est pratique
  • même si c'est cher

In this chapter, you are expected to link opinion formulas smoothly to the clause that follows, and to pronounce car, donc, alors, and même si intelligibly in connected speech.

Grammar: À mon avis…

Purpose: teach productive opinion formulas and agreement/disagreement language; distinguish reason connectors (parce que, car) from result connectors (donc, alors); and introduce a single fixed concession frame with même si — without introducing the subjunctive or free concessive constructions.

14.1 Stating an opinion

Building Block — Three ways to introduce an opinion
[je pense que] / [je trouve que] + [phrase]  |  [à mon avis] / [selon moi] + [phrase]
Je pense que and je trouve que are followed directly by a full clause and require the indicative, never the subjunctive, in this chapter. À mon avis and selon moi stand at the beginning of a sentence, set off by a comma, and are not followed by que. All four are broadly interchangeable in an everyday conversation; selon moi is slightly more formal-sounding than à mon avis.
Je pense que la ville est plus pratique. À mon avis, la campagne est plus calme.
I think the city is more practical. In my opinion, the countryside is calmer.

14.2 Agreeing and disagreeing

Language Focus

You already know être d'accord from earlier chapters. This chapter expands your control of agreement and disagreement, including softened disagreement, which is common and polite among friends.

  • Je suis tout à fait d'accord avec toi. I completely agree with you.
  • Je ne suis pas vraiment d'accord. I don't really agree. (softened disagreement)
  • Tu as peut-être raison, mais… You might be right, but… (softened disagreement)
  • Ça dépend. It depends. (hedge, not a full agreement or disagreement)

14.3 Giving a reason: parce que and car

Language Note — Two ways to say "because"

Parce que, recycled from Volume I, is the ordinary, all-purpose way to give a reason and answers the question pourquoi ? directly. Car introduces a reason without directly answering a spoken question; it is common in careful speech and especially in writing, and it usually follows a comma rather than beginning a new sentence. The two are not always freely interchangeable: parce que expresses a direct cause, while car more often adds an explanation or justification for the statement that was just made. In the simple sentences this chapter teaches, either choice is generally acceptable, but car should still not replace parce que as a standalone answer to pourquoi ?

  • Pourquoi tu préfères la ville ? — Parce qu'il y a plus de transports. Why do you prefer the city? — Because there is more transportation.
  • Je préfère la ville, car il y a plus de transports. I prefer the city, because there is more transportation.

14.4 Stating a result: donc and alors

Language Focus

Donc and alors both introduce a result or conclusion that follows logically from what was just said. Donc is slightly more common in careful or written argument; alors is very frequent in ordinary conversation and can also simply mean "so" in a more general, conversational sense. Both are broadly interchangeable in the everyday arguments this chapter teaches.

  • Il y a plus de transports en ville, donc c'est plus pratique pour moi. There is more transportation in the city, so it's more practical for me.
  • Tu préfères le calme, alors la campagne te conviendrait mieux. You prefer calm, so the countryside would suit you better.

14.5 Conceding a point: même si + indicatif

Building Block — A fixed concession frame, always with the indicative
[même si] + [présent, passé composé, or imparfait] + [, conclusion]
Même si introduces a circumstance or opposing point that does not change the speaker's main conclusion. Depending on context, that circumstance may be presented as factual, closer to English "even though" (même si j'ai habité en ville pendant des années…), or as possible or hypothetical, closer to English "even if" (même si c'est cher…). In both cases, unlike some other French concessive expressions, même si is always followed by the indicative — the présent, passé composé, or imparfait you already control — and never by the subjunctive. This chapter teaches only this one fixed concession frame; other concessive structures are developed in later volumes.
Même si la ville est pratique, je préfère la campagne. Même si j'ai habité en ville pendant des années, je me suis toujours senti plus à l'aise à la campagne.
Even though the city is practical, I prefer the countryside. Even though I lived in the city for years, I always felt more comfortable in the countryside.

Never use the subjunctive after même si: *même si ce soit cher is incorrect. Use the présent, passé composé, or imparfait, exactly as after ordinary si. This chapter's tasks require only one or two sentences with même si, always about a familiar, personal topic.

Practice — Choisir le bon connecteur

  1. Je préfère la ville, ___ il y a plus de choses à faire.
  2. ___ il y a beaucoup de bruit en ville, je préfère quand même y habiter.
  3. Tu aimes le calme, ___ la campagne te conviendrait bien.
  4. Pourquoi tu n'aimes pas la campagne ? — ___ c'est trop loin de tout.

Model Dialogue: Un petit désaccord amical

Context: Marc, Nadia, and Karim are discussing city versus small-town life. Register: informal, close friends, tu.

Marc : Moi, je pense que la ville est plus pratique. Il y a plus de transports, donc tout est plus facile. Me, I think the city is more practical. There's more transportation, so everything is easier.
Nadia : Je ne suis pas vraiment d'accord. À mon avis, la campagne est plus calme, et c'est important pour moi. I don't really agree. In my opinion, the countryside is calmer, and that's important to me.
Marc : Tu as peut-être raison sur le calme, mais il y a un inconvénient : c'est souvent loin de tout. You might be right about the calm, but there's a disadvantage: it's often far from everything.
Nadia : C'est vrai. Même si c'est parfois loin, je préfère quand même la tranquillité. That's true. Even if it's sometimes far, I still prefer the peace and quiet.
Karim : Moi, je suis d'accord avec les deux, en fait. Ça dépend. D'un côté, la ville est pratique ; de l'autre côté, elle est bruyante. Me, I actually agree with both. It depends. On one hand, the city is practical; on the other hand, it's noisy.
Marc : Et pour toi, quel est le plus grand avantage de la ville ? And for you, what's the biggest advantage of the city?
Karim : Je trouve que c'est le travail. Il y a plus d'offres d'emploi en ville, alors c'est plus facile de trouver un poste. I find that it's work. There are more job offers in the city, so it's easier to find a position.
Nadia : Ça, c'est vrai. Mais même si le travail est plus facile à trouver, je ne changerai pas d'avis sur le calme. That's true. But even if work is easier to find, I won't change my mind about the calm.
Marc : Tout à fait d'accord avec toi sur un point, au moins : chacun ses goûts ! Completely agree with you on one point, at least: to each their own!

This dialogue models opinion formulas (je pense que, à mon avis, je trouve que), agreement and softened disagreement, reason and result connectors (donc, alors), advantage/disadvantage vocabulary, and the fixed concession frame même si + indicative. No subjunctive appears anywhere in this dialogue.

Reading Practice

Un message de Karim

À mon avis, il vaut mieux avoir un travail qu'on aime plutôt qu'un travail bien payé, même si l'argent est important aussi. Je pense ça parce que je passe beaucoup de temps au travail, donc je préfère faire quelque chose d'intéressant. Un avantage d'un travail qu'on aime, c'est qu'on est plus motivé le matin. Un inconvénient, c'est que ce genre de travail n'est pas toujours très bien payé. Certains de mes amis ne sont pas d'accord avec moi : ils pensent que le salaire est plus important, car il faut bien vivre. Je comprends leur avis, même si je ne suis pas tout à fait d'accord. Culture and Communication

Culture in Context — Debating opinions among friends

Among friends, a certain amount of open disagreement and lively back-and-forth is often a normal, even enjoyable, part of French conversation, rather than a sign of tension. Softened disagreement (pas vraiment, tu as peut-être raison, mais…) remains common and polite, but direct disagreement is generally more accepted in casual conversation among friends than in more formal or professional settings, where a more careful, indirect style is often preferred.

Regional Variation — Everyday opinion formulas

À mon avis and je pense que are broadly understood and used across the Francophone world. Some regions and individual speakers may favor certain hedges or intensifiers over others in casual speech, but the core formulas taught in this chapter are safe defaults everywhere French is spoken.

Common Mistakes

Common Mistake — Using the subjunctive after même si

Error: Saying *même si ce soit cher, j'aime la ville.

Correction: Même si always takes the indicative, exactly like ordinary si: même si c'est cher, j'aime la ville.

Common Mistake — Confusing car with parce que in a direct answer

Error: Answering the question pourquoi ? with *car il y a plus de transports as a standalone reply.

Correction: Use parce que to answer a direct question: parce qu'il y a plus de transports. Reserve car for adding a reason within a longer sentence, not as a direct answer to pourquoi ?

Common Mistake — Following à mon avis with que

Error: Saying *à mon avis que la ville est pratique.

Correction: À mon avis and selon moi are followed directly by a clause, with no que: à mon avis, la ville est pratique. Only je pense and je trouve require que.

Writing Workshop — Model paragraph

Modèle — Un petit paragraphe d'opinion

À mon avis, il vaut mieux voyager seul au moins une fois. Je pense ça parce qu'on apprend à se débrouiller et à faire ses propres choix. Il y a moins de discussions sur l'itinéraire, donc c'est souvent plus simple à organiser. Un avantage de voyager seul, c'est la liberté totale. Un inconvénient, c'est qu'on ne peut pas partager les moments importants tout de suite avec quelqu'un d'autre. Même si c'est parfois un peu solitaire, je trouve que ça vaut la peine. Certaines personnes ne sont pas d'accord avec moi, car elles préfèrent toujours voyager en groupe. Je comprends leur avis, même si je ne le partage pas complètement.

In my opinion, it's worth traveling alone at least once. I think that because you learn to manage on your own and make your own choices. There are fewer discussions about the itinerary, so it's often simpler to organize. An advantage of traveling alone is total freedom. A disadvantage is that you can't immediately share important moments with someone else. Even if it's sometimes a bit lonely, I find that it's worth it. Some people don't agree with me, because they always prefer traveling in a group. I understand their view, even if I don't completely share it.

The paragraph contains eight sentences and demonstrates two opinion formulas (à mon avis, je trouve que); a reason with parce que and a result with donc; an advantage and a disadvantage; two uses of même si + indicative; a reason with car; and no subjunctive form anywhere. Other responses are acceptable when they meet all task requirements and remain within the grammar and vocabulary activated through Chapter 14.
