6

Se loger et organiser la maison

Housing and Home Management · Speak and Write French: Everyday Independence and Connected Communication

Chapter Objectives

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

  1. Describe a home, a housing problem, and the people involved in resolving it, such as a propriétaire, a plombier, or an électricien.
  2. Use y productively to replace a place already mentioned, beyond the formulaic uses of earlier chapters.
  3. Use en productively to replace a previously established de complement or quantity within the Chapter 6 patterns, including with avoir besoin de and s'occuper de.
  4. Use avoir besoin de and s'occuper de correctly, choosing en when the noun they govern has already been established.
  5. Express impersonal obligation with il faut + infinitive, and personal obligation with devoir + infinitive, and explain the difference between them.
  6. Recognize and use the formulaic expression il faudrait + infinitive as a fixed way of presenting a suggestion less directly than il faut.
  7. Pronounce en smoothly in connected speech, distinguish it from un, and maintain natural rhythm in phrases such as j'en ai besoin and je vais m'en occuper.
  8. Read and write a short message reporting a housing problem and requesting that it be resolved.

Communication Scenario

A month into her new life in Tours, Nadia is finally settled into her apartment — or so she thought, until the heating stops working on a cold evening. She calls Marc, half to vent and half to ask for advice, since he went through his own move to Bordeaux not long ago. At the same time, in his new studio in Marseille, Karim is dealing with a small leak under the kitchen sink and has to write to his landlord about it. This chapter opens Unit II, Gérer la vie pratique (Managing Practical Life), and turns from telling stories about the past toward handling the ordinary, sometimes frustrating logistics of daily life: describing a home, naming a problem, saying what needs to happen, and asking someone else to take care of it.

  1. Describe an apartment or house and a problem occurring in it.
  2. Refer back to a place already mentioned using y.
  3. Refer back to a thing or need already mentioned using en.
  4. State what needs to be done, both in general (il faut) and for a specific person (devoir).
  5. Ask someone, more gently, to take care of a problem.

Pronunciation Focus

Pronunciation Focus — Smooth en, and en versus un

This chapter leans heavily on the pronoun en, which appears constantly with avoir besoin de, s'occuper de, and il faut. In ordinary contemporary speech, do not treat the final t of faut as a required liaison target, even before a vowel-initial infinitive: il faut réparer and il faut en parler are both said with a silent t in everyday speech. Focus instead on maintaining a smooth rhythm across the whole phrase.

A. En is not un. The pronoun en is a nasal vowel, /ɑ̃/, the same nasal sound you already know from words like dans and enfant. It is easy to confuse with the article un, /œ̃/, a different, more forward nasal vowel, especially for learners whose first language does not distinguish nasal vowels this precisely. Compare j'en ai un ("I have one of them"), pronounced approximately [ʒɑ̃.ne.œ̃] — here both target nasal vowels occur within the same short phrase, which makes a useful listening and production drill.

B. En triggers liaison before a vowel. Unlike me, te, se, le, and la (Chapter 4), en does not lose a letter before a vowel-initial verb, because it has no final e to drop. Instead, before a vowel-initial verb, the normally silent final consonant of en is pronounced in liaison: j'en ai, on en a. Practice the phrase as one smooth rhythm group, without any written change to en itself.

C. Natural rhythm in en-phrases. Practice saying phrases such as j'en ai besoin, je vais m'en occuper, and il faut en parler as single, smooth rhythm groups, with en pronounced clearly but without any pause before or after it.

In this chapter, you are expected to: distinguish the nasal vowel of en from that of un in both listening and speaking; pronounce en smoothly before a vowel-initial verb without attempting to elide it; and maintain natural rhythm in connected phrases such as j'en ai besoin and je vais m'en occuper. Liaison after faut is not assessed in this chapter.

Grammar: Chez moi, il faut...

Purpose: expand the communicative, productive use of y and en; introduce verb government with de in avoir besoin de and s'occuper de; and contrast impersonal and personal obligation — without introducing any new tense or mood.

6.1 Y: replacing a place already mentioned

Language Focus

You have already seen y in fixed contexts such as il y a and short remarks like je compte y rester (Ch. 5). In this chapter, use y productively to replace a place that has already been identified in the conversation, usually introduced by à, chez, dans, or en (with a place). This is the productive boundary for Chapter 6: French also uses y in other grammatical environments, such as replacing certain complements introduced by à that are not places, but those uses are developed later in the volume. Once a place is established, repeating it by name every time is unnatural; y replaces it and sits directly before the conjugated verb, exactly like the object pronouns from Chapter 4.

  • J'habite à Tours depuis un mois. J'y suis très bien installée. I've been living in Tours for a month. I'm very well settled in there.
  • Tu vas rester longtemps dans cet appartement ? — Oui, je vais y rester au moins deux ans. Are you going to stay in that apartment a long time? — Yes, I'm going to stay there at least two years. y before the infinitive it governs, as with object pronouns (Ch. 4)
  • Le plombier va venir chez moi demain. Il va y trouver la fuite, j'espère. The plumber is coming to my place tomorrow. He'll find the leak there, I hope.

As in Chapter 4, when y depends on an infinitive rather than the conjugated verb, it moves to sit directly before that infinitive.

6.2 En: replacing a previously established de complement

Language Focus

In this chapter, use en productively to replace a previously established de complement or quantity, especially with avoir besoin de, s'occuper de (Grammar 6.3), and simple quantity expressions such as j'en ai deux ("I have two of them"). This is the productive boundary for Chapter 6: French also uses en in additional environments, which are developed later in the volume. When referring to a specific identified person, prefer stressed forms such as de lui, d'elle, and d'eux: Tu as des nouvelles de Léa ? — Oui, j'ai des nouvelles d'elle. En may refer to an indefinite or quantified person, as in J'ai besoin d'un électricien — J'en ai besoin; broader personal uses of en remain outside this chapter's productive scope.

  • Il y a une fuite sous l'évier. Le propriétaire est déjà au courant, je lui en ai parlé hier. There's a leak under the sink. The landlord already knows, I talked to him about it yesterday. en replaces de + la fuite, after parler à qqn de qqch
  • Tu as besoin d'un électricien ? — Oui, j'en ai vraiment besoin. Do you need an electrician? — Yes, I really need one.

This chapter's controlled productive system pairs a single pronoun (y or en) with the conjugated verb or the infinitive it governs. Combining en or y with another object pronoun in the same clause (for example, je lui en ai parlé, shown above only as a receptive model in context) is not required productively here and is developed further later in the volume; for your own production, keep y and en as the only pronoun in the clause.

6.3 Verb government with de: avoir besoin de, s'occuper de

Building Block — A verb that "wants" de
[avoir besoin de / s'occuper de] + [nom] → [en] + [avoir besoin / s'occuper]
Some French verbs are followed by de before their object, the way téléphoner à and écrire à (Ch. 4) are followed by à. When the noun after de has already been mentioned, en replaces de + that noun, and moves to sit directly before the verb that governs it.
J'ai besoin d'un plombier. Tu en connais un ? — Le chauffage ? Ne t'inquiète pas, je vais m'en occuper cet après-midi.
I need a plumber. Do you know one? — The heating? Don't worry, I'm going to take care of it this afternoon.

Compare the noun form and the pronoun form directly:

  • J'ai besoin d'un rendez-vous. → J'en ai besoin. I need an appointment. → I need one.
  • Je m'occupe du problème. → Je m'en occupe. I'm taking care of the problem. → I'm taking care of it.

For Chapter 6 production, use stressed forms such as de lui, d'elle, and d'eux when referring to a specific identified person: Je m'occupe de lui ("I'm taking care of him"). En may refer to an indefinite or quantified person, as in J'ai besoin d'un plombier — J'en ai besoin. Other personal uses of en exist in French but remain outside this chapter's productive scope.

6.4 Obligation: il faut versus devoir

Language Focus

When the person responsible must be named, use devoir + infinitive. When the responsible person does not need to be named, use il faut + infinitive. Il faut is impersonal: the subject il here does not refer to any person. Devoir, which you already know, names the person who has the obligation, because it conjugates for a specific subject.

  • Il faut réparer le chauffage. The heating needs to be repaired. (impersonal — no one specific is named)
  • Le propriétaire doit réparer le chauffage. The landlord has to repair the heating. (personal — the landlord specifically)
  • Je dois trouver un plombier. I have to find a plumber. (personal — the speaker specifically)

This chapter uses il faut + infinitive for impersonal obligation and devoir + infinitive when the responsible person is named. If you want to keep the sentence impersonal but still connect it to yourself, use il faut + infinitive with an implied "I/we," as in il faut trouver un plombier, or switch to devoir, as in je dois trouver un plombier. These example sentences illustrate the grammar only; who is actually responsible for a given repair depends on the type of problem and the lease, as discussed in Culture and Communication below.

6.5 Softening obligation: il faudrait

Building Block — A gentler way to say what's needed
[il faudrait] + [infinitif]
Il faudrait presents a proposed action less directly than il faut: it suggests something is a good idea or should happen, without stating it as bluntly as il faut. It can help soften a suggestion, but politeness also depends on the greeting, tone, explanation, request wording, and closing you use around it — il faudrait by itself does not make a whole request polite. Learn and use it here as a fixed expression, the way you learned avoir l'intention de (Ch. 3); its full conditional pattern, and how to build other conditional forms yourself, is developed later in the volume.
Il faut réparer le chauffage aujourd'hui. Il faudrait aussi vérifier la fuite sous l'évier, dès que possible.
The heating needs to be repaired today. It would also be a good idea to check the leak under the sink, as soon as possible.

Il faudrait can help soften a proposed action — for example, in a message to a landlord, as in this chapter's Writing Workshop — but it does not by itself make an entire request polite; the surrounding greeting, explanation, and closing matter just as much.

6.6 Recycling past-participle agreement with être and reflexives

Language Note — Previously learned agreement — No new rule

This chapter's new reflexive verb, s'installer, follows the same compound-past pattern you already know from Chapters 1–5: elle s'est installée, ils se sont installés. As before, the past participle agrees with the subject; nothing new is being asked of you here. Agreement with a preceding direct object, and the more advanced agreement patterns for reciprocal verbs introduced in Chapter 4, remain outside this chapter's scope and are developed later in the volume.

Model Dialogue: Il y a un problème !

Context: Nadia calls Marc one cold evening, a month after moving to Tours, because her heating has stopped working. Register: informal, close friends, tu.

Nadia : Marc, j'ai un gros problème ! Le chauffage est tombé en panne, et il fait froid dans tout l'appartement. Marc, I have a big problem! The heating has broken down, and it's cold throughout the whole apartment.
Marc : Oh non, ça tombe mal ! Tu as déjà prévenu ton propriétaire ? Oh no, what bad timing! Have you already informed your landlord?
Nadia : Pas encore. Je dois l'appeler, mais franchement, je ne sais pas trop quoi lui dire. Not yet. I have to call him, but honestly, I don't really know what to say to him.
Marc : C'est simple : il faut lui expliquer le problème et lui demander de s'en occuper rapidement. It's simple: you need to explain the problem to him and ask him to take care of it quickly.
Nadia : Tu as raison. Et pour le chauffage, tu penses qu'il faut appeler un électricien ou un plombier ? You're right. And for the heating, do you think we need to call an electrician or a plumber?
Marc : Préviens d'abord ton propriétaire. Selon la panne et le bail, il peut te dire qui doit s'en occuper et qui doit payer. Inform your landlord first. Depending on the breakdown and the lease, he can tell you who has to take care of it and who has to pay.
Nadia : D'accord, c'est plus clair comme ça. Et toi, à Bordeaux, tu as eu des problèmes comme ça, quand tu as emménagé ? Okay, that's clearer this way. And you, in Bordeaux, did you have problems like that, when you moved in?
Marc : Un peu ! Il y avait une fuite dans mon appartement. J'y ai fait venir un plombier assez vite, et depuis, tout va bien. A bit! There was a leak in my apartment. I had a plumber come there fairly quickly, and since then, everything's fine.
Nadia : Bon, alors j'appelle mon propriétaire tout de suite. Merci, tu m'as bien aidée ! Okay, so I'm calling my landlord right away. Thanks, you really helped me!

This dialogue recycles present, near future, passé composé / imparfait, locative y, and en with the scheduled de patterns from earlier chapters, and adds this chapter's new material: productive y (j'y ai fait venir un plombier, referring to Marc's apartment), productive en after a governing verb (lui demander de s'en occuper, referring to the problem), and the impersonal/personal obligation contrast between il faut and devoir. Notice that Marc deliberately avoids stating who is always responsible for a repair; instead, he tells Nadia to inform her landlord and let the specific problem and the lease determine who must act and who must pay, as discussed further in Culture and Communication below.

Writing Workshop

Complete the following linked writing tasks in order.

Tâche unique — Un message au propriétaire : Write a message of eight to twelve sentences to a landlord (real or invented), describing a housing problem and asking for it to be resolved. Your message must include:

Culture and Communication

Culture in Context — Renters, landlords, and repairs

Rental relationships are generally governed by a lease (un bail) and by local law, and responsibility for a given repair often depends on its cause, on routine maintenance duties, and on the terms of that lease; housing rules and conventions also vary from country to country and by region. A practical approach as a tenant is to inform the propriétaire or gestionnaire promptly and let them explain, based on the specific problem and the lease, who should intervene and who should pay. Raising a problem directly but politely, often in writing so that there is a record of the request, is a normal and expected part of renting; il faudrait and similar less-direct language are common in this kind of message, though real politeness also depends on the greeting, explanation, and closing that surround it. This chapter offers practical communication strategies, not legal guidance; specific rights and obligations should always be checked against local law and the individual lease.

Regional Variation — Housing types and vocabulary

Housing types and related vocabulary vary across the Francophone world: un appartement and un studio are broadly understood everywhere, but terms for specific arrangements, and the balance between renting and owning, differ by country, city, and generation. This chapter's vocabulary focuses on terms that are widely recognized across French-speaking regions.

Common Mistakes

Common Mistake — Repeating the noun instead of using y or en

Less natural repetition: Saying J'habite à Tours. Je suis très bien installée à Tours, repeating the place instead of replacing it. This is grammatical but stylistically repetitive.

More natural: Once a place has been established, replace it with y: J'habite à Tours. J'y suis très bien installée. The same applies to en for things already mentioned, as practiced throughout this chapter.

Common Mistake — Confusing en with un

Error: Pronouncing j'en ai besoin with the nasal vowel of un instead of the nasal vowel of en, or writing un where the pronoun en is required.

Correction: En (/ɑ̃/) and un (/œ̃/) are different words with different nasal vowels and different grammatical functions: en is a pronoun replacing de + noun, while un is an article or number. Practice them side by side, as in j'en ai un.

Common Mistake — Using il faut where devoir is needed

Error: Saying Il faut réparer le chauffage when the intended meaning is specifically "the landlord has to repair the heating."

Correction: If you want to name who is responsible, use devoir: Le propriétaire doit réparer le chauffage. Reserve impersonal il faut for situations where no specific person is being named, as explained in Grammar 6.4.

Common Mistake — Treating il faudrait as fully productive conditional grammar

Error: Trying to build new conditional forms from other verbs by analogy with il faudrait, such as inventing *je voudrait.

Correction: Il faudrait is taught in this chapter as a single fixed expression, not as a model for building other conditional forms yourself. The productive conditional system is introduced later in the volume; until then, use il faudrait only as a complete, memorized phrase.

Answer Key

Model Dialogue — Comprehension

  1. Nadia's heating has broken down; Marc advises her to inform her landlord and explain the problem to him.
  2. Marc tells Nadia to inform her landlord first; he explains that who must act and who must pay depends on the specific breakdown and on the lease, not on a fixed rule.

Speaking Practice — Y ou en ?

  1. J'y habite. y replaces the place à Tours.
  2. J'en ai besoin. en replaces d'un plombier after avoir besoin de.
  3. Je vais m'en occuper. en replaces du problème after s'occuper de.
  4. Nous allons y rester. y replaces dans cet appartement.

Speaking Practice — Il faut ou devoir ?

  1. Il faut réparer le chauffage. No one specific is named, so the impersonal form is used.
  2. Le propriétaire doit réparer le chauffage. The hypothetical situation names the landlord as the responsible person, so devoir is used.
  3. Je dois appeler le plombier. The speaker personally needs to act, so devoir is used with je.
  4. Softened reformulation of Item 1: Il faudrait réparer le chauffage.

Minor wording variations are acceptable in all four items above, as long as the required obligation structure (il faut, devoir, or il faudrait, as specified) remains correct.
