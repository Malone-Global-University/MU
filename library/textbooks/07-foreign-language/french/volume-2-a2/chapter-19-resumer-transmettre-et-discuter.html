Résumer, transmettre et discuter

Summary, Mediation, and Discussion · Speak and Write French: Everyday Independence and Connected Communication

Chapter Objectives

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

  1. Report what someone else says or thinks using selon / d'après and present-time dire que / penser que, while keeping more advanced tense transposition receptive only.
  2. Synthesize two or more viewpoints in a single summary using d'un côté… de l'autre côté and certains/certaines… d'autres.
  3. Open and close a spoken or written summary using en résumé, pour résumer, and en bref.
  4. Relay the same information to two different audiences, shifting from informal tu-register phrasing to formal vous-register phrasing.
  5. Fold already-active systems — cause/consequence connectors, hypothetical advice, and the relative pronouns dont and lequel — into a coherent summary rather than a list of disconnected sentences.
  6. Read and listen to an extended discussion involving more than one speaker and identify who holds which opinion.
  7. Write a short informal summary of a discussion for a friend, then rewrite the same content as a formal message for a different audience.

Communication Scenario

Karim's friend Sophie could not make it to the conversation with Marc and Nadia about the job offer, and she wants to know what everyone thinks. At the same time, Karim's manager has asked him to summarize what the team discussed at a recent meeting, for a short written update. Up to now, you have narrated single events (Chapter 16), given advice about one decision (Chapter 17), and described change over time (Chapter 18). This chapter asks you to step back further still: to gather several people's opinions into one coherent account, and to say the same thing twice — once casually, once formally — depending on who is listening.

  1. Tell someone what another person said or thinks, without quoting them word for word.
  2. Bring together more than one opinion into a single, organized summary.
  3. Open and close a summary clearly.
  4. Say the same thing to a friend and to a supervisor, adjusting the register appropriately.

Pronunciation Focus

Pronunciation Focus — Pacing a multi-voice summary

Summarizing several opinions at once puts new pressure on rhythm and pacing: if every clause is delivered at the same speed with no pause, a listener quickly loses track of who thinks what. When you use d'un côté… de l'autre côté or certains… d'autres, place a small, deliberate pause right before the second half of the contrast, and let your pitch reset slightly, as if opening a new sentence.

The required liaison you have practiced since Volume I remains active here, especially with the weak subject pronouns: ils ont dit (il-z-ont) and vous avez répondu (vou-z-avez) both link smoothly and predictably.

Practice these forms aloud, pausing clearly at the marked breaks:

  • D'un côté, Marc pense que… ¦ de l'autre côté, Nadia trouve que…
  • Certains sont d'accord ¦ mais d'autres ne le sont pas.
  • En résumé, ¦ tout le monde n'est pas du même avis.

No new sound-spelling feature is introduced in this chapter. You are expected to apply liaison, connected-speech linking, and rhythm-group pacing from earlier chapters to the specific task of keeping several reported viewpoints clearly separated for the listener.

Grammar: Résumer et transmettre

Purpose: teach productive formulas for reporting what someone else said or thinks, for synthesizing more than one viewpoint into a single organized account, and for shifting register when the same content is relayed to a different audience — all using tenses, connectors, and pronouns you already control. No new tense is introduced.

19.1 Reporting a statement: selon, d'après, dire que, penser que

Building Block — Reporting without quoting
[Selon / D'après] + [personne], + [clause] …   |   [Personne] + [dit / pense / trouve] + que + [clause]
Both patterns let you relay someone else's idea without repeating their exact words. Selon and d'après are followed by a noun or stressed pronoun, with the reported clause coming right after, and no que; dire/penser/trouver que uses a full verb instead. In this chapter, you report present-time or formulaic statements: présent stays présent, and conditionnel stays conditionnel, as in the examples below. French indirect discourse can involve more complex changes of tense, mood, and person, especially after a past reporting verb; that fuller system of tense transposition is receptive only for now and is developed productively later in the series.
Selon Marc, Karim devrait visiter la ville avant de décider. Nadia pense qu'il devrait accepter tout de suite.
According to Marc, Karim should visit the city before deciding. Nadia thinks he should accept right away.

19.2 Synthesizing more than one viewpoint

Language Focus

D'un côté… de l'autre côté organizes two contrasting opinions into a single, structured summary, rather than presenting them as a disconnected list. Certains… d'autres works the same way when the opinions belong to groups of people rather than to two named individuals. Both patterns recycle the contrast connectors from Chapter 14 (mais, cependant) but add explicit structure that helps a listener or reader follow who thinks what.

  • D'un côté, Marc trouve que c'est trop risqué ; de l'autre côté, Nadia pense que c'est une belle opportunité. On one hand, Marc feels it's too risky; on the other hand, Nadia thinks it's a great opportunity.
  • Certains collègues sont d'accord avec le projet, d'autres préfèrent attendre. Some colleagues agree with the project, others prefer to wait.

19.3 Opening and closing a summary

Language Note — Framing a synthesis

En résumé, pour résumer, and en bref all signal that a final, condensed statement is coming after several reported viewpoints. They function like a spoken or written frame around the body of a summary: several sentences of reported opinion, followed by one closing sentence that draws the discussion together.

  • En résumé, tout le monde encourage Karim à bien réfléchir avant de répondre. In summary, everyone is encouraging Karim to think carefully before answering.
  • En bref, les avis sont partagés, mais personne n'est vraiment contre le projet. In short, opinions are divided, but no one is really against the project.

19.4 Shifting register: relaying the same content differently

Review — One message, two audiences

You already control the tu/vous system, formal request patterns (Chapter 7), and formal correspondence conventions (Chapters 7–9). This chapter does not add a new grammar system for register; it asks you to apply what you already control to a mediation task: relaying identical information to a friend informally and to a supervisor or institution formally. Register shifting means changing address forms, opening and closing formulas, and sentence tone — not changing the facts being reported.

  • (À un ami) Marc pense que tu devrais visiter la ville d'abord. (To a friend) Marc thinks you should visit the city first.
  • (À un supérieur) Selon plusieurs collègues, il serait préférable de visiter la ville avant de répondre à l'offre. (To a supervisor) According to several colleagues, it would be preferable to visit the city before responding to the offer.

Notice that the second version avoids tu entirely, replaces the direct advice formula with a softer impersonal structure, and keeps the same underlying content as the first.

The subjonctif, the plus-que-parfait, and the passive voice remain receptive only in this chapter, exactly as in Chapters 16–18. You are not required to produce any of them in a Chapter 19 speaking or writing task.

Model Dialogue: Alors, qu'est-ce qu'ils ont dit ?

Context: Sophie, who missed the conversation between Karim, Marc, and Nadia about the job offer, asks Karim to fill her in. Register: informal, close friends; tu throughout.

Sophie : Alors, qu'est-ce que Marc et Nadia ont dit à propos de l'offre d'emploi ? So, what did Marc and Nadia say about the job offer?
Karim : Eh bien, d'un côté, Nadia pense que je devrais accepter tout de suite. Selon elle, c'est une belle opportunité. Well, on one hand, Nadia thinks I should accept right away. According to her, it's a great opportunity.
Sophie : Et de l'autre côté ? And on the other hand?
Karim : D'après Marc, ce n'est pas si simple. Il trouve que je devrais visiter la ville avant de me décider. According to Marc, it's not that simple. He feels I should visit the city before deciding.
Sophie : Et toi, tu es d'accord avec qui ? And you, who do you agree with?
Karim : Honnêtement, un peu avec les deux. En résumé, tout le monde m'encourage à bien réfléchir, mais personne n'est vraiment contre. Honestly, a bit with both. In summary, everyone is encouraging me to think carefully, but no one is really against it.
Sophie : C'est déjà une bonne nouvelle, non ? That's already good news, isn't it?
Karim : Oui. Je dois aussi transmettre un petit résumé à mon manager, mais là, je vais devoir changer un peu le ton. Yes. I also have to pass along a short summary to my manager, but for that, I'm going to have to change the tone a bit.

This dialogue models the reporting openers selon and d'après, the synthesis connector d'un côté… de l'autre côté, and the summary opener en résumé. Karim's final line anticipates the register shift developed in the Reading Practice and Writing Workshop below.

Speaking Practice

Pratique contrôlée — Selon ou d'après ?

With a partner, take turns reporting a short opinion using selon or d'après about a familiar topic (a friend's opinion, a family member's habit, a news item).

Pratique contrôlée — Deux avis, un résumé

With a partner, combine each pair of opinions into one sentence using d'un côté… de l'autre côté or certains/certaines… d'autres.

Item 1

Marc veut déménager. Nadia préfère rester.

Item 2

Des collègues aiment le nouveau projet. D'autres collègues hésitent.

Tâche communicative — Rapporter une discussion

With a partner (or a small group), hold a short discussion about a familiar topic (a decision, a change, an opinion), in which each speaker takes a slightly different position. Then, one member of the group must summarize the discussion aloud to someone who was not present, including, at minimum:

  • at least one reported opinion with selon or d'après;
  • at least one reported opinion with dire que or penser que;
  • at least one synthesis with d'un côté… de l'autre côté or certains… d'autres;
  • a closing sentence with en résumé, pour résumer, or en bref.

Do not read from a script; speak from the vocabulary and patterns of this chapter and earlier chapters.

Pratique individuelle — Solo alternative

Record yourself summarizing a real or invented discussion between two or three people you know. Your recording must include, in order:

  1. one statement of what the discussion was about;
  2. at least two reported opinions, using different reporting formulas;
  3. one synthesis sentence contrasting the opinions;
  4. one closing sentence with en résumé, pour résumer, or en bref.

Writing Workshop

Complete the following writing task.

Tâche principale — Un résumé, deux registres : Choose a real or invented discussion involving at least two people with different opinions (a decision, a change, a plan). Write two short texts:

  • Text 1 (informal, four to six sentences): a message to a friend summarizing the discussion, using tu if you address the friend directly;
  • Text 2 (formal, four to six sentences): an email relaying the same content to a supervisor, instructor, or institution, using vous and formal opening/closing conventions.

Both texts together must include:

  • at least one reported opinion with selon or d'après;
  • at least one reported opinion with dire que or penser que;
  • at least one synthesis with d'un côté… de l'autre côté or certains/certaines… d'autres;
  • a summary sentence in each text using en résumé, pour résumer, or en bref — this sentence draws the discussion together and does not need to be the very last sentence in the text;
  • consistent register within each text, and a visible shift between the two texts.

Do not use the subjonctif, the plus-que-parfait, or the passive voice in your own writing this chapter; all three remain recognition-only. Keep the underlying content identical between the two texts — only the register should change.

  1. Choose your discussion. Decide who holds which opinion.
  2. Draft the informal message. Report both opinions and include a summary sentence.
  3. Draft the formal email. Relay the same content, shifting address forms and tone, and include a summary sentence and a distinct closing formula (such as Cordialement).
  4. Compare the two texts. Confirm the content matches but the register does not.
  5. Revise. Confirm reporting formulas, agreement, accents, and punctuation are accurate throughout.

Liste de révision — Editing checklist:

  • Both texts together total eight to twelve sentences.
  • At least one reported opinion with selon or d'après appears.
  • At least one reported opinion with dire que or penser que appears.
  • At least one synthesis connector appears.
  • Each text includes a summary sentence using en résumé, pour résumer, or en bref.
  • Register is consistent within each text and visibly different between the two.
  • No subjonctif, plus-que-parfait, or passive-voice form appears anywhere.
  • Articles, agreement, verb forms, accents, apostrophes, and punctuation are accurate.

Culture and Communication

Culture in Context — Mediating between registers

Relaying the same information to different audiences is a routine part of professional and social life across the Francophone world: a colleague's casual complaint becomes a neutral note in a report; a friend's blunt opinion becomes a softened observation in an email. Learning to preserve the content of a message while adjusting its tone is a practical mediation skill, not simply a grammar exercise.

Regional Variation — Reporting formulas

Selon, d'après, and the summary formulas in this chapter are used and understood throughout the Francophone world. Formal correspondence conventions — openings, closings, and degree of formality expected in workplace email — vary somewhat by country and institution; the patterns in this chapter represent a safe, broadly recognized default rather than a single universal standard.

Common Mistakes

Common Mistake — Adding que after selon or d'après

Error: Saying *selon que Marc…, treating selon like dire que.

Correction: Selon and d'après are followed by a noun or stressed pronoun, with the reported clause right after and no que: selon Marc, il faudrait attendre.

Common Mistake — Mixing tu and vous within one text

Error: Writing a formal email that begins Bonjour Madame but later slips into tu devrais….

Correction: Register must remain consistent within a single text: il serait préférable de… or vous pourriez… keeps the same email formal throughout.

Common Mistake — Forcing an uncertain tense change when reporting a present-time statement

Error: Assuming that reporting a statement always requires shifting the verb into a different tense, and forcing an uncertain form as a result.

Correction: For the present-time and formulaic reporting practiced in this chapter, the reported clause keeps the tense of the original statement: if Marc says tu devrais visiter la ville, the report stays Marc pense que tu devrais visiter la ville, with no tense change required. Reported speech built on a past reporting verb can involve further changes of tense and mood; that fuller pattern is recognition-only for now, so do not force it into your own speech or writing this chapter.

Hello Mr. Girard, – I am passing along a summary of the opinions gathered regarding the proposed schedule change. On one hand, some team members feel this change would simplify organization. On the other hand, others consider its implementation would be too complicated right now. To summarize, the team remains divided, but no member firmly opposes the project. – I remain available to discuss this further. – Sincerely, Karim

Both texts report the same underlying content — divided opinions about a proposed change — while shifting register: informal address and casual framing in the message, formal opening/closing and impersonal phrasing in the email. The message's summary sentence is introduced by en bref; the email's summary sentence is introduced by pour résumer, and each email still ends with its own separate closing formula and name. Neither text contains a subjonctif, plus-que-parfait, or passive-voice form.
