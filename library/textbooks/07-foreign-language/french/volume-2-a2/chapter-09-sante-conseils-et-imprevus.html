Santé, conseils et imprévus

Health, Advice, and Unexpected Problems · Speak and Write French: Everyday Independence and Connected Communication

Chapter Objectives

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

  1. Describe common symptoms, minor health problems, and unexpected daily setbacks.
  2. Form the conditionnel présent productively for regular and high-frequency irregular verbs.
  3. Give advice using tu devrais / vous devriez + infinitive and tu pourrais / vous pourriez + infinitive.
  4. Give advice using à ta place / à votre place + conditionnel.
  5. Distinguish savoir + infinitive (knowing how) from pouvoir + infinitive (ability or possibility depending on circumstances, permission, availability, or physical capacity).
  6. Use ne...que to express restriction ("only"), and distinguish it from ne...pas and ne...plus.
  7. Recognize the conditional stem followed by the endings -ais, -ais, -ait, -ions, -iez, -aient in common advice forms and connected speech.
  8. Read and write a short exchange in which one person describes a problem and another offers advice using the conditionnel.

Communication Scenario

Between the appointments of Chapter 7 and the shopping of Chapter 8, ordinary life keeps producing small, unplanned problems. Marc wakes up with a sore throat two days before an important meeting in Bordeaux. Nadia, still finding her footing in Tours, twists her ankle lightly on a badly lit staircase. Karim's landlord's technician cancels at the last minute, right when Karim was counting on getting the leak fixed. None of these situations is a real emergency, but each calls for the same two things: describing what is wrong, and asking someone — a friend, a pharmacist, a colleague — what they should do about it. This chapter is about exactly that everyday exchange: naming a problem and giving or receiving advice about it.

  1. Describe a minor health problem or an unexpected setback.
  2. Ask for advice about what to do.
  3. Give advice using the conditionnel, both directly and with à ta place.
  4. Distinguish knowing how to do something from being able to do it.

This chapter's central grammatical work is the conditionnel présent, moving from the formulaic status it held in Chapters 6–7 (il faudrait, je voudrais, pourriez-vous) to productive, rule-based formation for regular verbs and a controlled set of high-frequency irregulars (devoir → devrais, pouvoir → pourrais, être → serais, avoir → aurais, aller → irais). No additional tense or mood system beyond the conditionnel présent is introduced here: the present, aller + infinitif, and passé composé / imparfait remain active from earlier chapters, and the futur simple is not taught or assessed in this chapter. Si + imparfait + conditionnel as a full hypothetical system is not introduced here; only the fixed frame à ta place, je + conditionnel is productive. No subjunctive form is introduced or assessed in this chapter.

Pronunciation Focus

Pronunciation Focus — Recognizing the conditionnel by ear

The conditional uses a stem commonly ending in r, followed by the endings -ais, -ais, -ait, -ions, -iez, -aient. In the singular and third-person plural forms, -ais, -ait, and -aient normally share the sound /ɛ/: je parlerais, il parlerait, ils parleraient. The nous ending is pronounced approximately /jɔ̃/, and the vous ending approximately /je/: nous irions, vous devriez.

The stem-final r often helps listeners recognize the conditional, but this is only a useful clue, not an infallible test: context and the subject pronoun remain important for distinguishing the conditionnel from other forms in fast, connected speech.

Practice these forms aloud:

  • je parlerais
  • tu devrais
  • il pourrait
  • nous irions
  • vous devriez
  • ils seraient

In this chapter, you are expected to: pronounce common conditional advice forms intelligibly, including a clear stem and the appropriate person ending; and pronounce the nous and vous conditional endings clearly, including the /j/ glide in -ions and -iez. This chapter does not require you to distinguish the conditionnel from the futur simple by ear; that contrast is developed later in the volume.

Grammar: À ta place, je...

Purpose: teach the productive formation of the conditionnel présent; apply it to giving advice with devoir, pouvoir, and à ta place; contrast savoir and pouvoir; and introduce ne...que — without introducing the futur simple as a productive system, the subjunctive, or the full si-clause hypothesis.

9.1 Forming the conditionnel présent

Building Block — Conditional stem + imperfect endings
[infinitive-based or irregular conditional stem] + [-ais / -ais / -ait / -ions / -iez / -aient]
For regular -er and -ir verbs, use the infinitive as the conditional stem. For regular -re verbs, remove the final e. Add the endings -ais, -ais, -ait, -ions, -iez, -aient — the same endings used for the imparfait. A small set of high-frequency verbs have irregular stems, which you must simply learn, but which still take exactly the same endings. These stems will appear again in a later chapter when the future tense is introduced.
parler → je parlerais, tu parlerais, il/elle/on parlerait, nous parlerions, vous parleriez, ils/elles parleraient
I would speak, you would speak, he/she/one would speak, we would speak, you would speak, they would speak

9.2 Giving advice: tu devrais and tu pourrais

Language Focus

Devoir in the conditionnel, followed by an infinitive, gives advice that sounds like a genuine recommendation: "you should." Pouvoir in the conditionnel, followed by an infinitive, offers a softer, more optional suggestion: "you could." Choose between them based on how strongly you want to recommend the action.

  • Tu as mal à la gorge ? Tu devrais te reposer. You have a sore throat? You should rest.
  • Tu pourrais aussi aller à la pharmacie, si tu veux. You could also go to the pharmacy, if you want.
  • Vous devriez appeler un médecin. You should call a doctor. (formal/plural vous)

9.3 À ta place, je... : advice through imagined substitution

Building Block — Advice from someone else's position
[à ta place / à votre place] + [je] + [conditionnel]
This fixed frame lets the advice-giver imagine themselves in the other person's situation and say what they would do. The subject of the conditionnel verb is always je (the advice-giver), never tu, even though the advice is meant for the listener.
À ta place, je me reposerais aujourd'hui. À votre place, j'irais à la pharmacie dès que possible.
In your place, I would rest today. In your place, I would go to the pharmacy as soon as possible.

Both advice patterns are common. Tu devrais... gives a direct recommendation. À ta place, je... presents the advice as the speaker's imagined choice rather than a direct instruction. Either form may sound gentle or forceful depending on tone, relationship, and context; neither is automatically more polite than the other.

9.4 Savoir versus pouvoir: knowing how versus being able to

Language Focus

In this chapter, savoir + infinitive expresses knowing how to perform a learned action. Pouvoir + infinitive expresses ability or possibility depending on circumstances, permission, availability, or physical capacity. English "can" often covers both meanings, which is why French learners must check which one is intended before choosing a verb.

  • Je sais nager, mais je ne peux pas nager aujourd'hui. I know how to swim, but I can't swim today. sais = learned skill; peux = today's circumstance
  • Karim sait réparer une petite fuite, mais il ne peut pas le faire sans les bons outils. Karim knows how to fix a small leak, but he can't do it without the right tools. sait = skill; peut = circumstance (missing tools)
  • Je sais utiliser cette application, mais je ne peux pas l'utiliser maintenant parce que mon téléphone ne fonctionne pas. I know how to use this application, but I can't use it now because my phone isn't working.

9.5 Ne...que: expressing restriction

Building Block — A member of the negation family with a different meaning
[ne] + [verbe] + [que] + [élément restreint]
Ne...que surrounds the verb exactly like ne...pas and ne...plus (Ch. 3), but it does not negate the action itself: it restricts it to a single element, meaning "only." Que goes directly before the word or phrase being restricted, not necessarily right after the verb.
Je n'ai qu'un médicament à la maison. Le pharmacien ne travaille que le matin cette semaine.
I only have one medication at home. The pharmacist only works in the morning this week.

Ne...que is grammatically a restriction, not a true negation: Je n'ai qu'un médicament means "I have only one medication," not "I don't have a medication." Compare with true negation: Je n'ai pas de médicament ("I don't have any medication").

9.6 Recycling reflexive verbs in the compound past

Language Note — Recognizing model forms

These reflexive verbs recycle patterns introduced earlier. In this chapter, learners should recognize and use the model forms provided, such as elle s'est foulé la cheville and elle s'est sentie mieux. The detailed object analysis governing reflexive past-participle agreement is not introduced or assessed here.

Model Dialogue: Ce n'est pas dans mes habitudes

Context: Marc calls Nadia the evening before his important meeting in Bordeaux, because he has woken up with a sore throat. Register: informal, close friends, tu.

Marc : J'ai un imprévu franchement pas pratique : j'ai mal à la gorge, et j'ai une réunion importante demain matin. I have a really inconvenient setback: I have a sore throat, and I have an important meeting tomorrow morning.
Nadia : Oh non ! Tu devrais te reposer ce soir, et tu pourrais aussi boire quelque chose de chaud. Oh no! You should rest tonight, and you could also drink something hot.
Marc : Oui, mais je ne sais pas si je vais pouvoir parler normalement demain. C'est un vrai problème pour la réunion. Yes, but I don't know if I'm going to be able to speak normally tomorrow. It's a real problem for the meeting.
Nadia : À ta place, j'irais à la pharmacie ce soir, avant la fermeture. Le pharmacien pourrait sûrement te conseiller. In your place, I would go to the pharmacy tonight, before closing. The pharmacist could probably advise you.
Marc : Bonne idée. Mais je n'ai qu'une heure avant la fermeture. Je dois me dépêcher. Good idea. But I only have an hour before closing. I need to hurry.
Nadia : Est-ce que tu pourrais aussi reporter la réunion, au pire ? Could you also postpone the meeting, at worst?
Marc : Non, ce n'est pas dans mes habitudes de reporter une réunion importante, et surtout, je ne peux pas la reporter cette fois : mes collègues viennent de loin. No, it's not like me to postpone an important meeting, and especially, I can't postpone it this time: my colleagues are coming from far away.
Nadia : D'accord. Alors va vite à la pharmacie, repose-toi bien ce soir, et tiens-moi au courant demain. Okay. So go quickly to the pharmacy, rest well tonight, and keep me posted tomorrow.

This dialogue models the two advice patterns of this chapter (tu devrais / tu pourrais and à ta place, je...), the savoir/pouvoir contrast (je ne sais pas si je vais pouvoir parler normalement vs. je ne peux pas la reporter), and ne...que (je n'ai qu'une heure). No subjunctive form appears anywhere in this dialogue.

Culture and Communication

Culture in Context — The pharmacist as a point of contact for minor problems

In France, pharmacies are commonly marked by a green cross, and pharmacists are often consulted about minor symptoms and non-prescription products. A pharmacist may explain how to use an available product or advise a customer to seek medical care when symptoms may require professional evaluation. Pharmacy signs, professional roles, available products, and access to care vary across the Francophone world.

The situations in this chapter concern minor, non-emergency problems and are designed for language practice, not medical diagnosis or treatment.

Regional Variation — Giving advice among friends

Advice among friends is generally direct but softened by the conditionnel across the Francophone world; the degree of directness some speakers use, and the specific home remedies recommended for minor ailments, can vary by region, family, and generation. Tu devrais and à ta place are both broadly understood and used across French-speaking regions.

Common Mistakes

Common Mistake — Using tu with à ta place instead of je

Error: Saying à ta place, tu te reposerais.

Correction: The verb after à ta place is always in the je form, because the speaker imagines themselves in the listener's place: à ta place, je me reposerais.

Common Mistake — Using pouvoir where savoir is needed

Error: Saying Je ne peux pas nager to mean "I don't know how to swim" (a lack of learned skill, not a circumstantial obstacle).

Correction: Use savoir for learned skill: Je ne sais pas nager. Use pouvoir for circumstance, permission, availability, or physical condition: Je ne peux pas nager aujourd'hui, j'ai mal à la cheville.

Common Mistake — Treating ne...que as true negation

Error: Understanding Je n'ai qu'un médicament as "I don't have a medication."

Correction: Ne...que restricts rather than negates: Je n'ai qu'un médicament means "I have only one medication." True negation requires ne...pas: Je n'ai pas de médicament ("I don't have any medication").

Common Mistake — Forgetting the person ending on the conditionnel stem

Error: Saying *je devoir aller or leaving the stem unconjugated.

Correction: The conditionnel always requires stem + ending: je devrais aller. The stem alone (devr-) is never used as a complete verb form.

  2. sais ; peux

Listening Practice

  1. Nadia has sprained her ankle (tu t'es foulé la cheville).
  2. Tu devrais vraiment te reposer ce week-end: advises rest; tu pourrais mettre de la glace dessus: suggests icing the ankle.
  3. À ta place, j'irais quand même voir un médecin: Léa says she would still go see a doctor.
  4. Je n'ai qu'une heure de libre demain: restricts Léa's free time to one hour.
  5. Léa uses pouvoir: tu ne peux pas encore marcher normalement, describing a temporary, circumstantial limitation rather than a lack of learned skill.

Reading Practice

  1. The technician who was supposed to repair Karim's leak cancelled at the last minute.
  2. Je n'ai qu'un jour de libre cette semaine: restricts Karim's available time to one day.
  3. The friend's advice: je rappellerais (conditionnel of rappeler) and tu pourrais aussi demander (conditionnel of pouvoir).
  4. Karim uses pouvoir: je ne peux pas le faire sans les bons outils, because the obstacle is a lack of tools (circumstance), not a lack of knowledge — he explicitly says je sais réparer une petite fuite moi-même, en théorie.
  5. Karim decides to call the agency again that same day.

Chapter Review — Part D

Successful performance is a six-plus-sentence exchange that includes one use of tu devrais or tu pourrais, one use of à ta place, je..., one meaningful contrast using both savoir and pouvoir, and one use of ne...que. Solo learners must record the full exchange on their own to meet this requirement.

Writing Workshop — Model response

Modèle — Model exchange

Marc : Salut Nadia. Je me sens très fatigué depuis hier. J'ai aussi mal à la gorge. Je n'ai qu'une journée avant ma réunion importante.

Nadia : Tu devrais te reposer ce soir. Tu pourrais aussi aller à la pharmacie pour demander conseil.

Marc : Je sais présenter mon projet, mais je ne peux pas parler normalement aujourd'hui.

Nadia : À ta place, je préparerais aussi un message pour mes collègues.

Marc : Bonne idée. Je vais me reposer et boire quelque chose de chaud.

Nadia : D'accord. Tiens-moi au courant demain.

Marc: Hi Nadia. I've felt very tired since yesterday. I also have a sore throat. I only have one day before my important meeting. Nadia: You should rest tonight. You could also go to the pharmacy to ask for advice. Marc: I know how to speak for a long time during a meeting, but I can't speak normally today. Nadia: In your place, I would prepare a message for my colleagues in case the problem continues. Marc: Good idea. I'm going to rest and drink something hot. Nadia: Okay. Keep me posted tomorrow.

This model contains twelve complete sentences and demonstrates a clearly described problem, tu devrais and tu pourrais, a meaningful savoir/pouvoir contrast, à ta place, je..., ne...que, and a closing that acknowledges the advice, all in standard written negation. Other responses are acceptable when they meet all task requirements and remain within the grammar and vocabulary activated through Chapter 9.

Practical Performance Task — Model dialogue

Nadia : Salut Marc. Qu'est-ce qui ne va pas ?
Marc : Je me sens mal et j'ai mal à la gorge. Je n'ai qu'une journée avant une réunion importante.
Nadia : Depuis quand as-tu mal à la gorge ?
Marc : Depuis ce matin, mais je me sens de plus en plus fatigué.
Nadia : Tu devrais te reposer ce soir.
Marc : Oui, mais je ne sais pas si je peux parler normalement demain.
Nadia : Tu pourrais aller à la pharmacie pour demander conseil.
Marc : Je sais présenter mon projet, mais je ne peux pas parler normalement aujourd'hui.
Nadia : À ta place, je préparerais aussi un message pour tes collègues.
Marc : Bonne idée. Je vais faire cela. Merci pour tes conseils.
Nadia : De rien. Repose-toi bien.

This model demonstrates every required performance feature: an opening question; a clear minor problem; a genuine follow-up question (Depuis quand as-tu mal à la gorge ?) and a connected answer; tu devrais; tu pourrais; à ta place, je... + conditionnel; a meaningful savoir/pouvoir contrast (je sais présenter mon projet, mais je ne peux pas parler normalement aujourd'hui); ne...que; consistent tu register; acknowledgement of the advice; and an appropriate closing. Other dialogues that meet the task's requirements are equally valid.

Practical Performance Task

Successful completion requires all three components: a spoken exchange meeting every listed element, at least one follow-up question with a connected answer, and a written exchange of eight to twelve sentences containing every required element listed in the rubric above. Partial completion of any one component does not meet the task's requirements.

Looking Ahead

Ce que vous savez faire maintenant — What you can do now

You can form the conditionnel présent productively for regular and high-frequency irregular verbs, give advice using tu devrais/tu pourrais and à ta place, je..., distinguish learned ability with savoir from circumstantial ability or possibility with pouvoir, and use ne...que to express restriction.
