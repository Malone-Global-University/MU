Parcours personnels

Personal Paths · Speak and Write French: Everyday Independence and Connected Communication

Chapter Objectives

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

  1. Tell the connected story of your life so far, moving smoothly between childhood, school, and more recent events.
  2. Combine passé composé and imparfait in the same passage to distinguish completed events from background, description, and habitual past.
  3. Use sequencing connectors such as d'abord, ensuite, puis, à ce moment-là, and enfin to organize a multi-event narration.
  4. Ask and answer questions about someone else's parcours (path, journey) using recycled question forms.
  5. Recognize that the three written endings -ais, -ait, and -aient share the same /ɛ/ sound in the forms practiced here, and rely on context or a previously stated noun, not the ending itself, to identify the subject.
  6. Distinguish a single completed turning point from an ongoing life circumstance in both speech and writing.
  7. Read and write a short account of a personal history that a reader could follow from beginning to end.
  8. Maintain accurate agreement of past participles with être verbs across an extended narration.

Communication Scenario

A few weeks after their reunion at the Gare de Lyon-Part-Dieu, Marc and Nadia meet again for a coffee, this time by arrangement rather than by chance. Nadia wants to know the fuller story behind what Marc told her in a hurry at the station: how he ended up in Bordeaux, what his childhood was like, and what led him to change cities. Marc, in turn, wants to hear the complete version of Nadia's path through university, her exchange program, and her decision to accept a new job in Tours. This chapter is about that fuller kind of storytelling — not a quick update between trains, but a connected account of a life, told so that someone else can really follow it.

  1. Narrate the major stages of a personal history in a logical order.
  2. Distinguish background and description from specific completed events.
  3. Ask follow-up questions that invite someone to say more about their past.
  4. Write a connected account of your own path that a reader could follow easily.

This chapter introduces no tense beyond the passé composé / imparfait system already consolidated in Volume I and recycled in Chapter 1. The instructional goal of this chapter is not a new tense, but the skill of combining these two familiar tenses in one connected story — using the imparfait for background and the passé composé for events, in the same narration. What is new is the vocabulary and connectors needed to organize several past events into one coherent account. No additional past tense, mood, or advanced narrative system is introduced.

Pronunciation Focus

Pronunciation Focus — One sound, three written endings: the imparfait

When you narrate a personal history, you will use the imparfait constantly for background and description. The three written endings -ais, -ait, and -aient share the same /ɛ/ sound, close to the vowel in è. In other words, je grandissais, tu grandissais, il grandissait, and ils grandissaient all end in the same sound.

This creates a real listening challenge: before a consonant, il grandissait and ils grandissaient can sound completely identical, because il and ils themselves are pronounced the same way before a consonant. You cannot rely on the pronoun's sound here. Instead, you rely on context — a name or noun mentioned earlier in the sentence or the conversation — to know whether one person or several people are meant.

Two further points recycle and extend Chapter 1's pronunciation work:

  • Nous and vous forms. nous grandissions and vous grandissiez keep the full vowel of -ions / -iez and are pronounced distinctly from the three endings above; do not reduce them to the same sound.
  • Liaison before a vowel. Before a vowel-initial verb, liaison can make the plural audible even though it is not audible before a consonant: il habitait and ils‿habitaient sound different, because the s of ils links to the vowel. The same happens with elles‿étaient and nous‿étions. This is the same liaison system from Volume I and Chapter 1, applied here to new verb forms — not a new grammatical system.

In this chapter, you are expected to: recognize that -ais, -ait, and -aient share one sound; understand that il and ils sound the same before a consonant, so you must rely on context, not the pronoun's sound, to identify the subject; maintain the distinct nous / vous forms; and use liaison correctly to distinguish singular and plural before a vowel-initial imparfait form.

Grammar: Raconter un parcours

Purpose: deepen control of the passé composé / imparfait contrast for connected, multi-event narration, and add sequencing connectors and past-participle agreement recycling — without introducing any new tense or mood.

2.1 Two jobs, one story: événement vs. décor

Language Focus

When you tell a life story, every past-tense sentence does one of two jobs. The passé composé tells what happened — a specific event, at one point in time. The imparfait sets the scene — what things were like, or what someone used to do, while that event was happening. You already know both tenses from Volume I; this chapter's new skill is choosing the right one for each sentence in a longer story.

  • Quand j'étais petit, j'habitais à Marseille avec mes parents. When I was little, I lived in Marseille with my parents. imparfait: ongoing childhood situation, no single completed moment
  • À douze ans, j'ai déménagé à Lyon avec ma famille. At twelve, I moved to Lyon with my family. passé composé: one specific completed event
  • À l'époque, je détestais le changement, mais ce déménagement a tout changé pour moi. At the time, I hated change, but that move changed everything for me. imparfait (attitude/background) + passé composé (the event and its outcome)

2.2 Sequencing connectors for a multi-event story

Building Block — Ordering a life story
[d'abord] → [ensuite / puis] → [à ce moment-là] → [enfin]
A life story usually has more than two events. Connectors like these tell your listener or reader what order things happened in, so you do not have to start every sentence with a date.
D'abord, j'ai fait des études de droit à Lyon. Ensuite, j'ai décroché un premier emploi dans une petite entreprise. Puis, à ce moment-là, une amie m'a parlé d'un poste à Bordeaux. Enfin, j'ai décidé de changer de vie.
First, I studied law in Lyon. Next, I landed a first job at a small company. Then, at that moment, a friend told me about a position in Bordeaux. Finally, I decided to change my life.

Notice that every verb in the example above is in the passé composé, because each sentence reports a distinct completed step. The connectors organize the sequence; they do not change which tense is required.

2.3 Recognizing a turning point

Language Focus

A specific completed event that marks a tournant (turning point) — the moment everything changed — normally takes the passé composé, because it moves the story forward. But you can still describe that period or moment itself with the imparfait, as in C'était un tournant important ("It was an important turning point").

  • Je ne savais pas encore ce que je voulais faire, et puis, du jour au lendemain, j'ai décroché ce stage à Bordeaux. I still didn't know what I wanted to do, and then, overnight, I landed this internship in Bordeaux. imparfait (uncertainty, ongoing) → passé composé (the turning-point event)
  • C'est là que je me suis rendu compte que je voulais changer de vie. That's when I realized I wanted to change my life. se rendre compte (recognition-only at this stage) in the passé composé: the moment of realization itself

2.4 Recycling past-participle agreement with être

Language Note — Previously learned agreement — No new rule

A personal history is full of être-auxiliary verbs of movement and change of state: je suis né(e), elle est partie, nous sommes arrivés, ils sont devenus. As in Chapter 1, the past participle already agrees with the subject in these cases; this is the same agreement pattern from Volume I, now recycled across a longer narrative. Agreement with a preceding direct object is not introduced in this chapter and will be developed later in the volume.

Model Dialogue: Depuis le lycée...

Context: Marc and Nadia meet for coffee in Lyon, a few weeks after their reunion at the train station. Register: informal, close friends, tu.

Nadia : Alors, raconte ! Comment tu es passé de Lyon à Bordeaux, exactement ? So, tell me! How exactly did you go from Lyon to Bordeaux?
Marc : Eh bien, d'abord, il faut savoir que je suis né à Marseille. Quand j'étais petit, on déménageait souvent, à cause du travail de mon père. Well, first, you should know that I was born in Marseille. When I was little, we moved often, because of my father's work.
Nadia : Ah bon ? Je ne savais pas ça. Et comment c'était, ce genre de vie ? Really? I didn't know that. And what was that kind of life like?
Marc : Franchement, à l'époque, j'avais horreur de ça. Je détestais changer d'école tous les deux ans. Ensuite, à douze ans, on a déménagé à Lyon, et là, on est enfin restés. Honestly, at the time, I hated it. I hated changing schools every two years. Then, at twelve, we moved to Lyon, and there, we finally stayed.
Nadia : D'accord, ça explique le lycée ! Et après, tu as fait des études de quoi ? Okay, that explains high school! And afterward, what did you study?
Marc : J'ai fait des études de droit, ici, à Lyon. Puis j'ai obtenu mon diplôme, et j'ai décroché un premier emploi dans une petite entreprise. Mais je ne me sentais pas à ma place. I studied law, here, in Lyon. Then I got my degree, and I landed a first job at a small company. But I didn't feel like I belonged there.
Nadia : Et c'est là que Bordeaux est arrivé dans l'histoire ? And that's when Bordeaux came into the story?
Marc : Exactement. Une amie m'a parlé d'un poste là-bas, et je me suis rendu compte que je voulais changer de vie. Du jour au lendemain, j'ai décidé de partir. Et voilà, trois mois plus tard, j'étais à Bordeaux. Exactly. A friend told me about a position there, and I realized I wanted to change my life. Overnight, I decided to leave. And there you go, three months later, I was in Bordeaux.
Nadia : Quel tournant ! Bon, à mon tour maintenant... What a turning point! Okay, my turn now...

This dialogue combines imparfait (background: on déménageait, j'avais horreur de ça, je ne me sentais pas à ma place) with passé composé (events: on a déménagé, j'ai fait des études, j'ai décidé de partir), organized with d'abord, ensuite, and puis, using the productive and receptive vocabulary of the chapter. Learners should understand the recognition-first expression se rendre compte que, but they are not required to reproduce it in speaking or writing.

Speaking Practice

Pratique contrôlée — Controlled practice: décor ou événement ?

With a partner, take turns reading the sentence pairs below aloud. For each pair, identify together which sentence sets the scene (imparfait) and which reports a completed event (passé composé), then say both sentences in the correct order to form a short two-sentence story.

Paire 1

a) Je voulais changer de vie.

b) J'ai décroché un nouvel emploi à Tours.

Paire 2

a) Nous avons déménagé à Lyon quand j'avais douze ans.

b) Avant, nous habitions à Marseille.

Paire 3

a) Elle ne savait pas encore ce qu'elle voulait faire.

b) Un jour, elle a rencontré la bonne personne au bon moment.

Paire 4

a) J'ai obtenu mon diplôme en juin.

b) À l'époque, j'étais très nerveux pour l'avenir.

For each pair, the imparfait sentence sets the background and the passé composé sentence reports the completed event; the natural order is usually background first, event second.

Pratique guidée — Guided practice: trois étapes de sa vie

Before telling a full autobiography, practice a shorter, guided version with a partner. Choose one of the three prompt sets below (or invent your own three stages) and tell the mini-story aloud, filling in the blanks with true or invented information.

Modèle guidé 1 — L'école

Quand j'étais petit(e), j'habitais à ___. D'abord, j'allais à l'école à ___. Ensuite, à ___ ans, ___ (passé composé). C'était un vrai tournant.

Modèle guidé 2 — Le travail

À l'époque, je voulais devenir ___. D'abord, j'ai fait des études de ___. Ensuite, j'ai décroché un emploi ___. Puis, ___ (tournant, passé composé).

Modèle guidé 3 — Un déménagement

Avant, nous habitions à ___. À l'époque, ___ (description à l'imparfait). Un jour, ___ (passé composé). Enfin, nous sommes ___ (arriver/rester) à ___.

Each guided model requires at least one imparfait background sentence, at least two passé composé events, and at least one sequencing connector, in the same pattern you will use for the full autobiography below. Your partner should listen for these three elements and tell you if one is missing.

Tâche communicative — Communicative task: mon parcours en cinq étapes

With a partner, take turns telling the story of your life (real or invented) in five connected stages, using d'abord, ensuite, puis, and enfin to organize it. Your account must include, at minimum:

  • one sentence describing your childhood or early background in the imparfait;
  • at least three specific completed events in the passé composé;
  • at least two sequencing connectors;
  • one clearly identified turning point;
  • at least one question your partner asks you about your path, and a connected answer;
  • appropriate informal register between friends throughout.

Do not read from a script; speak from the vocabulary and patterns in this chapter.

Pratique individuelle — Solo alternative

Record yourself narrating a personal history (your own or an invented character's). Your recording must include, in order:

  1. a background description of childhood or early circumstances (imparfait);
  2. at least three completed events told in sequence, using connectors (passé composé);
  3. one clearly marked turning point;
  4. an imagined follow-up question (say it as if someone had asked it) and a connected answer;
  5. a brief closing statement about where the person is now.

A narration that only lists events without any background description, or that does not mark a turning point, does not meet this task's requirements.

Writing Workshop

Complete the following linked writing tasks in order.

Tâche unique — Mon parcours personnel: Write an account of ten to fourteen sentences telling the connected story of your life so far (real or invented), from childhood to the present. Your account must include:

  • a background description of childhood or early circumstances, using the imparfait;
  • at least four specific completed events, using the passé composé;
  • at least three sequencing connectors (d'abord, ensuite, puis, enfin, or similar);
  • one clearly identified turning point;
  • correct past-participle agreement with être verbs where relevant;
  • a closing sentence connecting the story to your present situation.

This is a piece of neutral, connected writing rather than a spoken exchange; retain full standard negation with ne throughout, as in the reading passage above.

  1. Plan your account. List four or five key stages of the story before you begin writing.
  2. Draft the background. Describe the ongoing situation or feeling at the start of the story, using the imparfait.
  3. Draft the events. Narrate the completed stages in order, using the passé composé and sequencing connectors, as in the model reading passage.
  4. Mark the turning point. Make it clear to the reader which moment changed the direction of the story.
  5. Draft your closing. Connect the story to where things stand today.
  6. Revise. Confirm that background sentences use the imparfait, that event sentences use the passé composé, and that agreement, accents, and punctuation are accurate throughout.

  • My events follow a logical chronological order that a reader can easily understand.
  • Past-participle agreement with être verbs is correct where it applies.

Culture and Communication

Culture in Context — Telling a life story among friends

Among friends, French speakers often narrate a personal history informally, with interruptions, questions, and reactions from the listener, rather than as an uninterrupted monologue. Phrases such as alors, raconte ! ("go on, tell me!") invite this kind of interactive storytelling. In more formal contexts, such as a job interview or a written biography, the same events are usually organized into a smoother, less interrupted account, closer to the model reading passage in this chapter.

Regional Variation — Talking about moving and changing cities

Moving between cities for work or study, as Marc and Nadia both do in this chapter, is common throughout France and other French-speaking regions, though the reasons and the emotional weight attached to leaving one's home region can vary considerably by individual and by community. Expressions such as un tournant and changer de vie are broadly understood across the Francophone world and are not limited to any one region.

Common Mistakes

Common Mistake — Using passé composé for ongoing background

Error: Saying Quand j'ai été petit, j'ai habité à Marseille to describe an ongoing childhood situation.

Correction: Use the imparfait for background and ongoing states: Quand j'étais petit, j'habitais à Marseille. The passé composé forms above would incorrectly suggest that being a child and living in Marseille were single completed events rather than an ongoing situation.

Common Mistake — Using imparfait for a clearly completed turning point

Error: Saying Du jour au lendemain, je décidais de partir to report a single decisive decision.

Correction: The specific completed event that marks a turning point normally takes the passé composé: Du jour au lendemain, j'ai décidé de partir. The expression du jour au lendemain itself signals a sudden, completed change, which is a strong clue toward the passé composé. Note that you can still describe the turning point itself with the imparfait, as in C'était un vrai tournant ("It was a real turning point").

Common Mistake — Trying to hear il vs. ils before a consonant

Error: Assuming that il grandissait and ils grandissaient must sound different because they are spelled differently, and listening for that difference in the pronoun.

Correction: As explained in the Pronunciation Focus above, before a consonant il and ils sound the same, and -ait and -aient share the same sound. Rely on context or a noun mentioned earlier, not the pronoun's sound, to identify the subject. Before a vowel, liaison does help: il habitait versus ils‿habitaient are audibly different.

Common Mistake — Overloading a sentence with connectors

Error: Writing D'abord, ensuite, puis, j'ai fait des études de droit, stacking multiple sequencing connectors before a single event.

Correction: Use one sequencing connector per stage of the story, in order: D'abord, j'ai fait des études de droit. Ensuite, j'ai décroché un emploi. Each connector should introduce a new stage, not accumulate before the same event.

Turning pointThe learner clearly identifies one moment that changed the direction of the story.
InteractionThe learner asks or answers at least one follow-up question about the path being described.
Agreement accuracyPast-participle agreement with être verbs is correct where relevant.
Register controlThe learner maintains standard written negation with ne in the written component and appropriate informal spoken register in the spoken component.
Pronunciation and intelligibilityThe learner recognizes that the three written imparfait endings share one sound and uses context or a previously stated noun to identify the subject when forms sound identical, and uses liaison to make singular versus plural audible before a vowel.
Written-project completenessThe account contains ten to fourteen sentences and includes all required background, event, connector, turning-point, and closing elements.
Scope and accuracyProductive language comes from Volume I, Volume II Chapter 1, and Chapter 2, with accurate spelling, agreement, verb forms, accents, and punctuation.
Solo equivalenceA solo learner records the complete narration and imagined follow-up exchange, providing evidence equivalent to the partner task.

This chapter introduces no new tense or mood. Its new productive material is limited to sequencing connectors, turning-point vocabulary, and deliberate passé composé / imparfait contrast at connected-discourse length, as authorized by the Volume II matrices for Chapter 2.

Answer Key

Model Dialogue — Comprehension

  1. Marc's family moved often because of his father's work; at the time, he hated it, especially changing schools every two years.
  2. Marc's turning point was hearing about a position in Bordeaux from a friend and realizing he wanted to change his life; because of this, he decided to leave, overnight, and moved to Bordeaux three months later.

Chapter Review — Part B

  1. Quand elle était jeune, elle voulait devenir architecte.
  2. Un jour, elle a rencontré la bonne personne au bon moment.
  3. Nous habitions à Marseille avant de déménager à Lyon.
  4. Ils ont décidé de changer de vie du jour au lendemain.

Listening Practice

  1. Karim grew up in Toulouse, in a fairly modest family; at the time, he wanted to become an architect, but his parents did not have much money for schooling.
  2. His first job was at a bank, which he took just to earn a living; he hated it.
  3. Imparfait (background): Il détestait ce travail. Passé composé (event): Il a décroché un emploi dans une banque.
  4. The turning point was meeting an architect during a project, which made him realize he could still change his life; he decided, overnight, to go back to school.

Reading Practice

  1. Nadia was born in Tours but grew up in Lyon.
  2. At the time, she dreamed of traveling, but she did not yet know how.
  3. Imparfait: elle rêvait de voyager. Passé composé: elle a fait des études de communication à Lyon.
  4. Nadia's turning point was hearing about the exchange program in Spain during her last year of studies and realizing it was the opportunity she had been waiting for; the passage signals this with elle s'est rendu compte que c'était l'occasion qu'elle attendait depuis toujours.
  5. Model answer: Nadia was born in Tours, but she grew up in Lyon, where her parents worked at a hospital. She studied communication in Lyon, and during her last year, she joined an exchange program in Spain, where she learned a great deal. She then returned to France, and next month she is starting a new job back in Tours. Other reasonable summaries that follow the passage's chronology, expressed in the learner's own words, are acceptable.

Chapter Review — Part C

The turning point is marked by Ensuite, pendant sa dernière année, elle a entendu parler d'un programme d'échange en Espagne, et elle s'est rendu compte que c'était l'occasion qu'elle attendait depuis toujours, introduced by the connector ensuite.

Chapter Review — Part D

Successful performance is a six-plus-sentence narration that includes one background description in the imparfait, at least three completed events in the passé composé, at least two sequencing connectors, and one clearly marked turning point. Solo learners must record the complete narration on their own to meet this requirement.

Writing Workshop

Successful work contains ten to fourteen sentences and includes a background description in the imparfait, at least four completed events in the passé composé, at least three sequencing connectors, one clearly identified turning point, correct past-participle agreement with être verbs where relevant, and a closing sentence connecting the story to the present, all in neutral written prose that retains ne throughout.

Solo Alternative

Successful performance includes a background description, at least three sequenced completed events, one clearly marked turning point, an imagined follow-up question with a connected answer, and a brief closing statement about the present.

Practical Performance Task

Successful completion requires all three components: a spoken narration meeting every listed element, at least one follow-up question with a connected answer, and a written account of ten to fourteen sentences containing every required element listed in the rubric above. Partial completion of any one component does not meet the task's requirements.

Looking Ahead

Ce que vous savez faire maintenant — What you can do now

You can tell the connected story of a life from background to turning point, organize several past events in a logical sequence, distinguish description from completed action while speaking and writing, and recognize the imparfait's shared spoken ending across several different subjects.
