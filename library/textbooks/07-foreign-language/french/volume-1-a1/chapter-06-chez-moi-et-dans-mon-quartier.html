Chez moi et dans mon quartier

Home and Neighborhood · Speak and Write French: Foundations of Practical Communication

Chapter Objectives

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

  1. Name a small set of rooms in a home and a small set of neighborhood places.
  2. Use il y a with an article or a quantity to say what is present somewhere.
  3. Locate people and things using a controlled set of location expressions: dans, devant, à côté de, en face de, and près de.
  4. Choose the correct contracted form (au, aux, du, des) or unchanged combination (à la, à l', de la, de l') with support.
  5. Select the demonstrative determiner ce, cet, cette, or ces to point out a specific room or place, with support.
  6. Recognize the fixed expression il n'y a pas de... and use it optionally in a supported model.
  7. Complete a short, supported spoken and written description of a home and its immediate neighborhood.

Communication Scenario

Léa, the new student from Chapter 5, invites Amina over to see her apartment for the first time. Amina asks about the rooms, what is in them, and what the neighborhood is like nearby. This chapter opens Unit II: La vie quotidienne, where you move from talking about people to talking about the everyday spaces people live in.

  1. This chapter introduces il y a, contractions with à and de, a small set of location expressions, and demonstrative determiners. It does not introduce any new verb conjugation, and it does not develop full negation.
  2. Tu and vous, question formation, and the vocabulary from Chapters 1–5 remain active throughout. Use them alongside the new material in this chapter.

Pronunciation: Elision and Nasal Vowels in Place Vocabulary

Pronunciation Focus — Elision and nasal vowels you already know

This chapter reviews two pronunciation and spelling systems you have already begun, applied to new vocabulary.

Elision. Before a vowel sound, le and la become l', and the vowel is written with an apostrophe instead of being pronounced as a separate syllable. This chapter's vocabulary includes several elided nouns: l'école, l'appartement, and the supported word l'hôpital. Say each one as a single smooth syllable group rather than pausing between the article and the noun.

Nasal vowels. Several words in this chapter contain nasal vowels, and different spellings represent different nasal vowel sounds: the -in of jardin, the -on of salon, and the en in the final -ment of appartement each represent a distinct nasal vowel, not the same sound spelled three ways. Listen for the vowel quality; do not assume that a nasal spelling always leaves the following consonant silent. In banque, for example, the nasal vowel is followed by a clearly pronounced /k/ sound.

Language Focus: Il y a, Location, and Contractions

Purpose: describe what is present in a home or neighborhood, say where things are located, and point to specific rooms or places using demonstrative determiners.

6.1 Il y a — Saying what there is

Language Focus

Il y a means "there is" or "there are." It does not change for number: the same form is used whether one thing or several things are present. The pattern practiced in this chapter is il y a followed by an article or a quantity and then a noun.

Il y a — Form and use
FormExampleMeaning
il y a + article + nounIl y a une cuisine.There is a kitchen.
il y a + quantity + nounIl y a deux chambres.There are two bedrooms.
il y a + article + nounIl y a un parc.There is a park.

In the patterns practiced here, use il y a to present the existence of something, and use être with a location expression to locate something already identified. This is a helpful working distinction for this chapter's tasks; it is not a complete account of every context in which French uses these structures.

A supported fixed expression. You may see or hear il n'y a pas de..., meaning "there is no..." or "there are no...," as in the model dialogue's il n'y a pas de jardin. Recognize this expression and, if you like, reuse it exactly as modeled. You are not asked to build new negative sentences on your own in this chapter; a full explanation and practice of negation begins in Chapter 7.

6.2 Locating people and things

Building Block — Location pattern
[singular thing or person] + est + [location expression] + [reference point]
Use être to say where something is located, combined with one of the five location expressions from the vocabulary table above. In this chapter, the productive model uses a singular subject with est. Previously encountered fixed forms such as ce sont les chambres may still appear for recognition, but unrestricted plural sont is not a new production target here. Location expressions ending in de, such as à côté de and près de, combine with le and les the same way de does everywhere else, using the contractions below.
La cuisine est dans la maison. La boulangerie est à côté du marché.
The kitchen is in the house. The bakery is next to the market.

6.3 Contractions with à and de

Language Focus

À and de combine with the definite articles le and les to form a single contracted word: au, aux, du, and des. Before la or an elided l', à and de do not contract; they remain separate words.

À and de with the definite article
Articleà + articlede + article
leaudu
lesauxdes
laà lade la
l'à l'de l'

Au, aux, du, and des are true contractions: a single word replaces two. À la, à l', de la, and de l' are combinations that stay as two separate words.

  • Il y a une boulangerie au marché. There is a bakery at the market. (à + le → au)
  • La banque est près du parc. The bank is near the park. (de + le → du)
  • L'école est à côté de la banque. The school is next to the bank. (à + la stays à la)
  • Le parc est en face de l'hôpital. The park is across from the hospital. (de + l' stays de l')

6.4 Demonstrative determiners

Building Block — Pointing to something specific
ce (m.) / cet (m., before a vowel sound) / cette (f.) / ces (pl.) + noun
Cet is used before a masculine singular noun beginning with a vowel sound or a mute h. Its final t is pronounced, which prevents two vowel sounds from meeting directly. Ces is used for all plural nouns, whether masculine or feminine.
ce salon, cet appartement, cette chambre, ces fenêtres
this living room, this apartment, this bedroom, these windows

At this stage, ce, cet, cette, and ces are practiced for pointing out a specific item in view, without the added suffixes -ci and -là, which are outside this chapter's scope.

Model Dialogue: Bienvenue chez moi

Supported vocabulary in this dialogue: le balcon (the balcony), l'immeuble (the apartment building).

Context: Léa welcomes Amina to her apartment for the first time.

Léa : Bienvenue chez moi ! Voici le salon, et là, c'est la cuisine. Welcome to my place! Here's the living room, and there, that's the kitchen.
Amina : C'est joli ! Est-ce qu'il y a un jardin ? It's pretty! Is there a garden?
Léa : Non, il n'y a pas de jardin, mais il y a un balcon. Et là, ce sont les chambres. No, there isn't a garden, but there's a balcony. And over there, those are the bedrooms.
Amina : Comment est ton quartier ? What is your neighborhood like?
Léa : Il y a une boulangerie devant l'immeuble, et le parc est à côté de l'école. There is a bakery in front of the building, and the park is next to the school.
Amina : La banque est loin ? Is the bank far?
Léa : Non, elle est près du marché. No, it's near the market.

Notice how il y a introduces new information (il y a un balcon), while être locates something already known (le parc est à côté de l'école). The negative expression il n'y a pas de jardin is a fixed formula for recognition in this chapter.

  1. Name the type of home: J'habite dans un appartement / une maison.
  2. Name two rooms: Il y a...
  3. Make one il y a sentence about something in a room.

Culture and Communication

Culture in Context — Housing in Francophone cities

In many urban Francophone contexts, learners may encounter apartment buildings, neighborhood shops, public markets, schools, and parks. Housing patterns and access to services vary widely by city, region, income, and local infrastructure, so no single description applies to every Francophone community.

Regional Variation — The same word, different settings

The vocabulary in this chapter is used productively across France and much of the Francophone world, but what a word describes can vary. A marché may be open-air, indoor, permanent, or weekly, depending on the community; the term itself does not indicate which. This is a difference in local life, not in the language, and none of these forms is more "correct" than another.

Common Mistakes

Common Mistake — Forgetting to contract à or de with le and les

Error: Writing à le parc or de le marché instead of using the required contraction.

Correction: à + le always becomes au, and de + le always becomes du: au parc, du marché. Only la and l' stay unchanged, as à la, à l', de la, and de l'.

Common Mistake — Confusing il y a and être for location

Error: Introducing something already named with il y a instead of locating it with être.

Correction: In the patterns practiced here, use il y a only to introduce new information: Il y a un parc. Once the thing is named, locate it with être: Le parc est à côté de l'école.

Common Mistake — Using ce instead of cet or cette

Error: Writing ce appartement or ce chambre.

Correction: Use cet before a masculine noun beginning with a vowel sound: cet appartement. Use cette for any feminine singular noun: cette chambre.
