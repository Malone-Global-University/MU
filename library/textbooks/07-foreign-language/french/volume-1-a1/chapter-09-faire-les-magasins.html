Faire les magasins

Shopping · Speak and Write French: Foundations of Practical Communication

Chapter Objectives

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

  1. Identify common clothing and shopping items.
  2. Describe clothing using color and other common adjectives, with correct gender and number agreement and correct placement.
  3. Count and use the numbers 0–100 needed to state and understand prices.
  4. Ask and state prices, and ask about color and size using controlled, practical expressions.
  5. Complete a short, polite service encounter in a clothing shop.
  6. Recognize and begin to use the direct object pronouns le, la, l', and les before a single conjugated verb to refer back to a familiar item.
  7. Read a short shop advertisement and write a short shopping note or product description.

Communication Scenario

Léa needs a new jacket before winter arrives, so she goes to a clothing shop in her neighborhood. A salesperson helps her look at two jackets, ask about size, and decide which one to buy. This chapter continues Unit II, La vie quotidienne, by completing its sequence of everyday practical communication: after home and neighborhood (Chapter 6), daily routines (Chapter 7), and food and drink (Chapter 8), learners now practice a shopping transaction that requires describing items, asking about size and price, and referring back to an item without repeating its name.

  1. This chapter introduces adjective placement and agreement at an ordinary production level, numbers 0–100, prices, bounded size language, and an early, controlled introduction to the direct object pronouns le, la, and les before one conjugated verb.
  2. This chapter does not introduce comparison, explanation with parce que, relative clauses, object pronouns before an infinitive, or unscheduled spelling-change verbs. Those systems belong to later chapters. Vocabulary and grammar from Chapters 1–8, including the partitive article, quantity expressions, and polite requests with je voudrais, remain active and should be recycled wherever natural.

Pronunciation: Numbers, Liaison, and Enchaînement

Pronunciation Focus — Numbers before a vowel-initial word

Several numbers change how they sound depending on what follows them. It is important to distinguish two separate systems: liaison, in which a normally silent final consonant is pronounced only because a vowel-initial word follows, and enchaînement, in which a final consonant that is already pronounced simply carries forward smoothly onto the next vowel.

  • six livres [si livʁ] — before a consonant, the final x of six is silent.
  • six euros [siz øʁo] — before a vowel, the normally silent x is pronounced [z] and links forward. This is liaison.
  • dix euros [diz øʁo] — the same liaison pattern applies to dix: a normally silent final consonant appears as [z] only before a vowel.
  • huit livres [ɥi livʁ] — before a consonant, the final t of huit is normally not pronounced.
  • huit euros [ɥit øʁo] — before a vowel, the final t of huit is pronounced [t] and connects smoothly to the following vowel. This connection is generally treated as enchaînement rather than liaison, because here the [t] belongs to the number's own pronounced form in this environment rather than being a normally silent letter activated only by the following vowel, as happens with the [z] in six euros and dix euros.

vingt et un: the final t of vingt is pronounced here, giving [vɛ̃t e œ̃]. un keeps its nasal vowel and does not end with a pronounced [n].

Language Focus: Adjectives, Prices, and Direct Objects

Purpose: describe clothing precisely, ask about size, state and understand prices from 0 to 100 euros, and refer back to an item already mentioned without repeating its name.

9.1 Adjective placement

Language Focus

Most French adjectives, including all the color adjectives in this chapter, are placed after the noun they describe. A small set of very common, short adjectives are placed before the noun instead. This chapter uses two of them productively: grand (big, tall) and petit (small). Both still agree in gender and number with the noun.

  • une veste noire a black jacket
  • un grand manteau a big coat
  • une petite jupe verte a small green skirt petite (before noun) + jupe + verte (after noun)

Do not assume every descriptive adjective goes before the noun, as it typically does in English. Other before-noun adjectives exist in French, but they are not activated for production in this chapter.

Building Block — Asking and stating a price
Combien coûte [item] ? — [Item] coûte [number] euros.
Use combien to ask "how much," and the verb coûter ("to cost") to state the price. The number precedes euros, which stays plural except after un. Centimes may be added with et: dix euros et cinquante centimes.
Combien coûte cette veste ? — Elle coûte quarante-cinq euros.
How much does this jacket cost? — It costs forty-five euros.

9.3 Asking about size

Language Focus

Use the following controlled expressions to ask about and respond to questions of size and fit.

  • Quelle taille faites-vous ? What size do you wear?
  • Je fais du 38. I'm a size 38.
  • Je cherche une taille M. I'm looking for a size M.
  • Vous avez cette veste en taille L ? Do you have this jacket in size L?
  • C'est trop grand. / C'est trop petit. It's too big. / It's too small.
  • Cette taille est bonne pour moi. This size is good for me.

Clothing-size numbers and letters vary across Francophone regions and even across individual stores. This chapter uses the number and letter sizing common in France as the default productive model; treat other regional sizing systems as receptive or comparative information rather than a second system to memorize.

Chapter boundary: This chapter does not use comparative expressions such as plus cher que or moins cher que. Comparison is grammatically possible in French with many adjectives, including colors, but it is introduced productively in a later chapter. For now, state facts about items directly rather than comparing them.

9.4 Introducing direct object pronouns: le, la, les

Language Focus

A direct object receives the action of the verb directly, without a preposition: in I'll take the jacket, "the jacket" is the direct object of "take." French can replace a direct-object noun phrase with a short pronoun placed directly before the conjugated verb: le (masculine singular), la (feminine singular), or les (plural). Before a vowel sound, le and la both elide to l'.

Les pronoms compléments d'objet direct — Direct object pronoun forms
PronounReplacesExample
le / l'a masculine singular nounJe prends le pull. → Je le prends.
la / l'a feminine singular nounJe prends la veste. → Je la prends.
lesany plural nounJe prends les chaussures. → Je les prends.
  • Tu aimes cette veste ? — Oui, je l'aime beaucoup. Do you like this jacket? — Yes, I like it a lot.
  • Tu prends le pantalon ? — Non, je ne le prends pas. Are you taking the pants? — No, I'm not taking them.

At this stage, place le, la, and les only directly before a single conjugated verb, as shown above. Placement with infinitives, compound tenses, and combinations with other pronouns is developed in later chapters. This pronoun system is newly introduced in this chapter: expect controlled, guided practice rather than independent mastery.

This chapter recycles negation with ne...pas from Chapter 7 directly around the new object pronoun: notice that ne moves in front of the pronoun, as in je ne le prends pas.

Elision Reminder — le / la before a vowel

le and la become l' before a vowel or a silent h muet, as in l'hiver (the winter). They do not elide before an h aspiré, a small set of words where the initial h blocks elision and liaison even though no consonant is pronounced, as in le hall (the [store] hall/lobby). You do not need to memorize the full h aspiré list now; simply recognize that it exists and that not every vowel-like beginning triggers elision.

Model Dialogue: Faire les magasins

Context: Léa is shopping for a winter jacket at a clothing shop. A salesperson helps her.

Vendeuse : Bonjour ! Vous cherchez une veste ? Hello! Are you looking for a jacket?
Léa : Bonjour. Je cherche une veste pour l'hiver. Hello. I'm looking for a jacket for winter.
Vendeuse : Il y a une veste grise et une veste noire. Voici les deux vestes. There's a gray jacket and a black jacket. Here are both jackets.
Léa : Merci. Combien coûte la veste noire ? Thank you. How much does the black jacket cost?
Vendeuse : Elle coûte soixante-quinze euros. La veste grise coûte cinquante euros. It costs seventy-five euros. The gray jacket costs fifty euros.
Léa : Je peux essayer la veste grise ? Can I try on the gray jacket?
Vendeuse : Bien sûr. Quelle taille faites-vous ? Of course. What size do you wear?
Léa : Je fais du 38. I'm a size 38.
Léa tries on the gray jacket.
Léa : Cette taille est bonne pour moi. J'aime beaucoup cette veste. This size is good for me. I like this jacket a lot.
Vendeuse : Et la veste noire ? Vous ne l'aimez pas ? And the black jacket? You don't like it?
Léa : Si, mais je prends la veste grise. Je la prends, s'il vous plaît. Yes I do, but I'll take the gray jacket. I'll take it, please.
Vendeuse : Très bien. Ça coûte cinquante euros. Et avec ça ? Very good. That comes to fifty euros. Anything else?
Léa : C'est tout, merci beaucoup. That's all, thank you very much.

Note the affirmative si used to contradict a negative question (Vous ne l'aimez pas ? — Si...), a common conversational pattern presented here formulaically.

Culture and Communication

Culture in Context — Shopping and trying things on

In many French clothing shops, a salesperson will typically greet a customer on entry and offer help, and it is common courtesy to return the greeting rather than to ignore it. Asking to try on an item before buying is standard and expected practice, not a special request, and taking time to check size and price on a few options is generally seen as ordinary rather than indecisive.

Regional Variation — Currency and sizing

This chapter uses euros, the currency used in France and many other European Francophone regions, and clothing sizes common in France. In Quebec, prices are given in Canadian dollars (dollars) and sizing more often follows North American conventions; in parts of West and Central Africa, prices are commonly given in the West African or Central African CFA franc (franc CFA). The sentence patterns Ça coûte... and Je fais du... remain the same across regions; only the currency name and the size numbers or letters change. This textbook uses euros and French sizing as the default productive model.

Common Mistakes

Common Mistake — Placing every adjective before the noun

Error: Saying une noire veste, following English adjective order.

Correction: Most French adjectives, including all colors, follow the noun: une veste noire. Only a small set of common short adjectives such as grand and petit come before the noun.

Common Mistake — Forgetting object pronoun agreement

Error: Saying je le prends to refer back to la veste.

Correction: Since la veste is feminine, use la: je la prends. Match the pronoun to the gender and number of the noun it replaces, not to the noun's meaning.

Common Mistake — Separating the pronoun from the verb with ne...pas

Error: Saying je ne prends les pas instead of keeping the pronoun directly before the verb.

Correction: The object pronoun stays directly before the conjugated verb, and ne...pas wraps around both: je ne les prends pas.

Common Mistake — Placing a pronoun before an infinitive

Error: Saying Je peux la essayer ? or Je peux l'essayer ? to ask about trying on an item.

Correction: At this stage, keep the noun after the infinitive instead: Je peux essayer cette veste ? Placing an object pronoun before an infinitive is a later-chapter system.
