Pronunciation Review

Pronunciation Review — Liaison, enchaînement, and elision across Unit III

This chapter reviews connected-speech features already introduced in Chapters 11–14. No new pronunciation rule is presented here.

  • vous allez — liaison: the normally silent -s of vous is pronounced [z] before the vowel-initial verb.
  • on a — liaison: the normally silent final consonant of on is pronounced before the vowel-initial verb a.
  • plus intéressant — liaison: the final -s of plus links as [z] to the vowel-initial adjective.
  • avec une amie — enchaînement: the already-pronounced final consonant of avec links smoothly onto the following vowel, with no added sound.
  • l'après-midi and j'ai — elision: the vowel of le/la and je is replaced by an apostrophe before a vowel-initial word.

Practice drill: Read each pair aloud, and identify whether it shows liaison, enchaînement, or elision.

  1. on a mal
  2. avec une collègue
  3. j'ai mal à la tête
  4. vous allez tout droit

Language Review: Unit III Systems

Purpose: combine directions, weather and plans, health and advice, and comparisons and relative clauses in connected communication. This chapter introduces no new grammar system; it recycles and integrates Chapters 11–14, entirely in present time and the near future.

15.1 Directions and y

Language Focus — Review

Recall from Chapter 11 that direction-giving relies on the vous-form imperative and on y, which replaces a place already introduced with à and is placed before the conjugated verb.

  • Vous allez à la pharmacie ? Continuez tout droit, puis tournez à gauche. On y va ensemble. Are you going to the pharmacy? Continue straight ahead, then turn left. We're going there together.

15.2 Weather and near-future plans

Language Focus — Review

Recall from Chapter 12 that on is a frequent spoken subject pronoun for "we," that aller + infinitive expresses the near future, and that weather is described with impersonal il expressions. A weather statement and a plan are presented as two separate present-time sentences, not as a conditional clause.

  • Il fait beau samedi. On va faire une promenade au parc. The weather is nice Saturday. We're going to take a walk in the park.
  • Il pleut dimanche. On va rester à la maison. It's raining Sunday. We're going to stay home.

15.3 Fictional symptoms, necessity, and advice

Language Focus — Review

Recall from Chapter 13 that avoir mal à describes a symptom, and that devoir, pouvoir, and il faut express necessity, ability, and advice, each followed directly by an infinitive. All symptoms practiced in this chapter are fictional.

  • Marc a mal à la tête. Il faut se reposer. Il peut aussi boire de l'eau. Marc has a headache. He needs to rest. He can also drink water.

15.4 Indirect objects, comparison, and relative clauses

Building Block Review — Combining Chapter 14 structures
[lui/leur] + verb ... / [noun] + plus/moins/aussi + adjective + que + [noun] ... / [noun] + qui/que/où + clause
Lui or leur identifies the recipient of an action, plus/moins/aussi... que makes a comparison between two nouns, and qui, que, or où begins a genuine relative clause that adds information about a noun already named. Each structure can appear in its own sentence, or two ideas may be joined lightly, as long as no new complement-clause pattern is introduced.
Je donne les informations sur le quartier à Marc. Ce quartier est plus calme que le centre-ville. C'est le quartier où j'habite.
I give the information about the neighborhood to Marc. This neighborhood is quieter than downtown. It's the neighborhood where I live.
Je lui donne les informations sur le quartier qui est près de l'université.
I give him the information about the neighborhood that is near the university.

Model Dialogue: Une journée dans le quartier

Context: Léa helps Marc, a new exchange student, find his way, talk about the weekend weather, and mention a fictional minor symptom.

Marc : Excuse-moi, Léa. Je cherche la bibliothèque. Excuse me, Léa. I'm looking for the library.
Léa : Continue tout droit, puis tourne à gauche après la banque. C'est le bâtiment qui a une grande porte bleue. On y va ensemble ? Continue straight ahead, then turn left after the bank. It's the building that has a big blue door. Shall we go there together?
Marc : D'accord. Quel temps fait-il samedi ? Okay. What's the weather like Saturday?
Léa : Il fait beau samedi. On va faire une promenade dans le parc. Il pleut dimanche. On va rester à la maison. The weather is nice Saturday. We're going to take a walk in the park. It's raining Sunday. We're going to stay home.
(Marc s'arrête un moment.)
Marc : J'ai mal à la tête. I have a headache.
Léa : Il faut te reposer et boire de l'eau. Tu peux aussi aller à la pharmacie. You need to rest and drink water. You can also go to the pharmacy.
Marc : D'accord. Comment est ton stage ? Okay. How is your internship?
Léa : Mon stage est plus intéressant que mon cours de commerce. La directrice aide beaucoup les étudiants. Je vais lui écrire aujourd'hui pour la remercier. My internship is more interesting than my business class. The director helps students a lot. I'm going to write to her today to thank her.

This dialogue combines directions and y (Chapter 11), weather and a near-future plan (Chapter 12), a fictional symptom and advice (Chapter 13), and a comparison, a relative clause, and lui (Chapter 14), entirely in present time and the near future.

Tâche — Connected fictional message: Write a connected six-to-eight-sentence fictional message about a day in the community.

Your message must include:

  • one direction sequence and one accurate use of y;
  • one present-time weather statement;
  • one near-future plan using on va;

  • one advice sentence using il faut, devoir, or pouvoir;

Culture and Communication

Culture in Context — Community life and mutual help

Helping a new arrival find their way, discussing weekend weather plans together, and offering simple, general well-being advice are ordinary parts of community life in many Francophone settings, just as they are elsewhere. University communities in particular often build informal support networks among students, including exchange students who are new to a country or a city. These practices vary by institution, region, and personal circumstance.

Regional Variation — A reminder across Unit III

Across Chapters 11 to 14, you encountered receptive notes on regional differences in vocabulary for places, weather expressions, health-related terms, and school or work vocabulary, such as le cégep in Quebec. This chapter does not add new regional forms; it reminds you that the productive language you have used remains the broadly understood core model, while recognizing that Francophone communities vary in vocabulary and expression.

Common Mistakes

Common Mistake — Overusing the full place name instead of y

Less natural: On va à la bibliothèque. La bibliothèque est près du parc. Repeating the noun phrase this way is grammatically correct, but it sounds repetitive in connected speech.

More natural: When you want to avoid repeating a place phrase introduced with à, you can replace it with y: On va à la bibliothèque. On y va ensemble. Using y is a stylistic choice for cohesion, not a strict requirement.

Common Mistake — Confusing il faut with devoir

Error: Writing Tu dois de te reposer, inserting an unnecessary preposition after devoir.

Correction: Devoir is followed directly by an infinitive, with no preposition: Tu dois te reposer. Il faut + infinitive (il faut te reposer) and subject + devoir + infinitive (tu dois te reposer) are two separate, equally correct structures.

Common Mistake — Dropping que in comparisons

Error: Writing la bibliothèque est plus calme le bureau des stages when writing quickly, modeled on English.

Correction: When the second term of the comparison is stated, French requires que: la bibliothèque est plus calme que le bureau des stages.
