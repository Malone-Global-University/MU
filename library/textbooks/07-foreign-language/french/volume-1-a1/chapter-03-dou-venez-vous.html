3

D'où venez-vous ?

Origin and Languages · Speak and Write French: Foundations of Practical Communication

Chapter Objectives

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

  1. Use the forms je suis, tu es, and vous êtes to state identity and nationality.
  2. Use the forms j'habite, tu habites, and vous habitez, and je parle, tu parles, and vous parlez, to say where you live and what language you speak.
  3. Give a name, nationality, place of residence, and language in a short four-line personal profile.
  4. Ask and answer fixed questions about origin (D'où es-tu ? / D'où êtes-vous ?) and language (Tu parles français ? / Est-ce que vous parlez français ?).
  5. Use à, en, au, and aux with the small set of cities and countries practiced in this chapter.
  6. Recognize additional verb forms and vocabulary presented in reference tables and reading, without being required to produce them independently yet.

Communication Scenario

During a break between classes, students from different backgrounds are getting to know one another. They exchange a short, predictable set of facts: name, nationality, where they live, and one or two languages they speak. This chapter expands familiar question patterns through a small number of fixed and controlled questions about origin, residence, and language; it does not expect you to build every possible question freely yet.

  1. You will practice a small, well-controlled set of first-person and tu/vous verb forms.
  2. Country names carry their own gender, which affects the small word placed before them; this chapter teaches that pattern with a handful of practiced places.

Pronunciation: Liaison in vous êtes

Pronunciation Focus — vous êtes

Say vous êtes aloud: the normally silent final -s of vous is pronounced as a [z] sound because êtes begins with a vowel. The two words link so closely that they sound almost like one word, vou-zêtes. This is a required liaison, not an optional stylistic choice.

Compare this with a word that begins with a consonant sound, where no such linking happens: in vous parlez, the -s of vous stays silent because parlez begins with a consonant.

  • Listen for the nasal vowel in canadien. The masculine form ends in a nasal vowel with no consonant release, while the feminine form canadienne pronounces the n fully and loses the nasal quality.
  • Do not pronounce the final -s of français or américains in ordinary speech; it is normally silent in the examples used in this chapter.
  • Keep prior accents in place: américain carries no accent, but earlier vocabulary such as étudiant keeps its acute accent on the é. The accent in étudiant helps identify the vowel sound; it does not mark English-style word stress.

Language Focus: être, habiter, parler

Purpose: say who you are, where you live, and what language you speak, using a small, well-practiced set of verb forms.

3.1 The verb être — selected forms

Language Focus

Être ("to be") does not follow a regular pattern, so its forms are learned directly. This chapter asks you to actively use only three forms: je suis, tu es, and vous êtes. You may also use il est or elle est with support when describing a specific person in a short profile.

3.3 Asking questions

Language Focus

This chapter uses a small, controlled set of question patterns rather than asking you to generate every possible question type freely. You will practice one intonation question, one est-ce que question, and two fixed questions about origin.

  • Tu parles français ? Do you speak French? (rising intonation)
  • Est-ce que vous parlez français ? Do you speak French? (formal/plural)
  • D'où êtes-vous ? (fixed expression) Where are you from? (formal)
  • D'où es-tu ? (fixed expression) Where are you from? (familiar)

The two origin questions use a word-order pattern called inversion. Learn them as fixed, memorized units for now; you are not asked to build new inverted questions on your own in this chapter.

Language Building Block: Prepositions of Place

Building Block — Choosing the right preposition
à + city  ·  en + feminine country  ·  au + masculine country  ·  aux + plural country
For the cities and countries practiced in this chapter, use the following controlled pattern. French uses a different small word before a city than before a country, and the word before a country depends on that country's gender or number.
J'habite à Montréal. J'habite en France. J'habite au Canada. J'habite aux États-Unis.
I live in Montreal. I live in France. I live in Canada. I live in the United States.

Language Focus

To answer where you come from, this chapter teaches one bounded, productive formula: je viens de, followed by de, du, or des depending on the place, matching the same gender and number logic as en/au/aux. You will mainly produce this first-person form. You may occasionally encounter tu viens de… ? or vous venez de… ? in guided input, but you are not required to produce or conjugate these forms in this chapter.

  • Je viens de France. I come from France.
  • Je viens du Canada. I come from Canada.
  • Je viens des États-Unis. I come from the United States.

Answer an origin question about yourself with je viens de…. Tu viens de… ? is a question addressed to someone else, not an answer about yourself; do not confuse the two.

Model Dialogue: D'où es-tu ?

This chapter's title, D'où venez-vous ?, is another common formal way to ask about origin, built on the verb venir. It is presented receptively for now; the productive origin questions practiced in this chapter are D'où es-tu ? and D'où êtes-vous ?

Context: Two students, Amina and Thomas, meet during a break and get to know each other.

Amina : Salut ! D'où es-tu ? Hi! Where are you from?
Thomas : Je viens du Canada. J'habite à Montréal. Et toi ? I come from Canada. I live in Montreal. And you?
Amina : Je viens du Sénégal. J'habite au Sénégal. I come from Senegal. I live in Senegal.
Thomas : Tu parles français ? Do you speak French?
Amina : Oui, je parle français et un peu anglais. Et toi ? Yes, I speak French and a little English. And you?
Thomas : Je parle anglais. I speak English.

Every expression in this dialogue appears in the vocabulary or Language Focus sections above. Notice how the exchange stays within the productive origin formula, the residence pattern, and the six productive verb forms taught in this chapter. Nationality forms are practiced separately in the profile card and review below.

Listening Practice

If no audio recording is available, ask a partner, instructor, or text-to-speech tool to read the lines below aloud, or read them aloud to yourself slowly.

Listening script
Bonjour, je m'appelle Karim. Je suis marocain. J'habite au Maroc. J'étudie en Belgique. Je parle arabe et français.
Hello, my name is Karim. I am Moroccan. I live in Morocco. I study in Belgium. I speak Arabic and French.

In this script, marocain, le Maroc, la Belgique, and étudier are receptive: recognize them here for comprehension only. Do not use them in your own speaking or writing tasks in this chapter, and do not use marocain or belge as a model answer when practicing your own nationality.

  1. Listen to (or read aloud) the script above. Which country does Karim live in, and which country does he study in?
  2. List the two languages Karim mentions, in the order he says them.
  3. Find the form of être that Karim uses to describe himself.

Speaking Practice

Pratique contrôlée — Controlled practice: three profile statements

Say three short statements about yourself, one each with être, habiter, and parler, for example je suis américaine, j'habite au Canada, je parle anglais.

Tâche communicative — Communicative task: a short supported exchange

With a partner, or using the model below, ask and answer one fixed origin question and one language question:

— D'où êtes-vous ? — Je viens du Canada. J'habite à Montréal. — Vous parlez français ? — Oui, je parle français.

Pratique individuelle — Solo alternative

Record yourself saying a four-line imaginary profile: name, nationality, city or country of residence, and one language, using only the forms taught in this chapter.

Culture and Communication

Culture in Context — French across many countries

French is an official or widely used language in dozens of countries across Europe, Africa, the Americas, and the Pacific, not only in France. Asking someone where they are from is a natural and common way to begin a conversation in French-speaking communities, and the answer often reveals a connection to more than one country or language.

Regional Variation — Official languages and individual language use

French and English are Canada's two official languages, but this is a fact about the country's institutions, not a guarantee about any individual speaker. Many Canadians use mainly one of the two languages day to day, while others use both regularly. In the model dialogue, Thomas says that he speaks English; this is one individual's situation, not a description of every Canadian. Do not assume that every resident of a Francophone country uses French in the same way, or as their only language; multilingualism is common across much of the Francophone world, but it varies by person, region, and generation.

Common Mistakes

Common Mistake — Forgetting nationality agreement

Error: A woman writing je suis américain instead of je suis américaine.

Correction: Choose the practiced nationality form that matches you: add -e for the feminine form in the four pairs taught in this chapter.

Common Mistake — Capitalizing the nationality adjective

Error: Writing Elle est Française, capitalizing the adjective as English does.

Correction: French capitalizes the country name (la France) but not the nationality adjective (française) or the language name (le français).

Common Mistake — Answering an origin question with tu viens de

Error: Answering about yourself with Tu viens du Canada.

Correction: Answer with Je viens du Canada.
