Santé et bien-être

Health and Well-Being · Speak and Write French: Foundations of Practical Communication

Chapter Objectives

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

  1. Name common parts of the body and describe simple, fictional symptoms.
  2. Use avoir mal à with the correct article contraction to say where something hurts.
  3. Express necessity with the impersonal expression il faut plus infinitive.
  4. Express ability with the irregular verb pouvoir.
  5. Express personal obligation with the irregular verb devoir plus infinitive.
  6. Give simple direct advice using the imperative.
  7. Understand and produce short, present-time exchanges about how someone feels and what they should do.
  8. Apply accurate pronunciation of silent final consonants and established liaison patterns in health expressions.
  9. Recycle vocabulary and grammar from Units I and II, and from Chapters 11–12, while communicating about a new domain.

Communication Scenario

Léa is not feeling well today. She sends Marc a short message to explain how she feels, and Marc, playing the concerned friend, tells her what she should and should not do right now. Later, a short fictional message describes what a classmate named Paul needs to do because he is sick today. This chapter continues Unit III, Dans la communauté, by giving you the tools to talk about your body, your health, and simple present-time advice. All health situations in this chapter are fictional language practice, not medical guidance.

  1. Learn key vocabulary for the body, symptoms, and health.
  2. Use avoir mal à with correct contractions to describe pain.
  3. Express necessity with il faut plus infinitive.
  4. Express personal obligation with devoir, ability with pouvoir, and direct advice with the imperative.

Pronunciation Focus

Pronunciation Focus — Silent final consonants and established liaison

Body and health vocabulary offer useful practice with silent final consonants.

  • The final -s of le dos and le bras is silent in ordinary use, as is the final -d of le pied. Practice these as words with silent final consonants.
  • The contraction au in j'ai mal au dos is a single oral vowel sound, distinct from the nasal vowel in words like on; keep the two sounds separate as you practice.
  • Review the required liaison you already learned with vous avez (Chapter 4) and practice it again here: Vous avez mal à la tête ?

Practice the vocabulary examples le dos, le bras, and le pied aloud, keeping their final consonants silent. Then repeat Vous avez mal à la tête ? to review the liaison in vous avez.

Language Focus: Necessity, Ability, and Advice

Purpose: describe symptoms, express necessity, ability, and personal obligation, and give simple advice about health in the present.

13.1 Avoir mal à and article contractions

Language Focus

To say that something hurts, French uses avoir mal à followed by the definite article and the body part. French normally uses the definite article here, not a possessive, because the body part is already understood to belong to the speaker. à contracts with le and les, exactly as you learned in Chapter 6.

  • J'ai mal à la tête. I have a headache. (Literally: I have pain in the head.) à + la = à la (no contraction)
  • Marc a mal au dos. Marc's back hurts. à + le = au
  • J'ai mal aux jambes. My legs hurt. à + les = aux

English speakers often reach for a possessive here (my head hurts). Remember that French almost always uses the definite article with the body in this expression.

13.2 Expressing necessity with il faut

Building Block — Talking about necessity
il faut + [infinitive]
Il faut is grammatically impersonal because its subject il does not refer to a person, much like the il in il pleut from Chapter 12. The necessity itself may be general, or it may apply to a person identified by the context, as in Il faut te reposer. Devoir, by contrast, assigns the obligation directly to its grammatical subject.
Il faut se reposer.
You need to rest. / One needs to rest.
Language Focus
  • Il faut boire de l'eau. You need to drink water.
  • Il ne faut pas sortir aujourd'hui. You shouldn't go out today.

To make il faut negative, place ne...pas around faut, exactly as you learned for negation in Chapter 7.

13.3 Ability with pouvoir, obligation with devoir, and advice with the imperative

Language Focus

Four tools let you talk about health with different shades of meaning:

  • Il faut + infinitive is grammatically impersonal. The necessity may be general or may apply to a person identified by the context.
  • devoir + infinitive expresses a personal obligation. Tu dois te reposer means "You must rest" or "You need to rest" — grammatically a real obligation, even when said gently to a friend.
  • the imperative gives a direct instruction, as in Repose-toi ! or Bois de l'eau !
  • pouvoir + infinitive expresses ability or possibility, not obligation, as in Tu peux aller à la pharmacie. ("You can go to the pharmacy.")

Pouvoir and devoir are both irregular verbs. Like aller in Chapter 12, they are followed directly by an infinitive.

Model Dialogue: Ça ne va pas bien

Context: Léa messages Marc because she is not feeling well today. This is a fictional language exercise, not medical guidance.

Léa : Salut Marc. Ça ne va pas bien. J'ai mal à la tête et je tousse. Hi Marc. I'm not doing well. I have a headache and I'm coughing.
Marc : Oh non ! Tu as de la fièvre ? Oh no! Do you have a fever?
Léa : Oui, un peu. Yes, a little.
Marc : Il faut te reposer. Tu dois rester à la maison. You need to rest. You have to stay home.
Léa : D'accord. Je ne peux pas aller au cours aujourd'hui. Okay. I can't go to class today.
Marc : Bois de l'eau. Tu peux aussi aller à la pharmacie. Drink some water. You can also go to the pharmacy.
Léa : D'accord. Merci, Marc ! Okay. Thanks, Marc!

Notice how il faut expresses general necessity, devoir expresses Léa's personal obligation, pouvoir expresses what she is or is not able to do, and the imperative gives direct advice.

Writing Workshop

Complete the following linked writing tasks in order. All health details should be fictional.

Tâche 1 — Label symptoms: Write three short sentences describing where something hurts, using avoir mal à with at least two different article contractions.

Tâche 2 — Supported message: Write a short fictional message (four to six sentences) to a sick friend, addressing the friend's fictional illness throughout. Include:

  • one greeting;
  • one symptom describing the friend, using avoir mal à or another chapter health expression;
  • one necessity or obligation sentence, using il faut or devoir — not necessarily both;
  • one ability sentence using pouvoir;
  • one simple imperative or closing.

  2. Choose one piece of advice. Decide what your friend needs to do, using il faut or devoir.

  • An imperative or simple closing appears.

  • Accents, apostrophes, and punctuation are accurate throughout.

Culture and Communication

Culture in Context — The pharmacy as a common first stop

In France, a pharmacy (identifiable by a green cross sign, often lit and flashing) is commonly a first stop for minor health concerns, such as a cold or a headache, where a pharmacist can often give basic guidance. Practices and the legal role of pharmacists vary by country and region across the Francophone world, and this note describes a common pattern rather than a universal rule. For anything serious or persistent, seeing a doctor is the appropriate next step. This is cultural awareness for communication, not medical instruction.

Regional Variation — Health-related terms across the Francophone world

Healthcare institutions and related vocabulary vary across Francophone regions. In Quebec, for example, a CLSC (centre local de services communautaires) provides some community health services. This is a receptive cultural note; the core vocabulary in this chapter remains broadly usable across the Francophone world, and learners are not expected to produce region-specific institutional terms.

Common Mistakes

Common Mistake — Using a possessive instead of the article with body parts

Error: Writing j'ai mal à ma tête, modeled directly on English "my head."

Correction: French normally uses the definite article with avoir mal à: j'ai mal à la tête, not à ma tête.

Common Mistake — Forgetting the article contraction

Error: Writing j'ai mal à le dos instead of the correct contracted form.

Correction: à + le must contract to au: j'ai mal au dos.

Common Mistake — Confusing pouvoir and devoir

Error: Writing je dois jouer au foot to mean "I can play soccer."

Correction: devoir expresses obligation ("must"), while pouvoir expresses ability ("can"): je peux jouer au foot means "I can play soccer," while je dois jouer au foot means "I have to play soccer."
