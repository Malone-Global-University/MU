Dans la salle de classe

Classroom Language · Speak and Write French: Foundations of Practical Communication

Chapter Objectives

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

  1. Name common classroom objects and people using the correct definite or indefinite article.
  2. Identify a noun's grammatical gender — masculine or feminine — and its correct plural form.
  3. Use c'est and ce sont to identify a single thing or several things.
  4. Ask qu'est-ce que c'est ? and answer with a complete sentence.
  5. Distinguish singular and plural nouns in speech and in writing.
  6. Continue applying accurate accents and the tu/vous distinction from Chapter 1.

Communication Scenario

It is the second class meeting. The teacher holds up ordinary classroom objects one at a time and asks the students to name them. The students, still working with a small vocabulary, answer in short complete sentences, practicing the small grammatical system that lets French speakers say what something is, and whether there is one of it or several.

  1. In the identification patterns taught in this chapter, ordinary count nouns normally require an article. Learn each classroom noun together with its article.
  2. Notice which nouns are masculine and which are feminine as you learn them; guessing gender from meaning alone does not work reliably in French.

Pronunciation: Gender, Number, and Silent Letters

Written French marks plural with a final -s on the noun that is almost always silent. This means the noun forms livre and livres normally sound the same. However, the complete phrases le livre and les livres do not sound identical, because the articles le and les have different vowels. The article, not a spoken ending on the noun, tells you the number.

Pronunciation Focus — Hearing the article, not the noun ending

Say le stylo and les stylos aloud. The noun itself, stylo(s), sounds the same in both: the final written -s is silent. What actually changes is the article's vowel: le versus les. In ordinary speech, it is the article — not a spoken ending on the noun — that most reliably tells you whether you are hearing one item or several.

  • Do not add an English-style "s" sound to the end of a plural noun in ordinary speech; the written -s on the noun itself is silent.
  • Listen closely to the vowel of the article: le/la versus les is your main spoken clue to number for most nouns in this chapter.
  • Keep every accent from Chapter 1 in place when you write these new nouns, including the circumflex in fenêtre.

Before a noun beginning with a consonant sound, nothing links les or des to the noun: les livres, des tables. Before a noun beginning with a vowel sound, however, the final consonant of les or des is pronounced as an audible /z/, linking directly into the noun. This required liaison is what you hear in les ordinateurs and des étudiants. That /z/ sound comes from the article, not from pronouncing the noun's own plural -s.

  • les livres — no liaison; livres begins with a consonant sound.
  • les ordinateurs — required liaison; the s of les is pronounced /z/ before the vowel sound.
  • des étudiants — required liaison, for the same reason.

Language Focus: Articles, Gender, and c'est / ce sont

Purpose: name classroom objects and people with the correct article, and identify what something is using c'est and ce sont.

2.1 Grammatical gender and the definite article

Language Focus

Every French noun has a grammatical gender, masculine or feminine. This is a grammatical classification, not a statement about biological sex, and it applies even to objects with no natural gender at all, such as le stylo (masculine) or la porte (feminine). The definite article shows gender in the singular: le before a masculine noun, la before a feminine noun, and l' before either gender when the noun begins with a vowel sound, as in l'ordinateur. There is no reliable way to predict gender from a noun's meaning, so in this chapter, learn each ordinary count noun together with its article rather than on its own.

  • le livre the book (masculine)
  • la chaise the chair (feminine)
  • l'ordinateur the computer (vowel-initial, elided article)

2.2 The indefinite article and the plural

Language Focus

The indefinite article corresponds to English "a/an": un for masculine nouns and une for feminine nouns. In the plural, both the definite and indefinite articles simplify: les covers all definite plurals, and des covers all indefinite plurals, regardless of gender. Most nouns form the plural in writing by adding -s, though this ending is normally silent. Nouns ending in -eau, such as le bureau and le tableau, follow the regular -eau → -eaux spelling pattern instead.

  • un professeur → des professeurs a teacher → teachers
  • le tableau → les tableaux the board → the boards regular -eau → -eaux pattern

2.3 C'est and ce sont

Building Block — Identifying people and things
C'est + [article + singular noun] / Ce sont + [article + plural noun]
Use c'est to identify one person or thing, and ce sont to identify more than one. Both patterns normally keep the article; do not drop it as English sometimes does.
C'est un livre. Ce sont des cahiers.
This is a book. These are notebooks.
  • Qu'est-ce que c'est ? — C'est une chaise. What is this? — This is a chair.
  • Qu'est-ce que c'est ? — Ce sont des crayons. What are these? — These are pencils.
Language Note — A fixed formula for recognition: ce n'est pas

You may hear or read ce n'est pas... ("that's not...") used to correct someone, as in ce n'est pas un stylo, c'est un crayon. Treat this as a fixed, recognition-only expression for now. Negation as a productive grammar system — including how ne...pas works with other verbs — is introduced later in the course; this chapter does not require you to build negative sentences on your own.

You will meet il est and elle est as a separate, related pattern in a later chapter; this chapter's productive focus is only c'est and ce sont.

Model Dialogue: Qu'est-ce que c'est ?

Context: The teacher holds up objects one at a time and asks the class to identify them.

La prof : Regardez ! Qu'est-ce que c'est ? Look! What is this?
Léa : C'est un livre. It's a book.
La prof : Très bien ! Qu'est-ce que c'est ? Very good! What is this?
Marc : C'est une chaise. It's a chair.

The teacher holds up two pencils and a notebook together.

La prof : Et maintenant, qu'est-ce que c'est ? And now, what are these?
Léa : Ce sont des crayons et un cahier. Those are pencils and a notebook.
La prof : Parfait ! (Et regardez : ce n'est pas un stylo, c'est un crayon.) Perfect! (And look: that isn't a pen, it's a pencil.)

Notice that Léa switches from c'est to ce sont as soon as the teacher holds up more than one object. The teacher's parenthetical ce n'est pas... is the fixed recognition formula introduced above; you are not asked to build sentences like it yourself in this chapter. Regardez ("look") is receptive teacher language, just like entrez in Chapter 1 — recognize it, but you are not asked to produce classroom commands yourself yet. Très bien and parfait are fixed receptive classroom responses; simply recognize them as praise.

Listening Practice

If no audio recording is available, ask a partner, instructor, or text-to-speech tool to read the lines below aloud, or read them aloud to yourself slowly.

Listening script
Regardez la salle de classe. Voici une table, une chaise et un ordinateur. Et voilà des livres et des cahiers.
Look at the classroom. Here is a table, a chair, and a computer. And there are books and notebooks.
  1. Listen to (or read aloud) the script above. List every object mentioned, in French.
  2. Which objects are named in the singular, and which are named in the plural? How can you tell from the article alone?
  3. Listen for voici and voilà. Which one introduces the computer, and which one introduces the books and notebooks?

Regardez is receptive teacher language here, as in the model dialogue; you only need to recognize it.

Speaking Practice

Pratique contrôlée — Controlled practice: name the object

Point to five objects around you (real or imagined) and say what each one is using c'est un/une..., choosing the correct article for each noun from the vocabulary table.

Tâche communicative — Communicative task: quiz your partner

With a partner, take turns pointing at objects and asking qu'est-ce que c'est ? Your partner answers with a complete sentence using c'est or ce sont, correcting the article if needed.

Pratique individuelle — Solo alternative

Record yourself naming five objects visible near you: three in the singular with c'est and two grouped in the plural with ce sont. Listen back and check that each article matches the noun's gender and number.

Reading Practice

Dans la salle de classe — In the classroom

Voici une table et une chaise. C'est un livre. C'est un cahier. Ce sont des stylos et des crayons. Et voilà une étudiante et un professeur.

Tâche de lecture — Reading task:

  1. List every item and person mentioned in the passage, in French, with its article.
  2. Name one masculine noun and one feminine noun from the passage.
  3. Which items are described in the plural? Point to the word that tells you.

Writing Workshop

Complete the following linked writing tasks in order.

Tâche 1 — Sort by gender: Copy the Core Vocabulary nouns into two columns, masculin and féminin, keeping each article attached to its noun.

Tâche 2 — Complete the identification: Fill in the blanks with c'est or ce sont and the correct article.

Fill in the blank
1. ______ ______ tableau.   2. ______ ______ chaises.   3. ______ ______ ordinateur.
1. This is a board. 2. These are chairs. 3. This is a computer.

Tâche 3 — Identification cards: Choose five items from your own bag, desk, or the classroom (real or imagined). For each one, write one separate identification sentence with c'est or ce sont, as if labeling an index card. Sentences do not need to connect to one another.

  1. Sort the vocabulary by gender first. This reinforces article-noun pairs before you write full sentences.
  2. Complete the fill-in-the-blank identification. Check that each blank matches singular or plural correctly.
  3. Write your five separate identification sentences. Keep every noun within this chapter's vocabulary, and keep each sentence independent.
  4. Read your sentences aloud once. Listen for whether your articles clearly signal singular or plural.
  5. Check every article against the vocabulary tables. Correct any gender or number mismatch.

Liste de révision — Editing checklist:

  • Every noun has an article, and the article matches the noun's gender.
  • c'est is used for one item and ce sont for more than one.
  • Plural nouns and articles agree consistently within each sentence.
  • All accents from Chapter 1 vocabulary are preserved.
  • No new vocabulary or negative sentence patterns from outside this chapter have been used.

Culture and Communication

Culture in Context — Classrooms across the Francophone world

Classroom objects, routines, and vocabulary can vary in detail across French-speaking regions, but the words in this chapter are widely understood wherever French is used for education. Being able to name objects precisely is a practical first step toward participating in any French-speaking classroom, whether in France, Quebec, Senegal, or elsewhere.

Regional Variation — A note on gender consistency

Grammatical gender is generally stable across major varieties of French for the everyday nouns in this chapter. Occasional regional or historical variation in gender does exist for a small number of words elsewhere in French, but it is not a concern for the vocabulary presented here. This course will label any such variation explicitly if and when it becomes relevant.

Common Mistakes

Common Mistake — Dropping the article

Error: Writing c'est livre instead of c'est un livre, following the English pattern of naming a noun alone.

Correction: In the identification patterns taught in this chapter, ordinary count nouns normally require an article. Keep the article attached to the noun in speech and writing.

Common Mistake — Guessing gender from meaning

Error: Assuming table must be masculine because English "table" has no gender, and writing le table.

Correction: French grammatical gender does not follow English meaning. La table is feminine; learn each noun's gender directly with its article.

Common Mistake — Using c'est with plural nouns

Careful standard model: In the careful standard model used in this course, write and practice ce sont des stylos, not c'est des stylos, when identifying more than one item.

Note: You may later hear c'est des stylos in informal spoken French; recognize it, but do not use it as your productive model in this chapter. Switch to ce sont as soon as you are identifying more than one person or thing.

Answer Key

Model Dialogue — Comprehension

  1. Léa says c'est un livre, "it's a book."
  2. She uses ce sont because the teacher is now holding up more than one object (two pencils and a notebook), so the plural identification pattern is required.

Writing

  1. le cahier, la fenêtre, l'ordinateur, la chaise
  2. les bureaux (regular -eau → -eaux pattern); les tables (regular -s plural)

Reading

  1. Items and people, with articles: une table, une chaise, un livre, un cahier, des stylos, des crayons, une étudiante, un professeur.
