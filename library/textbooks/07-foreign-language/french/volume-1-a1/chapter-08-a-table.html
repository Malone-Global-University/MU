À table !

Food and Drink · Speak and Write French: Foundations of Practical Communication

Chapter Objectives

By the end of this chapter, you will be beginning to:

  1. Name common foods and drinks and identify them with the correct article: the partitive articles du, de la, and de l' for unspecified amounts of mass foods and drinks, and the plural indefinite article des for unspecified plural countable items.
  2. Distinguish the partitive article from the plural indefinite article des and from the definite article you already know.
  3. Use a small set of supported present-tense forms of manger, boire, prendre, and vouloir, along with j'aime, j'adore, and je préfère, to talk about food preferences.
  4. Express quantities using expressions such as un peu de, beaucoup de, and une bouteille de, and ask about quantity using combien de.
  5. Make a polite request using je voudrais, and accept or decline an offer using a fixed expression.
  6. Make a food or drink expression negative, changing the article to de or d' after ne...pas.
  7. Recognize and optionally reuse two fixed expressions with the pronoun en.
  8. Read a basic menu and order food politely in conversation and in writing.

Communication Scenario

Léa is at an outdoor market with her friend Karim, buying food for dinner. At the cheese stall, she asks for what she needs, and the vendor asks how much she would like. Later, at home, she and Karim look at what is available and decide what to eat and drink with the meal. This chapter continues Unit II by moving from the rhythms of a daily routine to a specific, recurring everyday task: talking about food, reading a simple menu, and ordering politely.

  1. This chapter introduces the partitive article, the plural indefinite article des, quantity expressions, combien de, polite requests with je voudrais, offer-and-response formulas, a small set of supported food verb forms, and a controlled, formulaic introduction to the pronoun en. It does not introduce numbers and prices, adjective placement rules, or freely placed direct object pronouns; those are the focus of Chapter 9.
  2. Vocabulary and grammar from Chapters 1–7, including reflexive verbs, telling time, sequencing words, and ne...pas, remain active and should be recycled wherever natural.
  3. This chapter is a regular instructional chapter. It develops several systems that are still forming; a full cumulative assessment of Unit II occurs later in the volume.

Pronunciation: du, un, and Liaison with des

Pronunciation Focus — /y/, the nasal vowel in un, elision, and liaison

Du and tu contain the same front rounded vowel /y/. Keep it distinct from the vowel /ø/ in deux: round your lips and keep your tongue forward for du, rather than letting it slide toward the vowel in deux.

  • In un peu de, the nasal vowel in un should stay nasal and not add a pronounced n sound at the end.
  • Before a vowel sound, the singular article appears as l'. The partitive form is therefore de l', as in de l'eau and de l'huile. Do not analyze this as de + le becoming de l'; de + le normally contracts to du.
  • Des before a vowel-initial word, as in des œufs or des oranges, takes required liaison: the final -s is pronounced as [z] linking into the vowel.

When making a polite request such as Je voudrais du pain, s'il vous plaît, use smooth, courteous intonation rather than an abrupt or command-like delivery.

Language Focus: Articles, Quantities, Requests, and En

Purpose: talk about food and drink as unspecified amounts or unspecified plural items, ask about and express quantity, ask for food politely and respond to an offer, and refer back to something already mentioned without repeating the noun.

8.1 The partitive article

Language Focus

French uses a special article, the partitive article, to talk about "some" of a mass or unbounded substance that is not normally counted as individual units, such as bread, water, or rice.

L'article partitif — Forms of the partitive article
FormUsed beforeExample
dumasculine singular mass noundu pain
de lafeminine singular mass nounde la salade
de l'singular mass noun beginning with a vowel soundde l'eau

The partitive article is not the same as the definite article le/la/l'/les. Du pain refers to an unspecified amount of bread. Le pain can refer either to identified bread or to bread as a general category, depending on context.

  • Je voudrais du pain et de la salade. I would like some bread and some salad.
  • Je voudrais le pain qui est sur la table. I would like the bread that is on the table.
  • J'aime le pain. I like bread (in general).

8.2 The plural indefinite article des

Language Focus

Des is not a form of the partitive article. It is the plural indefinite article, used before an unspecified number of countable plural items, the same family as un and une for one whole countable item.

Comparaison des articles — Comparing article types

  • Il y a des légumes dans le réfrigérateur. There are vegetables in the refrigerator. (unspecified plural indefinite)

8.4 Expressing and asking about quantity

Building Block — Quantity expressions with de
[quantity word] + de / d' + [noun, no article]
When a specific quantity word such as beaucoup, un peu, assez, une bouteille, or un morceau is used, the article disappears entirely and only de (or d' before a vowel sound) remains, regardless of the noun's gender or number.
Je voudrais un peu de fromage et une bouteille d'eau.
I would like a little cheese and a bottle of water.
Building Block — Asking about quantity with combien de
Combien de + [noun] + voulez-vous ?
Use this fixed service-interaction pattern to ask how much or how many of something someone would like. Answer with a quantity expression or a number of countable units.
Combien de fromage voulez-vous ? — Un peu, s'il vous plaît.
How much cheese would you like? — A little, please.

8.5 Making a polite request, and accepting or declining an offer

Language Focus

Use je voudrais ("I would like") instead of je veux ("I want") to make a request sound polite in a shop, market, café, or restaurant. When someone offers you something with vous voulez...?, respond with a fixed acceptance or refusal.

  • Je voudrais du fromage, s'il vous plaît. I would like some cheese, please.
  • Vous voulez du thé ? — Oui, volontiers. Would you like some tea? — Yes, gladly.
  • Vous voulez du café ? — Non, merci. Would you like some coffee? — No, thank you.

8.6 Negation with the partitive and the plural indefinite article

Building Block — Ne...pas de after basic negation
[subject] + ne / n' + [verb] + pas + de / d' + [noun, no article]
You previously encountered il n'y a pas de... as a supported formula. In this chapter, you extend that pattern to food and drink expressions after basic negation. The partitive article du, de la, de l' and the plural indefinite article des normally become de (or d' before a vowel sound) after ne...pas. This change does not normally happen after être.
Je prends du café. → Je ne prends pas de café. Elle mange des œufs. → Elle ne mange pas d'œufs. C'est du fromage. → Ce n'est pas du fromage.
I have coffee. → I don't have coffee. She eats eggs. → She doesn't eat eggs. It's cheese. → It isn't cheese.

8.7 A controlled introduction to the pronoun en

Language Focus

The pronoun en can replace a partitive expression or a quantity so that you do not have to repeat the noun. At this stage, you will recognize en in two fixed expressions and may optionally reuse them. Its full placement rules with other pronouns are developed later in the course.

  • Tu veux du pain ? — Oui, j'en voudrais un peu. Do you want some bread? — Yes, I would like a little (of it).
  • Il y a du fromage ? — Oui, il y en a. Is there cheese? — Yes, there is (some).

Controlled practice: Complete each exchange with en.

  1. Tu veux du pain ? — Oui, j'___ voudrais un peu.
  2. Il y a du fromage ? — Oui, il y ___ a.

This chapter uses en only in these two modeled, formulaic patterns. You are not yet expected to place en freely in every sentence type; that control develops in later chapters.

Model Dialogue: Au marché

Context: Léa is buying food at an outdoor market stall before making dinner with Karim.

Vendeur : Bonjour ! Vous désirez ? Hello! What would you like?
Léa : Bonjour. Je voudrais du fromage et du pain, s'il vous plaît. Hello. I would like some cheese and some bread, please.
Vendeur : Bien sûr. Combien de fromage voulez-vous ? Of course. How much cheese would you like?
Léa : Un morceau, s'il vous plaît. Je voudrais aussi une bouteille d'eau, s'il vous plaît. A piece, please. I would also like a bottle of water, please.
Vendeur : Très bien. Et avec ça ? Very well. Anything else?
Léa : C'est tout, merci. That's all, thank you.
Later, at home.
Karim : Il y a de la salade dans le réfrigérateur ? Is there any salad in the refrigerator?
Léa : Non, il n'y a pas de salade, mais il y a du riz et de la viande. No, there isn't any salad, but there is rice and meat.
Karim : Parfait. Et pour boire, tu veux du jus d'orange ? Perfect. And to drink, do you want some orange juice?
Léa : Oui, j'en voudrais un peu. Merci ! Yes, I would like a little (of it). Thanks!

Receptive expressions in this dialogue: vous désirez ? (a common formal way for a vendor or server to ask what you would like) and bien sûr (of course).

Culture and Communication

Culture in Context — Buying food across Francophone communities

Food shopping practices vary by country, city, neighborhood, household, budget, and available infrastructure. Open-air markets, supermarkets, small shops, cooperatives, street vendors, and other systems may coexist across Francophone communities, and no single routine describes how everyone shops. Where a vendor-and-customer exchange does take place, greetings and polite formulas such as bonjour, s'il vous plaît, and merci are generally expected as part of the interaction, not as optional extras.

Regional Variation — Naming meals across the Francophone world

The mealtime vocabulary in this chapter (le petit-déjeuner, le déjeuner, le dîner) reflects the France-based system used as this textbook's default productive model. In Quebec, le déjeuner commonly refers to breakfast, le dîner to the midday meal, and le souper to the evening meal. This is a labeled regional difference in mealtime vocabulary, not an error in either variety. The Quebec terms are presented here as regional recognition vocabulary; you are not asked to produce them yet.

Common Mistakes

Common Mistake — Calling des a partitive article

Error: Describing des as one of the "forms of the partitive article," alongside du, de la, and de l'.

Correction: Des is the plural indefinite article, used for unspecified plural countable items such as des œufs. The partitive article family is limited to du, de la, and de l', used for unspecified amounts of mass or unbounded foods and drinks.

Common Mistake — Using the definite article instead of the partitive

Error: Saying je voudrais le pain when asking for "some bread" at the table.

Correction: Use the partitive article for an unspecified quantity: je voudrais du pain. Le pain can mean identified bread or bread as a general category, depending on context, which is not what is meant here.

Common Mistake — Keeping an article after a quantity expression

Error: Saying beaucoup de la salade instead of dropping the article after the quantity word.

Correction: After a quantity expression, only de remains: beaucoup de salade, not beaucoup de la salade.

Common Mistake — Forgetting to change the article after ne...pas

Error: Saying je ne prends pas du café instead of changing the article after negation.

Correction: After ne...pas, the article becomes de (or d' before a vowel sound): je ne prends pas de café. The same change applies to des: je ne mange pas d'œufs.
