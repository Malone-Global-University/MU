Raconter et rester en contact

Connected Stories · Speak and Write French: Foundations of Practical Communication

Chapter Objectives

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

  1. Sequence a story using connectors such as d'abord, ensuite, puis, après ça, and enfin.
  2. Combine the passé composé and the imparfait confidently across an entire narration, drawing on the systems developed in Chapters 16, 17, and 18.
  3. Tell a connected story about a memorable event, in speech and in writing.
  4. Ask someone to tell a story and ask follow-up questions about what happened.
  5. Write a short personal message or email that narrates recent or past news to stay in touch with someone.
  6. Understand a longer spoken or written narrative that mixes both past tenses and several connectors.
  7. Review and self-check the core grammar, vocabulary, and pronunciation systems developed since Chapter 1.
  8. Apply accurate pronunciation and connected speech across a full narrative passage, including required liaison, enchaînement, auxiliary–participle rhythm, and the shared /ɛ/ vowel of -ais, -ait, and -aient without pronouncing their final consonant letters.

Communication Scenario

A few weeks after visiting his grandmother Mireille in Tours, Marc writes her a long email to stay in touch. He wants to tell her about a trip he just took with his friend Nadia, describe what the two of them did step by step, and mention how things have changed since his last visit. Mireille writes back, continuing the family habit of telling connected stories about ordinary life. This chapter brings together everything you have learned about narrating the past — from the classroom language of Chapter 2 to the imparfait of Chapter 18 — so that you can tell a complete, connected story rather than a list of separate sentences.

  1. Tell a story in order, from beginning to end.
  2. Mix background description with the events that actually happened.
  3. Ask someone else to tell their story and follow up with questions.
  4. Write a personal message that keeps a friend or family member updated.

Pronunciation Focus

Pronunciation Focus — Connected narration and rhythm

Telling a full story places several previously learned pronunciation systems in sequence: the shared /ɛ/ pronunciation of -ais, -ait, and -aient from Chapter 18, with their final consonant letters remaining silent, the auxiliary-plus-participle rhythm of the passé composé from Chapters 16–17, and the liaison and enchaînement patterns recycled since Chapter 1.

Pay attention to connected speech across the narrative. In nous avons and nous étions, required liaison produces an audible /z/ before the following vowel. In a phrase such as avec elle, the already pronounced final /k/ of avec links smoothly into the following vowel through enchaînement. Sequencing connectors should also be grouped naturally with the sentence that follows: D'abord, nous sommes arrivés; Ensuite, nous avons cherché un taxi. No new sound or connected-speech rule is introduced here; this is a chance to apply everything at ordinary narrative speed.

Grammar: Raconter une histoire complète

Purpose: consolidate the passé composé and imparfait system from Chapters 16–18, and use sequencing connectors to organize a complete, connected narration rather than isolated sentences.

19.1 Why a story needs connectors

Language Focus

A list of separate sentences in the passé composé and the imparfait can be grammatically correct but still fail to sound like a story. Connectors show the listener or reader how the events relate to one another in time, and they signal which sentence is the next step in the sequence rather than a new, unrelated idea.

  • D'abord, nous sommes arrivés à la gare. Ensuite, nous avons cherché un taxi. Finalement, nous sommes arrivés à l'hôtel très tard. First, we arrived at the station. Next, we looked for a taxi. Finally, we arrived at the hotel very late.

19.2 Reviewing the passé composé / imparfait system

Building Block — The narration pattern, reviewed
[imparfait: background/description] + connector + [passé composé: event] + connector + [passé composé: next event]...
This is the same pattern introduced in Chapter 18, section 18.4, now extended across an entire story rather than a single sentence. In the common controlled narrative pattern practiced here, the imparfait normally presents background, description, or an ongoing situation, while the passé composé normally presents foregrounded events that advance the story. The final choice still depends on the speaker's intended viewpoint and context.
Il faisait beau, et nous étions très contents de partir. D'abord, nous avons pris le train jusqu'à Tours. Pendant le voyage, Nadia dormait, et moi, je regardais par la fenêtre. Tout à coup, le train s'est arrêté. Finalement, nous sommes arrivés avec une heure de retard.
The weather was nice, and we were very happy to leave. First, we took the train to Tours. During the trip, Nadia was sleeping, and I was looking out the window. Suddenly, the train stopped. Finally, we arrived an hour late.

Notice that no new tense appears in this example: every verb form is either the imparfait or the passé composé, exactly as taught in Chapters 16–18. What is new is only the organizing work done by the connectors.

19.3 Asking someone to tell their story

Language Focus

To invite someone to narrate, French speakers commonly use Raconte-moi... (informal) or Racontez-moi... (formal or plural), followed by a specific topic such as ton week-end or ton voyage. Follow-up questions typically use question words and formulas already active since earlier chapters: Qu'est-ce qui s'est passé ?, Et après ?, Qu'est-ce qui s'est passé ensuite ?, and Pourquoi ?. Treat Qu'est-ce qui s'est passé ? as a fixed question formula meaning "What happened?"; learners are not required to analyze or generalize the underlying relative-clause structure in this chapter.

  • Raconte-moi ton week-end. Qu'est-ce qui s'est passé ? Tell me about your weekend. What happened?
  • Raconte-moi ton voyage. Et après ? Tell me about your trip. And then?

19.4 Personal messages that narrate

An email or letter that gives news often opens with a greeting and a question such as Quoi de neuf ?, narrates one or two connected events using the pattern above, and closes with an informal closing such as Écris-moi vite ! or Grosses bises. This chapter does not introduce new formal-letter conventions; formal correspondence remains outside the scope of this volume.

Model Dialogue: Raconte-moi ton week-end

Context: Marc calls his friend Nadia the day after their trip to Tours to talk about what happened, before he writes to his grandmother.

Nadia : Alors, raconte-moi ! Qu'est-ce qui s'est passé après mon départ ? So, tell me! What happened after I left?
Marc : D'abord, j'ai appelé ma grand-mère. Elle était très contente, et elle m'a raconté sa vie à Tours quand elle était jeune. First, I called my grandmother. She was very happy, and she told me about her life in Tours when she was young.
Nadia : Et ensuite ? And then?
Marc : Ensuite, je me suis promené dans le quartier où elle habitait autrefois. Il faisait très beau. Pendant que je marchais, j'ai rencontré un de ses anciens voisins ! Then, I walked around the neighborhood where she used to live. The weather was very nice. While I was walking, I met one of her former neighbors!
Nadia : C'est incroyable ! Qu'est-ce qui s'est passé après ça ? That's incredible! What happened after that?
Marc : Il m'a invité chez lui, et nous avons parlé pendant deux heures. Finalement, je suis rentré très tard, mais j'étais tellement content. He invited me to his house, and we talked for two hours. Finally, I got home very late, but I was so happy.
Nadia : Tu vas écrire à ta grand-mère pour lui raconter tout ça ? Are you going to write to your grandmother to tell her all that?
Marc : Oui, je vais lui écrire ce soir. Elle adore avoir des nouvelles. Yes, I'm going to write to her tonight. She loves getting news.

Notice how the dialogue uses d'abord, ensuite, après ça, and finalement to sequence the exchange, while il faisait très beau, j'étais tellement content, and pendant que je marchais supply background using the imparfait, exactly as in Chapter 18.

Speaking Practice

Pratique contrôlée — Controlled practice: put the story in order

Read the four unordered sentences below. Put them into a logical sequence, then retell the story aloud using d'abord, ensuite, puis, and finalement. More than one connector arrangement may be acceptable if the sequence remains logical.

  • Marc est rentré chez lui très tard.
  • Marc a rencontré un ancien voisin de sa grand-mère.
  • Marc s'est promené dans l'ancien quartier de sa grand-mère.
  • Le voisin a invité Marc chez lui.

Tâche communicative — Communicative task: raconte-moi ton week-end

With a partner, take turns asking Raconte-moi ton week-end. Qu'est-ce qui s'est passé ? Tell a connected story of at least six sentences. Use at least three sequencing connectors, at least two imparfait forms for background or description, and at least three passé composé forms for foregrounded events. The listener asks at least two relevant follow-up questions.

Pratique individuelle — Solo alternative

Record yourself narrating a memorable event from your own life, real or invented, meeting the same requirements as the communicative task above: at least six connected sentences, at least three sequencing connectors, at least two imparfait forms, and at least three passé composé forms. Then record two questions that a listener could reasonably ask about your story, and record a comprehensible response to each question.

Writing Workshop

Complete the following linked writing tasks in order.

Tâche 1 — Sentence connection: Put the following five sentences into a logical order and combine them into one connected passage. Use at least four appropriate sequencing connectors.

  • Nadia et Marc sont arrivés à Tours.
  • Ils ont pris le train samedi matin.
  • Marc a rencontré un ancien voisin de sa grand-mère.
  • Ils ont déjeuné chez Mireille.
  • Marc est rentré très tard.

Tâche 2 — Personal email: Write a short email (eight to ten sentences) to a friend or family member narrating a recent event. Include a greeting, at least three sequencing connectors, at least three passé composé verbs, at least two imparfait verbs for background or description, one question inviting the recipient to reply, and an appropriate informal closing.

  1. Choose your event. Pick a real or imagined event with at least three connected steps.
  2. List your events in order. Write a short list of what happened, step by step.
  3. Add background. Decide where description, weather, age, time, or ongoing situations belong, using the imparfait.
  4. Draft your email. Connect your events with sequencing connectors, and open and close appropriately.
  5. Read your email aloud once. Listen for enchaînement across connectors and correct auxiliary and agreement in the passé composé.
  6. Revise. Confirm every connector is used correctly and every past-tense verb matches its function.

Liste de révision — Editing checklist:

  • The email has a greeting and an appropriate informal closing.
  • At least three sequencing connectors organize the narration.
  • Foregrounded completed events normally use the passé composé in the controlled narratives practiced here, with correct auxiliary and agreement.
  • Background, description, habits, and ongoing situations normally use the imparfait when that is the intended narrative viewpoint.
  • Every tense choice matches the intended viewpoint and context.
  • Verb forms, articles, and adjective agreement are accurate throughout.
  • Accents, apostrophes, and punctuation are accurate throughout.
  • No vocabulary or grammar from outside Chapters 1–19 has been used.

Culture and Communication

Culture in Context — Staying in touch by writing

Sending a message, an email, or a letter to give personal news after a visit is a common and valued way to maintain family and friend relationships across the Francophone world, though the preferred medium — a phone call, a text message, an email, or a handwritten letter — varies by generation, family, and personal habit. Do not assume that every family follows the same pattern that Marc and Mireille do here.

Regional Variation — A note on this system

Narrative connectors such as d'abord, ensuite, puis, and finalement are used widely in everyday storytelling across the Francophone world. Informal spoken narration may also use après repeatedly as a conversational transition. This is a register and style difference rather than a strict correctness distinction. This course uses ensuite, puis, and après ça to help learners build a varied productive connector range.

Common Mistakes

Common Mistake — Stringing events together without connectors

Error: Writing Je suis arrivé. J'ai cherché un taxi. Je suis arrivé à l'hôtel. as three disconnected sentences.

Correction: Add connectors to show sequence: D'abord, je suis arrivé. Ensuite, j'ai cherché un taxi. Finalement, je suis arrivé à l'hôtel.

Common Mistake — Using the passé composé for all background description

Error: Writing Il a fait beau, et j'ai été content to describe the ongoing weather and mood surrounding an event.

Correction: In the controlled narrative pattern practiced here, background description normally takes the imparfait: Il faisait beau, et j'étais content.

Common Mistake — Overusing "et après" for every transition

Error: Repeating et après, et après, et après throughout an entire narration.

Correction: Vary connectors across the narration: d'abord, ensuite, puis, après ça, and finalement each mark a different point in the sequence.

One acceptable sequence is:

  1. D'abord, Marc s'est promené dans l'ancien quartier de sa grand-mère.

Another logically ordered connector sequence may also be accepted.
