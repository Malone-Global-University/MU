Unit II Assessment: A Practical Day · Speak and Write French: Foundations of Practical Communication

Chapter Objectives

By the end of this chapter, you will have demonstrated that you can:

  1. Describe a home, apartment, or neighborhood using il y a, il n'y a pas de, location prepositions, demonstrative determiners, and contractions with à and de.
  2. Explain a daily schedule using present-tense verbs, selected reflexive verbs, times, and frequency expressions.
  3. Use ne … pas and ne … pas de to express negation, including negation with reflexive verbs.
  4. Discuss food and drink using partitive articles, quantity expressions with de, and polite ordering formulas already taught.
  5. Use en to replace a partitive or quantity expression in authorized patterns.
  6. Describe clothing and other items using adjective placement, adjective agreement, and basic size and color vocabulary.
  7. Use direct-object pronouns le, la, l', and les before one finite present-tense verb.
  8. Work with numbers and prices from 0 through 100.
  9. Demonstrate comprehension of two short practical listening passages.
  10. Read and understand two short practical texts using only activated vocabulary and grammar.
  11. Write six to eight controlled sentences describing a practical day.
  12. Write a brief note of four to six sentences to a correspondent.
  13. Edit a short passage to identify and correct exactly ten errors involving activated Unit II systems.
Assessment chapter: Chapter 10 is the Unit II cumulative assessment. It introduces no new major grammar. Every task draws only on systems activated in Chapters 6–9, with continued recycling of material from Chapters 1–5. If a task is difficult, the chapter reference in each section header guides you back to the right review source.

Communication Scenario

A student from Abidjan, Côte d'Ivoire, is visiting your city for one week. You want to describe your apartment and neighborhood, plan a typical day together, discuss what to eat, and help her find a warm sweater to buy. Chapter 10 checks whether you have all the French you need for these four practical tasks.

  1. Think about each domain: describing where you live, explaining your daily routine, talking about food, and handling a shopping interaction. Each corresponds to one of the chapters in Unit II.
  2. Notice that real conversation about a day requires combining many systems at once — articles, verbs, negation, pronouns, and numbers all appear together. The tasks here let you practice exactly that kind of integration.
  3. After each task, note which areas feel solid and which feel less secure. Use the self-assessment checklist at the end to plan your review before Unit III.

How to Use This Chapter

Work through the sections in order. The grammar and pronunciation review sections are concise summaries — not re-teaching sections. If a system needs deeper review, go back to the chapter listed in brackets.

The five assessment tasks — listening, speaking, reading, writing, and editing — each have a clearly stated focus. The performance task at the end requires you to combine skills from all four domains.

Grammar Review

The tables and examples below summarize the major grammar systems introduced in Chapters 6–9. Chapter numbers in brackets show where each system was first taught.

Il y a and negative existence [Ch. 6]

  • Il y a un salon et deux chambres. There is a living room and two bedrooms.
  • Il n'y a pas de garage. There is no garage.
  • Il y a des commerces près d'ici. There are shops nearby.

After il n'y a pas, the article changes to de (or d' before a vowel sound), regardless of the gender or number of the noun that follows.

Location prepositions and contractions [Ch. 6]

Prepositions

  • dans — in, inside
  • sur — on
  • sous — under
  • devant — in front of
  • derrière — behind
  • entre — between
  • à côté de — next to
  • en face de — across from
  • près de — near
  • loin de — far from
  • chez — at the home of

Contractions

  • à + le → au
  • à + les → aux
  • de + le → du
  • de + les → des
  • à la, à l', de la, de l' do not contract.

Demonstrative determiners [Ch. 6]

Forms change with the gender and initial sound of the following noun.

Masculine singular, before vowel or mute h cet cet appartement this / that apartment
Feminine singular cette cette chambre this / that room

Present tense, reflexive verbs, and ne … pas [Ch. 7]

Present-tense verbs, reflexive verbs, and basic negation are the core systems of Chapter 7. In negation, ne … pas wraps around the conjugated verb. For reflexive verbs, ne goes before the reflexive pronoun and pas goes after the verb.

  • Je me lève à sept heures. I get up at seven o'clock.
  • Elle ne se couche pas tard. She doesn't go to bed late.
  • Nous ne travaillons pas le dimanche. We don't work on Sundays.
  • Il prend le bus à huit heures. He takes the bus at eight o'clock.
Grammar Reminder — Reflexive verbs in negation

Word order: ne + reflexive pronoun + verb + pas. Example: Il ne se rase pas (He doesn't shave). Do not place pas between the reflexive pronoun and the verb.

Partitive articles, quantity de, and en [Ch. 8]

Partitive articles express an indefinite quantity of something uncountable.
Context Article Example English
Masculine singulardudu painsome bread
Feminine singularde lade la confituresome jam
Before vowel or mute hde l'de l'eausome water
After negation (ne … pas)de / d'pas de sucreno sugar
After quantity expressionde / d'un kilo de tomatesa kilo of tomatoes

The pronoun en replaces a partitive noun phrase or a quantity expression. It is placed before the conjugated verb.

  • — Tu veux du café ? — Oui, j'en veux. Do you want some coffee? — Yes, I want some.
  • — Il y a du lait ? — Non, il n'y en a pas. Is there any milk? — No, there isn't any.
  • — Tu prends combien de croissants ? — J'en prends deux. How many croissants? — I'm taking two.

Keep en to these three patterns at this stage. Do not combine it with advanced clitic sequences or negation beyond ne … pas.

Adjective agreement, direct-object pronouns, and prices [Ch. 9]

Descriptive adjectives agree with the noun they describe. Most follow the noun. Direct-object pronouns le, la, l', les replace a definite noun phrase and are placed directly before the conjugated verb.

  • Je prends cette veste. → Je la prends. I'm taking this jacket. → I'm taking it.
  • Il aime ce pull rouge. → Il l'aime. He likes this red sweater. → He likes it.
  • Je ne prends pas le pantalon. → Je ne le prends pas. I'm not taking the trousers. → I'm not taking them.
Language Focus

Direct-object pronouns and word order in negation: The pronoun sits between ne and the verb: Je ne le prends pas. Word order: ne + pronoun + verb + pas. At this stage, keep direct-object pronouns before one finite present-tense verb only.

Regional Variation — Numbers in Belgium and Switzerland (Receptive)

Belgian French commonly uses septante for 70 and nonante for 90, while retaining quatre-vingts for 80. Swiss French commonly uses septante and nonante; huitante for 80 is used in some Swiss regions, while octante is rare or dated. These regional forms are standard in their communities. They appear here for recognition only — this course uses the metropolitan French counting system for all productive tasks.

Pronunciation and Spelling Review

The following pronunciation and orthography systems were introduced or developed in Unit II. Review each point before completing the written assessment tasks.

Pronunciation Review — Connected speech in Unit II phrases
  • Il y a as a rhythmic phrase: Il y a runs together in speech as a single rhythmic unit. This is connected pronunciation, not elision. Elision occurs only when a specific short word loses its final vowel before a vowel sound: n'y (from ne y), l'appartement, j'habite, de l'eau.
  • Elision — the rule: Certain short French words elide before a word beginning with a vowel sound or h muet. Common examples include le, la, je, me, te, se, ne, and de. Their final vowel is removed and replaced by an apostrophe: l'appartement, j'habite, n'y a pas, de l'eau. Elision does not occur before h aspiré.
  • Liaison in plural patterns: les enfants [lez‿ɑ̃fɑ̃]; nous avons [nuz‿avɔ̃]. These are mandatory liaison contexts that continue from earlier chapters throughout Unit II. The final written consonant of the first word is pronounced and links to the following vowel-initial syllable.
  • Enchaînement: A final pronounced consonant links naturally to the following vowel-initial syllable even without liaison. Example: une amie — the [n] of une flows into the vowel beginning amie; une école — the [n] of une flows into the first syllable of école. This resyllabification is normal and applies whenever a word ending in a pronounced consonant is directly followed by a vowel-initial word.
Pronunciation Review — Vowel contrasts in Unit II vocabulary
  • Front rounded vowels: deux contains [ø] — lips rounded, tongue forward and mid-high; beurre and heure each contain [œ] — the same rounded position but more open. The vowel in du is [y] — a high front rounded vowel, produced with the tongue high and the lips tightly rounded. [y], [ø], and [œ] are three distinct sounds.
  • Nasal vowels in numbers and articles: cent [sɑ̃]; vingt [vɛ̃]; un bon prix [ɛ̃ bɔ̃ pʁi]. Do not add a nasal consonant after the vowel in these words.
  • Partitive vowel contrasts: du [dy] — high front rounded — versus de la [də la] — schwa followed by open [a]. These differ in vowel height and lip rounding; the distinction helps listeners identify the article in context.

Orthography checklist for this assessment

Before submitting any written task in this chapter, verify:

  • Contractions are correct: au, aux, du, des — never à le, de le.
  • Apostrophes are correct in elision: de l'eau, il n'y a pas, je m'appelle.
  • Demonstrative determiners agree with the noun: ce / cet / cette / ces.
  • Adjective agreement endings are present and correct, including silent -e and -es endings.
  • Direct-object pronouns are spelled correctly before vowel-initial verbs: je l'aime (not *je le aime).
  • Partitive articles shift to bare de / d' after negation: je ne mange pas de viande.
  • Number words are hyphenated correctly: quatre-vingts, soixante-dix-neuf, quatre-vingt-un.
  • Days of the week and months are lowercase in French.
  • Written negation ne … pas is complete in all formal written tasks.
  • All required accents are present: réfrigérateur, déjeuner, boulangerie, épicerie.

Listening Note — Partitive articles in connected speech

In Passage B the speaker uses several partitives: du café, du pain, du beurre. In connected speech each article runs directly into the following noun. Listen for the article + noun pattern as a single rhythmic unit. Notice also the negative: je ne mange pas de céréales — the article shifts to bare de after negation.

Speaking Assessment

Task 2A — Controlled question and answer

Answer the following questions in complete, short sentences. Draw on vocabulary and grammar from Chapters 6–9.

  1. Où est-ce que tu habites ? Il y a combien de pièces chez toi ? Where do you live? How many rooms are there?

Task 2B — Short role-play (4–6 exchanges)

Work with a partner. Student A plays the visitor from Abidjan; Student B plays the host. Have a short conversation covering these points. Use tu throughout.

  2. Visitor: ask what time the host gets up and what they eat for breakfast.
  3. Host: answer, including one partitive article and one negative sentence.
  4. Visitor: say Je cherche un pull chaud. Ask: Il y a un magasin de vêtements près d'ici ? Host: confirm and state a color and a price within 0–100.

Task 2C — Solo alternative

Say six to eight short sentences describing your morning routine and what you want from the shops today. Include:

  • Two reflexive verbs.
  • One partitive article in a food or drink context.
  • One negative sentence with ne … pas or ne … pas de.
  • One item you are looking for (je cherche…) with its color and a price within 0–100.
Communication Note — Polite interaction in shops

In French-speaking shops and markets, it is normal to greet before making any request: Bonjour, madame / monsieur. A request framed as Je voudrais… (I would like…) or Bonjour, je cherche… (I'm looking for…) sounds natural and polite. To ask a price: C'est combien ? / Combien ça coûte ? To complete a purchase: Je le prends. / Je la prends. These formulas are used across the Francophone world.

Culture in Context — Dakar and Francophone West Africa

Dakar is the capital of Senegal, a West African country where French is the official language of government, education, and public life, alongside Wolof and other national languages. Several West and Central African countries use the franc CFA (Communauté Financière Africaine) as their currency. Francophone West Africa represents one of the world's largest and fastest-growing communities of French speakers, and its cities, literature, music, and daily culture are an important part of the global Francophone world.

Culture in Context — Brussels and Belgian French

Brussels is the capital of Belgium and the headquarters of the European Union and NATO. Belgium has three official languages — Dutch, French, and German — and three language communities: the Flemish Community, the French Community, and the German-speaking Community. Editing Assessment — Ten corrections

Error 1 — Contraction
à le → au
à + le must contract to au before a masculine singular noun. Corrected sentence: J'habite au troisième étage d'un immeuble moderne.

Error 2 — Demonstrative determiner
ce chambre → cette chambre
chambre is feminine singular; the correct demonstrative is cette, not ce. Corrected sentence: Il y a cette chambre et un salon.

Error 3 — Adjective gender agreement
une veste noir → une veste noire
veste is feminine singular; noir must add -e to agree: noire. Corrected sentence: Dans la chambre, il y a une veste noire.

Error 4 — Adjective number agreement
des chaises rouge → des chaises rouges
rouge must agree in number with the plural noun chaises: add -s. Corrected sentence: Les chaises dans le salon sont rouges.

Error 5 — Reflexive-pronoun form
je me se lève → je me lève
The reflexive pronoun for first-person singular is me. Only one reflexive pronoun is used. Corrected sentence: Le matin, je me lève à sept heures.

Error 6 — Partitive article
de la café → du café
café is masculine singular; the correct partitive is du, not de la. Corrected sentence: Je prends du café et du pain.

Error 7 — Article after ne … pas
pas du sucre → pas de sucre
After negation (ne … pas), the partitive article changes to bare de regardless of gender. Corrected sentence: Je ne mange pas de sucre.

Error 8 — Direct-object-pronoun gender
je le cherche → je la cherche
The pronoun replaces cette veste bleue, which is feminine singular. The correct direct-object pronoun is la, not le. Corrected sentence: Je cherche cette veste bleue — je la cherche dans le magasin.

Error 9 — Direct-object-pronoun placement
je prends la → je la prends
A direct-object pronoun must be placed before the conjugated verb, not after it. The pronoun la replaces cette veste bleue and must precede prends. Corrected sentence: Je la prends tout de suite.

Error 10 — Number spelling
quatre-vingts-cinq → quatre-vingt-cinq
When another digit follows quatre-vingts, the plural -s is dropped: quatre-vingt-cinq. Corrected sentence: Le pull coûte quatre-vingt-cinq euros.

Corrected passage in full

J'habite au troisième étage d'un immeuble moderne. Il y a cette chambre et un salon. Dans la chambre, il y a une veste noire. Les chaises dans le salon sont rouges. Le matin, je me lève à sept heures. Je prends du café et du pain. Je ne mange pas de sucre. Je cherche cette veste bleue — je la cherche dans le magasin. Je la prends tout de suite. Le pull coûte quatre-vingt-cinq euros.
