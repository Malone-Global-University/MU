Études et vie professionnelle

School and Work · Speak and Write French: Foundations of Practical Communication

Chapter Objectives

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

  1. Name common school subjects, workplaces, and job titles.
  2. Describe a course of study or a job in simple present-time terms.
  3. Use indirect object pronouns lui and leur to say to whom something is given, told, or explained.
  4. Compare people, things, and activities using plus... que, moins... que, and aussi... que.
  5. Connect ideas using the relative pronouns qui, que, and où.
  6. Understand and produce short, controlled exchanges about studies, jobs, and comparisons between them.
  7. Apply accurate liaison and enchaînement in phrases such as un examen and avec une collègue.
  8. Recycle vocabulary and grammar from Units I and II, and from Chapters 11–13, while communicating about a new domain.

Communication Scenario

Léa, now feeling much better, meets with a career counselor at her university to talk about her studies and her plans for a summer internship. The counselor asks about Léa's classes and compares two possible internships at two different companies. Later, Léa writes a short message to a friend comparing her preferred internship to another option. This chapter continues Unit III, Dans la communauté, by giving you the tools to talk about school, work, and how things compare to one another.

  1. Learn key vocabulary for school subjects, workplaces, and jobs.
  2. Use the indirect object pronouns lui and leur to talk about giving, telling, and explaining.
  3. Compare people and things with plus, moins, and aussi... que.
  4. Connect ideas with the relative pronouns qui, que, and où.

Pronunciation Focus

Pronunciation Focus — Liaison and enchaînement

Liaison makes a normally silent final consonant audible when the next word begins with a vowel sound. Enchaînement carries a final consonant that is already pronounced into the next vowel-initial word, so the two words flow together without a pause. School and work vocabulary give useful practice with both.

  • Liaison: in un examen, the normally silent -n of un is pronounced before the vowel-initial noun.
  • Liaison: in des étudiants, the normally silent -s of des is pronounced as a linking [z] before the vowel-initial noun.
  • Liaison: in plus intéressant, the final -s of plus links as [z] before the vowel-initial adjective.
  • Enchaînement: in avec une collègue, the final -c of avec, which is already pronounced [k], carries smoothly into the vowel-initial word that follows.

Pronunciation drill: Say each phrase aloud three times, linking the sounds as marked: un examen [œ̃n‿ɛgzamɛ̃], des étudiants [dez‿etydjɑ̃], plus intéressant [plyz‿ɛ̃teʁesɑ̃], and avec une collègue [avɛk‿yn kɔlɛg].

In some varieties of French, un merges with the nasal vowel in in, giving [ɛ̃] instead of [œ̃]. The broad-standard pronunciation taught here is [œ̃]; the merged form is a regional and individual variation, not an error.

Language Focus: Indirect Objects, Comparison, and Relative Clauses

Purpose: talk about school and work, say to whom something is given or explained, compare people and things, and connect ideas with relative clauses.

14.1 Indirect object pronouns lui and leur

Language Focus

An indirect object receives the action of a verb through the preposition à, usually a person: you telephone someone, you explain something to someone. French replaces à + [person] with lui (to him, to her) or leur (to them). Like the direct object pronouns you learned in Chapter 9, lui and leur are placed directly before the conjugated verb.

  • Je téléphone à la conseillère. → Je lui téléphone. I telephone the counselor. → I telephone her. à + la conseillère = lui
  • Le professeur explique la leçon aux employés. → Le professeur leur explique la leçon. The professor explains the lesson to the employees. → The professor explains the lesson to them. aux employés = leur
  • Elle ne lui donne pas son numéro. She doesn't give him/her their number. ne + lui + verb + pas

Lui and leur do not show gender: lui can mean "to him" or "to her," and the surrounding context makes the meaning clear. This is different from the direct object pronouns le and la, which you already know. In this chapter, lui and leur are used only with clear, explicitly named people or organizations, such as a specific company or a specific person mentioned just before the pronoun. Practice these exact transformations: à Sophie → lui; aux employés → leur; aux entreprises → leur.

14.2 Comparing people and things

Building Block — Making comparisons
plus / moins / aussi + [adjective] + que + [person or thing]
To compare two people or things, French places plus (more), moins (less), or aussi (as) before the adjective, and que before the second, explicitly named term of the comparison. The adjective still agrees with the noun it describes, exactly as you learned in Chapter 4.
Ce stage est plus intéressant que l'autre stage.
This internship is more interesting than the other internship.
Language Focus
  • Mon cours d'histoire est plus intéressant que mon cours de maths. My history class is more interesting than my math class.
  • Cette entreprise est aussi grande que l'université. This company is as big as the university.

French requires que before the second term of a comparison: plus intéressant que l'autre. English may sometimes omit a relative marker such as "that," but it does not normally omit "than" when the second term is stated. Note that this comparison que is a different word-function from the relative pronoun que you will learn next, even though the spelling is the same.

14.3 Relative clauses with qui, que, and où

Language Focus

A relative clause adds information about a noun without starting a new sentence. qui replaces the subject of the added clause, que replaces its direct object, and où replaces an expression of place.

  • J'ai un professeur qui explique très bien. I have a professor who explains very well. qui = subject of "explique"
  • Le stage que je cherche est à Paris. The internship (that) I am looking for is in Paris. que = direct object of "cherche"
  • C'est l'entreprise où travaille mon frère. That's the company where my brother works. où = place

Unlike English, French cannot drop que: "the internship I am looking for" must become le stage que je cherche, with que stated explicitly. This chapter asks you to use one relative clause at a time in your own writing and speaking; you do not need to combine qui, que, and où in the same sentence.

Model Dialogue: Un entretien avec la conseillère

Context: Léa meets with Madame Girard, a career counselor at her university, to talk about her studies and a possible summer internship.

Mme Girard : Quelle matière préférez-vous, Léa ? Which subject do you prefer, Léa?
Léa : Je préfère l'histoire. Mon cours d'histoire est plus intéressant que mon cours de maths. I prefer history. My history class is more interesting than my math class.
Mme Girard : Très bien. Vous cherchez un stage qui utilise le français ? Very good. Are you looking for an internship that uses French?
Léa : Oui. Je préfère une entreprise où on parle français tous les jours. Yes. I prefer a company where French is spoken every day.
Mme Girard : J'ai les coordonnées de deux entreprises. Je vais leur téléphoner cette semaine. I have the contact details of two companies. I'm going to telephone them this week.
Léa : La première entreprise est-elle aussi grande que la deuxième ? Is the first company as big as the second one?
Mme Girard : Non, elle est plus petite. La première entreprise semble intéressante pour vous. Je vais lui écrire aujourd'hui. No, it is smaller. The first company seems interesting for you. I'm going to write to it today.
Léa : Merci beaucoup, madame ! Je préfère le premier stage. Thank you very much, ma'am! I prefer the first internship.

Notice how leur refers clearly to the two companies (introduced as deux entreprises), how lui refers clearly to the one company named just before it (la première entreprise), how plus/aussi... que compares two explicitly named things, and how qui and où connect ideas without starting new sentences.

Writing Workshop

Complete the following linked writing tasks in order.

Tâche 1 — Compare two classes or jobs: Write three short sentences comparing two fictional classes, jobs, or internships, using at least two different comparative structures (plus... que, moins... que, aussi... que).

Tâche 2 — Supported message: Write a short message of four to six sentences to a friend about a fictional course, job, or internship. Include one greeting, one description, one comparison, one relative clause using qui, que, or où, one sentence using either lui or leur, and one closing. You do not need to use more than one relative pronoun.

Model message
Salut Amina ! Je cherche un stage qui est intéressant. La première entreprise est plus petite que la deuxième. La conseillère va lui écrire aujourd'hui. Je préfère le premier stage. À bientôt !
Hi Amina! I'm looking for an internship that is interesting. The first company is smaller than the second. The counselor is going to write to it today. I prefer the first internship. See you soon!
  1. List your ideas. Decide which fictional classes, jobs, or internships you want to describe and compare.
  2. Choose your comparison. Decide what is more, less, or equally interesting, difficult, or big.
  3. Draft your message. Move from greeting, to description, to comparison, to a closing.
  4. Read your message aloud once. Listen for liaison in phrases like plus intéressant.
  5. Check your relative clause. Confirm that que is never dropped and that qui, que, or où is used correctly.

Liste de révision — Editing checklist:

  • The message is four to six sentences long.
  • The message includes exactly one relative clause (qui, que, or où).
  • The message includes at least one comparative structure (plus/moins/aussi... que), correctly formed with que.
  • lui or leur appears at least once, correctly placed before the verb, with a clear antecedent.
  • Adjectives agree with the nouns they describe in comparisons.
  • No passé composé, imperfect, or conditional si clause appears anywhere in the message.
  • Accents, apostrophes, and punctuation are accurate throughout.
  • No vocabulary or grammar from outside Chapters 1–14 has been used.

Culture and Communication

Culture in Context — Internships in French university life

A stage is a common part of higher education in France and other Francophone settings. University career offices, sometimes called a bureau des stages, help students find internships that connect academic study to professional experience. Compensation requirements depend on duration and applicable regulations. In France, longer student internships may require minimum compensation. Exact rules, duration, academic requirements, and institutional practices vary by country, field, and institution.

Regional Variation — School and work vocabulary across the Francophone world

Educational vocabulary can differ across Francophone regions. In Quebec, for instance, le cégep refers to a stage of post-secondary education between high school and university that does not exist in the same form in France. Workplace vocabulary can also vary: some expressions common in Belgium or Switzerland differ from those used in France or in Francophone Africa. Internship practices likewise differ across the Francophone world. This is a receptive cultural note; the core vocabulary in this chapter remains broadly usable across the Francophone world.

Common Mistakes

Common Mistake — Confusing direct and indirect object pronouns

Error: Writing je la téléphone to mean "I telephone her."

Correction: téléphoner takes an indirect object with à, so the correct pronoun is lui: je lui téléphone.

Common Mistake — Dropping que in comparisons or relative clauses

Error: Writing ce stage est plus intéressant l'autre, or le stage je cherche.

Correction: French requires que in both structures: ce stage est plus intéressant que l'autre, and le stage que je cherche. English does not normally drop "than" in a comparison, but it can drop "that" in a relative clause; French never drops que in either case.

Common Mistake — Confusing qui and que

Error: Writing j'ai un professeur que explique très bien, using que where the professor is the subject of the added clause.

Correction: Since the professor is the subject of "explique," use qui: j'ai un professeur qui explique très bien. Use que only when the noun is the direct object of the added clause.
