Se déplacer en ville

Getting Around Town · Speak and Write French: Foundations of Practical Communication

Chapter Objectives

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

  1. Name a small set of essential places and transportation expressions in a town or city.
  2. Ask how to reach a place using simple, direct questions.
  3. Give basic directions using motion verbs such as aller, prendre, continuer, tourner, and traverser.
  4. Give instructions using the imperative, mainly in the vous form, with limited tu use.
  5. Use the pronoun y in controlled sentences to refer to a place already named.
  6. Sequence directions using d'abord, puis/ensuite, and enfin.
  7. Recognize the previously consolidated liaison and elision patterns as they appear in direction-related phrases.
  8. Recycle vocabulary and grammar from Units I and II while communicating about a new domain.

Communication Scenario

Léa has just moved to a new neighborhood, and today she needs help finding her way around: reaching the train station and locating the nearest pharmacy. Along the way, she asks a stranger for directions. This chapter opens Unit III, Dans la communauté, by giving you the tools to ask for, understand, and give simple directions in French.

  1. Learn a focused set of transportation and place vocabulary connected to getting around a town.
  2. Ask direct questions about location and direction.
  3. Give instructions using the imperative.
  4. Use y in controlled sentences to talk about a place without repeating its name.

Pronunciation Focus

Pronunciation Focus — French r, liaison, and enchaînement in direction language

This chapter recycles the French r, already active since Chapter 1, in several high-frequency direction verbs: tournez, traversez, prenez, and près. The French r is produced toward the back of the mouth, unlike the English r. Practice it slowly in these direction verbs before speeding up.

This chapter also recycles two already-consolidated connected-speech patterns:

  • Liaison — a normally silent final consonant is pronounced before a following vowel sound, in an authorized grammatical environment. Examples: les arrêts, un arrêt.
  • Enchaînement — a final consonant that is already pronounced in isolation moves smoothly into the next vowel-initial word. Examples: avec elle, une grande avenue.

Liaison depends on a silent consonant becoming audible; enchaînement depends on a consonant that was already audible simply linking forward. This chapter does not introduce any new liaison or enchaînement environment beyond what earlier chapters have already consolidated, and it does not treat optional verb liaison as automatic.

Language Focus: Motion, Directions, and the Imperative

Purpose: ask for and give simple directions, using motion verbs, the imperative, and the pronoun y.

11.1 Asking for directions

Language Focus

Questions about location and direction use direct question forms you already know: où and est-ce que. To ask how to get somewhere, French commonly uses pour aller à... followed by the destination.

  • Pour aller à la gare, s'il vous plaît ? How do I get to the train station, please?
  • Où est la pharmacie ? Where is the pharmacy?
  • Il y a un arrêt de bus près d'ici ? Is there a bus stop near here?

11.2 Aller and venir

Language Focus

Aller describes movement away from the current reference point, toward a destination. Venir describes movement toward the speaker, the listener, or another point already established in the conversation. Both verbs can be followed by a destination; the difference is about perspective, not about whether a destination is mentioned. Origin — where someone is coming from — is expressed with de, not by the choice of verb alone.

  • Je vais à la gare. I am going to the train station. (movement away from here)
  • Tu viens chez moi ce soir ? Are you coming to my place tonight? (movement toward the speaker)
  • Elle vient de Marseille. She comes from Marseille. (origin, expressed with de)

11.3 The imperative for instructions

Language Focus

The imperative gives instructions directly, without a subject pronoun. For regular -er verbs, the tu form drops the final written -s from the present-tense form. The vous form is identical to the present-tense vous form. This chapter prioritizes the vous imperative, since asking a stranger for directions normally calls for vous; the tu form appears only where the relationship is clearly informal.

  • Tournez à gauche. (vous) Turn left.
  • Tourne à gauche. (tu) Turn left. tourner → tu tournes → tourne (no -s)
  • Continuez tout droit. Puis, traversez le pont. Continue straight ahead. Then, cross the bridge.
  • Ne tournez pas ici. Don't turn here.

11.4 Introducing the pronoun y

Building Block — Using y for a place already mentioned
[subject] + y + [verb]
y replaces a place that has already been named, usually introduced by à, chez, or dans. It is placed directly before the conjugated verb, in controlled declarative sentences, or attached after the imperative in the fixed forms vas-y and allons-y.
Tu vas à la gare ? — Oui, j'y vais maintenant.
Are you going to the train station? — Yes, I'm going there now.

This chapter develops y only before a finite (conjugated) verb, such as j'y vais or vous y allez. Placing y before an infinitive, or combining it with another pronoun such as en, is not part of this chapter. Before y, the imperative of aller keeps its final -s: vas-y, not va-y. Use y only where it naturally replaces a place you have already named — not in every sentence.

Model Dialogue: Pour aller à la gare ?

Context: Léa, new to the neighborhood, stops a passerby to ask for directions to the train station.

Léa : Excusez-moi, madame. Pour aller à la gare, s'il vous plaît ? Excuse me, ma'am. How do I get to the train station, please?
La dame : Bien sûr. D'abord, continuez tout droit jusqu'au carrefour. Ensuite, tournez à gauche. Of course. First, continue straight ahead to the intersection. Then, turn left.
Léa : D'accord. Et c'est loin d'ici ? Okay. And is it far from here?
La dame : Non, ce n'est pas très loin. Traversez le pont. La gare est juste en face, à côté de la pharmacie. No, it's not very far. Cross the bridge. The station is right opposite, next to the pharmacy.
Léa : Merci beaucoup ! Et l'arrêt de bus, il est aussi près d'ici ? Thank you very much! And the bus stop — is it also near here?
La dame : Oui. L'arrêt est juste là, devant la pharmacie. Yes. The stop is right there, in front of the pharmacy.
Léa : Parfait, j'y vais maintenant. Merci encore ! Perfect, I'm going there now. Thanks again!

Notice how y replaces à l'arrêt de bus, the most recently named place, and how d'abord and ensuite order the woman's first two instructions.

Speaking Practice

Pratique contrôlée — Controlled practice: give one instruction

Using the vous imperative, give one true instruction for how to get from your home to a nearby place, using one motion verb.

Échange guidé — Guided short exchange

With a partner, use the fictional route from the Reading Practice section to act out one short exchange:

  1. Learner A asks for directions to the museum: Pour aller au musée, s'il vous plaît ?
  2. Learner B gives three or four ordered directions, using d'abord, puis/ensuite, and enfin.
  3. Learner A asks one confirmation or distance question, such as C'est loin ?
  4. Learner B answers briefly, using y naturally if the museum has already been named.
  5. Both close politely with merci / de rien.

Notes and the fictional route are allowed as support.

Writing Workshop

Using the fictional route from the Reading Practice section, write four to six short sentences explaining how to go from the train station to the museum.

  1. Open politely. Begin with a short greeting or opening phrase, such as Bonjour. Pour aller au musée, c'est facile.
  2. Give three or four ordered instructions. Use d'abord, puis/ensuite, and enfin with the vous imperative.
  3. Include one distance or time expression. For example, C'est à dix minutes à pied.
  4. Include one natural use of y. Refer back to the museum once it has already been named.
  5. Read your route aloud once. Listen for the French r in your direction verbs.

Modèle — Model:

Bonjour. Pour aller au musée, depuis la gare, tournez à droite. Ensuite, continuez tout droit dans la rue Victor-Hugo. Puis, traversez le pont. Le musée est en face de la pharmacie. C'est à environ dix minutes à pied. J'y vais souvent le week-end.
Hello. To get to the museum, from the station, turn right. Then, continue straight ahead on rue Victor-Hugo. Then, cross the bridge. The museum is opposite the pharmacy. It's about ten minutes on foot. I often go there on weekends.

Liste de révision — Editing checklist:

  • The route includes a greeting or opening.
  • The route includes three or four ordered instructions with sequencing words.
  • One distance or time expression appears.
  • y appears once, naturally, referring to the museum after it has already been named.
  • Accents, apostrophes, and punctuation are accurate throughout.
  • No vocabulary or grammar from outside Chapters 1–11 has been used.

Culture and Communication

Culture in Context — Asking strangers for directions

In many French-speaking communities, addressing a stranger to ask for directions typically opens with a polite formula such as excusez-moi, followed by madame or monsieur, before the question itself. Skipping this opening can come across as abrupt, even when the underlying request is simple. In an exchange like this, vous may address one unfamiliar adult politely, or more than one person; the surrounding context tells you which meaning applies.

Regional Variation — Getting around across the Francophone world

Public transportation options vary across the Francophone world: some cities rely heavily on le métro, others mainly on le bus, and rural areas may have limited public transportation. This variation is receptive awareness only; the productive standard for this chapter remains the vocabulary presented above.

Common Mistakes

Common Mistake — Keeping the final -s in the tu imperative

Error: Writing tournes à gauche as an instruction.

Correction: The tu imperative of regular -er verbs drops the final -s: tourne à gauche. This applies only to the imperative, not to the ordinary present tense.

Common Mistake — Placing y after the verb

Error: Writing je vais y instead of the correct word order.

Correction: The pronoun y comes directly before the conjugated verb: j'y vais. Do not place it after the verb, as English word order might suggest.

Common Mistake — Removing the -s before y in the imperative

Error: Writing va-y as the instruction "go ahead."

Correction: Before y, the tu imperative of aller keeps its final -s: vas-y. Learn this as a fixed formula.
